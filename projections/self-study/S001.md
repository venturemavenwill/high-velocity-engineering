# Session 1 — Deciding whether to build it

*Self-study edition. Part of the [self-study projection](/projections/self-study/README.md). The facilitated version of this session is [S001](/wiki/seminars/S001.md), and the paper defending its design is [WP-001](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-001.md).*

---

## Before you read anything else

**This page is arranged in a deliberate order, and the order is load-bearing.**

Two of the activities below only work if you meet them before you meet the explanation. If you read the explanations first, they stop being activities and become demonstrations of something you already know.

There is a reason for this beyond preference. The design this session uses was tested with and without help during the opening phase, and the version that helped learners toward the answer produced *less* learning, not more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). The gating below is not stylistic.

**Work through the numbered sections in order. Do not skip to a section marked "read this after".**

**Time.** Roughly three hours if you do the workflow, of which about 25 minutes is section 1. You can split it across two sittings at the break marked in section 4. Section 9 needs a calendar and five minutes.

**What you need.** A text editor with GitHub Copilot, or any AI assistant that can be given custom instructions and can fetch web pages. Section 5 says what to do if you have neither.

## 1 · Answer these first (25 minutes, write them down)

The brief you are working from is in [Appendix A](#appendix-a--the-brief). Read it once, then answer the five questions below **in writing**, before reading anything else on this page.

Writing them down matters. You will be asked to compare your answers against a criterion later, and a remembered answer is not comparable — you will unconsciously improve it. **Write them in a file you will not edit.**

1. The customer describes a solution in unusual technical detail. What does that specificity tell you?
2. What would have to be true for the right answer to be **do not build this**?
3. Who in the room loses if this succeeds?
4. The brief assumes a platform capability exists. How would you check, in under ten minutes, without asking the customer?
5. What could you learn today that would stop the engagement?

**Do not look anything up. Do not use an assistant.** A wrong answer written now is worth more to you than a right answer retrieved now, and that is a finding rather than an encouragement — see section 7.

> **Checkpoint.** Do you have five written answers? If any is blank, write *"I have no answer"* rather than leaving it empty. A recorded blank is data; an empty line is not.

## 2 · Why this session is about qualification (15 minutes)

Forward-deployed engineering places engineers inside the customer's context rather than behind a product boundary. The work starts with a person and a problem rather than with a backlog.

That means the first real decision is not *how* but *whether* — and it is made when you know least. You have a brief, a room, and a date. You do not yet have the problem.

**Here is the uncomfortable part, and it is the honest starting point for this whole programme.**

The largest source behind this material is a body of internal practice documentation from a customer-embedded engineering organisation inside a large technology vendor. It is detailed and experienced. **It contains no worked example of declining an engagement.** Its case studies all succeed. Its only failure narrative is acknowledged as synthetic. It states no exclusion criteria — no domain, scale or regulatory limit, and no description of a customer who would be unsuitable.

There is one recorded refusal: a proposal to replace human reviewers in a legal-review process was judged irresponsible, because those reviewers catch issues with legal consequences.

So this session is **written past its source**. The argument is that a practice which cannot refuse work cannot be said to exercise judgement.

**That is an argument, not a finding.** Nothing anywhere establishes that qualification improves outcomes. If you want to disagree with this session, that is the place to push, and the disagreement is respectable.

> **Checkpoint — answer before continuing.** In one sentence: what is the difference between *this practice does not document refusing* and *this practice does not refuse*? Which one does the evidence actually support?
>
> *(The evidence supports the first. The session assumes something closer to the second. That gap is real and this page is not going to close it.)*

## 3 · Why the customer's account is weak evidence (20 minutes)

People give unreliable answers about their own problems, and not because they are dishonest.

Fitzpatrick's argument is that people will tell you what they can see would please you ([research/05-fde-craft/fitzpatrick-the-mom-test.md](/research/05-fde-craft/fitzpatrick-the-mom-test.md)). You asked a question with a visible preferred answer, and got it.

The repair has two parts:

- **Ask about past behaviour, not future intention.** *"Would you use a tool that did X?"* asks about a hypothetical self. *"Walk me through the last time this went wrong"* asks about an event that either happened or did not.
- **Ask for specifics, not generalities.** A general answer is a summary someone has already made, and the summarising is where the information was lost.

Three kinds of bad data, each with a repair:

| What you get | Why it is worthless | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **Compliments** | Measures your rapport, not their problem | Deflect and return to a specific past event |
| **Generalities** | A pre-made summary; the detail is gone | Anchor to one concrete episode: *when did that last happen?* |
| **Unbidden feature requests** | A solution wearing the clothes of a need | Dig for the motivation under it, rather than accepting or refusing |

**Limits of this source, since you are reading alone and cannot ask.** It is a practitioner book, not a study. It reports no measurement. The copy this repository read carries no publisher, year or edition. Use it as a well-argued heuristic.

> **Checkpoint.** Rewrite this to pass: *"Do you think your team would adopt an agent for this?"*
>
> *(Something like: "What was the last new tool this team was asked to adopt, and what happened?" The test is whether the answer is a memory or a prediction.)*

## 4 · Specificity is a signal, not a specification (10 minutes)

A brief stated with unusual precision means thinking has already happened somewhere you cannot see. Someone chose that architecture and rejected alternatives.

**The brief is the output of a decision process you were not part of.** The interesting content is the process.

Three shapes this takes:

- **The solved problem** — they arrived with a solution and never articulated a problem. *Sign:* the request names a product before it names a difficulty.
- **The buried problem** — the real difficulty is mentioned once, in passing, inside a conversation about something else. *Sign:* you leave with a clear brief and no surprise.
- **The absent problem** — they have genuinely lost track of what they were solving, because it moved across handoffs. *Sign:* nobody can say who first raised it.

> **Checkpoint.** Go back to [Appendix A](#appendix-a--the-brief). Which of the three is it? Write your answer down; [Appendix C](#appendix-c--what-the-brief-was-hiding) tells you what was planted, and you should not read it yet.

**— Natural break. If you are splitting this across two sittings, stop here. —**

## 5 · The platform decision, and checking one claim yourself (45 minutes)

This is where qualification stops being conversation and becomes engineering.

Most briefs contain an assumed platform capability. The customer believes something is possible, available in their region, or compatible with their compliance posture. **That belief is checkable, and checking it is qualification.**

### 5.1 The three axes

Microsoft's Cloud Adoption Framework states the criteria for choosing an agent build path as **technical capabilities, timeline, and control requirements** ([research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md)). Capability, speed, control. The triple generalises well past agent-building.

The same guidance splits build paths by service model: **Foundry** as platform-as-a-service with your choice of models; **Copilot Studio** as software-as-a-service for low-code building; **GPUs and containers** as infrastructure-as-a-service.

Two published statements are directly useful in qualification because briefs routinely assume them away:

- **Foundry's basic setup "lacks network isolation"** and is described as being for rapid prototyping. **Prototyping topology and production topology are different things, and moving between them is a deliberate act** rather than a late configuration detail.
- **Copilot Studio is "designed for small-scale deployments, such as department-level solutions."** A brief assuming enterprise-wide rollout on that tool is outside its stated envelope, and the symptoms appear as governance and lifecycle problems rather than as functional failures.

> **Read this as what it is.** This is first-party product documentation written to guide selection. Its authority is *definitional* — it tells you what the products are, not that choosing them this way works. It publishes no adoption data, no failure rates and no cost comparisons.

> **Perishability warning, and it applies to this whole section.** Product names, tier names and feature boundaries in this area change frequently, and the note behind this section marks itself highly perishable. **If what you find today contradicts what is written above, what you found is right and this page is stale.** Record the date you checked. Treat the three axes as durable and every product name here as a dated example.

### 5.2 The check

**Goal: test one feasibility claim in the brief against first-party documentation, without asking the customer.**

The tool is the Microsoft Learn MCP Server attached to an AI assistant in agent mode. It is a remote endpoint, needs no authentication and costs nothing, which is why this programme opens with it — you can hold one regardless of licensing.

**Step 1.** Add the Learn MCP Server to your editor's MCP configuration.

**Step 2.** **Confirm the agent will actually use it.** Attaching a server is not the same as invoking its tools, and the gap fails silently rather than loudly — declining the trust prompt leaves your requests running without the tools and without an error. *A connected server is an available tool, not a used one.*

**Step 3.** Write an instructions file so that a missing answer becomes visible instead of being smoothed over:

```markdown
---
applyTo: "**/*.md"
---

# Feasibility checking

- Every claim about a platform capability MUST carry a citation to a fetched
  documentation page, in the same sentence as the claim.
- If no fetched source supports a claim, say "not found in the documentation"
  rather than answering from general knowledge.
- Always record the date the page was fetched.
```

**Step 4.** **Verify the file actually reached the model.** Send one prompt, then look at your assistant's request log or output channel and confirm the instruction text is in the payload. *An instructions file you have not seen in a payload is a file, not a specification.*

**Step 5.** Ask your question. For the Appendix A brief: *does the tool named here support the deployment scale the brief assumes, and where is that stated?*

**Step 6.** Read the answer for what is **not** there. The in-sentence citation rule is what makes an absence visible.

**Step 7.** Ask it to argue that the engagement should be declined. Keep the transcript.

> **If you cannot run this.** Do steps 5 and 6 by hand: search the vendor's documentation site for the tool named in the brief plus the word *scale* or *limits*, and record what you find and the date. You lose the step-4 lesson about specifications not arriving, which is worth returning for later. **This substitute is weaker and you should know which part you are missing.**

> **Checkpoint.** What did the assistant say that carried no citation? That sentence is the one to distrust — not because it is wrong, but because nothing in it tells you whether it is.

## 6 · Read this after sections 1 and 5 — the comparison (25 minutes)

**This is the part that does the work, and it is the part most readers skip.**

Presenting a correct answer next to someone's wrong one produces no measurable benefit on its own. Only learners *instructed to compare them* improved. The instruction is the mechanism, not the exposure.

So: open the file with your five answers from section 1. Do not edit it. Alongside it, work through this.

### 6.1 Your item 2 against the criterion

**The criterion.** A disqualifying condition passes if a reader can describe an observation that would trigger it.

- *"If the customer is not committed"* — **fails.** No observation triggers it.
- *"If no named person in operations will give two hours a week for six weeks"* — **passes.** Someone either agrees or does not.

Now write, in one sentence each:

1. What is the difference between what you wrote for item 2 and the criterion?
2. Could your condition ever fire? Describe the scenario, or write *"it could not"*.
3. Rewrite it so it can.

**If you wrote "I have no answer" for item 2, that is the most common result and it is the one the session is designed to produce.** Write your condition now, from the criterion.

### 6.2 Your item 4 against the three axes

Which of capability, timeline and control did your item-4 answer not think to check?

**That omission is worth more to you than the recall was**, because it is specific to how you already think.

### 6.3 Your item 1 against section 4

You wrote something about what specificity tells you. Compare it to the three shapes. Did you treat specificity as *information about the problem* or as *information about a decision made elsewhere*?

## 7 · What this does not establish (10 minutes)

Read this before you decide how much to believe.

- **Nothing here shows qualification improves outcomes.** No source measures it. The practice corpus records one refusal and no declined engagement, and publishes no outcome data of any kind.
- **The platform statements are definitional and dated**, from documentation that publishes no adoption or failure-rate data.
- **The teaching design's evidence comes from a population unlike you.** The Productive Failure studies behind section 1's gating are school, university and laboratory studies. Its author states it works in adult and corporate settings and **cites nothing for that claim**. Applying it to a working professional is an extrapolation. It is a reasonable one — an independent workplace-training literature reaches the same ordering conclusion ([research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md](/research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md)) — but it is an extrapolation and you should hold it as one.
- **Your instrument is unreliable in a known direction.** AI capability is jagged: the boundary between competence and failure does not track how hard a task feels, and outside it you get confident error rather than refusal ([research/04-professional-formation/dellacqua-jagged-technological-frontier.md](/research/04-professional-formation/dellacqua-jagged-technological-frontier.md)). Separately, experienced developers have been observed to be slowed by AI assistance while believing they were sped up. **The note behind the first of those rests on an abstract; its shape is usable and no number in it is.**
- **No effect size appears anywhere in this projection.** That is a standing rule of this repository. If you find a magnitude quoted here, it is a defect.

**The largest thing this page cannot give you.** Refusing work is a social act before it is an analytical one, and whether it is available to you depends on whether dissent is survivable where you work. That is an environmental condition, not a skill, and **a page cannot install it.** You may leave here able to construct a disqualifying condition and unable to say it out loud. The facilitated programme addresses this two stages later; this page raises it now because reading alone means nobody else will.

## 8 · Two predictions about yourself (10 minutes)

**Read the resolution rules before you predict.** A prediction whose resolution rule you wrote afterwards is not an instrument.

**Resolution rules, fixed now:**
- P1 resolves when you finish section 6.1 — *did your original item-2 condition survive unchanged?* Binary. No partial credit.
- P2 resolves at your day-30 review in section 9 — *can you state the three axes without looking?* Binary.

**Now predict.** Write **yes** or **no** for each:

- **P1.** My item-2 answer, as originally written, described a condition capable of firing.
- **P2.** In thirty days I will be able to name the three selection axes unprompted.

**A caution about what this measures.** The facilitated session resolves these against a cohort. You are one person. **A single observation about yourself is not evidence about people in general**, and if P1 resolves *yes* for you that does not make the session's premise wrong — it makes you one case. Treat it as information about your own calibration and nothing else.

## 9 · The schedule (5 minutes now, then as dated)

Retrieval works when it is effortful and spaced. The gaps below are chosen to be uncomfortable rather than convenient, and the prompts are written out because *"review your notes"* is not a retrieval prompt.

| When | Do this, closed book | Then |
|---|---|---|
| **Tomorrow** | Write the three selection axes. Write your disqualifying condition from memory. | Check against section 5.1 and your section 6.1 rewrite |
| **In 3 days** | Write the three kinds of bad conversational data and one repair for each. | Check against the table in section 3 |
| **In 10 days** | Take any real request you have received at work. Write a disqualifying condition for it that could fire. | Apply the section 6.1 criterion yourself |
| **In 30 days** | Resolve P2. Then: what is the difference between a practice that does not document refusing and one that does not refuse? | Compare against your section 2 checkpoint answer |

**On the 10-day item.** That is the first one that is not recall. It is the one that matters, and it is the one you are most likely to skip because it takes longer and has no answer key.

## Appendix A — the brief

> **This is a constructed brief, not a real engagement.** It was written for this exercise. Do not cite it as a case.

**From:** Director of Operations, a mid-sized logistics firm
**To:** you

> We need an AI agent in Copilot Studio that reads our carrier exception emails and updates the shipment records automatically. We want it live across all four regions by the end of the quarter — that's about 900 people in operations touching it daily. Legal has signed off in principle. We've already mapped the email formats, and we know the connector we need. Our team lead Priya has done the flow design; she just needs an engineer to finish it.
>
> Honestly the main thing is speed. Last quarter we lost two contracts over exception handling delays and the board is watching.

**Three things are planted in this brief.** [Appendix C](#appendix-c--what-the-brief-was-hiding) names them, after you have done section 4.

## Appendix B — the five items, and what each is for

> Read this only after section 6.

| Item | What it is testing | What most people write |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Whether you read specificity as information about the problem or about a prior decision | A statement about the customer's clarity or competence |
| 2 | **Whether you can construct a falsifiable stopping condition** | Nothing, or something that could never fire |
| 3 | Whether you consider the political shape of the room | Usually answered well |
| 4 | Whether you have a checking reflex at all | A plan to ask the customer — which was excluded by the question |
| 5 | Whether items 2 and 4 connect | Rarely connected to 2 |

**Item 2 is the session.** Items 1, 3 and 4 exist so that its emptiness is visible to you by contrast. If all five had been hard, you would have concluded the questions were unfair rather than that one of them was different.

## Appendix C — what the brief was hiding

> Read this only after section 4.

**1 · The scale claim contradicts the tool's stated envelope.** 900 daily users across four regions, on a product the vendor positions for department-level deployments. That is checkable in ten minutes and is exactly the section 5 exercise.

**2 · The problem is buried and it is not exception emails.** *"We lost two contracts over exception handling delays."* The stated problem is email parsing. The felt problem is contract loss. Those may have different causes, and nothing in the brief connects them.

**3 · The decision was made elsewhere and by someone not in the room.** *"Priya has done the flow design; she just needs an engineer to finish it."* The architecture is settled, the alternatives are already rejected, and the person who rejected them is not the person briefing you. **"Just needs an engineer to finish it" is the single most informative phrase in the brief.**

**A fourth, if you found it, is worth more than the three.** *"Legal has signed off in principle"* — in principle is not sign-off, and this is an automated system acting on records with contractual consequences. The one recorded refusal in the entire practice corpus behind this programme was about exactly this shape: automating away a review step that catches issues with legal consequences.

## Appendix D — what you are missing by working alone

**Stated plainly, because a substitute presented as an equivalent is a worse problem than an acknowledged gap.**

| What the room gives | Why it cannot be simulated | Substitute offered here | Honest strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| Someone whose only job is to say whether your condition could fire | An adversary you construct shares your blind spots | The section 6.1 criterion, applied by you | **Weaker.** You will pass conditions a stranger would not |
| A cohort distribution to resolve predictions against | One person is not a distribution | Section 8 resolves against yourself, and says so | **Much weaker.** One observation |
| Hearing others' item-2 answers | The variety is the lesson | [Appendix B](#appendix-b--the-five-items-and-what-each-is-for)'s "what most people write" column | **Partial.** Second-hand, and not measured |
| Someone senior disclosing a real engagement they should have declined | The safety comes from the status asymmetry | **None offered.** | — |

**The last row has no substitute and none is invented.** It is the single largest thing lost by working alone in this session, and the honest response is to name it rather than to offer a journalling prompt in its place.
