# Q8 · Stage 8 · Transfer

**Sessions:** [S071](/wiki/seminars/S071.md)–[S080](/wiki/seminars/S080.md) · **Modules:** [M15](/wiki/modules/M15.md), [M16](/wiki/modules/M16.md)
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** one in every session — an agent configuration corpus read at a pinned commit, APIM AI gateway policies with Foundry deployment configuration, Microsoft Agent Framework evaluations, Microsoft Purview DSPM for AI, custom instructions as transferable capability, Foundry hosted agents and their operating burden, and the whole stack executed by the recipient unaided
**The question this stage answers:** can they run it without you?

---

## What this stage is for

Something is running in production. It was built by people who are leaving. This stage covers the interval between those two facts.

The interval has two halves and they are frequently confused. The first is **estate hygiene** — the orderly removal of the delivery team's own assets, access and credentials from a customer's environment. The second is **transfer of a going concern** — putting a running system, and the ability to keep it running, into somebody else's hands.

**Only the second is an engineering problem.** The first is a control problem, it is well understood, and it can be executed to a high standard by an organisation that has never once asked whether the customer can operate what it left behind.

## Why this stage is where the source runs out

The practice corpus behind this programme is unusually good on the first half and silent on the second, and the asymmetry is the most instructive thing in it.

On hygiene it is excellent. **The withdrawal trigger is a state change in an authoritative register rather than a person remembering** — every environment is tagged to a project, the register is declared the source of truth for project status, and marking a project closed is what initiates decommissioning. Reusing an environment after project close is named a policy violation rather than an inefficiency, explicitly because the asset is bound to a project identity. Access revocation is a two-party act. A person's departure is treated as a security event with consequences across the organisational boundary, triggering credential rotation inside the customer's estate, performed jointly with the customer. That is the work of people who have handled incidents.

On transfer there is **nothing**. No description of handing over the running system, its infrastructure definitions, its pipelines, its runbooks, its evaluation harness, its cost model or its monitoring. No named receiving owner. No exit criteria. No acceptance. No support taper. **And no evidentiary requirement that the teardown was performed at all** — the trigger is mechanised and the proof is not.

The model on offer asks *is our house clean?* It does not ask *can they run it without us?* The one place the second instinct surfaces — that the customer should be left holding the cost-modelling capability, because they will need it once the team disengages — appears as a reflection inside a narrative about business value, not as a step in any exit procedure.

So: **[M15](/wiki/modules/M15.md) teaches the source's mechanism and completes it. [M16](/wiki/modules/M16.md) is written past the source entirely**, and every session in it says so.

## What it inherits from Stage 7

Stage 8 does not re-derive these and is entitled to send them back.

- **An operational readiness argument** with the monitoring that supports it, and an incident history that is not empty. Produced across S061–S065.
- **A measurement the learner will defend**, with its baseline, its attribution position and the name of whoever is responsible for establishing causation. Produced across S066–S070.
- **A cost model**, built while the system was being designed rather than after. Produced at S064.

A stage that inherits none of these can still run its hygiene half. It cannot run its transfer half, because there is nothing to transfer except source code.

## What it hands to Stage 9

- **A transfer manifest with named receiving owners**, including at least one item recorded as unaccepted, with the consequence of that stated.
- **A rehearsal record naming what the recipient could not do.** Stage 9 asks what improves next time; an engagement that produced no observed incapacity has not supplied it with anything to work on.
- **A dependency disposal record** — what was transferred, what was simplified away, what was documented and accepted, and what was removed.

## The strands active here

- **PE — professional engagement.** What the engineer is accountable for, to whom, and what happens at the boundary where their judgement meets somebody else's authority. This stage is where the boundary is literally crossed: credentials, environments and obligations move from one organisation to another.
- **ME — measurement and evidence.** In this stage ME is mostly about **evidence of an act** rather than evidence of an effect. A teardown that left no artefact did not demonstrably happen; a capability nobody observed is a hope.

## The supplied situation

**This is invented for this programme.** No organisation, customer, supplier, sector body or individual described here exists, and nothing in it is drawn from any engagement. It continues [Brief A](/wiki/quarters/Q1.md) — the building-systems maintenance operator — because a transfer stage that begins with a fresh scenario would let learners skip the part where they meet their own earlier decisions.

### Where the engagement has got to

A system is live at a subset of the operator's sites. It drafts fields of the compliance record from the field engineers' free-text notes; a back-office reviewer accepts, amends or rejects each draft; rejections are logged with a reason code. It has been running long enough to have an incident history.

**What exists as artefacts.** Infrastructure definitions and application code in a repository. A deployment pipeline. A monitoring dashboard. An evaluation set assembled from records the external assessor previously returned. A cost model. An unmitigated-risk list from the security work. An impact assessment. A measurement baseline, established later than it should have been and covering only part of the record.

**Who is on the receiving side.**

- **The operations director**, the original sponsor. Still supportive, no longer close to it, and has a new priority.
- **A back-office team leader**, who has become the de facto operator: they read the rejection log, they notice when drafts get worse, and they are the only person outside the delivery team who has ever changed a routing threshold. They hold no budget, no change authority and no access to the cloud subscription.
- **A small technology function** that runs the handheld application and procures everything else. They did not ask for this system and were not in the room when it was scoped.
- **A managed-service supplier** that operates the operator's cloud estate under a contract. They will hold the credentials after the delivery team leaves. Nobody from the supplier has attended a single session of the engagement.
- **The external assessor**, who samples compliance records and has never spoken to anyone technical at the operator.

### The facts that make it difficult

Each of these exists to fire a specific session.

- The evaluation set was assembled by the delivery team from returned records, using a selection rule that was never written down.
- Two thresholds in the review-routing logic were set by a delivery engineer late in a working week and have no recorded provenance.
- The cost model is a spreadsheet held on a delivery engineer's laptop.
- One environment is tagged to the project in the operator's asset register. A second was created early, before tagging was enforced, and is tagged to nothing.
- A credential issued to a named delivery engineer holds write access inside the customer's estate.
- The escalation path in the current runbook ends at a delivery engineer's personal contact details.
- The back-office team leader is willing and under-authorised. The technology function is capable, unwilling, and correct that this was done to them rather than with them.
- The engagement's end date is fixed. The assessor's next sampling window does not move because of it.

## What this stage does not establish

**That any handover taught here produces a system that survives.** No source behind this programme reports whether any delivered system was still running months later, was maintained by the customer, or was abandoned. The practice corpus records no sustainment evidence of any kind, and no post-deployment record for any of its cases.

So the stage teaches a shape with two very different warrants inside it, and the papers keep them apart. **The hygiene mechanism is documented practice** — it exists, it is specific, and this programme reports it as what one organisation does, not as what works. **The transfer half is design reasoning with no external warrant.** It is constructed from an absence, by the same move Stage 1 makes, and it is subject to the same objection: inferring a practice from its omission in a document is weak.

**One further limit, stated because learners will otherwise mistake the stage for something it is not.** Nothing here is a rehearsal of leaving a real system with real people who will really be woken by it at night. The programme observes a simulation, over a short interval, with an assessor playing a recipient who cannot be genuinely harmed by a bad handover. Every claim this stage makes about entrustment is bounded by that.
