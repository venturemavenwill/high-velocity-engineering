# S049 · What the Agent Did That You Cannot Explain

## The situation

[S048](/wiki/seminars/S048.md) was about code you cannot explain. This session is about **behaviour** you cannot explain — the executor did something you did not ask for, or did not do something you did, and you have no account of why.

The two are different problems and only one of them can be read. Code sits still while you study it. Behaviour happened once, under conditions you did not fully record, on a host and a model that will both have moved by next term.

The pinned repository takes review seriously enough to decompose it, and the decomposition is the session's durable content: **orientation is separated from judgement**, so that establishing the facts of a change is a different act from forming an opinion about it; **depth is scaled to assessed risk** rather than to preference; **the lenses a reviewer applies are fixed** rather than left to habit; and **severity is normalised** so that two reviewers produce comparable findings. Findings persist under stable identifiers, as decisions did at [S047](/wiki/seminars/S047.md).

Then it does the thing that makes it a good teaching object rather than a good product. **It tells you what its own checks are worth.** Its behavioural graders test **output shape, not correctness**, and the repository says so in its own documentation. Some of its checks are **advisory** while others are **authoritative**, and which one a check is depends on **the tier it runs in rather than on the check itself.** The revision this programme pins is not all-green: one job failed, most were skipped, and none of that is visible in a summary.

Answer these before instruction.

1. Name one thing your executor did that you did not ask for and cannot explain.
2. What did you do to find out, and what made you stop?
3. Which of your checks are advisory and which are authoritative, and how do you know?
4. What does a passing check tell you about the work?
5. What would you have to see to conclude that the executor's explanation of itself was true?

**Item 5 is the session's hardest and most useful item.** The obvious move — ask it why — produces another output from the same process, and that output is subject to exactly the scrutiny you were trying to shortcut.

## What you must produce

An **inexplicability register** for your increment.

Each entry: what the executor did; what you expected instead; what you did to establish why; **what made you stop investigating**; what evidence would settle it; and whether you shipped anyway.

Alongside it, a **check inventory**: every automated check in your pipeline, each labelled advisory or authoritative, with **how you established the label** — read the configuration, made it fail on purpose, or assumed.

## What good looks like

- **The register is non-empty**, or its emptiness is defended with what was looked for and where.
- **Every entry has a stopping rule** and the rule is stated as a decision rather than as exhaustion.
- **At least one entry names the evidence that would settle it**, and the evidence is obtainable in principle.
- **The check inventory's labels were established, not assumed**, and at least one was established by making the check fail.
- **A shipped inexplicability is marked as shipped**, with what would be watched in production.
- **An explanation produced by the executor is recorded as a claim, not as a finding**, and the register says what corroborated it.
- **The register distinguishes *I do not know* from *nobody could know*.** They call for different next steps.

## How it goes wrong

- **The self-explanation accepted** — you asked it why and wrote down the answer. *Warning sign:* your register's evidence column contains the executor's own words.
- **The green summary** — the pipeline is passing, so nothing is unexplained. *Warning sign:* your check inventory has no advisory entries.
- **The advisory read as a gate** — a check that cannot block anything is treated as though it had. *Warning sign:* you have never seen that check red.
- **The unregistered ship** — the anomaly was real, the date was fixed, and nothing was written down. *Warning sign:* you remember the anomaly and cannot find it in the record.
- **The investigation with no floor** — a whole day disappears into one anomaly of no consequence. *Warning sign:* nobody set a stopping rule before you started.
- **The reattributed anomaly** — every unexplained behaviour is the host's fault, the model's fault or the customer's environment. *Warning sign:* your register contains no mistake of your own.

## Do it

**First, re-install the disposition from [S001](/wiki/seminars/S001.md) on top of the one this module has been building.** For four sessions the executor has been an instrument you delegate to. For this session it is a subject you interrogate. **Both dispositions have to be available at once, and the switch has to be deliberate**, because an engineer who only ever delegates will not notice an anomaly and an engineer who only ever interrogates will never ship.

**Second, establish one check's tier by making it fail.** Break something the check should catch and watch what happens to the change. **A check whose consequence you have never observed is a check whose tier you have assumed**, and the distinction between advisory and authoritative is, on the pinned repository's own account, the one most likely to be misread by somebody looking at a green summary.

**Third, look for the failing job.** The revision this session pins is not all-green. Find the failure in the record rather than in this page, and notice how much looking it took. **The lesson is not that a build failed. It is that a mostly-green summary conceals a specific fact, and that concealment is the normal condition of every dashboard you will be shown by a supplier.**

**Fourth, set a stopping rule before you investigate.** Write it down: what you will do, in what order, and what result ends the investigation. Then follow it. **The rule is the assessed artefact, not the outcome** — an anomaly you stopped investigating on a stated rule is professionally handled, and one you stopped investigating because you got bored is not, even if the code is identical.

**Fifth, ask the executor to explain itself, and then treat the answer correctly.** Record it as a claim. Say what would corroborate it. Go and look. **An explanation you cannot corroborate is a hypothesis with good grammar.**

## Show it

Give somebody your check inventory and ask them to pick one label and ask you how you know.

If the answer is *it says so in the configuration*, that is acceptable and weak. If the answer is *I made it fail and watched what happened*, that is the standard. **The gap between those two answers is the session.**

## What this does not establish

Nothing here shows that any of this catches anything.

The pinned repository is the clearest available worked example of a control system with **no committed evidence that its controls work.** It contains graders, validators, conformance lanes and baseline comparisons, and it stores **no result corpus** — the location for results is an empty placeholder. Its own documentation states that behavioural checks test output shape rather than correctness, that conformance checking is advisory by default and lacks established calibration, and that human oversight remains necessary because the project ships no model and no safety classifier and because host behaviour varies.

**That candour is the reason it is the anchor**, and it is also the limit on everything observed through it: a result seen on one host with one model is not a result about the corpus.

**The register, the stopping rule and the check inventory are this design's inventions.** No source proposes them. The claim that a stated stopping rule makes an abandoned investigation defensible is an argument about professional accountability, not a finding, and nobody has tested whether it changes what engineers do.

**The one claim this session makes without hedging is definitional:** a passing check is a claim about a check. Whether it is also a claim about the work depends entirely on what the check does, and that is a question about the check rather than about its colour.
