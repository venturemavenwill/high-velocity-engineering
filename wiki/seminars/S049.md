## The situation

[S048](/wiki/seminars/S048.md) was about code you cannot explain. This session is about **behaviour** you cannot explain — the executor did something you did not ask for, or did not do something you did, and you have no account of why.

The two are different problems and only one of them can be read. Code sits still while you study it. Behaviour happened once, under conditions you did not fully record, on a host and a model that will both have moved by next term.

There is a whole industry of instrumentation for exactly this, and you should use it. Traces, spans, logs, dashboards, evaluation over stored runs. **But the honest opening of this session is the thing the instrumentation does not tell you**, and it is a documented absence rather than a criticism.

**The vendor publishes no measure of how often the agent's iteration converges, and none of how often it reports completion on work that is wrong.** Not a bad number — no number. That silence is not an oversight to be complained about; **it is the working condition, and it is this session's teaching object.** You are operating a system whose base rate of confident false completion is unknown to you and, so far as this repository can establish, unpublished by the people who built it. Every practice in this session follows from that.

Carry one correction from earlier in the programme, because learners routinely mis-remember it in the reassuring direction. **The approval prompt before a terminal command or a non-built-in tool is a dismissible default, not a boundary.** It is auto-confirmable per session, per solution or permanently, and the vendor's own quickstart material instructs the reader to select the autopilot option, which removes the approvals entirely. **The thing that looks like your last line of defence is a preference the documentation recommends turning off.**

**Answer these in writing before reading further.**

1. Name one thing your executor did that you did not ask for and cannot explain.
2. What did you do to find out, and what made you stop?
3. Which of your checks are advisory and which are authoritative, and how do you know?
4. What does a passing check tell you about the work?
5. What would you have to see to conclude that the executor's explanation of itself was true?

**Item 5 is the session's hardest and most useful item.** The obvious move — ask it why — produces another output from the same process, and that output is subject to exactly the scrutiny you were trying to shortcut.

## What you must produce

An **inexplicability register** for your increment.

1. What the executor did, and what you expected instead.
2. What you did to establish why — including which stored trace you read and what you looked for in it.
3. **What made you stop investigating**, stated as a rule you wrote before you started rather than as a description of when you got tired.
4. What evidence would settle it, and whether that evidence is obtainable.
5. Whether you shipped anyway, and what you will watch in production if you did.

Alongside it, a **check inventory**: every automated check in your pipeline, each labelled advisory or authoritative, with **how you established the label** — read the configuration, made it fail on purpose, or assumed.

And a one-line **silence note** at the top of both: the measure you would need in order to know how unusual this behaviour was, and the fact that nobody publishes it.

## The material

### You can only control a system to the extent that you can observe it

The production machine-learning literature imports its observability framing from control theory and states the governing asymmetry plainly: **you can only control a system to the extent that you can observe it** ([research/02-technical-foundations/crowe-machine-learning-production-systems.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/crowe-machine-learning-production-systems.md)). It then makes the framing operational with two properties that are worth more than the slogan. **Alertability** — metrics and thresholds that make a failure unambiguous. **Actionability** — enough signal to support finding the cause. A stream you can neither alert on nor act from is cost, not observability.

The same source separates **functional monitoring**, which watches predictive behaviour and the distribution of what arrives, from **non-functional monitoring**, which watches failures, latency and resource use — and notes that the second matters regardless of the first, because if the system is down it does not matter how good the model is.

Two of its named sensitivities are exactly the ones that produce a behaviour you cannot explain. **Entanglement**: changing one thing changes everything, so a small upstream difference reappears somewhere unrelated. **Configuration**: hyperparameters, versions and feature lists live in configuration, and the slightest error there can cause radically different behaviour that ordinary software tests will not pick up. **Both mean that the cause of an anomaly is frequently not in the region where the anomaly appeared**, which is why your investigation needs a stopping rule rather than a completion criterion.

**The source's limits are recorded and heavy.** It is practitioner guidance from a commercial publisher, not research: no study design, no comparison group, no measurement that its practices outperform anything. Three of its four authors worked at one large vendor, so its default toolchain is that vendor's and alternatives are named briefly. Some of its foundational claims rest on **anonymous practitioner testimony** that cannot be checked. And **every quantity in it is a property of a particular system after a particular training run**; the note's own rule is that mechanism and direction transfer and magnitudes do not. **No figure from it appears here.**

### What a trace is, and the honest stopping rule nobody likes

A **trace** is a logical grouping of log records; for a conversational system the trace is the conversation ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/husain-your-ai-product-needs-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/husain-your-ai-product-needs-evals.md)). The practical requirement attached to it is unglamorous and correct: **searching, filtering and reading traces are the essential features** of whatever tooling you pick, and a generic viewer is usually not enough — the argument is for a small domain-specific viewer that shows which feature and which scenario is in play, filters across them, and links out to the underlying record.

On how much to read, that source refuses to give a number and gives a heuristic instead: **keep reading until you are not learning anything new**, with the flat statement that **you can never stop looking at data — no free lunch exists.** That is an honest position and it is not a stopping rule, which is exactly why this session requires you to write one. **A heuristic that terminates when you stop learning terminates when you stop paying attention**, and those two are indistinguishable from the inside at the end of a long day.

**Its limits:** a practitioner essay resting on one company's case study, with no control condition, no measured improvement and no released dataset. Its tooling references are dated. **Take the pattern, not the product.**

The openly published handbook is worth reading on this topic for two opposite reasons. It supplies one genuinely useful and under-taught distinction: **logs, metrics and traces are separated by data volume and processing cost rather than by semantics** — which answers what each instrument costs to keep rather than what each one means — and it frames tracing as an optimisation instrument rather than an incident-response one, stating that its purpose is not reactive ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md), pages read 2026-07-30). It also states a standing review rule worth stealing: **collect and measure what you need rather than what you can**, and any data collected but not aggregated or alerted on must be reviewed to see whether it is still required.

And the opposite reason. **Its observability section is the most rotted part of the site.** Its tracing page is about ten lines and does not define a span, a trace context or propagation; those definitions live on two other pages that are not filed as pillars. Its standards page still quotes a roadmap targeting a year long past and calls a mature project very recent. Several of the tools it recommends are archived or deprecated. **A section on observing systems that has not itself been observed for years is the sharpest available demonstration of why a standard is cited by page and by the date that page was read, never as a whole** — and this handbook contradicts itself in at least a dozen places without noticing and asserts four unsupported magnitude claims, which **this repository records as specimens and reproduces nowhere.**

### A green check is a claim about a check

The pinned configuration corpus takes review seriously enough to decompose it, and the decomposition is durable content ([research/06-microsoft-platform/hve-core-repository.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/hve-core-repository.md)). **Orientation is separated from judgement**, so establishing the facts of a change is a different act from forming an opinion about it. **Depth is scaled to assessed risk** rather than to preference. **The lenses a reviewer applies are fixed** rather than left to habit. **Severity is normalised** so that two reviewers produce comparable findings. Findings persist under stable identifiers.

Then it does the thing that makes it a good teaching object rather than a good product. **It tells you what its own checks are worth.** Its behavioural graders test **output shape, not correctness**, and the corpus says so in its own documentation. Some of its checks are **advisory** while others are **authoritative**, and **which one a check is depends on the tier it runs in rather than on the check itself.** Its own note records that this is the distinction most likely to be misread by somebody looking at a green summary.

And the revision this programme pins is **not all-green**: one job failed, most were skipped, and none of that is visible in a summary. **That is part of the evidence rather than a blemish on it**, and finding it is a workflow step below.

**Its limits are extreme.** Perishability is total on paths, counts and versions. Host and model dependence is absolute — a result observed on one client and model is not a result about the corpus. It contains its own marketing, using benefit vocabulary its own machinery does not support. **It contains graders and no committed result corpus**; the storage location is an empty placeholder.

### Evaluating what actually happened, rather than what you thought to ask

The mechanism that makes a trace more than an archive is that **stored responses can be evaluated "from logs or previous runs… without re-running the agent"** ([research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-framework-and-evaluations-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-framework-and-evaluations-docs.md)). That single capability is what separates production evaluation from synthetic testing, because synthetic queries only test what you thought to ask and a trace contains what actually occurred.

Two further details bear on an anomaly investigation. **Repetitions exist "to detect non-deterministic behavior"**, which is the documentation's own acknowledgement that one sample from a stochastic system is not a measurement — so an anomaly seen once is an observation and not a rate. And the tool-usage catalogue decomposes failure into **selection, input accuracy, call success and output utilisation**, of which **two — the right tool with wrong arguments, and a successful result ignored — produce a system that reports success and is wrong.** That is the failure class this session is named after, and having four words for it instead of *it did something weird* is most of the diagnostic value.

**The silence in this source is the point.** It is definitional and accurate about what the tooling computes. **No agreement rate with human judgement is published for any evaluator, no calibration data, and no rationale for any default threshold.** Most of these evaluators are model-judged. **Assert no agreement figure for any of them.** The pages were read through search excerpts rather than full fetches; most agent evaluators are marked preview; one consulted page is an explicitly superseded variant.

**And the gap this session used to declare is closed.** Until this term the anchor named a surface that no note in this repository covered, and the session said so rather than inventing warrant. **A note now exists** ([research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-agent-build-and-runtime.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-agent-build-and-runtime.md)), and the two subsections below are what it supplies.

**Read the correction as a correction, because the two things that were missing were never the same thing.** What was missing was an account of what a trace contains and how to walk it; that is now available and is taught below. What is still missing is any account of how *complete* a trace is, and any measure of how often the loop this session is named after arrives anywhere. **Closing the first did not touch the second**, and a learner who leaves thinking the anchor's silence was an artefact of a missing note has misread both.

### What a span tree contains, and how to walk it

The platform adopts **OpenTelemetry semantic conventions** wholesale and stores traces in Azure Monitor Application Insights. Tracing is **generally available for prompt and hosted agents and in preview for workflow and external agents** ([research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-agent-build-and-runtime.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-agent-build-and-runtime.md)).

The entry point is cheaper than most people expect. **Server-side tracing requires no code change**: connect an Application Insights resource to the project and the platform captures. Client-side instrumentation layers on afterwards, when you need visibility into your own application code around the agent call, and exports to Azure Monitor, to a console, or to **any OTLP-compatible backend**. A third path traces locally against a local collector.

**Message content is not captured by default.** An environment variable switches it on, and the documentation warns in the same sentence that the content may contain personal data. **That default is correct and it is worth understanding rather than overriding on the first day:** you get the shape of every run and none of the words until you decide otherwise, and deciding is a design act with a redaction rule attached.

What a trace captures, when it is on, is stated at two levels: inputs, outputs, tool usage, retries, latencies and costs; and more specifically user inputs and agent outputs, tool calls and results, token consumption, and time signals. **The published reason this is needed is the best thing in the documentation and is exactly this session's problem.** An agent run may have many steps; the sequence varies with the input; the inputs and outputs at each stage may be long; **and each step may nest — an agent invokes a tool, which uses another process, which invokes another tool — so when a top-level output is wrong it can be genuinely hard to determine where the error was introduced.** Two questions are named as the ones tracing exists to answer: **where did this response come from**, and **which step introduced an error or a latency spike**.

The multi-agent conventions are published, and they are what turn *read the trace* into a route you can follow. Built on OpenTelemetry and **W3C Trace Context** in collaboration with an external partner, they define a span **`execute_task`** carrying task planning and event propagation; child spans under **`invoke_agent`** for **`agent_to_agent_interaction`**, **`agent.state.management`** (effective context, short- and long-term memory), **`agent_planning`** (internal planning steps) and orchestration; the attributes **`tool_definitions`** and **`llm_spans`**; the attributes **`tool.call.arguments`** and **`tool.call.results`** under **`execute_tool`**; and an **Evaluation** event carrying a name, an error type and a label.

**So the walk has a shape.** Enter at `execute_task`. Descend through `agent_planning` to see what it decided to do, through `invoke_agent` to see what it handed off and to whom, and land on `execute_tool` to read the arguments it actually sent and the results it actually got back. **That path is the account you previously could not give**, and it is why the anchor moved from a shape to a surface.

Alongside the trace the portal exposes a **Conversation** view — conversation history, response information and tokens in a run, **ordered actions, run steps and tool calls**, and the inputs and outputs between user and agent — **searchable by response id or by a trace id that maps to the conversation.** That is the mechanism that makes after-the-fact reconstruction possible without re-running anything, which is the property [S062](/wiki/seminars/S062.md) will need and the property a stochastic system otherwise denies you. An agent running outside the platform can be brought onto the same surface by exporting OpenTelemetry to the project's Application Insights and **setting `gen_ai.agent.id` on every span.**

**The limits are the heaviest attached to any source in this programme and they travel with every sentence above.** It is **the most perishable material in this repository, and it perished visibly while being read.** Four pages were read in full; roughly thirty more were reached only through search excerpts, with tables frequently truncated, **so no list here is exhaustive.** Several consulted pages are explicitly superseded and still render, including one whose own first paragraph calls the model it documents deprecated. **Preview status is pervasive and load-bearing.** The note's own instruction is to re-verify any syntax against the version you are running before writing a line of it, and this session assesses no syntax and no key name.

### What the trace will not tell you, and what nobody publishes

Everything above is what the documentation says. This is what it does not, and **each of these absences was searched for deliberately rather than merely not encountered.**

**There is no measure of tracing completeness.** The documentation states what tracing captures. It says nothing about what proportion of an agent's decisions appear in a trace, whether any step can go unrecorded, what sampling does to completeness, or how a reader would detect a missing span. **A trace is presented as a record and never as a sample, and no page states the difference.** That is the sentence to carry out of this session, because everything you are about to conclude from a span tree rests on an assumption of completeness that nobody has warranted.

**There is no retention period owned by the platform.** The portal exposes a bounded window of recent traces, and **that window is a display limit rather than a retention policy** — retention and sampling follow the Application Insights and Log Analytics configuration you own and pay for. A team that reads *managed* as *managed for me* will find no retention policy where it expected one. **How long your agent's reasoning is kept is a decision somebody makes in a resource you configure, or fails to make.**

**Tracing is off until somebody turns it on**, only an account owner or owner can turn it on, and once it is on **anyone holding Log Analytics Reader on the connected workspace can read prompts and completions**, with a further role required where the underlying tables are protected ([research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-control-plane-and-governance.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-control-plane-and-governance.md)). **The documentation's own security guidance is short and correct:** keep secrets, credentials and tokens out of prompts, tool arguments and span attributes; redact or minimise personal data before it reaches telemetry; and treat trace data as production telemetry under the same access controls and retention you apply to logs. **A trace is where the reasoning becomes inspectable and where the sensitive data goes. Both are true at once.** **That note is read at mixed depth — seven pages fetched in full and roughly twenty through excerpts — is definitional rather than evidential, is actively renaming its own roles, and leans on preview features throughout; its numbers are dated platform parameters and none is reproduced here.**

**And the silence this session was named after survives intact.** Traces capture retries. **Nothing published says how often retrying arrives anywhere, how often a loop terminates without converging, or how often an agent reports completion on work that is wrong.** The new note confirms that absence by dedicated search rather than assuming it. **You now have a far better instrument and exactly the same ignorance about the base rate**, which is a more precise position than the one you had and not a more comfortable one.

Three further absences bear on any number you might be tempted to report from this surface. **Continuous evaluation samples production responses and links the scores to traces — and evaluation runs are silently skipped once an hourly ceiling is reached.** Conversation-level evaluation offers a default uniform sampler and a service-managed heuristic biased toward *interesting* traces, and **the heuristic is not documented at all** — what it optimises, how it decides and what it systematically misses are unstated. **A sample drawn by an undocumented rule, capped by a limit that skips without telling you, has no reportable coverage.** And **no agreement between any built-in evaluator and human judgement is published** — no correlation, no calibration, no threshold rationale — **confirmed by a dedicated search that returned the human-labelling feature, the rubric documentation and the evaluator catalogue and no agreement figure of any kind.** The platform now ships the instrument for collecting human labels and publishes no comparison of its own judges against them. **Assert none. Measure it yourself or do not cite it.**

### Why asking it why is not an investigation

The obvious move when an executor does something inexplicable is to ask it. The reason this is not an answer is structural rather than cultural.

The position paper this rests on holds that auto-regressive models cannot by themselves plan, and — in the clause that matters here — **cannot self-verify, because self-verification is after all a form of reasoning** ([research/02-technical-foundations/kambhampati-llms-cant-plan.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/kambhampati-llms-cant-plan.md)). An explanation of past behaviour is a verification claim about that behaviour. **It comes from the same process, with the same reliability, and it is fluent for the same reasons the original output was fluent.** The constructive half of the same argument gives the remedy: correctness comes from an **external** verifier and flexibility from the generator, and neither is trusted with the other's job. **Your corroboration must come from outside the thing being corroborated — a trace, a log, a re-run, a check made to fail.**

**The paper's limits:** it is a position paper, its claims are arguments and have been contested, it targets auto-regressive models as of early 2024, and models trained explicitly for reasoning postdate the argument. **The architectural discipline is what transfers, and it is correct whether or not model reasoning improves.**

Two further reasons not to trust a fluent account. If you reach for a model as a judge of the anomaly, **self-enhancement bias** means a judge from the same family as the system under test is not independent, and **limited reasoning** means a judge cannot reliably grade a problem it cannot itself solve — which is precisely your situation ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/zheng-mt-bench-chatbot-arena.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/zheng-mt-bench-chatbot-arena.md)). **That work predates current models; its bias taxonomy has held up better than its figures, none of which is reproduced here.** And capability with these systems is **jagged**, so the boundary is uneven and the failure mode outside it is a persuasive wrong answer in the same register as a correct one rather than an error ([research/04-professional-formation/dellacqua-jagged-technological-frontier.md](/research/04-professional-formation/dellacqua-jagged-technological-frontier.md)). **That note was written from an abstract; the shape is usable and no number in it is.**

## The workflow

**Establish one check's tier by breaking something, then investigate one anomaly under a rule you wrote first.**

**Step 1 — record the pin, and record the silence beside it.** Commit or version, host, model, date — and one line stating the measure you would need to know how unusual this behaviour is, and that nobody publishes it. **The silence note is not decoration. It is the sentence that stops you drawing a rate from a single observation.**

**Step 2 — re-install the adversarial disposition on top of the delegating one.** For three sessions the executor has been an instrument you delegate to. For this session it is a subject you interrogate. **Both dispositions have to be available at once and the switch has to be deliberate**, because an engineer who only ever delegates will not notice an anomaly and one who only ever interrogates will never ship.

**Step 3 — establish one check's tier by making it fail.** Break something the check should catch and watch what happens to the change. **A check whose consequence you have never observed is a check whose tier you have assumed**, and advisory-versus-authoritative is, on the corpus's own account, the distinction most likely to be misread by somebody looking at a green summary.

**Step 4 — look for the failing job.** The revision this session pins is not all-green. Find the failure in the record rather than on this page, and notice how much looking it took. **The lesson is not that a build failed. It is that a mostly-green summary conceals a specific fact, and that concealment is the normal condition of every dashboard a supplier will ever show you.**

**Step 5 — write the stopping rule before you investigate, and put it in the register.**

```yaml
# .observations/register.yaml   <- SHAPE, NOT SCHEMA. Verify any product keys separately.
silence_note: >
  To know whether this is rare I would need the rate at which the executor
  reports completion on wrong work. NO SUCH MEASURE IS PUBLISHED. Therefore
  nothing below is a rate; every entry is one observation under one pin.
pin: { host: "<client+version>", model: "<model+version>", date: "<YYYY-MM-DD>" }

entries:
  - id: IX-01
    observed: "Rewrote the retry helper it was not asked to touch."
    expected: "Change confined to the routing module."

    stopping_rule:            # WRITTEN BEFORE THE INVESTIGATION STARTED
      steps:                  # in this order, and no others
        - "Read the stored trace for the run; locate the tool call that edited the file."
        - "Check the instruction set delivered in that request payload (S046 step 4)."
        - "Re-run the same task twice; record whether the edit recurs."
      stop_when: "Any step yields a cause, OR all three complete, OR 45 minutes elapse."
      # THE RULE IS THE ASSESSED ARTEFACT. The outcome is not.

    investigation:
      trace_read: "run-2f9c"
      what_i_looked_for: "tool_selection and tool_input_accuracy on the edit call"
      repetitions: 2          # one sample from a stochastic system is not a measurement
      result: "Recurred once of two. Cause not established."
      stopped_because: "all three steps completed"   # NOT 'I got tired'

    executor_explanation:     # RECORDED AS A CLAIM, NEVER AS A FINDING
      claim: "Said it refactored for consistency with a neighbouring module."
      corroboration_sought: "Is that module's style actually what was produced?"
      corroboration_found: "No. The produced style matches neither module."
      status: "claim not corroborated"

    would_settle_it: "The delivered instruction payload for the failing run, which was not captured."
    obtainable: "Yes, if payload capture is enabled before the next run."
    shipped_anyway: true
    watching_in_production: "Any further edit outside the routing module, alerted."
```

**Step 6 — evaluate the stored trace rather than re-running from scratch, and walk the span tree before you score it.** Stored responses can be scored without re-running the agent, which means you can ask what actually happened rather than what you would think to ask now. Score the anomalous run against the tool-usage decomposition — was the wrong tool selected, were the arguments wrong, did the call fail, or was a good result ignored? **Four words instead of *it did something weird* is most of the diagnostic value.**

Then do the thing the anchor now makes possible. **Open the trace and descend it**, writing down at each level what you learned and what you still cannot account for. The published route is `execute_task` at the top, `agent_planning` for the internal steps, `invoke_agent` for a handoff, and `execute_tool` at the leaf, where `tool.call.arguments` and `tool.call.results` are the two attributes that usually settle it.

```text
# .observations/IX-01.spanwalk.txt   <- SHAPE, NOT SCHEMA. Span and attribute names
# are the published multi-agent conventions; verify them against the version you are
# running before you rely on a key. Record the version you checked.

execute_task                          what I expected: routing change only
│                                     what the span says: task accepted, one subtask
├─ agent_planning                     ACCOUNTED FOR? partly — a plan step names
│                                     "consistency pass", which I did not ask for
├─ agent.state.management             ACCOUNTED FOR? no — I do not know what was in
│                                     effective context, and content recording is OFF
└─ invoke_agent
   └─ execute_tool  (file edit)
        tool.call.arguments           path = src/retry/helper.ts   <-- THE FINDING
        tool.call.results             ok
        ACCOUNTED FOR? the WHAT is settled. The WHY is not.

STOPPED BECAUSE: all three steps of the rule completed.       # not 'I got tired'
STILL UNEXPLAINED: why the plan step existed at all.
COMPLETENESS: UNKNOWN. No measure of tracing completeness is published, so I cannot
              say whether a step is missing from this tree. This is a record that I
              am NOT entitled to read as a sample.
```

**The last line is the assessed thinking, not the tree.** A span tree tells you what was recorded. **Nothing published tells you what proportion of what happened was recorded**, so *the trace does not show it* and *it did not happen* are different statements and only one of them is available to you.

**Step 7 — ask the executor to explain itself, then treat the answer correctly.** Record it as a claim. Say what would corroborate it. Go and look. **An explanation you cannot corroborate is a hypothesis with good grammar**, and it came from the process you were investigating.

**Step 8 — use the agent to attack your own register, not to fill it.** One prompt: *for each entry, argue that my stated cause is wrong and name the observation that would show it.* Then read what came back. Keep whatever is better than your own reasoning and record what you rejected — and record separately that this instrument shares a model family with your executor and is therefore not independent of it. **An agent that endorsed your register was not used as an instrument.**

**Step 9 — run the same task twice, with content recording off and then on, and write the redaction rule that makes the second one shippable.** Message content is off by default; an environment variable turns it on; the documentation warns in the same breath that the content may contain personal data. **Compare what each trace can and cannot answer about your anomaly.** Then write the two sentences that have to exist before the second configuration is allowed anywhere near a customer's tenant: what will be redacted before it reaches telemetry, and who — by name or by role — holds read access to the workspace it lands in. **Anyone with Log Analytics Reader on that workspace can read prompts and completions.** If you cannot name that set of people, you have not turned content recording on; you have leaked it.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That a check's colour and a check's consequence are unrelated until somebody has seen the check red — and that finding out takes minutes and almost nobody has done it. **You will also observe the asymmetry that gives this session its name: your instrumentation can tell you what happened in enormous detail and cannot tell you whether what happened was unusual**, because the base rate is not published and your own sample is one.



## What good looks like

- **The register carries a silence note**, and no entry in it is stated as a rate.
- **The register is non-empty**, or its emptiness is defended with what was looked for and where.
- **Every entry has a stopping rule that was written before the investigation began**, stated as a decision rather than as exhaustion.
- **At least one entry names the evidence that would settle it**, and says whether that evidence is obtainable.
- **The check inventory's labels were established, not assumed**, and at least one was established by making the check fail.
- **A shipped inexplicability is marked as shipped**, with what will be watched in production.
- **An explanation produced by the executor is recorded as a claim, not as a finding**, with what was sought as corroboration and what was found.
- **The register distinguishes *I do not know* from *nobody could know*.** They call for different next steps.

## How it goes wrong

- **The rate from one observation** — the anomaly happened once and is described as rare, or as common. *Warning sign:* your register contains a frequency word and your sample is one.
- **The self-explanation accepted** — you asked it why and wrote down the answer. *Warning sign:* your register's evidence column contains the executor's own words.
- **The green summary** — the pipeline is passing, so nothing is unexplained. *Warning sign:* your check inventory has no advisory entries.
- **The advisory read as a gate** — a check that cannot block anything is treated as though it had. *Warning sign:* you have never seen that check red.
- **The approval mistaken for a boundary** — the confirmation prompt is cited as the control that would have caught it. *Warning sign:* you cannot say whether it is still switched on, or who switched it off.
- **The unregistered ship** — the anomaly was real, the date was fixed, and nothing was written down. *Warning sign:* you remember the anomaly and cannot find it in the record.
- **The investigation with no floor** — a whole day disappears into one anomaly of no consequence. *Warning sign:* nobody set a stopping rule before you started.
- **The reattributed anomaly** — every unexplained behaviour is the host's fault, the model's fault or the customer's environment. *Warning sign:* your register contains no mistake of your own.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The register and the check inventory are carried into [S050](/wiki/seminars/S050.md), where every check you cite must be labelled and the basis of the label stated.

**1 · Sort four statements into what a passing check licenses.** For each, say what it establishes about the work.

- *The behaviour grader passed.* · *The schema validator passed.* · *The pipeline is green.* · *The reviewer approved.*

> **Model answers.** The behaviour grader establishes something about **output shape and not correctness** — the pinned corpus says so about its own graders, which is why it is a good teaching object. The schema validator establishes conformance to a declared structure, which is real and narrow. *The pipeline is green* establishes that no authoritative check failed, which is silent about every advisory one and about every check that was skipped. The reviewer's approval establishes that a person said so, and [S044](/wiki/seminars/S044.md) is where you learned what a review record does not cover. **The general rule: a passing check is a claim about a check. Whether it is also a claim about the work depends entirely on what the check does, and that is a question about the check rather than about its colour.**

**2 · Break one check and watch.** Run workflow step 3. Record which check, what you broke, and what happened to the change.

> **What you should find.** Frequently, nothing — the check went red and the change proceeded. **That is the advisory tier doing exactly what it is designed to do, and it is invisible in a summary.** The corpus's own documentation records that a check can be advisory at one tier while a scheduled run treats the same check as authoritative, so the label belongs to the location and not to the check. **If you cannot make any check fail on purpose, that is the finding** — record it, because a control you cannot exercise is a control you cannot characterise.

**3 · Go back to your own item 2, then break it.** Find what you wrote for opening item 2 — what you did to find out, and what made you stop. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then write a stopping rule properly, for a fresh anomaly, before you investigate it.

> **Criterion.** A stopping rule passes if somebody else could execute it and arrive at the same stopping point. It names the steps, their order, and the condition that ends the investigation — a cause found, the steps exhausted, or a bounded time elapsed. **It fails if it describes a state of the investigator**: *until I was satisfied*, *when it stopped being productive*, *when I ran out of ideas*. The honest heuristic in the practitioner literature — keep reading until you are not learning anything new — is explicitly not a stopping rule, and its author says so by adding that you can never stop looking at data. **A rule that terminates when you stop learning terminates when you stop paying attention, and those are indistinguishable from the inside.**
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Score the anomaly against the tool-failure decomposition.** Take one entry and classify it: wrong tool selected, right tool with wrong arguments, call failed, or good result ignored.

> **What to notice.** That two of the four — wrong arguments and ignored output — **produce a system that reports success and is wrong**, which is why they are the hardest to detect and the most worth naming. Note also that classifying an anomaly is not explaining it. **A vocabulary makes the thing discussable and does not make it understood**, and an entry classified but not caused should stay in the register as unexplained.

**5 · Write the silence note, then use it.** Write the sentence stating the measure you would need and the fact that it is not published. Then read your own register looking for any word that implies a frequency.

> **Scoring criterion.** A pass names the measure precisely — the rate at which the executor reports completion on work that is wrong, or the rate at which its iteration converges — and states that no such measure is published. **A fail says the tooling is unreliable**, which is a judgement rather than an absence, and which you cannot support. **The purpose of the note is not humility. It is to stop you writing *this rarely happens* on the basis of one week.**

**6 · Try to corroborate one self-explanation.** Ask the executor why it did the thing. Record the answer as a claim, name what would corroborate it, and go and look.

> **What you should find.** Frequently that the explanation is plausible, well-formed and unsupported by the trace. The structural reason is not that the system is dishonest: **an explanation of past behaviour is a verification claim, and the position this session rests on holds that these systems cannot self-verify because self-verification is itself a form of reasoning** ([research/02-technical-foundations/kambhampati-llms-cant-plan.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/kambhampati-llms-cant-plan.md)). **That paper is a position paper whose claims are arguments and have been contested, scoped to models as of early 2024** — so take the architectural discipline, which is that corroboration must come from outside the thing being corroborated, and do not present the empirical claim as settled.


**7 · Walk one span tree and mark the level at which your account ran out.** Take the anomaly from your register, descend from `execute_task` to `execute_tool`, and at each level write *accounted for* or *not accounted for* rather than a summary.

> **What you should find.** That the tree settles the **what** far more often than it settles the **why** — `tool.call.arguments` usually names the thing that was touched, and `agent_planning` usually shows that a step existed without showing why it was chosen. **That asymmetry is the honest yield of tracing and it is worth more than a fuller answer would be**, because it tells you which half of your register entry is evidence and which half is still a hypothesis. Note also what your walk depended on: with content recording off you have the shape and not the words, which is the correct default and a real limit on what the walk can conclude. **And record the version of the conventions you checked** — this surface is the most perishable material in the programme, several of its pages are explicitly superseded and still render, and this exercise assesses the walk and never the key names.

**8 · Ask what the trace does not contain, and find out that nobody knows.** Write one sentence stating what proportion of your agent's decisions appeared in the trace you just read, then try to source it.

> **What you should find.** That you cannot write the sentence, because **no measure of tracing completeness is published anywhere** — not a dropped-span rate, not a sampling-loss figure, not a statement of what fraction of runs yields a usable trace, and no guidance on how a reader would detect a missing span. **A trace is presented as a record and never as a sample, and no page states the difference.** The practical consequence is a rule you should now apply for the rest of the programme: *the trace does not show it* and *it did not happen* are different claims, and only the first one is yours to make. **A fail here is an entry in your register that treats a complete-looking tree as a complete one.**

## What this does not establish

Nothing here shows that any of this catches anything.

**The central absence is the session's subject and it is worth stating flatly.** No measure is published of how often the agent's iteration converges, and none of how often it reports completion on work that is wrong. **You cannot compute a rate from your own week**, and a session that let you leave believing otherwise would have taught the opposite of what it intended. Every entry in your register is one observation under one pin.

The pinned corpus is the clearest available worked example of a control system with **no committed evidence that its controls work.** It contains graders, validators, conformance lanes and baseline comparisons, and it stores **no result corpus** — the location for results is an empty placeholder. Its own documentation states that behavioural checks test output shape rather than correctness, that conformance checking is advisory by default and lacks established calibration, and that human oversight remains necessary because the project ships no model and no safety classifier and because host behaviour varies. **That candour is the reason it is the anchor**, and it is also the limit on everything observed through it: a result seen on one host with one model is not a result about the corpus.

**The evaluation framework's documentation is definitional and silent where it matters.** It describes accurately what the tooling computes and publishes **no agreement rate with human judgement for any evaluator, no calibration data and no threshold rationale** — so it is no evidence that what it computes is right. It was read through search excerpts rather than full page fetches, most agent evaluators are preview, and one consulted page is an explicitly superseded variant.

**The hole where the anchor's product surface used to be is filled, and filling it changed less than you might hope.** This repository now holds a note covering tracing and observability in the vendor's agent platform, and the session teaches the surface rather than refusing it. **That note is the most perishable material in this repository and it perished visibly while being read**: four pages read in full and roughly thirty through search excerpts with tables truncated; several consulted pages explicitly superseded and still rendering; preview status pervasive and load-bearing; every number in it a dated platform parameter and none reproduced here ([research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-agent-build-and-runtime.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-agent-build-and-runtime.md)). **It is first-party documentation with a definitional purpose: it is an accurate account of what the platform is configured to do and no evidence at all that doing so produces any outcome.** The governance note beside it is the same genre with the same limits ([research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-control-plane-and-governance.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-control-plane-and-governance.md)). The internal synthesis that names observability as a governance pillar remains **not publicly accessible, not citable externally, and its quantitative claims carry no methodology and are repeated nowhere in this wiki.**

**And the two absences the session is actually built on are unchanged by any of it.** No measure of tracing completeness exists, so a span tree is a record you are not entitled to read as a sample. No measure of self-correction convergence exists, so your register is still a set of observations and not a rate. **Both were confirmed by dedicated search rather than assumed** — which is a stronger form of absence than the one this session used to record, and it is worse news rather than better.

**The observability literature is practitioner guidance, not research.** It has no study design, no comparison group and no measurement that its practices outperform alternatives; three of its four authors worked at one vendor whose toolchain is its default; some of its foundational claims rest on anonymous practitioner testimony; and every quantity in it is a property of a particular system after a particular run. **No figure from it appears here.** The openly published handbook's observability section is the most rotted part of that site, recommending archived and deprecated tools and quoting a roadmap years out of date — which is used here as a specimen and not as guidance.

**The register, the stopping rule and the check inventory are this design's inventions.** No source proposes them. The claim that a stated stopping rule makes an abandoned investigation defensible is an argument about professional accountability, not a finding, and nobody has tested whether it changes what engineers do.

**The one claim this session makes without hedging is definitional:** a passing check is a claim about a check. Whether it is also a claim about the work depends entirely on what the check does, and that is a question about the check rather than about its colour.
