## The situation

Every engagement that failed had a review afterwards, and the review found causes. Most of those causes were available to somebody in the room on the first day.

The pre-mortem is the same review, run before the work, on a failure that has not happened. It is not a risk register and it is not pessimism. It is a generative exercise with a specific trick in it: **you are told the outcome, so you are producing explanations rather than predictions**, and people are markedly more forthcoming when they are explaining something than when they are forecasting it.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught.

1. Your engagement has failed. It is over, it is public, and the reason is known. Write the reason.
2. Write four more, and make at least two of them non-technical.
3. Which of your five would somebody in the room have been reluctant to say out loud on day one? Why?
4. For each of the five, name the cheapest thing that would tell you now whether it is happening.
5. Which of them are you going to accept rather than act on, and who owns that acceptance?

**Item 1 is stated as a fact in the past tense, and the grammar is the instrument.** *What could go wrong* invites a list of hedged possibilities. *It failed — why* invites an account. Notice which one you found easier.

## What you must produce

A **pre-mortem record** with four columns and a header.

- **The header** names who was in the room, who was invited and did not come, and who was not invited. [S007](/wiki/seminars/S007.md)'s question, applied to your own team.
- **Cause** — stated as something that happened, in the past tense, specifically enough that somebody could tell whether it was occurring.
- **Route** — for each cause, one of: a spike question, a constraint to verify, an observation to make, or an acceptance.
- **Owner** — a role, for every row, including the accepted ones. **An accepted risk with no owner is an ignored risk with better manners.**

## The material

### The inversion is the instrument, and the grammar is where it lives

A risk register asks *what could go wrong*. A pre-mortem asserts that it already did and asks *why*. Those look like the same question and they are not, because they put the person answering into two different cognitive positions.

Asked to forecast, a person is being asked to be right about the future, and the safe response is a hedge. Every failure mode arrives wrapped in *may*, *could* and *potentially*, because a specific prediction that does not come true is embarrassing and a vague one never is. Asked to explain, a person is being asked to account for something that is stipulated as true, and hedging becomes pointless — you cannot hedge about whether a thing happened when you have been told it did. **The stipulation removes the cost of being specific, and specificity is the only thing that makes a cause checkable.**

The openly published playbook this module draws on encodes exactly this and does it in one sentence. Its feasibility-spike page opens the investigation with a pre-mortem framed on a single question: **"Imagine the project has failed. What problems and challenges caused this failure?"** ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md), page read 2026-07-30). Note what the sentence does: the first clause is an instruction to stipulate, the second is a request for causes in the past tense. **Nothing in it asks for probability, severity or likelihood**, and a room that starts scoring is no longer doing a pre-mortem.

The source attaches a fixed length to the exercise and a shorter recurring length to the share-out that follows it. **Neither number is reproduced here**, for the reason [M07](/wiki/modules/M07.md) gives: a duration lifted out of one organisation's calendar and printed in a curriculum becomes a norm against which learners judge their own without ever asking where it came from. What transfers is the shape — fixed, short, and bounded before it starts. **Set your own, state it in the record, and defend it against the decision waiting.**

That publication offers **no comparative evidence** that any of its practices outperform alternatives, which is true of the document throughout. Its pages carry footer dates spread across several years, roughly a fifth of the site is stubs or link lists, it contradicts itself in at least a dozen places without noticing, and it asserts four unsupported magnitude claims which this repository records as specimens and reproduces nowhere.

### The failure the exercise degrades into, and the one it cannot fix by itself

Two degradations are common and only one of them is discussed.

**The risk register in fancy dress** is the visible one. The causes come back hedged, conditional and generic — *scope may expand*, *the customer could be slow to respond*, *integration might prove complex* — and could have been written about any engagement in any industry. Nothing on the list can be checked, so nothing on it can be routed, so the record changes nothing and is filed. **The diagnostic is mechanical: read your causes and count the modal verbs.**

**The polite pre-mortem** is the one that matters more and is harder to see, because the record looks fine. The real cause is known to three people in the room and none of them says it, usually because it implicates the plan, the sponsor, or somebody senior who is present. What comes back is a competent list of secondary causes, and the exercise has produced a document whose most useful content is the thing it does not contain.

This session's response — everybody writes alone, contributions are collected without attribution, then the room works on the pooled list — is the standard mechanism and **it is not sufficient.** A team with a strong prior about what may be said will produce an anonymous list that respects the same prior. The additional move the session requires is that whoever is running it **states plainly, before the writing starts, that a cause implicating the plan, the sponsor or the team is the kind being asked for, and puts one of their own on the list first.** That is design reasoning with no external warrant and it is offered as such.

### A cause is only worth writing if you can say what would show it happening

This is where a pre-mortem stops being a discussion and starts being engineering, and it is the bridge into the platform work.

For every cause, the useful question is not how likely it is. It is: **what would I observe if this were happening now?** A cause with no observable is not a cause, it is a mood. And once the question is asked in that form, the routing follows almost mechanically — some causes are settled by asking a named person, some by checking a constraint that was recorded as a belief, some by looking at something, and only a few by running an investigation. **Routing everything to a spike is how a pre-mortem becomes a quarter of work.**

The evaluation literature has worked out what the observable looks like for the causes that concern a system's behaviour, and it is worth taking directly. The core move is decomposition: break the system's scope into **features** — what it can do — and **scenarios** — situations each feature must handle — and let each scenario become an assertion ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/husain-your-ai-product-needs-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/husain-your-ai-product-needs-evals.md)). A pre-mortem cause names a scenario. *The system returned confident answers on documents it had no access to* is a scenario. *We ran out of time* is not.

The same source supplies the rule that stops learners writing evaluations they cannot live with: unlike traditional unit tests, a full pass rate is explicitly not required, because **"Your pass rate is a product decision."** A pre-mortem-derived check is allowed to fail. That is what it is for. **The limits are real and travel with it**: it is a practitioner essay resting on a single company's case study, with no comparison group, no measured improvement and no released data. Its recommendations are well-specified hypotheses, not findings.

### "The quota never arrived" — a cause with a published mechanism rather than a guess

Almost every row in a pre-mortem is a guess about a mechanism. You believe something could happen, you can describe roughly how, and nobody can check the description. **Occasionally a cause has a mechanism the responsible party has published**, and when that happens the row stops being an opinion and becomes a reading. This is the best worked example the session has.

The capacity your agent's models run on is allocated **per subscription, per region and per model, in tokens per minute**, and more of it is obtained by submitting a request ([research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-control-plane-and-governance.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-control-plane-and-governance.md)). The stated policy is worth reading twice: **"Quota increase requests are processed in the order they're received, and priority goes to customers who actively use their existing quota allocation. Requests that don't meet this condition might be denied."**

There is a genuine logic in it. Capacity is finite per region, and rationing it towards demonstrated consumption is defensible. **Read as a delivery dependency, though, it is a request with no published response time, no published approval rate and no published appeal route, whose stated prioritisation rule works against a new engagement specifically** — because a new engagement has, by definition, consumed nothing yet. That is not an accusation. It is the published rule, applied to your circumstances, and it is the difference between a fear and a mechanism.

Three further facts bind, and each of them closes a route a team would otherwise assume was open.

**A shared pool exists for temporary testing**, which softens the pilot case, and its duration is given as depending on the use case — which is to say it is unquantified and cannot be planned against.

**Some models cannot have their quota increased at all.** Models from partners and the community do not support increases, with one publisher excepted. **A request that cannot be made is not a request that is slow; it is a closed route**, and it belongs on the pre-mortem as a fact rather than as a risk.

**And some limits are stated as fixed service limits that cannot be increased in any circumstances** — the agent service's own file, message and tool ceilings. **You design within those or you do not use the service**, and a design that assumed otherwise has a cause with no mitigation available at any price.

Notice what the grammar of this row looks like when it is written properly. *We could not get enough capacity* is a hedge wearing a past tense. **"The increase request went in during week one and was still unanswered in week six; the published prioritisation rule gives priority to customers already consuming their allocation, and we were not one"** is an account, and every clause in it is checkable by somebody who was not there.

**The limits on that note are heavy and go with every sentence.** It is first-party documentation whose authority is **definitional, not evidential** — it states what the platform permits and commits to, and measures nothing. **There is no adoption data, no failure-rate data, no benchmark and no evidence that any recommended pattern produces a better outcome than its alternative.** Seven of its pages were fetched in full and roughly twenty more were read only through search-returned excerpts, so their structure is reconstructed and their tables may be truncated. Its role names were renamed while the documentation was being written and the pages say so. **And the one community thread showing a customer frustrated by quota latency establishes that the question is asked and nothing whatever about typical response times** — a Q&A answer is not documentation and no inference about duration may be drawn from it.

### The observable, the two traps, and the row you are not allowed to route

A cause with no observable is a mood. This one has two, and they are both queries rather than investigations — which is exactly the routing lesson the previous section asked for.

**A usages interface answers consumption against limits. A model-capacities interface answers what capacity is available for a given model in a given region.** So the cause is checkable today, by somebody who already has access, in less time than it takes to argue about it. **Routing it to a spike would be the classic error**: a spike is what you run when no cheaper instrument exists, and here two exist and are documented.

**Two traps sit inside that, and both produce a confidently wrong answer rather than an error.**

The first is that **the capacity interfaces return information for models that are retired and no longer available.** A capacity report will describe, without complaint, a model you cannot deploy. **The observable must therefore be paired with a lifecycle check**, and a row that records only the capacity answer has recorded half of it.

The second is a permissions asymmetry. **Viewing usage, editing an allocation and requesting an increase are three different permissions**, so the person who can read the number may not be the person who can ask for more, who may not be the person who will be told the answer. **A cause whose observable requires three roles is a cause whose owner column cannot be a team.**

And then the part this session exists for. **This row cannot be mitigated, because you do not own it.** It is an acceptance, and an acceptance needs an owner by role, a chase cadence, and a written plan for the answer being no — a smaller region, a different model, a delayed slice, or an engagement that stops. **An accepted risk with no owner is an ignored risk with better manners**, and this is the row where that sentence stops being a slogan and starts naming somebody.

**One recursive detail belongs here rather than in the workflow.** The continuous form of the evaluation you are about to write is itself sampled and itself capped: runs are **skipped** once an hourly ceiling is reached, and the ceiling is a configurable default nobody has justified in public. **So the check competes with the system it checks for the same finite allocation**, and a quality number reported without knowing whether the ceiling was hit is a number whose denominator nobody can state. That is [S068](/wiki/seminars/S068.md)'s subject arriving early, and it is here because it is a *cause*: *the dashboard was green because most of the traffic was never scored* is exactly the kind of thing a pre-mortem is for.

### The evaluation you write before the system exists

Here is the move this session's platform work turns on, and it is the pre-mortem's logic carried one step further than a record.

A pre-mortem says the failure has already happened. The corresponding engineering act is to **write the check that would have caught it, now, before the thing that could pass it exists.** The evaluation will fail. It has nothing to run against. That is not a defect in the evaluation and it is the entire point: an evaluation written after a system exists is shaped by what the system happens to do, and an evaluation written before it is shaped by what you were afraid of.

The Agent Framework's evaluation model is small enough to hold in mind and is built around three types: **`EvalItem`**, "a single item to evaluate — wraps the full conversation and derives query/response via a split strategy"; **`Evaluator`**, "a provider that scores items — local checks, Azure AI Foundry, or any custom implementation"; and **`EvalResults`**, "aggregated results from an evaluation run" ([research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-framework-and-evaluations-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-framework-and-evaluations-docs.md)). Three types is a good sign: it says an evaluation is a function from an interaction to a judgement, aggregated over a set, and that everything else is a detail of who judges.

The two-tier evaluator design is the part that maps onto your routing. **`LocalEvaluator`** "runs checks locally without API calls — ideal for inner-loop development, CI smoke tests, and fast iteration". **`FoundryEvals`** connects to a cloud service for model-judged scoring, defaulting to relevance, coherence and task adherence, and "when items contain tool definitions, it automatically adds tool call accuracy". The two can be mixed in one run. **That is the cost-and-speed gradient of your pre-mortem routing expressed in an API**, and it is worth noticing that the architecture teaches the discipline: cheap deterministic checks constantly, expensive judged checks less often.

The catalogue's decomposition of tool failure is the most directly useful thing in it for a pre-mortem, because it names failure modes a room would otherwise merge into *it did not work*. Choosing the wrong tool, calling the right tool with wrong arguments, the call failing technically, and ignoring or misreading a successful result are four separate things with four separate evaluators. **Two of those — wrong arguments and ignored output — produce a system that reports success and is wrong**, which is exactly the class of cause a pre-mortem is most valuable for surfacing and least likely to surface unprompted.

### The decision that silently determines what your number means

One design choice in that framework deserves its own place in this session, because getting it wrong produces an evaluation that answers a question nobody asked.

**"Multi-turn conversations must be split into query and response halves for evaluation. How you split determines *what you're evaluating*."** Three strategies are provided. **Last turn**, the default, splits at the last user message and suits response quality at a specific point. **Full** treats the first user message as the query and everything after as the response, and suits task completion and overall trajectory. **Per-turn** scores each exchange independently.

Consider an agent that gets its first attempt wrong and recovers. **It scores well under Full and badly under Per-turn.** Neither score is wrong; they answer different questions. **A team that never chose deliberately is reporting an answer to a question it did not ask** — and if your pre-mortem cause was *the system misled the user on its first response and nobody noticed the correction came too late*, only one of those strategies can see it.

The same source records two further design facts that a pre-mortem-derived evaluation needs. **Repetitions** exist "to detect non-deterministic behavior" — the acknowledgement that one sample from a stochastic system is not a measurement. And **pre-existing responses from logs can be evaluated without re-running the agent**, which is what makes evaluation of real traffic possible rather than only of the queries you thought to ask.

**The limits here are serious and must be stated whenever this material is used.** It is first-party product documentation, definitional rather than empirical, read through search-returned excerpts rather than full page fetches. Many evaluators are marked **preview** and one consulted page is a **classic** variant explicitly superseded. Most consequentially: **no agreement rate with human judgement is published for any evaluator, no calibration data, and no account of where the default thresholds came from.** Most of these are model-judged. **This wiki asserts no evaluator's agreement with human judgement, because none is published**, and a threshold you inherited is a policy you did not choose.

### Your first evaluation is wrong, and that is a finding rather than a failure

The honest complication comes from the research on evaluation design itself, and a session that hid it would be teaching a workflow the literature says does not survive contact with real outputs.

Using a model to grade a model does not solve the trust problem; it relocates it, because **"LLM-generated evaluators simply inherit all the problems of the LLMs they evaluate, requiring further human validation"** ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/shankar-evalgen.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/shankar-evalgen.md)). The repair is alignment: hand-grade a subset yourself, and prefer the evaluator implementation whose verdicts best reproduce your own grades.

The finding that bears hardest on this session is named **criteria drift**: **"users need criteria to grade outputs, but grading outputs helps users define criteria."** That is a genuine circularity rather than a workflow inefficiency. And the sharper claim behind it is that some criteria appear to be **dependent on the specific outputs observed**, which "raises serious questions for approaches that assume the independence of evaluation from observation of model outputs".

**Read plainly, that is an argument against what this session asks you to do.** If some criteria only become articulable after seeing outputs, then an evaluation written before the system exists cannot be complete. **The session accepts that and claims something narrower**: the pre-mortem-derived evaluation is not the final rubric, it is the part of the rubric that fear can supply, and it is the only part that can be written while nobody has a stake in it passing. The parts that arrive later arrive from looking, and they are welcome. **What cannot be recovered later is the check nobody had a reason to write once the system was working.** That claim is design reasoning and has no external warrant.

The drift evidence is qualitative and from a small interface study; the paper establishes that the phenomenon occurs and matters, **not how often or how much**, and the tool it describes is a research prototype.

### What the corpus cannot help with, in an instructive way

The internal practice corpus is thinner here than the playbook and its failure is specific. It records **no failed engagement at all**, and its only failure narrative is a synthetic composite that the material itself labels fictional ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md)). **A practice with no failures has no pre-mortems worth reading**, because the causes it would list have never been checked against a case where they fired.

It does supply one antipattern worth carrying into the room: a workshop whose labs run on sample data rather than the customer's own scenarios. That is a cause, it is stated in the past tense, and it is checkable — which makes it a better model of the form than anything else the corpus contains. It is access-controlled, paraphrased here and never quoted, and contains **no cost side of any kind** and not one external citation in roughly ninety documents.

## The workflow

**Turn three pre-mortem causes into an evaluation that runs today and fails today, using the Agent Framework's evaluation model.**

**Step 1 — generate before you discuss, and bound it before you start.** Everybody writes causes alone, in the past tense. Contributions are collected without attribution. Only then does the room work on them. Discussion first produces the room's most confident member's list with everybody else's names on it. **Write your bound in the record before the writing starts.**

**Step 2 — route, and route cheaply.** For each cause, name the observable and then the cheapest instrument that produces it: a question to a named person, a constraint check from [S006](/wiki/seminars/S006.md), a look at something, a spike question for [S031](/wiki/seminars/S031.md), or an acceptance with an owner. Only the causes about the system's own behaviour go into step 3.

**Step 3 — write the dataset before the system.** Create `evals/premortem.jsonl`, one row per cause, in the framework's test-data format. Each row is a scenario a working system would survive and the feared system would not.

```jsonl
{"id":"PM-01","cause":"The agent answered from a document the caller was not entitled to see.","query":"Summarise the pricing terms for the Northbridge account.","expected_tool_calls":["search_documents"],"expected_output":"REFUSAL_OR_NO_RESULT","note":"Caller holds no Northbridge entitlement."}
{"id":"PM-02","cause":"The agent called the right tool with the wrong arguments and reported success.","query":"Close the ticket raised yesterday about the failed export.","expected_tool_calls":["find_ticket","close_ticket"],"expected_output":"EXACTLY_ONE_TICKET_CLOSED","note":"Two tickets match export. Only one was raised yesterday."}
{"id":"PM-03","cause":"The agent recovered on turn three and nobody noticed the first answer had already been acted on.","query":"What is the notice period on this contract?","expected_output":"CORRECT_ON_FIRST_RESPONSE","note":"Score per-turn. The default split cannot see this row fail."}
```

**Step 4 — choose the split strategy deliberately and write down why.** Last turn is the default and it will hide PM-03 entirely, because an agent that recovers looks fine when you read only its last answer. Full will hide it too. **Per-turn is the only strategy that can see the cause you wrote**, and discovering that is the point of the exercise. Record the strategy beside every row it applies to. **A score reported without its split strategy is a number whose question is unknown.**

**Step 5 — write the local checks first and only then reach for a judge.** PM-02 is deterministic: count the tickets closed. Write it as a local check that runs with no API call, in the inner loop and in continuous integration. PM-01 may be deterministic too, depending on how entitlement is expressed. Anything needing an opinion goes to a model-judged evaluator, and **you have now acquired a second measurement problem**, because no agreement rate with human judgement is published for any of them.

**Step 6 — set the threshold and justify it in one sentence.** Model-judged evaluators on this platform return a pass or fail with a reason, and for scale-based ones a numeric score against a threshold. **The threshold is a policy choice separable from the measurement**; moving it changes the pass rate without changing the system. Write the sentence that defends yours. If the only defence is that it was the default, say so — that is a finding about your evaluation, recorded honestly.

**Step 7 — run it and expect it to fail.** There is nothing to pass it. Capture the failure output and put it in the pre-mortem record beside the cause it came from. **You have converted a fear into an artefact that keeps asking the question after everybody has stopped worrying about it**, which is the only durable output of this session.

**Step 8 — attack your own record with the instrument, at two named targets.** Do not ask the agent to review your pre-mortem. Give it the design and your causes and ask for **causes you did not name, with a bias towards the non-technical**; then give it your JSONL and ask **which of your rows would pass under a different split strategy, and which of your causes have no row at all.** Keep what you could not have produced, discard the generic, and record the split. **A model's list of project risks is long, plausible and mostly about any project rather than about yours** — the discard rate is the measurement.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That a check written before the system is a different object from a check written after it, and that the difference is visible in what the two are afraid of. **It also makes the split-strategy decision concrete**: you will write a cause the default strategy cannot see, and watch it pass. Nothing in the output marks that as a problem, which is why the choice has to be made deliberately and stated whenever a number is reported.

**The row you do not own, and the record that has to carry it.**

Every cause above became a check because it was about your own system. **This one cannot, and the discipline is in refusing to pretend otherwise.**

**Step A — write the capacity cause as an account, with the mechanism cited.** Past tense, specific, with the published prioritisation rule quoted in the row and the date you read it. **A cause whose mechanism you can cite outranks four you invented**, and it is the only row in the record a reader can check without knowing your project.

**Step B — run the observable before the meeting ends.** Query consumption against limits, and query available capacity for the model and region your design names. **Then cross-check the answer against lifecycle status**, because the capacity interfaces report on models that are retired and cannot be deployed. Record both answers and the date. **This is a query, not a spike**, and routing it to an investigation is the failure exercise 4 counts.

**Step C — fill the row and accept it explicitly.** Put it in the record with the other causes and let it look different from them, because it is.

```yaml
# docs/premortem/record.yaml — the row you do not own
- cause: >
    The capacity increase went in during week one and was still unanswered in
    week six. The published rule prioritises customers already consuming their
    allocation; we were a new engagement and had consumed nothing.
  mechanism_cited: "provider quota policy, read <DATE>"   # NOT a guess
  observable:
    - "usage against limit, queried <DATE>: <ANSWER>"
    - "capacity for <model>/<region>, queried <DATE>: <ANSWER>"
    - "lifecycle status for <model>, queried <DATE>: <ANSWER>"   # capacity APIs
                                                                 # report retired models
  route: acceptance          # NOT mitigation. We do not own this.
  owner: "<ROLE>"            # by role. 'the team' is not an owner.
  chase: "<CADENCE>"         # who asks, how often, and of whom
  plan_if_no: >
    NAME IT. A different region, a different model, a later slice, or the
    engagement stops. An acceptance with no plan_if_no is a hope.
  closed_routes:             # facts, not risks
    - "partner and community models: increases are not supported at all"
    - "agent service file, message and tool limits: stated as fixed, cannot be raised"
  permissions:
    - "who may VIEW usage: <ROLE>"
    - "who may REQUEST an increase: <ROLE>"   # frequently not the same person
```

**Step D — attack the acceptance, not the cause.** Give the agent your filled row and ask it, in order, for **the closed route you have not noticed**, then **the sentence in `plan_if_no` that is a hope rather than a plan**, then **what has to be true for your chase cadence to work when the owner leaves.** Keep what beats your own reasoning; record the discard rate as before. **Do not ask whether the row is good.** An acceptance that the instrument endorsed is an acceptance nobody has read.

**What this second half makes observable.** That a pre-mortem contains two kinds of row and only one of them becomes a check. **The rows you own become executable and the rows you do not own become named people**, and a record that produced only the first kind has quietly restricted the exercise to the part of the world the team controls — which is not where most engagements fail.

## What good looks like

- Causes are written in the past tense as accounts, not in the conditional as risks.
- **At least one cause is organisational, political or commercial**, and it is stated as plainly as the technical ones.
- **At least one cause was uncomfortable to write**, and the record says which and why.
- Every cause is routed, and the routes are not all *spike it*. A cause that a single question to a named person would settle should be routed there.
- **At least three causes became executable checks**, and each check states the split strategy under which it is scored.
- **At least one cause is explicitly accepted**, with an owner and with the condition that would reopen it.
- **The record changed something.** Either the design, the charter from [S031](/wiki/seminars/S031.md), or the order of the work — and the record says which.

## How it goes wrong

- **The risk register in fancy dress** — the causes are hedged, conditional and generic, and could have been written about any project. *Warning sign:* your causes contain the words *may*, *could* or *potentially*.
- **The polite pre-mortem** — the real cause is known to three people in the room and none of them says it. *Warning sign:* nothing on the list would embarrass anybody present.
- **The all-technical failure** — every cause is an engineering cause, in a discipline whose own material says resistance originates in ownership of process rather than in technical incapacity. *Warning sign:* no row names a person's interests.
- **The ratified plan** — the pre-mortem is run after the plan is fixed, so nothing it produces can change anything. *Warning sign:* the record's outputs are all *monitor*.
- **The unowned acceptance** — a cause is accepted by the group, which means by nobody. *Warning sign:* an owner column with blanks in it.
- **The check that cannot see its own cause** — the row passes because the unit of analysis hides the failure it was written for. *Warning sign:* you accepted the default split strategy without stating it.
- **The exhaustive list** — forty causes, none routed, filed. *Warning sign:* the record took longer to produce than the cheapest test on it would have taken to run.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The pre-mortem record is submitted with the finding at [S035](/wiki/seminars/S035.md), where the routing and the acceptances are read against what actually happened.

**1 · Rewrite five hedges as accounts.** Take these and rewrite each in the past tense, specifically enough that somebody could tell whether it is occurring now.

- *Scope may expand beyond what was agreed.* · *The customer could be slow to provide access.* · *Integration might prove more complex than expected.* · *There is a risk of stakeholder disengagement.* · *Model performance may not meet expectations.*

> **Model answers.** *Three capabilities nobody costed were added in week five, each by a different person, and none went through the design record.* · *We asked for read access in week one and were still asking in week six, because the request needed a security review nobody had mentioned.* · *The case system returned a different record shape for archived cases and we found out in week four.* · *The sponsor stopped coming to the weekly session after week three and sent nobody.* · *The system was right on the cases we chose and wrong on the ones the operations team chose, and we did not run the second set until week seven.* **The test is whether a person could go and look today.** If they cannot, it is a mood.

**2 · Find the cause your default strategy cannot see.** Run workflow steps 3 and 4. Write one cause concerning an agent's first response, score it under the default split and under per-turn, and record what each says.

> **What you should find.** That an agent which recovers well scores well under the default and badly under per-turn, and that **nothing in either output tells you which question was asked.** The documentation states this directly — how you split determines what you are evaluating — and it is the most consequential and most easily missed decision in the whole evaluation model. **If you reported a score this term without stating a split strategy, you reported an answer to a question you did not choose.**

**3 · Go back to your own item 3, then break it.** Find what you wrote for opening item 3 — which of your causes somebody would have been reluctant to say out loud, and why. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then write the cause properly, in the past tense, and name the observable that would show it happening now.

> **Criterion.** An answer passes if it names a person's *interests* rather than a person's character or a general reluctance. **"People are uncomfortable challenging leadership" fails** — true everywhere, checkable nowhere. **"The sponsor's own team built the system we are replacing, and the cause that says so implicates their work in front of their director" passes**, because it names who loses, what they lose and in front of whom — and because it tells you who to talk to alone. **If every cause you wrote was technical, notice that**: the discipline whose material you are reading holds that resistance originates in ownership of process rather than in technical incapacity.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Route ten causes and count the spikes.** Take your pooled list and route every row. Then count how many went to *spike it*.

> **Scoring criterion.** More than a third routed to spikes means the routing has not been done — it has been deferred, with the word *spike* standing in for *we will think about this later*. **Most causes are settled by asking somebody, looking at something, or checking a constraint that was recorded as a belief.** A cause becomes a spike question only when no cheaper instrument exists, and the row should name the cheaper instruments considered and why each was insufficient.

**5 · Grade your own judge.** Take one model-judged evaluator you configured in step 5. Hand-label a small set of outputs yourself, compare your labels to its verdicts, and write what you found.

> **What to notice.** Two things, and the second matters more. First, wherever you disagree, **you cannot tell from the evaluator's output which of you is wrong** — the reason field makes the judgement auditable, which a bare number would not, and auditable is not the same as correct. Second: **the documentation publishes no agreement rate with human judgement for any of these evaluators, no calibration data and no account of where the default thresholds came from.** That absence is why this exercise exists. Using a model to grade a model does not solve the trust problem, it moves it ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/shankar-evalgen.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/shankar-evalgen.md), **whose evidence for this is qualitative and from a small interface study**).

**6 · Predict your own criteria drift.** Write down which of your pre-mortem-derived criteria you expect to still hold once you have seen real outputs, and which you expect to have replaced. Seal it. Reopen it at [S035](/wiki/seminars/S035.md).

> **What to notice.** The literature's claim is that some criteria are **output-dependent** — they become articulable only after looking, which "raises serious questions for approaches that assume the independence of evaluation from observation of model outputs". **That is an argument against the exercise you have just done**, and this session accepts it rather than answering it. The narrower claim it defends is that a check written while nobody has a stake in it passing is the one you will never write later. **Your sealed prediction is the only instrument in this module that tests whether that is true.**

**7 · Sort your causes by whether anybody has published the mechanism.** Take your pooled list and mark each row *invented*, *reported* or *published* — invented if the mechanism is your own reasoning, reported if a person told you, published if a party who owns the thing has written the rule down. Then rewrite one *invented* row about an external dependency as a *published* one.

> **What you should find.** That almost every row is invented, that the published ones are nearly all about platform dependencies rather than about people, and that **a published row is shorter and harder to argue with**. The capacity row is the model: the provider states that increase requests are processed in order of receipt and that priority goes to customers already consuming their allocation, and that requests failing that test might be denied ([research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-control-plane-and-governance.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-control-plane-and-governance.md), **definitional and not evidential; no response time, approval rate or appeal route is published anywhere; most of its pages were read through search excerpts and their tables may be truncated**). **What makes that row good is not that it is frightening. It is that a reader who has never met your customer can check it.**
>
> **Two failure shapes to watch for.** A learner who marks a row *published* because a vendor page mentions the topic has confused a citation with a mechanism — the test is whether the page states the rule that would produce the failure. And a learner whose *published* row asserts how long the request will take has invented the part that matters: **no duration is published, and a row that supplies one has smuggled a guess into the strongest column in the record.**

## What this does not establish

Nothing here shows that pre-mortems prevent failures.

The playbook supplies the protocol and offers **no comparative evidence** for it, which is true of that document throughout. Its pages carry footer dates spread across several years, roughly a fifth of it is stubs or link lists, **it contradicts itself in at least a dozen places without noticing**, and it asserts four unsupported magnitude claims which this repository records as specimens and reproduces nowhere. Cite it by page and by the date the page was read.

**The claim that stating an outcome as fact produces fuller explanations than asking for a forecast is direction and mechanism only**, cited from general knowledge and not verified in this repository. **No magnitude is asserted.**

The internal practice corpus cannot help here in a specific and instructive way. It records **no failed engagement at all**, and its only failure narrative is a synthetic composite that the material itself labels fictional. **A practice with no failures has no pre-mortems worth reading**, because the causes it would list have never been checked against a case where they fired. It also contains **no cost side of any kind** and not one external citation in roughly ninety documents, and it is access-controlled, so nothing in it can be checked by a reader of this page.

**The claim that anonymised generation surfaces suppressed causes is design reasoning with no external warrant.** It is held because the alternative — asking a room to volunteer the thing that implicates its own leadership — has an obvious mechanism working against it, and that is an argument rather than a finding.

**The evaluation literature behind this session is practitioner and qualitative work, not measurement.** The features-and-scenarios decomposition and the three-level hierarchy come from a practitioner essay resting on one company's case study, with no comparison group, no measured improvement and no released data; its recommendations are well-specified hypotheses. The criteria-drift finding is established interpretively from a small interface study — it shows that the phenomenon occurs and matters, **not how often or how much** — and the tool it describes is a research prototype.

**The platform material is first-party documentation, definitional and not empirical**, read through search-returned excerpts rather than full page fetches. Many of the evaluators named are **preview** and one consulted page is a **classic** variant explicitly superseded. Most consequentially: **no agreement rate with human judgement, no calibration data and no threshold rationale is published for any evaluator.** This wiki therefore **asserts no evaluator's agreement with human judgement**, because none exists to assert. A pass rate produced by a model-judged evaluator is a measurement whose instrument has not been calibrated in public.

**And the capacity row is a documented mechanism, not a documented outcome.** The provider states the prioritisation rule and states nothing about how it plays out: **no service level for a quota request, no response time, no approval rate, no rejection-reason distribution and no appeal route beyond resubmission.** The temporary shared pool's duration is given as depending on the use case, which is unquantified and unplannable. **Nothing published says how often a request of any shape is granted, or how long anybody has waited**, so the row states a mechanism and a risk and cannot state a likelihood — which is the correct shape for a pre-mortem row and a poor shape for a plan. The one community thread on the subject shows a customer frustrated by the wait and **establishes only that the question is asked**; a Q&A answer is not documentation and no duration may be inferred from it. **This session asserts no probability, no duration and no magnitude anywhere in that row**, and a learner who wrote one has invented the strongest-sounding part of their own record.

**The recursive point about the evaluation harness is also mechanism-only.** Continuous evaluation samples, and skips runs at an hourly ceiling whose default nobody has justified in public. **No study, comparison or false-negative rate exists showing that continuous evaluation catches anything a manual review would not**, and the stated benefit is a claim about what the feature is for rather than a finding about what it does.
