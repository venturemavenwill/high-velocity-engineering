# S023 · Non-Functional Requirements That Can Be Verified

**Module:** [M05](/wiki/modules/M05.md) · **Quarter:** [Q3](/wiki/quarters/Q3.md) · **Complexity class:** 2
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** Azure API Management AI gateway and the Microsoft 365 Agent Store validation criteria — non-functional requirements with an enforcement point you own, beside published thresholds enforced by a party who is not the customer and measured by an instrument nobody names
**Whitepaper:** [WP-023](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-023.md)
**Retrieves from:** S006, S021, S022
**Entry state:** either

---

## The situation

Everything in your requirement set so far was volunteered. Somebody wanted it and said so.

**The requirements that end engagements are not volunteered.** Nobody asks for availability until it is absent, for portability until they are moving, for data-handling discipline until somebody has looked at production data, or for maintainability until the person who wrote it has left.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught.

1. Name three properties this system must have that nobody has asked for.
2. Which of them is the customer's policy rather than your engineering choice?
3. Which one, violated once, ends the engagement?
4. Who owns each of them inside the customer's organisation — by role, not by name?
5. **What is your evidence that the number you just wrote down is the right number?**

**Item 5 is the session and most people have no answer to it.** The number came from a document, or from the last engagement, or from a catalogue of examples somebody circulated. That is a real origin and it is an acceptable one, provided it is written down as the origin rather than presented as a specification.

## What you must produce

A **non-functional requirement set**, in the same eight-field capture form as [S022](/wiki/seminars/S022.md), with the same two fields marked — the measurement and the verification method — and one addition that this session imposes: **every threshold carries its origin.**

The set is produced by walking the published enumeration of non-functional categories one at a time, and for each category writing either the requirement or the sentence saying why it does not apply here. Pages read 2026-07-30.

And one more thing, which is the closing exercise: **the category the enumeration does not have**, added by you and marked as added.

## The material

### These requirements have no advocate in the room

Every property in this session shares one structural feature: **nobody in the discovery raised it, because nobody in the discovery is accountable for it.**

The sponsor is accountable for outcomes and speaks about outcomes. The operational manager is accountable for a process and speaks about the process. The people accountable for availability, for data handling, for what happens when the author leaves, are usually in a different part of the organisation, were not in the room, and — as [S007](/wiki/seminars/S007.md) established — may not have been invited.

**The consequence is that these requirements enter the engagement late, from a review function, after the design is fixed.** At that point they are experienced as obstruction, they are argued about rather than engineered, and the argument is conducted in the vocabulary of compliance rather than of design.

They arrive by one of two routes: from an enumeration, or from an incident. This session uses an enumeration and accepts that the enumeration is a poor instrument. **A poor instrument used early beats a good one used late**, and that is an argument rather than a finding.

### The enumeration is a list somebody wrote, and its defects are teaching material

The published section this session walks lists categories including availability, capacity, data integrity, disaster recovery, performance, portability, maintainability, internationalisation, interoperability, scalability, usability, accessibility, reliability and privacy ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md)). Pages read 2026-07-30.

**Read as a checklist it is useful. Read as a taxonomy it is not, and the source itself shows why.** The section has no index page — it is the one address in the whole publication that does not resolve. Only some of its category pages follow the structure the section itself proposes; two of the strongest abandon it entirely and are inherited essays. Scalability is two bullets. Interoperability is four. One page's list rendering is broken. The privacy index is two sentences.

**A menu that implies completeness is not a completeness claim.** That is why the session closes by requiring you to add a category the list does not have. Candidates that recur in this kind of work and are absent or thin here: what happens to the system when the engagement ends; what the customer must be able to observe without you; what evidence the system must produce about its own behaviour; and what it costs to run at the volume actually expected.

Two pages in the section are worth reading closely because they are the best in it. The reliability page states the operating stance directly — "If a user ever makes a mistake, don't ask: 'how could somebody possibly do that?', do ask: 'how can we prevent this from happening in the future?'" — and concedes that it is a known fact that systems cannot target complete uptime, which is a rare admission in a document of this kind. And the privacy page states an absolute: **"Never directly access production data."** That is the shape some non-functional requirements have, and it matters that you notice it: **there is no threshold version of that sentence.** A requirement written as a target where the customer's policy makes it absolute has been silently downgraded.

### Every threshold carries its origin, and the origin most learners have is a catalogue

The same publication supplies the eight-field capture form from [S022](/wiki/seminars/S022.md), and on the same page a large catalogue of example thresholds — response times, availability figures, coverage targets, complexity limits. **That catalogue carries no citation of any kind.** The numbers are illustrations by the authors' own framing, and they sit on the page in the same flat declarative register as the publication's best-sourced material.

An engineer who lifts a number from it has done nothing wrong. **An engineer who lifts it and strips the provenance has manufactured a specification, and the only difference between the two is a clause.**

The complete reading of that publication found the same defect elsewhere in it — a cost claim about code review, a velocity claim about pairing, a defect-cost multiple attributed through a link that can no longer be followed, and vendor efficiency figures with bands wide enough to be unfalsifiable. **None of the four is reproduced here or anywhere in this wiki.** The pattern is the lesson: the document does not distinguish between a practice it has reasoned about and a number it has repeated, and nothing on the page tells you which you are reading.

**The playbook's other limits.** Its pages carry individual footer dates spanning several years, so it has no single currency; pin every claim to a page and that page's own date. It offers no comparative evidence that any of its practices outperform alternatives and does not claim to.

### Latency and throughput are architectural consequences, not settings

This is the technical content the session exists to deliver, and it is why a latency or availability figure chosen because it sounds serious is worse than no figure at all.

Inference is not one operation. **Processing the prompt and generating the response are different workloads with different bottlenecks** ([research/02-technical-foundations/pope-scaling-transformer-inference.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/pope-scaling-transformer-inference.md)). Prompt processing handles many tokens at once and can keep the accelerator busy; generation produces tokens one at a time and is dominated by moving weights and cached state through memory rather than by arithmetic. The practical consequence is that **raw compute capability is a poor predictor of the latency a user experiences**, and that a context-length increase can cost more than it looks, because the cached attention state rather than the parameter count is what limits long-context serving. That paper reports specific operating points on specific hardware; **no number from it appears here, and its concrete results are tied to an accelerator topology that has since moved on.**

Throughput has the same character. Serving many requests at once is the throughput lever, and what limits how many fit is memory management of that cached state rather than arithmetic ([research/02-technical-foundations/kwon-pagedattention-vllm.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/kwon-pagedattention-vllm.md)). The named waste mechanisms are fragmentation and duplication; both cap how many requests fit; and how many fit caps throughput. **The improvements reported in that work are stated at matched latency against named 2023-era baselines and are conditional on longer sequences, larger models and more complex decoding. No figure from it appears here.**

**What you take from both is a shape rather than a number.** Latency and throughput trade against each other, so any honest performance claim is a claim about a curve rather than a point. A requirement that names one without the other has not specified an operating point. And **a requirement naming a latency figure with no statement of which workload, at what prompt length, at what concurrency, has named a number and not a property.**

The engineering position that closes this is that an evaluation is an experiment: a reported figure needs an interval and a count ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md)). Applied here: **a non-functional requirement stated as a single number, with no distribution and no observation window, cannot be missed and cannot be met.** The practitioner treatment says the same thing operationally — because latency is a distribution, the average can mislead, so report percentiles ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/huyen-ai-engineering.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/huyen-ai-engineering.md)). **Both sources' limits:** the first is analytical, its illustrative magnitudes rest on stylised assumptions, and it addresses comparing scores rather than choosing what to score; the second is a practitioner book that reports most of its figures second-hand from other people's studies.

### A non-functional requirement needs an enforcement point, and the gateway is one

Here is where this session stops being a document exercise.

The argument published for an AI gateway is that the reverse proxy in front of model endpoints is the correct place to enforce cost control, quota fairness, authentication, resilience and observability ([research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md)). The framing is unusually honest: this is "not a separate offering" but an extension of an existing API gateway, which makes the intellectual claim visible — **AI traffic is API traffic with different resource semantics, not a new category of thing.**

The five stated challenges are worth memorising as a checklist, because they are exactly the concerns that do not appear in a prototype: authenticate and authorise access; load balance across endpoints; monitor and log interactions; manage token usage and quotas across multiple applications; and enable self-service for developer teams. **Every one is a multi-tenant concern.** A single application talking to a single model needs none of them, which is precisely why they are invisible until the second application appears.

The scalability reasoning is the sharpest thing on the page and it generalises. Tokens are the scarce resource; providers assign quota as tokens per minute; that quota must be distributed "across your model consumers, such as different applications, developer teams, or departments within the company", and as the portfolio grows "you need to make sure that one app doesn't use the whole TPM quota and block other apps from accessing the backends they need." **That is a noisy-neighbour problem, and the general lesson is that a shared finite resource with no per-consumer accounting will be consumed by whichever consumer is least careful.**

Four mechanisms convert prose into an enforceable requirement.

**The token limit policy** enforces a rate or a longer-period quota, keyed on "any counter key, such as subscription key, originating IP address, or an arbitrary key defined through a policy expression." It can precalculate prompt tokens at the gateway, "minimizing unnecessary requests to the AI service backend if the prompt already exceeds the limit" — a fail-fast design that rejects at the edge rather than paying to discover the rejection downstream. **The counter key is the requirement's unit of fairness, and choosing it is an engineering decision that most requirement documents never state.**

**Metric dimensions are what make a cost requirement enforceable.** A policy can attach dimensions such as client address, API identifier and a user header to emitted token metrics. **Total consumption tells you the bill; consumption per API per user tells you who to talk to** — and attribution is what makes governance possible at all.

**The circuit breaker takes its recovery timing from the backend's `Retry-After` header** rather than from a fixed interval, which is a better design because it lets the failing service state its own recovery time rather than being guessed at. And **priority load balancing exists to saturate paid-for dedicated capacity before overflowing to shared capacity**, which is the direct operational consequence of having bought reserved throughput.

**Semantic caching is the one to be careful with.** It reuses previous completions "by comparing the vector proximity of the prompt to prior requests", and it trades correctness for cost and latency. **The similarity threshold is a correctness knob**: set it loosely and the system returns an answer to a question that was not asked. **The documentation does not discuss this at all.** That gap is yours to fill, and it is a perfect worked example of this session's thesis — a performance requirement met by a mechanism that silently weakens a correctness requirement nobody wrote down.

One caveat on the page prevents the obvious misreading, and it is a general distributed-systems lesson dressed as a product caveat: scaling the gateway does not scale the backends, which must also be scaled and co-located regionally. **Scaling the front door does not widen the corridor behind it.**

**Its limits.** First-party product documentation with a commercial frame; it argues for a gateway and does not discuss the latency, cost or operational burden the gateway itself adds. **It publishes no performance data, no cost-saving measurement and no comparison against any other product** — the benefits it describes are plausible mechanisms rather than measured outcomes. Capability availability varies by service tier, so any exercise must confirm what the available tier supports. Several features are preview. Policy names and attributes drift between revisions. And it does not address the privacy implications of logging prompts and completions, despite recommending logging for billing and auditing.

### A threshold set by somebody who will enforce it, and an instrument nobody names

The gateway is **your** enforcement point, chosen by you and operated by you. There is a second kind, and the difference between them is the reason it belongs in this session rather than in a publishing module: **a requirement enforced by a party who is not the customer and not you.**

An agent submitted to the Microsoft 365 Agent Store passes a published set of must-fix requirements before it is admitted ([research/06-microsoft-platform/agents-toolkit-and-sdk-family.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/agents-toolkit-and-sdk-family.md), read 2026-07-31). Most of them are architectural, cheap to design for and expensive to retrofit. Every response from a custom engine agent must carry an AI label, citations and a feedback control. Responses must be streamed, and must be followed by suggested prompts. The agent must declare a named scope in two separate places in its manifest. Each command needs a small stated range of sample prompts — not fewer, and not more. An Adaptive Card response must carry its preview and its content in one template, must represent more than branding, and must include a metadata URL. The agent's name must be identical across three different files. Compatibility must hold across several named client surfaces. And **an agent supporting API or Model Context Protocol actions must request explicit user permission before making any server call.** A manifest schema floor applies now, and a higher floor applies from a stated future date to agents operating in channels.

There is also a content rule on the store listing itself, and it is worth a moment because of what it implies. Short descriptions must not contain instructional phrases — the guidance gives examples of the *if the user says X, ignore previous instructions* shape — nor marketing superlatives, URLs, emojis, or "hidden characters like hexadecimal, binary, or unconventional symbols". **The vendor is treating its own app catalogue as an untrusted-input surface.** That is a non-functional requirement on a *document*, which is unusual enough to notice, and it is the same conclusion the agent-configuration literature reaches from the other direction.

**Two of the requirements are numeric.** Response time is specified as a set of percentile obligations, and availability as a figure with a worked gloss attached explaining what it means in calls. **Neither number appears in this wiki and neither should enter your requirement set as a benchmark.** They are contractual admission thresholds set by one party for its own catalogue; reproducing them here would be the exact failure this session exists to prevent, which is a number acquiring the authority of a specification by being repeated somewhere respectable.

**Now read them as this session reads a threshold, and the finding arrives immediately.**

**No instrument is published. No population. No observation window. No sampling rule.** It is not stated whether the vendor measures these properties, whether the publisher is expected to, or what evidence is accepted in place of a measurement. Compare that against what exercise 3 will ask of your own latency requirement — the workload, the prompt length, the concurrency, the percentile — and notice that a gate with the power to refuse your product satisfies none of it. **A requirement enforced by a gate whose measurement method is unstated is this session's subject wearing a uniform.** The number is real, the consequence of missing it is real, and what would count as missing it is not written down anywhere.

The silence extends past the method to the outcome. **There is no published submission pass rate, no rejection rate, no distribution of rejection reasons, no median time to approval, and no statement of how many resubmissions are typical.** A team can read exactly what is required of it and cannot learn how often anybody meets it — which means the risk this gate poses to a delivery date is unquantifiable from published material, and belongs on a register as an open dependency rather than in a plan as a step.

One further constraint is stated once, on one page, with no alternative offered: **publishing through this toolchain is unsupported in Government tenants.** Treat that as a real constraint, re-verify it before advising anyone, and note where it belongs — [S026](/wiki/seminars/S026.md), as a constraint whose authority is not on your engagement.

**And there is a companion check with a knowable and much narrower reach**, which is worth separating so it is not over-credited. A responsible-AI validation runs at manifest time and inspects **the declared name, description and instructions**, blocking publication where the declared text encourages harm, provokes argument, attempts to bypass guidelines or violates copyright. It inspects nothing the agent does, no tool, no data source and no produced answer. **No calibration is published for it** — it is not stated whether the check is deterministic or model-judged, no false-positive or false-negative rate is given, no appeal path is described beyond making updates and trying again, and **no evidence is offered that a manifest passing the check yields an agent that behaves acceptably.** File it against the risks it genuinely reduces and refuse to file it against the rest; [S053](/wiki/seminars/S053.md) is where that discipline is assessed.

**Its limits, and they are heavier than the gateway's.** Read 2026-07-31. **The Agent Store validation guidelines and the marketplace certification policies were not fetched at all** — the requirements above are the subset returned by search excerpts, so **this is not the complete gate and must never be presented as one.** Several of the pages behind it were read only through excerpts, with page structure reconstructed. The ergonomics claims in the surrounding documentation — that the toolchain "streamlines" and "dramatically simplifies" — are marketing register with no measurement anywhere and carry no weight. **No measure exists of how much any of this reduces time to a working agent, and no claim about delivery duration is supported by any of it.** And the names decay unusually fast: every principal name in this toolchain has changed at least once, one package has changed name twice, and one component ships today under two live package names with two commands.

## The workflow

**Take one non-functional requirement, give it an enforcement point, then find the correctness requirement your enforcement quietly weakened.**

Steps 1, 2, 5, 6 and 7 need no subscription and no deployment. Steps 3 and 4 need a gateway you can configure; if you do not have one, write the policy and reason about it — **the policy is the artefact this session wants, and running it is a bonus.**

**Step 1 — walk the enumeration and write the requirement or the refusal for every category.** Expect the refusals to be the harder writing, because a refusal has to say why. Silence is not a declaration: an engagement that decided portability was irrelevant and wrote that down has made a decision; one that never mentioned it has made the same decision invisibly.

**Step 2 — put an origin next to every number.** Where the honest answer is that you copied it, write that.

**Step 3 — choose the counter key before you choose the limit.** Per subscription, per application, per team, per end user. **This is the fairness decision, and it is a requirement rather than a configuration** — a quota keyed on the wrong dimension enforces the wrong policy perfectly.

**Step 4 — write the policy fragment that enforces one requirement, and the metric that observes it.**

```xml
<!-- Illustrative. Policy names, attributes and tier availability drift between
     revisions; verify against current documentation before use. -->
<inbound>
  <!-- NFR-07: no single consuming application may exhaust the shared token
       quota. The counter key is the requirement's unit of fairness.
       Origin of the limit: policy | contract | observed baseline | borrowed, uncited
       Owner inside the customer: a named role, not your team -->
  <llm-token-limit counter-key="@(context.Subscription.Id)"
                   tokens-per-minute="STATE IT, AND STATE ITS ORIGIN"
                   estimate-prompt-tokens="true"
                   remaining-tokens-variable-name="remainingTokens" />

  <!-- NFR-09: token spend must be attributable to a consumer.
       A metric without dimensions tells you the bill and not who to talk to. -->
  <llm-emit-token-metric namespace="llm-metrics">
    <dimension name="API ID" value="@(context.Api.Id)" />
    <dimension name="Consumer" value="@(context.Subscription.Id)" />
  </llm-emit-token-metric>
</inbound>
<backend>
  <!-- NFR-12: a failing backend must be given its own recovery time rather
       than a guessed one. Circuit-breaker trip duration is taken from the
       backend's Retry-After header, and is configured on the backend resource. -->
</backend>
```

**Step 5 — find the requirement your enforcement weakened.** Every mechanism above buys one property at the expense of another. A quota buys fairness and costs availability to the consumer who hits it. A cache buys latency and cost and **spends correctness**. A circuit breaker buys isolation and costs completeness. Write the sentence naming the trade for the mechanism you chose, and say who agreed to it.

**Step 6 — attack your own set with the agent.** Give it the non-functional set, the policy and nothing else, with one instruction: *build a system that satisfies every one of these and that the author would refuse to operate.* Then ask a second question: *which of these numbers could I move without anybody noticing?* Record what it produced and what you changed.

The second question is the one that matters. **A threshold nobody would notice moving is a threshold with no consequence attached**, and a non-functional requirement with no consequence is a preference that has been formatted.

**Step 7 — hand the set to somebody whose only instruction is to ask, for every number, *where did that come from?*** Answers of "it's standard", "that's what we normally use" and "the client expects it" are struck through on the spot. What survives is your non-functional set. **What was struck goes back to you with the number intact and the origin missing, which is the state most of these documents are in when they are signed.**

**Step 8 — take one requirement imposed on you from outside and supply the four fields its author did not.** Pick a published store criterion, a partner's service commitment, a regulator's stated target — anything with a number and an enforcer who is not you. Write the instrument, the population, the observation window and the sampling rule **that you will use**, and mark every one of them as your own construction rather than the enforcer's.

```yaml
# docs/nfr/externally-enforced.yaml
# One entry per requirement imposed by a party who can refuse the product.
# The point of the file is the `published_by_enforcer: false` lines.

- id: NFR-21
  statement: "Response latency must satisfy the store's stated percentile obligations."
  enforced_by: "Catalogue operator. Not the customer, and not us."
  consequence_of_missing: "Submission refused. The product does not ship."
  origin: "published gate criterion"          # a real origin, and not ours
  threshold: "AS PUBLISHED — do not restate the figure in teaching material"

  # Everything below is OURS. The enforcer published none of it, and the
  # `published_by_enforcer` block is the honest part of this file.
  measurement:
    instrument: "gateway-emitted latency metric at the edge"
    population: "which requests? all, or one command, or one client surface?"
    window:     "over what period, ending when?"
    sampling:   "every request, or a sample at what rate?"
    workload:   "prompt length and concurrency, without which the figure is not a property"

  published_by_enforcer:
    instrument: false
    population: false
    window:     false
    sampling:   false
    workload:   false
    # Five falses. It is not even stated whether the enforcer measures this
    # itself, whether we are expected to, or what evidence it would accept.

  verification_method: >
    Who runs it, on what, and when. If the answer is "we find out at submission",
    write that. It is an honest verification method and a terrible one.

  owner_inside_customer: "NONE — nobody on their side is accountable for this"
  schedule_risk: >
    No pass rate, no rejection-reason distribution and no time to approval are
    published. This is an unbounded external dependency, not a plan step.
    Register it at S026 with an authority and a date.
```

**Step 9 — write what happens when it is missed, before you find out.** Two sentences. Who is told, and what the fallback is. **An externally enforced threshold with no fallback is a single point of failure wearing a compliance label**, and the fallback is a design decision that costs nothing today and cannot be made at all on the day of the refusal.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That a non-functional requirement is enforceable or it is not, and that the enforcement point is a place in an architecture rather than a paragraph in a document. Writing the policy forces three decisions the document let you leave implicit: the unit of fairness, the observation dimension, and the property you agreed to spend. **Steps 8 and 9 add the fourth**, which is that a requirement can be perfectly enforceable by somebody else and completely unmeasurable by you — and that discovering which of the two you are holding is a ten-minute reading exercise you can do before anybody commits to a date.

## What good looks like

- **Every category is either present or explicitly declared out of scope, with a reason.** Silence is not a declaration. An engagement that decided portability was irrelevant and wrote that down has made a decision; one that never mentioned it has made the same decision invisibly.
- **Every threshold has a stated origin.** Acceptable origins include: the customer's existing policy, a contractual obligation, an observed baseline, a regulator's published requirement, and *borrowed from a catalogue that carries no source*. **That last one is acceptable exactly once per number, in writing, and it is the origin most learners actually have.**
- **Each requirement names an owner inside the customer's organisation**, by role. A non-functional requirement with no owner on the customer's side has nobody to defend it when it becomes inconvenient, which is the moment it matters.
- **Where the customer's policy makes a property absolute, it is written as absolute** rather than as a target. Some data-handling constraints are of this kind — production data is not touched, and there is no threshold version of that sentence.
- **The property whose single violation ends the engagement is identified as such**, and its verification runs before the thing that could violate it, not after.
- **One category is added that the published enumeration does not cover**, with a note saying it was added. The enumeration is a list somebody wrote, not a taxonomy with warrant.

## How it goes wrong

- **The borrowed catalogue** — the numbers are all from the example bank, none carries an origin, and the set now looks like a standard. *Warning sign:* your thresholds are round, and several of them are the same round numbers as the last project's.
- **The un-owned property** — the requirement is well written and nobody on the customer's side is accountable for it. *Warning sign:* the owner field contains your own team.
- **The enumeration read as complete** — you worked through the categories the navigation showed you. The published section's own index page does not exist, some of its category pages are two bullets long, and most but not all of them follow the section's own structure. **A menu that implies completeness is not a completeness claim.** *Warning sign:* your set has exactly the categories the list had, in the list's order.
- **The aspirational availability figure** — a number chosen because it sounds serious, with no instrument that could observe it and no consequence attached to missing it. *Warning sign:* nobody asked what it would cost.
- **The constraint discovered at assurance** — a property that was always mandatory arrives during a security or compliance review, after the design is fixed. *Warning sign:* nobody from the customer's risk, security or data functions has read this set.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed here.** One thing from this session is checked at [S025](/wiki/seminars/S025.md): that every threshold carries a stated origin, and that *borrowed from a catalogue that carries no source* is declared where it is true.

**1 · Write four refusals.** Pick four categories from the enumeration that do not apply to your engagement and write the sentence saying why, for each.

> **Criterion.** A passing refusal names the property, says what would have to be true for it to matter, and states that it is not true here. "Portability is out of scope" fails. "The system runs only in the customer's existing tenant, and the contract contains no exit clause requiring relocation; if an exit clause is added, this becomes a requirement" passes. **The refusals are harder than the requirements and that is the point:** a refusal has to say why, and a requirement can hide behind a number.

**2 · Distinguish an absolute from a target.** For each of the following, say whether it admits a threshold at all:

- *Production data is not accessed directly.*
- *Ninety-fifth-percentile response time under the expected concurrency.*
- *Personal data is not written to application logs.*
- *Recovery time after a regional failure.*

> **Model answers.** The first and third admit no threshold — they are absolutes stated by policy, and the published privacy guidance states the first one in exactly that form. The second and fourth are targets and need a distribution, a workload and an observation window. **A requirement written as a target where the customer's policy makes it absolute has been silently downgraded, and the downgrade will be discovered by somebody in an assurance review after the design is fixed.**

**3 · Price a latency requirement properly.** Take one latency requirement and rewrite it to name the workload, the prompt length, the concurrency and the percentile rather than the average.

> **What you should find.** You cannot do it without asking somebody, and the person who knows is usually not the person who asked for the number. **That is the finding.** Note also which half of the work you were specifying: processing the prompt and generating the response are different workloads with different bottlenecks, and a single figure that spans both is a figure about neither.

**4 · Go back to your own item 5, then break it.** Find what you wrote for opening item 5 — your evidence that the number you wrote down is the right number. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then write the origin properly for every number in your set.

> **Criterion.** A passing origin is one of: the customer's existing policy, a contractual obligation, an observed baseline, a regulator's published requirement, or *borrowed from a catalogue that carries no source*. **The last is acceptable in writing and unacceptable silently**, and it is the origin most learners actually have. "Industry standard" is not an origin; it is a claim about other people that you have not checked.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**5 · Choose the counter key, then break it.** Using the workflow, write the token-limit policy for one requirement. Then name the consumer your chosen counter key treats unfairly.

> **What you should find.** Every counter key is a fairness policy and every fairness policy has a loser. Keyed per subscription, a team with one busy application starves its own quiet ones. Keyed per end user, a batch process has no identity to key on. Keyed per application, a shared internal library is invisible. **The question is not which key is correct. It is whether you can name who pays**, and whether the person who pays has been told.

**6 · Add the missing category.** Write one non-functional requirement in a category the published enumeration does not contain, in the same form, and mark it as added.

> **Scoring criterion.** A passing addition is real for your engagement, is not a rewording of a category the list already has, names an owner inside the customer's organisation, and carries a threshold with an origin or an explicit statement that it is absolute. **The commonest good answers concern what happens after you leave**, which is the largest single absence in the source corpus behind this programme and is worth noticing as such.
>
> **Then compare.** Look back at your answer to opening item 3 — the property whose single violation ends the engagement. Is it in your set with an owner and a verification that runs *before* the thing that could violate it? If the verification runs afterwards, you have written a detector rather than a requirement.

**7 · Audit a gate that can refuse your product.** Take one published set of admission criteria that a third party will enforce against your work. Sort every criterion into three piles: **architectural** — something you must build in and cannot bolt on; **declarative** — something you must state correctly in a manifest or a listing; and **numeric** — something with a figure attached. Then, for the numeric pile only, answer four questions: by what instrument, over what population, in what window, at what sampling rate?

> **What you should find.** That the architectural pile is the expensive one and the numeric pile is the unanswerable one. **You will not be able to answer any of the four questions**, because no instrument, population, observation window or sampling rule is published for any of the numeric criteria — it is not even stated whether the enforcer measures them or expects you to. That is the finding, and it is the same finding as exercise 4 with the roles reversed: **here you are the one being handed a number with no origin, by somebody with the power to refuse you.**
>
> **Two things a passing answer must also record.** That no pass rate, rejection-reason distribution or time to approval is published either, so the gate's effect on your schedule is unquantifiable from anything you can read — which makes it a register entry at [S026](/wiki/seminars/S026.md) with an authority and a date, not a task in a plan. And that **you do not reproduce the published figures in your own requirement set as though they were yours.** A gate criterion is an obligation you inherited; restating it without its enforcer and its silence converts it into a specification you appear to have chosen.
>
> **A caution on the exercise itself.** The criteria available to read are a subset — the guidelines and certification policies behind them were not fetched in full for this programme. **Treat your sorted list as incomplete by construction**, and say so, which is the honest version of the same admission this session demands of a category enumeration.

## What this does not establish

**The category enumeration is a list, not a validated taxonomy.** It was read in full on 2026-07-30; most but not all of its category pages follow the structure the section itself proposes, several are a few lines long, and **the section has no index page at all** — the one address in the whole publication that does not resolve. Nothing establishes that the categories are exhaustive, correctly cut, or the right ones for any particular system.

**The threshold catalogue carries no citation of any kind.** Its numbers are illustrations by the authors' own framing, they sit in the same flat declarative register as the publication's best-sourced pages, and nothing on the page distinguishes them. This session uses them as an example bank and **forbids them as benchmarks**, which is the strongest defensible use. The same publication reaches for a magnitude in several other places and warrants it in none; **none of those figures is reproduced anywhere in this wiki.**

**The claim that an unstated non-functional requirement surfaces later at higher cost is mechanically plausible and unmeasured here.** Direction only.

**The inference material establishes mechanism and not magnitude.** Both technical sources report specific figures on specific hardware against specific baselines, and **no number from either appears here.** One predates paged memory management, continuous batching, flash attention variants and speculative decoding, and is foundational rather than current; the other's headline comparison is against 2023-era systems and is explicitly conditional on sequence length, model size and decoding strategy. What transfers is the shape: prefill and decode are different workloads, memory rather than arithmetic is usually the binding constraint, and latency and throughput trade against one another.

**The gateway documentation publishes no performance or cost data of any kind.** It argues for a gateway and does not discuss the latency, cost or operational burden the gateway itself adds; it makes no comparison against any other product; capability availability varies by tier; several features are preview; and policy names and attributes drift between revisions. **The correctness risk of semantic caching is not discussed in it at all** — that analysis is this repository's, and the privacy consequences of logging prompts and completions are likewise absent from a page that recommends the logging.

**The store criteria recorded here are a subset and are not the complete gate.** The validation guidelines and the marketplace certification policies were not fetched; what appears above is what search excerpts returned on 2026-07-31, with page structure reconstructed rather than confirmed. **Absence at one reading is not proof of absence in the product**, and a team advising a real submission must read the current guidelines in full rather than this session.

**The published figures are contractual admission thresholds and are not measurements of anything.** They license no claim about how any agent performs, they are not reproduced anywhere in this wiki, and the session forbids their adoption as benchmarks on exactly the grounds it forbids the example catalogue's. **That no instrument, population, observation window or sampling rule accompanies them is a finding about the published material at one reading**, arrived at by searching for those things and not finding them — not a statement by any source that they do not exist. The same holds for the absence of a pass rate, a rejection-reason distribution and a time to approval. **No calibration, false-positive rate or appeal path is published for the responsible-AI manifest check**, and no evidence is offered anywhere that a manifest passing it yields an agent that behaves acceptably; the runtime check is a separate mechanism and is not evaluated here.

**Nothing here establishes that walking a list finds the requirements that matter.** It establishes that omitting a category becomes visible, which is a weaker and more defensible claim.
