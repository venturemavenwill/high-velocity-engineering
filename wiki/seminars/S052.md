# S052 · A Threat Bound to a Component and a Flow

**Module:** [M11](/wiki/modules/M11.md) · **Quarter:** [Q6](/wiki/quarters/Q6.md) · **Complexity class:** 4
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** Microsoft Defender AI security posture management — a threat that names a component and a data flow rather than a category
**Whitepaper:** [WP-052](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-052.md)
**Retrieves from:** S007, S032, S042, S051
**Entry state:** this-programme-only

---

## The situation

You have the model from [S051](/wiki/seminars/S051.md). Now the threats, under one rule that will do more work than anything else in the module.

**A threat survives only if it can be bound to at least one named component and at least one numbered flow. Anything that cannot be is discarded, and the discard is logged with its reason.**

Answer these in writing before instruction.

1. Write a threat you believe applies to your system. Which component? Which flow number?
2. If your answer to item 1 was *all of them*, what did you actually write down?
3. Name a way your system could be harmed that involves no dataflow at all.
4. Where did your list of candidate threats come from, and what was that source unable to know about?
5. What would a threat model with no open questions and no discarded threats be evidence of?

**Item 3 is the session.** Everything you produce there is real, is a threat to the customer, and will be excluded by the rule you are about to adopt. Item 5 has one honest answer and it is not *thoroughness*.

## Before you begin: a restriction that applies to this session and no other

**Threat categories and control classes only.** You may state what a class of attack targets and what property it violates. **You may not write down how to perform one**, and neither may anything you submit.

The material this module is derived from contains operational detail and worked examples. **None of it is reproduced anywhere in this programme, and the omission is a decision rather than a gap.** The restriction has a real teaching cost — you will produce a thinner artefact than a practitioner would — and it is accepted deliberately, because this work is public and because a threat model that reads as a manual has changed what it is.

## What you must produce

**Two artefacts, and the second is the assessed one.**

1. **A threat registry**, in which every entry carries an identifier, a bound component, a bound flow number, the property it violates, and a ranking.
2. **A compact index** — a deliberately lossy projection, a few columns per entry — for the next stage to join against.
3. **A reverse index** from component to threat, so a reader can start from a part of the system rather than from a list.
4. **An exclusion register.** Every candidate you discarded, its logged reason, and — the part that does not exist in the source material — **a grouping of those discards into the categories they fall into.** Not a list of individual losses. The shape of what your rule cannot see.
5. **A provenance line on the registry**: what supplied your candidate threats, and when that source was current.

## The material

### The rule, and the three things it buys

The design this module rests on carries one specification decision that does more work than everything around it: **a threat survives only if it can be bound to at least one named component and at least one numbered flow. Anything that cannot be is discarded, and the discard is logged with its reason** ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-agentic-threat-modelling.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-agentic-threat-modelling.md)). That source is internal and access-controlled; it is paraphrased here, quoted nowhere, and it **supplies no external warrant for anything.**

The rule buys three things and it is worth being precise about which.

**It makes a threat testable.** *Prompt injection* is a category. *Untrusted content arriving on flow 3 into `ingest-worker`, whose output is consumed by a component with write authority* is a claim somebody can construct a test for. A category cannot fail a test because it does not say what would count as failing.

**It makes a threat assignable.** A component has an owner. A category does not, and a threat model composed of categories generates work items nobody can accept.

**It makes a threat closable on evidence.** The same design's closure criterion is that a claim of *implemented* must cite a file and a location, re-derived from the workspace rather than asserted by a person. **You cannot re-derive evidence for a threat that names nothing.**

The practitioner judgement behind the rule is sharper than the rule itself: **generic mitigations are worse than absent ones, because they cannot be assessed, implemented or falsified.** An absent mitigation is visibly missing. A generic one occupies the slot where the missing thing would have been noticed.

### What the rule costs, and why the cost is a category rather than a list

Now the part the source does not confront.

The binding requirement systematically removes anything about **the operator, the supply chain, human process and out-of-band administration** — each discarded with an entirely plausible logged justification that nobody reads. Those are not edge cases. They are, in most organisations, where the harm actually comes from.

**The design's own evaluator then explicitly refuses to deduct for threats that are missing**, listing them separately as unscored suggestions. That is a defensible choice — a scorer that penalised the absence of the unknowable would be unusable — and it means **the score is silent on the pipeline's most consequential failure mode.** Recall is unmeasured, and the design declines to measure it.

The deeper asymmetry is worth naming because you will meet it again at [S053](/wiki/seminars/S053.md). **Precision is defended exactly where recall matters.** The mapping stage of that design is elaborately instrumented against over-mapping — signal hierarchies, semantic gates, anti-junk-drawer rules, a stated preference for leaving a threat unmapped. Nothing comparable guards against under-mapping. The machinery is all on the side of the error that produces a tidy document.

This session's response is the **exclusion register**, and specifically the requirement that discards be **grouped into named categories** rather than listed as incidents. A list of forty individually reasonable discards is unreadable and therefore unread. Four named categories — operator action, supply chain, human process, out-of-band administration — is a sentence a reviewer can argue with. **A method that never states its own scope exclusions invites the reader to assume it has none**, and the source states none anywhere across fourteen agent definitions.

### The threats worth naming are the ones the platform has had to name

The workload-posture documentation is unusual among vendor material in naming AI-native attack classes rather than restating classical ones ([research/06-microsoft-platform/purview-dspm-for-ai-and-defender-ai-spm-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/purview-dspm-for-ai-and-defender-ai-spm-docs.md)). Its list is **"prompt injections, wallet attacks, model theft, and data poisoning, while increasing susceptibility to known risks such as data breaches and denial of service."**

Two of those deserve attention because they are genuinely new in kind rather than in target.

**A wallet attack drives cost rather than downtime.** It follows directly from usage-based billing, and classical security models do not cover it because classical systems did not charge per unit of work. The property it violates is availability, but by a route that leaves every service healthy and every log clean.

**Data poisoning** targets the corpus rather than the code, which means the affected component is one your model does not usually draw — the ingestion path, the labelling process, the document library somebody else owns.

The documentation also names the two moments a model touches proprietary data: **grounding and fine-tuning**. That is a useful binding heuristic in its own right — those two are where data can leak *by construction* rather than by attack, and both are flows on your diagram.

And it names **attack path analysis** over **"toxic combinations"**: individually acceptable configurations that compose into a breach. **A combination is a property of your model and not of any component in it**, which is why [S051](/wiki/seminars/S051.md) came first. You cannot bind a combination to one component; you bind it to a path, and the path is a sequence of flow numbers.

**Read that source for its taxonomy and not for its assurance.** It is first-party and definitional. **No detection rate, no false-positive rate and no coverage measurement is published for any of it — assert none, because none exists.** One of its two pages is explicitly a superseded classic version; the other was read through search excerpts rather than fetched in full. Its stated operational bounds — how much of an estate is scanned, how often, how long results take to appear — are paraphrased here rather than reproduced: **a bounded sample, on a periodic cadence, with a latency measured in days.** That is the resolution of the instrument, and an engineer who cannot state those three properties does not know what their inventory covers.

### A second taxonomy, from a literature with no product to sell

The production machine-learning literature supplies an independent split that is more useful for binding than the vendor list, because it is organised by what the attacker gets rather than by what the attack is called ([research/02-technical-foundations/crowe-machine-learning-production-systems.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/crowe-machine-learning-production-systems.md)). It takes the division from a privacy research organisation and reports it as **informational harms** — membership inference, model inversion, model extraction — against **behavioural harms** — poisoning and evasion.

That split binds better because each half implicates a different part of a dataflow model. Informational harms are about what leaves on an outbound flow. Behavioural harms are about what arrives on an inbound one. **A threat you cannot place on one side of that division is usually a threat you have not finished thinking about.**

The same source carries the observation that should govern how you rank: **"a model with good performance 'on average' may still exhibit failure modes that are not apparent by looking at an aggregate metric."** It says that about model quality and it is exactly true of threat registries. A registry with good aggregate coverage can be uniformly blind to one class, and no summary of it will show that. **Slicing is mandatory, and which slices matter is a domain question rather than a statistical one** — which is what the reverse index is for.

**Its limits are heavy and must travel with it.** Practitioner guidance from a commercial publisher: no study design, no comparison group, no measurement that its practices outperform anything. Three of its four authors worked at one large vendor. Some foundational claims rest on **anonymous practitioner testimony** that cannot be checked. Every quantity in it is a property of one system after one run; **mechanism transfers, magnitudes do not, and none appears here.**

### The catalogue ceiling, which nobody records and everybody inherits

Here is the failure that will outlive every other lesson in this session.

In the source design, threats, controls, the resource catalogue and the document template all come from **one knowledge base**, and inventing outside it is forbidden. The consequence is exact: **the pipeline's blind spots are that catalogue's blind spots.** And **no stage records the catalogue's version, age or coverage anywhere in any output.** A stale catalogue produces a confident, well-formatted, out-of-date threat model containing no signal that this happened.

This generalises past that design and past this module. Whatever supplies your candidate threats — a published catalogue, an internal standard, a scanner's rule set, a model's training data — has a currency and a coverage, and neither is visible in its output. **The provenance line on your registry is the cheapest possible defence and almost nobody writes one.**

The retrieval failure literature makes the same point in a different register: a stage-wise vocabulary is what converts "the answer was wrong" into a locatable defect ([research/02-technical-foundations/barnett-rag-failure-taxonomy.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/barnett-rag-failure-taxonomy.md)). *Our threat model was inadequate* is a mood. *Our threat model inherited a catalogue that predates agentic retrieval* is a defect with a fix. **That source is an experience report over three systems with no frequency data, no severity data and no controlled comparison; its named failure points are recorded in this repository as reconstructed rather than quoted, and are not relied on here.**

### Why item 3 was the session

You were asked to name a way your system could be harmed that involves no dataflow at all. Whatever you wrote is real, is a threat to your customer, and **will be excluded by the rule you have just adopted.**

That is not an argument against the rule. It is the reason the exclusion register exists, and it is why this session refuses to teach the binding discipline without teaching its cost in the same hour. **A practitioner who has internalised the rule and never seen what it removes has been given a blind spot and told it is rigour.**

## The workflow

**Enumerate against your own characteristics checklist, bind or discard, then let a scanner disagree with your registry.**

**Step 1 — enumerate against the checklist, not against a mnemonic.** The characteristics checklist from [S051](/wiki/seminars/S051.md) is the routing key: it says which architectural traits your system has, *with evidence*, and therefore which classes of threat are in play at all. A mnemonic asks the same questions of every system. **The checklist asks the questions your system earned.**

**Step 2 — record the provenance line before the first entry.** At the top of `security/model/threats.yaml`: what supplied your candidates, and when it was current. If you cannot date it, write that you cannot.

**Step 3 — make the binding rule mechanical rather than moral.** Add to `.github/instructions/model.instructions.md`:

```markdown
---
applyTo: "security/model/threats.yaml"
---

# Threat registry rules

- Every entry MUST have a non-empty `component` and a non-empty `flow` that
  exist in components.yaml and dataflows.yaml. No exceptions, no placeholders.
- If a candidate cannot be bound, do NOT bind it loosely. Move it to
  exclusions.yaml with a `reason` and a `category`.
- NEVER write operational detail. State what a class of attack targets and
  what property it violates. Do not state how it is performed.
- Every entry states `property_violated` from the closed list at the top.
- `source` and `source_dated` are required on the file, not on entries.
```

**Step 4 — bind or discard, and never bend.** For each candidate, name the component and the flow. If you cannot, discard it and write the reason and the category. **Do not attach it to something adjacent so that it survives.** A forced binding produces an entry an engineer will investigate and find nothing behind, and [S053](/wiki/seminars/S053.md) will then dutifully map a control to it.

**Step 5 — emit the compact index and the reverse index.** A few columns per entry for the join, and a component-to-threat view. Then read the reverse index looking for a component with no threats. Sometimes that is correct. Usually it means you enumerated by walking your own mental model of the system rather than by walking the model on disk.

**Step 6 — let the scanner disagree with you.** Open the workload-posture recommendations for your resources in Defender for Cloud, and read the attack paths rather than the recommendations. **For each path it proposes, ask whether your registry has an entry bound to that sequence of flows.** Expect two findings: paths you missed, and — more instructive — recommendations that are *not threats at all*. A recommendation says do this. A threat says this could happen here. **Converting the first into the second without adding a component and a flow is how a registry fills up with entries nobody can test.**

**Step 7 — re-read your discard log adversarially.** Nobody in the source design ever does this. Read it as somebody who wants the system harmed, and ask of each reason: *is this true, or is it merely formatted like a reason?*

**Step 8 — group the discards, then attack the grouping.** Write the exclusion register with named categories. Then give an agent **only the compact index and the exclusion register — not the registry** and prompt: *name a class of harm that is absent from both of these, and say which component it would have to touch.* Keep what beats your own reasoning; record what you rejected. **An agent handed the surviving threats will validate them, because reading a list of plausible entries is not a test of anything. The discards are where the information is.**

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That a scanner's output and a threat registry are different genres, and that the conversion between them is exactly where unbindable material gets bound anyway. You will watch a recommendation try to become a threat, and you will have to decide whether it names anything.

## What good looks like

- **Every surviving threat names a component and a flow number**, and a reader can go to that component.
- **No threat is true of software in general.** If the entry would survive being pasted into another system's model unchanged, it is not a finding about this system.
- **The compact index is genuinely lossy** and you can say what it dropped.
- **The exclusion register is grouped**, and the groups are named as categories rather than as incidents. Operator action. Supply chain. Human process. Out-of-band administration. Whatever else yours contains.
- **Each discard reason is one you would defend out loud**, because the failure mode here is a reason that is individually plausible and collectively catastrophic.
- **The registry states where its candidates came from**, and what that source could not have known.
- **Nothing in it is operational.**

## How it goes wrong

- **The general threat** — an entry that is true of all software, bound to a component because something had to go in the column. *Warning sign:* the entry would survive being copied into another system's model.
- **The plausible discard** — every reason in your log is defensible on its own, and the pile of them removes an entire class of risk. *Warning sign:* you never re-read the log after writing it.
- **The catalogue ceiling** — your threats are exactly the threats your source knew about, and its blind spots are now yours, invisibly. *Warning sign:* you cannot say how old the source is.
- **The empty exclusion register** — the rule excluded nothing, which means it was not applied. *Warning sign:* you feel good about coverage.
- **The forced binding** — a real threat about people or process is attached to a component so that it can survive. *Warning sign:* the flow number is arbitrary and you know it.
- **The recommendation wearing a threat's clothes** — a scanner's advice is pasted in as an entry. *Warning sign:* the entry is phrased as an imperative.
- **The operational drift** — the entry starts explaining how. *Warning sign:* somebody could follow it.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed today.** The exclusion register is assessed at [S055](/wiki/seminars/S055.md), and the registry is the input [S053](/wiki/seminars/S053.md) joins against.

**1 · Paste test.** Take three entries from your registry and paste each into a description of a completely different system.

> **Scoring criterion.** An entry passes if it becomes false or nonsensical in the other system. It fails if it reads perfectly well — which means it was never a claim about your system, only a claim about software. **The component and flow columns do not save an entry from this test**; a generic threat with a component name attached is still generic, and the binding has made it worse by making it look checked.

**2 · Hand somebody only your discard log.** Not the registry. Their job is to find the threat you should not have dropped, and to say what it would have cost you. Then ask them a second question: *what class of harm is missing from both lists?*

> **What you should find.** That the second question is much harder and much more valuable, and that most reviewers cannot answer it without a prompt. Offer them the categories: operator, supply chain, human process, out-of-band administration, and anything arising from correct behaviour used for an unsanctioned purpose. **A reviewer given a list of plausible surviving threats validates them.** That is not a test; it is a reading.

**3 · Go back to your own item 3, then break it.** Find what you wrote for the opening item — a way your system could be harmed that involves no dataflow at all. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then place it correctly: in the exclusion register, in a named category, with the reason it cannot bind.

> **Criterion.** A correctly handled unbindable threat is one that appears in the exclusion register under a category name, with a reason stating *why the binding rule cannot represent it* rather than why it is unlikely or unimportant. **The two failing moves are opposite and equally common.** One is to force it into the registry against a component that does not really carry it — which produces an entry an engineer will investigate and find nothing behind. The other is to drop it silently because it did not fit the format — which is how a method's scope shrinks without anyone deciding that it should.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Sort your registry by the informational/behavioural split.** Take every entry and place it: does the attacker learn something that should not have left, or does the system behave in a way it should not have?

> **What to notice.** Entries that refuse to sort. Those are usually one of two things — a compound entry describing a whole attack path rather than a threat, which should be split and bound to a sequence of flows; or an entry that names a technique rather than a harm, which cannot be sorted because it never said what the attacker gets. **A taxonomy is useful mainly for the entries it rejects.**

**5 · Read the attack paths, not the recommendations.** Run workflow step 6 and produce two counts: paths your registry already covers, and proposed items that are recommendations rather than threats.

> **What you should find.** That the second count is substantial, and that converting those items into registry entries is tempting and wrong. *Use private endpoints* is a control looking for a threat. The threat is that a named component is reachable from a network it should not be reachable from, over a numbered flow. **[S053](/wiki/seminars/S053.md) is where controls belong, and putting one here means the mapping stage will later map a control to a control and call it coverage.**

**6 · Date your source, closed book.** Without looking, write down what supplied your candidate threats and when it was last current. Then check.

> **Scoring criterion.** A pass names the source and either dates it or states plainly that it cannot be dated. A fail names the source with no date and no admission — which is the exact condition the source design ships in, across every one of its outputs. **The failure here is not ignorance of the date. It is producing a confident artefact that carries no signal that the date is unknown.**

## What this does not establish

**Recall is unmeasured here and it is unmeasured in the source, which explicitly declines to measure it.** The evaluator in the material behind this module refuses to deduct for threats that are absent, listing them separately as unscored suggestions. That is defensible — a scorer that penalises the absence of the unknowable is unusable — and it means the resulting score is silent on the most consequential failure mode available.

**This session does not fix that.** The exclusion register makes one class of omission visible: the class your own binding rule removed, which you saw go. **It cannot make visible the threats nobody thought of**, and no artefact in this programme can.

**Nothing establishes that a bound threat model finds more real vulnerabilities than an unbound one.** The binding rule is a practitioner judgement carried from experienced people, and its consequence — that everything about operators, supply chain, human process and out-of-band administration disappears — is a structural fact rather than a measurement. The rule and its blind spot are the same rule, and this session's position is that the only honest response is to write the blind spot down. **That source is internal and access-controlled: nothing in it can be linked, attributed or checked by you, and it reports no outcome evidence of any kind.**

**The vendor taxonomy is definitional and unquantified.** It names attack classes and describes what discovery and posture assessment do. **It publishes no detection rate, no false-positive rate and no coverage measurement, so nothing in it is evidence that a scanner finds what is there.** One page behind it is explicitly superseded by a version not read here; the other was read through search excerpts. Its operational bounds are the resolution of the instrument and are paraphrased rather than reproduced.

**The second taxonomy comes from practitioner guidance, not research.** No study design, no comparison group, no measurement that its practices outperform alternatives. It reports the informational/behavioural split at second hand from a privacy research organisation. Mechanism transfers; no figure from it appears here.

**The restriction on operational detail has a real cost and it is accepted deliberately.** Your artefact is thinner than a practitioner's would be. A threat model that reads as a manual has changed what it is, and this material is public.
