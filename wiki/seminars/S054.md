## The situation

This is the most important hour in the stage, and the claim it turns on is older than everything it will be demonstrated on.

A user asks your system a question. The retrieval step returns ten passages. Three of them come from documents that user has never been entitled to read. The generation step reads all ten and produces a paragraph.

**No document was returned. No link was shown. Nothing in the transcript records a violation. And the user now knows what was in those three documents.**

That is the whole session. **Authorisation is a property of the retrieval path, not of the answer.** A system that retrieves what a user may not read and then summarises it has leaked, and it has leaked in a form that carries no citation, no filename and no trace back to the thing it exposed. **Trimming after retrieval is not trimming.**

The claim is durable and it predates every product in this programme by decades. It is the reference-monitor position: the check belongs at the point of access, before the content is used for anything, because once content has been read into a computation the computation's output is not governed by the content's permissions. The vendor's access-control patterns are the current instance of a very old argument, and they will change.

**Answer these in writing before reading further, and keep the answers.**

1. In your retrieval path, name the exact step at which the asker's identity is evaluated against a document's permissions. If you cannot name a step, say what that means.
2. Your system returns an answer and cites nothing. What did the user just learn, and from what?
3. Somebody's access is revoked at nine o'clock. At five past nine they ask a question. What happens — and what did you have to assume in order to answer?
4. Your documents are chunked before they are embedded. Where do the permissions live?
5. What is the **smallest** change that would make an unauthorised retrieval visible after the fact?

**Item 1 is the one that matters** and most people cannot answer it, because in most systems there is no such step — there is an application that constructs a filter, which is a different thing. **Item 3 has no comfortable answer** and the honest one is a sentence about a window nobody has measured.

## What you must produce

1. **An authorisation trace** of your retrieval path: every step from the asker's request to the generated answer, naming at each step which identity is in scope and what it is compared against. **A step where nothing is compared is a step you must mark as such.**
2. **A trimming statement**: where trimming happens relative to retrieval, and what that ordering means for what has been read.
3. **A revocation-window statement**: the mechanism by which a source-side permission change reaches the index, and the fact that it is not immediate. If you have measured your window, record it; if you have not, say so.
4. **A chunk-projection statement**: whether permission metadata is projected onto every chunk, with the evidence you checked rather than the assumption you made.
5. **A laundering trace** — one value from your own [S051](/wiki/seminars/S051.md) attribute table, followed from where it was produced to where it was consumed, naming the step where its qualification was lost.
6. **A double score.** Assess your plan twice: once counting every filled cell, once counting only cells you can trace to an observation. **Report both. The gap is the artefact.**
7. **The smallest change**, with its cost stated in the currency it is paid in.

## The material

### An index is a copy, and copies do not inherit their originals' access rules

Start with the sentence that generates the entire problem. **A search index is a copy of source content, and copies do not automatically inherit the access rules of their originals** ([research/06-microsoft-platform/azure-ai-search-and-acl-patterns.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/azure-ai-search-and-acl-patterns.md)). Permissions must be deliberately propagated, and every mechanism for doing so is a mechanism somebody has to build, operate and keep in step.

The documentation states why retrieval-augmented generation raises the stakes rather than merely repeating them: document-level access control **"is essential for building secure AI agentic systems grounding data, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) applications, and enterprise search solutions that require authorization checks at the document level."**

The reason is the one the session opened with, and it is worth stating in the form that makes it unarguable. **A conventional search interface that returns a forbidden document at least shows the user a link they cannot open. A retrieval-augmented system reads the document's contents into a generated answer.** The leak is not a listing. It is the substance. Access control therefore has to happen before generation, which means it has to happen in retrieval, which means the permission model must live in or alongside the index.

**That chain of three consequences is the durable content of this session.** Everything below is the current instance.

### Trimming after retrieval is not trimming

**Security trimming** is defined as removing unauthorised documents from a result set rather than blocking the query. Read that definition adversarially and the problem is visible in it: a result set exists before it is trimmed, and something produced it.

Four approaches exist in the current platform, and the selection rule matters more than the mechanisms. **Reuse the authoritative permission system rather than reimplementing it.** Custom identity systems or push-model indexes get **security filters**. Content with existing file-system-style assignments gets **native access-control-list and role-scope preservation**. Content already governed by enterprise classification gets **sensitivity labels**. Collaboration-platform content gets that platform's own permission ingestion.

The most instructive of the four is the one the documentation is candid about. **Security filters are "string comparison":** the application passes a user or group identity as a string, which populates a query filter. And the documentation's own contrast is unusually direct — the native pattern's metadata **"is recognized as Microsoft Entra ID authentication, whereas the security trimming workaround is simple string comparison."** **The word *workaround* is the documentation's own.**

What that means structurally is the sentence to carry: **a string filter is only as trustworthy as the code that supplies the string, and nothing at the search service validates that the caller is who the filter says they are. The security boundary sits in the application, not in the service.** That is not a criticism of the approach — it is generally available, API-agnostic and the universal fallback — it is a statement of where the trust lives, which is exactly the [S053](/wiki/seminars/S053.md) surface question asked one layer down.

The native pattern moves the boundary, and its shape is the design insight. The service performs **two checks in sequence**: first for the application's permission to access the index at all, then, **given an extra token on the request**, for the user or group permissions on documents that would be returned, excluding those that do not match. **Application authorisation and user authorisation are separate concerns evaluated separately** — the app is trusted to query, the user is trusted to see particular documents. A system that collapses those two into one has not simplified anything; it has deleted a check.

One operational recommendation from the same page is worth taking whole: **use group access over individual user access**, for manageability. Every permission model that enumerates individuals decays into a permission model nobody can audit.

### The set returned is not the set authorised, and the gap is structural

Here is the part that no security documentation says and that this programme is in a position to say.

Retrieval at scale is **approximate**. The graph index underneath most vector search is a hierarchy of proximity graphs over nested subsets, searched by greedy descent from a sparse top layer through progressively denser ones, with each layer's links spanning a characteristic distance scale ([research/02-technical-foundations/malkov-yashunin-hnsw.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/malkov-yashunin-hnsw.md)). Search moves toward the query until no neighbour improves, then drops a layer. The whole construction exists because exact search degenerates to a linear scan and is unusable at scale; **approximate methods trade a bounded amount of recall for large gains in speed.**

The disk-resident line of work makes the trade explicit in the way an engineer needs. Its evaluation reports recall, latency, throughput and density **together**, on the stated methodological grounds that each can be traded against the others and **a claim stated on only one axis is uninformative** ([research/02-technical-foundations/subramanya-diskann.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/subramanya-diskann.md)). **Recall is an operating point you choose, not a property you have.**

Now compose that with authorisation. **The candidate set is computed by the index structure. The permitted set is computed by the identity system. Nothing guarantees that these two agree, because they are different machinery answering different questions.**

Two consequences follow and both are structural rather than defects.

**Filter after retrieval and you filtered an approximate set.** The documents that survive are authorised; the documents that were read in order to produce the candidate set were not necessarily. If any part of your pipeline — a reranker, a summariser, a cache, a log — touched the candidate set before the filter, the filter is downstream of the exposure.

**Filter during retrieval and the filter interacts with the traversal.** A graph search that cannot enter a region because its nodes are excluded may not reach the region beyond it. The authorised set and the reachable-under-filter set are not the same set, and the difference is a function of the index's shape rather than of anybody's policy.

**Neither of the two sources above is about security and neither says anything about authorisation.** They are algorithms papers, and this is a property imported from them rather than a finding reported by them. **Their limits: both are systems papers evaluated on standard benchmarks against contemporary baselines. Every quantity in them is a property of a particular index on a particular dataset, and none is reproduced here.** What transfers is the shape of the trade, which is durable and which no product release changes.

### Chunking silently discards permissions

The single most instructive failure in this topic is the quietest one.

When a skillset chunks documents — which is standard practice for retrieval — **the permission metadata fields move from indexer field mappings to index projections**, and without that projection, **"chunk-level references aren't filtered."**

Read that plainly. **An unprojected chunk is an unprotected chunk.** The parent document is correctly permissioned. The chunks derived from it are not. Retrieval returns chunks. **The system appears to work, the permissions appear to be configured, and the content is available to anyone who asks the right question.**

This is the failure to look for first in any system you inherit, and the reason is that everything about it looks correct from every position except the one that checks a chunk.

### Enforcement lags the source, and the window is unmeasured

The documentation is careful and repetitive about the limitation, and this is the single most important operational sentence on the page. Enforcement **"evaluates the caller's Microsoft Entra claims against the permission metadata that's already stored in the index"**, and permission changes in the source system **"are only reflected in search results after that metadata is synchronized to the index through the source-specific mechanism."** The preview terms say the same thing more bluntly: **a timing lag occurs before permission changes are recognised.**

**A revocation is not immediate.** Somebody who left the organisation this morning may still be retrievable-against this afternoon, in the sense that documents they were removed from are still marked as theirs in a copy.

**No latency figures are given anywhere**, so **the size of the revocation window is unknown and must be measured per deployment.** That is not a criticism; it is the working condition, and it is why your plan needs a sentence about it rather than a reassurance.

Two further asymmetries in the collaboration-platform pattern are traps because they are plausible in the wrong direction. **Changes to items with unique permissions are detected and refreshed on each successful indexer run, while changes inherited from a parent scope require an explicit resynchronisation.** The inherited case is both the more common one and the one that does not self-heal. And **the platform's information-management policies that gate user access "aren't evaluated, ingested, or honored at query time"** — an explicit, stated gap between what the source system enforces and what the index enforces.

One more, which belongs in your [S052](/wiki/seminars/S052.md) registry rather than here: ingesting those permissions requires **full-control permission across all sites** in order to read content and access-control lists. **Requiring full control over everything in order to read permissions is a real privilege consideration and should be taught as one.**

**The source's limits are heavy and mostly forward-looking.** Most of the page describes a **preview** API surface, and preview surfaces change without notice. The documentation **explicitly disclaims responsibility for compliance, geographic boundaries, testing and mitigations** — that responsibility falls on the implementer. **It contains no measurements of synchronisation latency, no failure-rate data, and no security assessment of the four approaches against each other.** It is trustworthy for what the system does and silent on how often it does it correctly under load. Feature availability differs between API versions and packages, so any exercise must pin versions.

### The failure taxonomy does not centre this, and that is informative

The standard engineering taxonomy for retrieval-augmented systems is built from three deployments in three domains, and its diagnostic value is that it turns "the answer was wrong" into a locatable stage ([research/02-technical-foundations/barnett-rag-failure-taxonomy.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/barnett-rag-failure-taxonomy.md)). Its named failures sit across the corpus, retrieval, context assembly and generation.

**Several of them are retrieval-path failures with security consequences that the taxonomy does not read that way.** A document that should have been retrieved and was not is a quality failure; a document that should not have been retrieved and was is the same stage seen from the other side. Content that survives consolidation into the context window is content the generator has read, whatever the final answer says. **Access control is a failure mode this taxonomy does not centre**, and noticing that a widely used diagnostic vocabulary has no word for your problem is itself a finding about the field.

The taxonomy's own strongest claim bears directly on you: **validation of such a system is only feasible during operation**, because the space of queries, documents and interactions cannot be adequately anticipated before deployment. **A permissions defect will not appear in pre-release testing**, because pre-release testing asks the questions you thought of, with the identities you had.

**Its limits:** an experience report, not a study. **No frequency data, no severity data, no controlled comparison.** Three specific systems, not a representative sample. It predates widespread agentic retrieval. **Its seven failure-point names are recorded in this repository as reconstructed rather than quoted**, and they are not relied on here.

### The same structure, one layer up: how a guess becomes a fact

Now turn the instrument on the artefacts you have been building, because the structure you have just met in retrieval also occurs in your own plan, and once you can see it in one place you can see it in the other.

In the chained pipeline this module rests on, **the attribute stage is told to infer missing security properties from best practice, forbidden from marking those inferences in the table, and instructed to resolve conflicts in the more secure direction. The provenance survives only in a separate justification section — which the assembly stage is explicitly instructed to exclude when embedding the table into the delivered plan. The review stage then scores authentication, authorisation and transport encryption by counting non-empty cells in that very table** ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-agentic-threat-modelling.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-agentic-threat-modelling.md)).

**A guess enters as an assumption and leaves as a stated fact, with its provenance removed by design and biased optimistic. The chain fills in the answers and later grades itself on them.** A plan can score well on encryption because an earlier stage assumed encryption.

No step in that sequence is unreasonable. Each is defensible in isolation and each was written by somebody solving a real problem. **The composition is the defect, and no participant can see it from where they stand.**

**That is structurally identical to the retrieval case.** Content enters with a permission; retrieval drops the permission; generation produces an answer; the answer carries no permission and the reader treats it as knowledge. A value enters with a provenance; the format forbids the marker; assembly drops the justification; the scorer treats the cell as evidence. **In both, something enters qualified and leaves unqualified, and the next step consumes the unqualified thing as though it had been established.** That is what this session means by laundering, and it is why the two halves are one session rather than two.

Three further findings from that source belong here because they tell you what your own score is worth. **The proxy checks are trivially satisfiable** — most reduce to whether some mitigation somewhere mentions a thing, and the definition of a *specific* mitigation is that it names a concrete technology, **so naming a technology you have not deployed scores full marks.** **The scores measure documents, not systems**, which one constraint says plainly while the presentation — a score, a tier, severity colours, a findings table — contradicts everywhere. And **self-verification is self-report**: every stage ends in a numbered checklist that the model runs against its own output. Those are prompt-engineering devices that improve output quality and **they are not controls**, because the thing being checked and the thing checking are the same. **In one case the checklist is rendered into the deliverable with the model's own ticks, which makes a configured control wear the visual language of an audit trail.**

**That source is internal and access-controlled. It is paraphrased here, quoted nowhere, attributable to nothing, and it supplies no external warrant for any claim. It reports no outcome evidence of any kind, and not one of its calibration constants cites a study, a measurement, a pilot or a rationale. None is reproduced here.**

### Why this is where the module's data-posture argument lands

One connection back to [S053](/wiki/seminars/S053.md), because it explains why this is urgent rather than merely correct. The data-posture documentation's argument is that generative AI **"amplifies the problem of oversharing data"** by surfacing content that is obsolete, over-permissioned or lacking governance controls ([research/06-microsoft-platform/purview-dspm-for-ai-and-defender-ai-spm-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/purview-dspm-for-ai-and-defender-ai-spm-docs.md)).

**Deploying capable retrieval converts every dormant permissions error in an organisation into a live one.** The permissions were always wrong. Nobody was reading. **Your retrieval system reads everything, instantly, on behalf of whoever asks** — and then, if you have built it the way most people build it, summarises what it read.

**First-party and definitional: no detection rate, no false-positive rate and no coverage measurement is published — assert none.** One page behind that note is an explicitly superseded classic version; the other was read through search excerpts. Its assessment bounds are a bounded sample on a periodic cadence with a latency measured in days.

### Three documented instances, and why they change the standard of proof

Everything above was argued. What follows is **conceded** — three first-party statements, in three different documentation sets, each describing something that enters qualified and leaves unqualified, with no attacker anywhere in the story.

That difference matters for how you use them. **You are not being asked to accept that laundering is possible. You are being asked to read three places where the vendor states that it is the default**, and then to notice that in all three the system continues to look correct from every position except the one that checks.

### Instance one: the output does not inherit the label

The agent control plane's data-governance integration publishes a list of constraints on reading protected material, and they are strict ([research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-365-and-agent-identity.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-365-and-agent-identity.md)). Files must be **explicitly shared** with the agent. Where a label applies encryption, that label must **explicitly grant the agent the rights to view and to extract**. And a label configured to cover everyone in the organisation, or every authenticated user, **is not sufficient** — the agent must be named.

So the input path is about as tightly drawn as a permission model gets. Then comes the sentence that undoes it: **newly created content from the agent does not inherit sensitivity labels from the source items, and is therefore neither automatically labelled nor automatically encrypted.**

Read the shape of that rather than the product. **The protection was attached to a container. The agent's output is a new container. The label did not follow.** The organisation did everything the documentation asked, and the summary of a protected document is an unprotected document. **No attack was performed, no control failed, and nothing in the sequence produced an error.**

This is the same claim the session opened with, arriving from the label side instead of the permission side, and it is stronger evidence because the vendor states it rather than the programme inferring it. **Authorisation — and classification, which is authorisation's bookkeeping — is a property of the path, not of the answer.**

### Instance two: the guardrail at the agent replaces the guardrail at the model, entirely

The second instance is a safety control rather than a permission, and its shape is identical ([research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-agent-build-and-runtime.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-agent-build-and-runtime.md)).

An agent runs on a model deployment, and both can carry a content guardrail. The published rule is stated as an important note: **risks are detected according to the guardrail assigned to the agent, not the guardrail of its underlying model, and the agent's guardrail fully overrides the model's.** Inheritance resolves in one direction only — a custom guardrail if one is assigned, otherwise the model deployment's, and the platform default only if the deployment has none.

The documentation's own worked example is the part to read, because it shows the hole rather than hiding it. A model deployment is configured strictly. The agent above it declares a looser setting for its input and its output **and declares nothing for tool calls.** The published expected behaviour: the user's query is scanned; **the tool call generated internally, including the content sent to the tool, is not scanned at all; the tool's response is not scanned at all**; the final output is scanned.

**Override is total, not additive.** The stricter setting one layer down does not fill the gap, because it is not consulted. **A stage the agent's guardrail does not name is a stage nobody scans**, and the estate's control inventory will nonetheless record that content filtering is configured, because it is.

That is this session's subject exactly, one layer up from retrieval. **The assurance looks intact from above.** A reviewer who checks the model deployment finds a strict guardrail and stops. **The four stages of an agent's execution are not four applications of one control; they are four places a control may or may not have been named**, and the naming happens in a different object from the one a reviewer instinctively opens.

One further property belongs with it because it constrains what you can do about it: for agents, **there is no annotate-only mode.** The control blocks or it is absent. A team that wants to observe before enforcing has no supported way to do it here, which turns what would have been a measurement into a decision.

**A caution about provenance, and it is itself the lesson.** A widely repeated claim that the platform's default guardrail policies cannot be edited or deleted, and that protection against indirect prompt injection cannot be added to them, comes from **a community forum answer with a vendor respondent, not from documentation.** This programme records it as community content and does not rely on it. **What documentation alone supports is narrower and still enough: defaults exist, they are a floor, and the documented route to stronger protection is a separate policy applied alongside rather than a stricter default.** Notice how easily the first version would have entered a security plan as a fact.

### Instance three: permission awareness is a capability, not a default

The third instance closes the loop back to retrieval, and it is the one most likely to be in your own system already ([research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-iq-intelligence-layer.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-iq-intelligence-layer.md)).

The managed knowledge layer above the search service groups knowledge sources under a single endpoint, and **every summary page describing it uses the phrase permission-aware.** The frequently-asked-questions page states the operative rule: user-level access controls are enforced **only** where the knowledge source supports document-level permissions **and** where those permissions have been explicitly configured for synchronisation — and **unless a knowledge source's own documentation explicitly states out-of-the-box support for user permissions, document-level access controls are not automatically honoured.**

**Permission enforcement is opt-in, per source, and the default is off.** One remote collaboration source is the exception that proves it: it enforces through a retrieval interface that carries access lists and sensitivity labels, and requires end users to hold a specific licence. Everything else must be configured.

Join that to the session's first claim and the composition is exact. **An index is a copy, and copies do not inherit their originals' access rules. The copying machinery will run without being told to carry the rules across. And the resulting system will look like it works** — because it will answer, fluently, with citations, for everybody.

**Permission-aware and permissions-enforced are different claims**, and one of them is a description of a capability the product has while the other is a description of a state your deployment is in. **A control inventory that records the first has recorded a fact about a price list.**

**The limits on all three sources travel with them and are heavy.** All three are first-party and definitional. The control plane had been generally available for a short time only when read, with substantial parts preview or early-access and a dated migration inside this material's life; roughly thirty of its supporting pages were read as search excerpts rather than fetched in full. **Agent guardrails are preview**, and preview status across that build surface is pervasive and load-bearing. The managed knowledge layer's own components are largely preview and its documentation contradicts its marketing on exactly the point above, which is why the frequently-asked-questions page is cited rather than the summary. **None of the three publishes a detection rate, a false-positive rate, a bypass rate or any coverage measurement for any control named here — assert none, because none exists.** No measure of permission-trimming completeness is published anywhere: no false-negative rate, no false-positive rate, no revocation-propagation time, from any source.

## The workflow

**Trace the authorisation path, then demonstrate a leak against your own system, then measure the window you cannot look up.**

**Step 1 — write the trace before you touch anything.** One row per step from request to answer. For each: what identity is in scope, what it is compared against, and what happens on mismatch. **A step where nothing is compared gets `none` and stays in the table.**

```yaml
# security/model/authz-trace.yaml   <- SHAPE, NOT SCHEMA. Pin and verify any
# product keys, headers and API versions separately; most of this surface is preview.
path:
  - step: "app receives request"
    identity_in_scope: "end user (bearer token)"
    compared_against: "none"          # <- the application has not yet done anything
  - step: "app builds retrieval query"
    identity_in_scope: "end user, as a STRING the app constructs"
    compared_against: "filter field in the index"
    trust_boundary: "IN MY CODE"      # the service does not validate this claim
    note: "documentation calls string-comparison trimming a WORKAROUND"
  - step: "index retrieves candidates"
    identity_in_scope: "application identity only"
    compared_against: "index-level data-plane role"
    approximate: true                 # candidate set is an ANN result, not an exact set
  - step: "trim"
    position_relative_to_retrieval: "AFTER"   # <- if this says AFTER, say what read the untrimmed set
    what_read_the_untrimmed_set: ["reranker", "log sink"]
  - step: "generation"
    identity_in_scope: "none"
    compared_against: "none"
    note: "the answer inherits no permission from any passage it was built from"

chunking:
  chunked: true
  permission_fields_projected_onto_chunks: UNKNOWN   # <- go and check. Do not infer.
  evidence: ""                                        # a field name and where you saw it

revocation:
  mechanism: "index synchronisation from the source system"
  immediate: false                    # the documentation states a timing lag
  window_measured: false              # NO LATENCY FIGURE IS PUBLISHED. Measure yours.
```

**Step 2 — stand up two personas with different entitlements.** Same corpus, same questions, different identities. This is the documentation's own suggested exercise and it is the only one that settles the argument.

**Step 3 — demonstrate the leak, then close it.** Ask a question whose answer lives in a document only one persona may read. **Watch the other persona receive the substance without receiving the document.** Then close it with a permission filter and a scoped user token on the request, and confirm the two-stage check is doing what you think: the application's own data-plane role, then the per-document check against the user's claims.

**Step 4 — break chunk projection on purpose.** Chunk a permissioned document without projecting its permission fields onto the chunks, and observe the chunks being returned. **This is the single most instructive failure in the topic** and it takes minutes.

**Step 5 — measure your revocation window.** Remove a permission in the source system. Then ask, repeatedly, until the content stops being retrievable. **Record the elapsed time as your number for your deployment.** Do not generalise it; nothing published gives you a figure and yours is one observation of one configuration. If the content is still retrievable when you run out of session, record that instead — it is a more useful finding than a tidy one.

**Step 6 — run the laundering trace on your own plan.** Pick one cell from your [S051](/wiki/seminars/S051.md) attribute table. Follow it through every artefact you have produced and write down which one still carries its provenance and which does not. **The step where the answer changes is the defect's address.**

**Step 7 — score twice.** Count every filled cell in your plan. Then count only cells you can trace to an observation. **The second number is what your evidence supports; the first is what your artefact will be read as claiming.**

**Step 8 — use the agent to attack your trace, not to complete it.** One prompt, with the trace and the plan attached: *name a step in this path where content is read before authorisation is evaluated, and name what would have to be true for that to be safe.* Then a second: *for each cell in this table, argue that the author inferred it rather than observed it.* Keep what beats your reasoning; record what you rejected. **An agent asked to review a trace will confirm it, because a trace is internally coherent. An agent asked to find the read-before-check has to walk it.**

**Step 9 — write down the four stages, and mark which ones your guardrail names.** Input, the tool call your agent generates including the content it sends, the tool's response, and the final output. **A stage your agent's guardrail does not name is unscanned**, whatever is configured on the model beneath it. Then extend the trace file, because a stage nobody scans is a step where nothing is compared, which is the same row shape you have already been writing.

**Step 10 — for every knowledge source, go and read whether it says so.** Not whether the layer above it is called permission-aware. Whether that source's own documentation explicitly states out-of-the-box support for user permissions, and whether synchronisation was actually configured for your deployment. **Two different facts, both checkable, and neither one inferable from the other.**

```yaml
# security/model/authz-trace.yaml   <- SHAPE, NOT SCHEMA. Most of this surface is
# preview. Confirm every stage name and every capability claim on the day you use it.
guardrail_stages:
  # Override is TOTAL, not additive. The model's stricter setting is not consulted.
  - stage: user_input
    named_in_agent_guardrail: true
  - stage: tool_call_generated          # includes the content sent TO the tool
    named_in_agent_guardrail: false     # <- UNSCANNED. Nothing errors.
  - stage: tool_response
    named_in_agent_guardrail: false     # <- UNSCANNED. Nothing errors.
  - stage: final_output
    named_in_agent_guardrail: true
  note: >
    The estate inventory records "content filtering configured" and that is true.
    Two of four stages are unscanned. Both statements hold at once.

knowledge_sources:
  - name: "policy-library"
    docs_state_user_permission_support: UNKNOWN   # <- read the SOURCE's page.
    permission_sync_configured: UNKNOWN           # <- read your OWN deployment.
    # Enforcement requires BOTH. Absent either, document-level access control is
    # not honoured, and the system answers anyway.

output_classification:
  source_items_labelled: true
  generated_output_inherits_label: false          # documented default
  # The protection was on the container. The output is a new container.
```

**Step 11 — trace one labelled document to one generated answer and record what the answer carries.** Take a source item with the strictest classification your environment offers, put it through the retrieval path, and write down the classification of the artefact that comes out. **Do not reason about it. Look at it.** Then write the sentence a reader of your plan would need in order not to assume inheritance, because they will assume it.

**Step 12 — attack the layer above, not the layer you built.** One prompt, with your stage table and your knowledge-source table attached: *assume a reviewer checks the model deployment and the knowledge layer's summary description and stops there. List every conclusion they would reach that this evidence does not support.* Keep what beats your reasoning; record what you rejected. **An agent asked whether your configuration is correct will check the configuration. An agent asked what a shallow reviewer would wrongly conclude has to find the layer where the assurance stops being true.**

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That a system with correct permissions, correct configuration and a passing test suite will hand a user the contents of a document they cannot open — and that nothing in the interaction looks wrong from either end. You will also observe the revocation window, which is the number nobody publishes and everybody's plan implicitly assumes is zero.

## What good looks like

- **The authorisation trace names every step**, including the ones where nothing is compared. A step marked `none` is the most valuable row in the table.
- **The trimming statement says where trimming sits relative to retrieval**, and if it is after, it names what read the untrimmed set.
- **The trust boundary is located.** You can say whether the service validates the caller's identity or whether your own code asserts it.
- **The chunk-projection statement rests on evidence you checked**, not on a reasonable assumption. A field name and where you saw it.
- **The revocation window is stated as measured or as unmeasured**, and never as zero.
- **The laundering trace names steps and artefacts**, not tendencies. *The model made things up* is a failed trace.
- **The double score shows a gap**, or the trace explains why your artefact has no inferred cells — which usually means you never marked any.
- **The smallest change is genuinely small**, and its cost is stated in the currency it is paid in: a wider table, a longer document, a slower assembly, a reader who has to read more.
- **Nothing in your submission claims your score means anything about the system.**

## How it goes wrong

- **The trimmed answer** — documents are filtered out of the result set after something has already read them. *Warning sign:* your trace has a reranker, a cache or a log above the trim step.
- **The unprojected chunk** — the document is permissioned and the chunks are not. *Warning sign:* you answered item 4 from memory rather than by looking.
- **The assumed revocation** — the plan is written as though removing an entitlement takes effect immediately. *Warning sign:* the word *revoked* appears with no window beside it.
- **The application-as-boundary** — a string filter constructed by your own code is treated as an authorisation check. *Warning sign:* you cannot name what would happen if that string were wrong.
- **The general complaint** — you write that generated content is unreliable and stop. *Warning sign:* your trace has no step names in it.
- **The big fix** — you redesign the chain. *Warning sign:* your fix requires more than one team to agree.
- **The single-defect reading** — you find the dropped provenance and miss the optimistic resolution, or the reverse. *Warning sign:* your fix is a single change and you have not asked what it leaves behind.
- **The audit-trail costume** — your deliverable renders your own verification checklist, ticks and all. *Warning sign:* it looks like evidence and it is a self-report wearing a uniform.
- **The score as measurement** — you report a number, with a tier or a colour, and let the reader take it as posture. *Warning sign:* you would be uncomfortable writing *this measures the document, not the system* directly underneath it.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed today.** The authorisation trace, the revocation-window statement and the double score are assessed at [S055](/wiki/seminars/S055.md) as constituents of the plan.

**1 · Two personas, one corpus, one question.** Run workflow steps 2 and 3.

> **What you should find.** That the unauthorised persona receives the substance of a document it cannot open, in fluent prose, with no citation and no signal that anything was withheld — because nothing was withheld. **Then close it and check the close properly:** confirm that both stages of the check are running, the application's own data-plane role *and* the per-document check against the user's claims. **A close that only demonstrates the application can query has demonstrated the wrong half.**

**2 · Break chunk projection, then find out how you would have caught it.** Run workflow step 4. Then write down what check, alert or test would have detected this in a system you did not build.

> **What you should find.** For the first half, that the chunks come back. For the second, that you cannot name a check — because the parent document is correctly permissioned, the configuration is correct, and every test you would normally write passes. **The only reliable detection is a per-persona retrieval test at chunk granularity**, which almost nobody writes, and writing one is the highest-value thing you will take from this session.

**3 · Go back to your own item 1, then break it.** Find what you wrote before instruction — the step at which the asker's identity is evaluated against a document's permissions. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then write the step properly, or record that no such step exists.

> **Criterion.** An answer passes if it names a step at which **a comparison happens between the asker's claims and a document's permission metadata**, and says which side of retrieval that step sits on. It fails if it names a place where your application constructs a filter — that is your code asserting an identity, and the documentation's own contrast is that this is string comparison rather than recognised authentication. **The most common failing answer is a correct description of a security filter, offered as an answer to a question about authorisation.** They are different things: the first is a mechanism you built, the second is a check something performs on your behalf, and the difference is where the trust sits.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Measure your revocation window.** Run workflow step 5.

> **Scoring criterion.** A pass records an elapsed time for one deployment under one configuration, and states plainly that it is one observation and that **no latency figure is published anywhere.** A fail reports it as a property of the platform. **A second fail, and the more common one, is not running the exercise and writing that revocation is handled** — which is what the plan says by default, because nothing in the plan's format prompts anybody to ask.

**5 · Find the two defects in your own laundering trace, and say why one fix is not enough.**

> **Model answer.** The two are **marker loss** — provenance existed and was discarded at a named step — and **optimistic resolution** — where sources disagreed, the more secure reading was adopted, so the unmarked value is not merely unsourced but **biased in a known direction**. Restoring the dropped provenance fixes the first and leaves the second: you would then have visible provenance on values that still lean systematically towards the more secure reading. Making conflict resolution neutral fixes the second and leaves the values unmarked. **Neither fix is sufficient and neither is a superset of the other**, which is why a single change is usually the wrong answer to opening item 5 — and why finding one that is not is the exercise.

**6 · Hand the delivered plan to somebody who has not seen your model, and ask two questions.**

> **First: which of these values did the author observe?** If they cannot tell, the laundering happened in your artefact, and the fact that *you* can tell is not a defence — **you will not be reading it in eighteen months and the person who is will not have your working files.** **Second: what would you now do differently having read this plan?** If the answer is nothing, the plan has produced a score and no work.

**7 · Classify your own self-verification checklists, closed book.** Find every place in your artefacts where a model checks its own output and reports that it did.

> **Scoring criterion.** Each one is a **configured control**, not an executed check, because the thing being checked and the thing checking are the same. A pass classifies them all correctly and identifies any that are rendered into the deliverable with their ticks intact — **which makes a configured control wear the visual language of an audit trail, and is this session's whole subject in miniature.**
>
> **Then compare.** Look at your answer to opening item 5 — the smallest change that would make an unauthorised retrieval visible after the fact. Does your answer depend on something checking itself? If so, you have proposed a countermeasure with the defect you have just learned to name.

**8 · Find the stage nobody is scanning.** Run workflow step 9 against a real agent, or against the documentation's own worked example if you have no agent to hand.

> **Scoring criterion.** A pass names the four stages, marks which are covered by the guardrail assigned to the agent, and states explicitly that a stricter setting on the underlying model does not fill an omitted stage because **override is total rather than additive.** A fail reports that content filtering is enabled — which is true, is what the estate's inventory says, and answers a different question. **The most instructive part is the second half:** write the sentence a reviewer would put in an assurance document after checking only the model deployment. It will be a true sentence about the model and a false sentence about the agent, and you now have a name for that.

**9 · Two entitlements, one knowledge base, two sets of citations.** Ask the same question as two users with different entitlements against a knowledge source whose permission synchronisation you have not verified. **Compare the citations, not the answers.**

> **What you should find.** That the answers are similar and the citation lists are identical, which is the observable signature of enforcement not being in force. **Compare answers and you will argue about phrasing; compare citations and the question is settled.** Then check the documentation for that specific source rather than the layer above it, because **permission-aware describes a capability the product has and permissions-enforced describes a state your deployment is in**, and only the second one protects anybody. **If your environment has no second identity available, record that as the finding** — a permission model you cannot test with two identities is a permission model nobody has tested, and saying so is worth more in the plan than a reassurance.

## What this does not establish

**Nothing here measures how often any of this happens.** Not how often a retrieval returns content the asker was not entitled to, not how long a revocation window actually is in any deployment, not how often chunk projection is missed, and not whether inference laundering has ever led to a system being harmed. **No such measurement exists in any source available to this programme, and your own week produces one observation of one configuration.**

**The access-control documentation is first-party, definitional and mostly preview.** Most of the page describes a preview API surface that changes without notice. It **contains no measurements of synchronisation latency, no failure-rate data, and no security assessment of the four approaches against each other** — they are compared on fit, not on strength, and a course must supply that analysis rather than inherit it. **It explicitly disclaims responsibility for compliance, geographic boundaries, testing and mitigations**, which fall on the implementer. It is trustworthy for what the system does and silent on how often it does it correctly under load.

**The two retrieval-algorithm sources say nothing whatever about security.** They are systems papers about approximate nearest-neighbour indexes, evaluated on standard benchmarks against contemporary baselines. **The property this session imports from them — that the returned set is approximate and that recall is an operating point rather than a guarantee — is theirs; the security consequence drawn from it is this programme's inference and is not a finding either paper reports.** Every quantity in them is a property of a particular index on a particular dataset and none is reproduced here.

**The failure taxonomy is an experience report over three systems.** No frequency data, no severity data, no controlled comparison, no representative sample. It predates widespread agentic retrieval. **Its seven failure-point names are recorded in this repository as reconstructed rather than quoted.** Its most useful claim for this session — that validation is only feasible during operation — is a strong position to be examined rather than an axiom, and it has real consequences for release governance in regulated settings.

**The chain this session traces upstream has never been calibrated against human judgement in any form** — no gold standard, no held-out sample, no inter-rater statistic, no pilot, no statement that any produced score was ever compared with an expert's assessment of the same plan. **More than one review agent operates on the same document, on different scales, applying different working definitions of what counts as specific, without referencing each other and with no rule for what happens when they disagree.** Their self-verification verifies computation rather than validity. **The chain can prove it added its own numbers up correctly, and can prove nothing else about them.**

**Every weighting, band boundary, threshold and partial-credit fraction in that material is a designer's judgement rendered as a number, and not one cites a study, a measurement, a pilot or a rationale.** None is reproduced here. **The point is not that the numbers are wrong — it is that they are assertions presented in the visual grammar of observations**, and a score with a tier and a colour will be read as a measurement by every reader who did not build it. That source is also internal and access-controlled: nothing in it can be linked, attributed or checked by anybody reading your work.

**The smallest-change exercise is this programme's invention** and has no warrant beyond the argument that a fix nobody can afford is not a fix.

**The one claim this session makes without hedging is definitional and old:** authorisation is a property of the retrieval path rather than of the answer, because a computation's output is not governed by the permissions of the content it read. That predates every product named here and will outlast all of them.

**The three documented instances establish defaults, and nothing else.** They are first-party statements that output does not inherit source labels, that an agent's guardrail fully overrides its model's, and that document-level access control is honoured only where a source supports it and somebody configured synchronisation. **They establish that these are the default behaviours. They establish nothing about how often any of them causes harm**, because **no detection rate, no false-positive rate, no bypass rate and no coverage measurement is published for any control named here, and none may be asserted.** **No measure of permission-trimming completeness exists anywhere** — no false-negative rate, no false-positive rate, no revocation-propagation time, from any of the three sources or from any other read for this programme.

**Two of the three describe surfaces that are preview**, and preview status across the build surface is pervasive and load-bearing. The control plane had been generally available only briefly when it was read, with a dated migration falling inside this material's life, and roughly thirty of its supporting pages were read as search excerpts rather than fetched in full. **The managed knowledge layer's summary pages contradict its own frequently-asked-questions page on the permission default**, which is why this session cites the second and not the first — and that contradiction is itself perishable in either direction.

**One adjacent claim in the same area is a community forum answer rather than documentation** — that the default guardrail policies cannot be edited or deleted and cannot be given indirect-injection protection — and it is recorded here as community content and relied on nowhere. **It is repeated in this session only because it demonstrates how readily a forum answer enters a plan as a fact**, which is the session's own subject applied to the session's own sources.
