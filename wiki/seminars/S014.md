## The situation

Forty minutes in. One person has answered every question. One has not spoken. Two are having their own conversation at the end of the table. The person whose environment you needed for the second exercise cannot get it working, and somebody has just asked a question you cannot answer.

You will handle some of this badly. The session is about which parts you are allowed to handle badly.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught.

1. Somebody says: *we tried this three years ago and it did not work.* What are your next two sentences?
2. A participant has not spoken for an hour. Write down what you will do. Now write what would happen if the reason is that their manager is in the room.
3. You cannot answer a question. What are your options, and which of them costs you least later?
4. You are running behind. Name the thing you will cut, and say who decides.
5. Which of the situations above would you rather handle badly than handle by breaking a rule?

**Item 5 has an answer and most people do not have it yet.** By the end of the session you should. Item 3 is the one that will surprise you, because the option that costs least later is the one that feels worst at the time, and you will be able to name it only after you have watched somebody else take it.

## What you must produce

Two artefacts.

A **rehearsal record** covering three assigned situations. For each one:

1. The situation as it appeared, in your own words.
2. What you did.
3. **Which prohibition you were closest to breaking**, and whether you broke it.
4. What the situation was evidence of, if anything, about the customer rather than about the room.

The record is marked against the prohibitions. **It is not marked against whether the room recovered.**

An **escalation design**, on the assigned platform, in which:

5. The condition under which the conversation stops is stated, separately from what happens next.
6. The recipient of the handover is named, with what they are given.
7. The fallback — what happens when the system does not understand rather than when it fails — is designed as a distinct case, and the distinction is written down.

## The material

### Local success, global cost

A facilitator in a deteriorating room has one dominant motive, and it is not learning about the customer. It is to make the deterioration stop.

Almost every intervention that stops it costs something invisible at the time. Answering the sceptic ends the objection and removes the person best placed to tell you what will block the work. Filling the silence ends the discomfort and narrows the answer. Guessing at an unanswerable question restores your standing and puts an unrecorded fiction into somebody's notes. Cutting the participant exercise because you are behind protects the schedule and removes the only part where the customer speaks.

**Each of those is locally successful and globally expensive**, and the local success is the only signal available in the room. That is why this session marks handling against prohibitions and refuses to mark it against recovery.

### Redirect the force; do not mirror it

The negotiation literature names the move and it is not firmness. **Negotiation jujitsu** is the instruction not to mirror a positional or personal attack but to redirect it into analysis: treat a stated position as one possible option and ask about the interests and principles behind it; do not defend an idea reflexively but invite criticism and ask which interests it fails to meet; recast a personal attack as a description of the problem; and after asking a question, let the silence stand rather than rescuing the other party from answering ([research/05-fde-craft/fisher-ury-getting-to-yes.md](/research/05-fde-craft/fisher-ury-getting-to-yes.md)).

That source supplies three further things this session uses directly.

**Separate the people from the problem**, on the grounds that criticism of a proposal is heard as criticism of a person; do not infer intentions from your own fears; describe your own observations and their effects rather than accusing anybody of motives.

**Negotiate the process when the process is the problem.** Its taxonomy of tactics is a vocabulary for a difficult room — an intentionally stressful setting, personal attacks on competence or punctuality, escalating demands after apparent progress, an absent party invoked as an immovable veto, calculated delay, and take-it-or-leave-it framing. The instruction attached to it is the part that matters here: **recognise the tactic, raise it explicitly, and negotiate the rules** — and do not diagnose every difficult behaviour as manipulation.

**The alternative to agreement is the standard.** What you do if this conversation ends without agreement is what any offer must beat, on both sides. A facilitator who has not thought about what happens if the session produces nothing will accept anything rather than let that happen.

**Its limits.** A practitioner framework developed through analytic distinction and worked cases. Its historical examples illustrate mechanism without isolating causes; **it reports no controlled comparison of anything**, and no effectiveness claim for teaching it is warranted by it. Its own third-edition question section concedes that positional bargaining is economical in narrow cases, that fair standards conflict, and that cultural and gender patterns are clues for inquiry rather than predictions about an individual.

### Silence is evidence about the conditions before it is evidence about the person

Before you decide what to do about somebody who is not speaking, list what it might cost them to speak.

Withholding at work is usually not shyness. It is a rapid and not fully conscious calculation in which staying quiet has an immediate and certain benefit and speaking has a delayed and uncertain one ([research/04-professional-formation/edmondson-psychological-safety.md](/research/04-professional-formation/edmondson-psychological-safety.md)). That asymmetry does not need a difficult person to produce it, and it does not announce itself — **a moment where somebody could have spoken and did not leaves no trace except in the person who held back.**

The same source records a set of taken-for-granted rules people hold about when it is appropriate to speak to those above them: do not criticise something the boss may have helped create; do not speak unless you have solid data; do not speak up if the boss's boss is present; do not say anything negative about the work in a group. **Read that list against your attendance sheet and the silent participant stops being a mystery.**

So the intervention with any chance of working is a change to the conditions rather than an appeal to the individual. Ask a question that has a factual answer they own. Ask it of them by name and by expertise. Take the manager out of the segment. Split the room. **Then see whether the silence was about them.**

The interviewing literature agrees and adds the diagnostic discipline: a reticent participant may have a different rhythm, need more structure or more silence, be reacting to the topic, connect better with another interviewer, or be inhibited by observers or by a manager — and the field team must **diagnose what is happening rather than label the person difficult** ([research/05-fde-craft/portigal-interviewing-users.md](/research/05-fde-craft/portigal-interviewing-users.md)). It also separates discomfort from danger, and is explicit that persisting through discomfort is a judgement rather than an obligation.

**Their limits.** The psychological-safety material rests on a trade book read in full for this repository **whose underlying empirical paper has not been read here**; its narrative review reports no disconfirming study, and its leadership framework is untested. The interviewing manual **reports no controlled comparison, no measured effect and no validated threshold.** Direction and mechanism only; no magnitude is asserted.

### The convenient belief attached to the sceptic

The corpus's difficult-situation catalogue is the most professionally mature material in the collection, and its sceptic entry is the strongest thing in it: validate the history, ask what was different last time, ask what would need to be true now, and recruit them explicitly as the person who can explain the obstacles ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md)).

Attached to it is a claim that sceptics who feel heard often become the practice's strongest advocates. **That is a practitioner belief with no evidence behind it, and it is a comfortable one to hold**, because it converts a person who is telling you something inconvenient into a future asset. The session teaches the handling and quarantines the claim.

**Its limits.** Access-controlled internal material held only as a white-labelled paraphrase that no public reader can check. **No handling in the catalogue is reported to have been tried and compared with any alternative**, no situation is reported with its outcome, and the prohibitions — the most valuable part — are the least justified part of an already unjustified artefact.

### Why the rehearsal marks the near-miss rather than the outcome

There is a reason to rehearse under conditions that produce errors, and a reason not to expect the rehearsal to look good.

The error-management training literature defines its intervention as active exploration **plus** explicit instruction that errors are expected and informative, with the trainer supplying **no further assistance when an error occurs** ([research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md](/research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md)). Two of its findings shape this session. The advantage of such training is **absent when measured during training and present afterwards** — so a rehearsal that looks bad in the room is not evidence that the design is failing. And the advantage is largest on **adaptive transfer**, meaning structurally novel problems rather than harder versions of the practised one, which is exactly what an unrehearsed difficult room is.

**Its limits, and they are substantial.** Every effect is relative to another training method; **no study used a no-training control**, so nothing speaks to absolute effectiveness. **No study measured on-the-job performance** — all outcomes are laboratory transfer tasks, almost all software training, mostly with students. The variant in which learners study others' errors rather than making their own was **excluded from that literature by definition**, and whether it works as well is an open question the authors raise and do not answer. Applying any of it to facilitation rehearsal is this session's inference.

### What a conversation does when it stops going well

Now the platform, and the reason it belongs in this session rather than another.

An escalation design answers three questions that a facilitator answers by instinct: **when does this stop, what happens next, and who receives it.** Written as configuration, they separate. In a room they do not, which is why a facilitator under pressure improvises all three at once and calls the result judgement.

The platform makes one distinction that is worth carrying into the room: **failing to understand and failing to work are different cases and need different designs.** A conversation that has not understood needs a different next move from one whose tool call errored, and a design that routes both to the same place has thrown away the more useful of the two signals.

It also forces the handover to be concrete. The vendor documentation's own axes make the constraints visible: declarative agents are "limited to Copilot's orchestrator and models" and "rely on user-initiated interactions", while custom engine agents own their orchestration and can "trigger actions automatically, even without direct user input" ([research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md)). **An agent that cannot act without being asked cannot escalate on its own initiative**, which decides what your fallback can be before you design it. And the low-code path's stated limits — less fine-grained control over actions and response formatting, a rich-card schema ceiling, department-scale positioning — bound what a handover can carry with it.

**Its limits.** First-party vendor selection guidance, read through search excerpts rather than full page fetches, comparing Microsoft options only with each other. **No adoption data, no failure-rate data and no evidence that any recommended mapping produces better outcomes.** Product names are in active flux; a limitation recorded today may be closed tomorrow.

## The workflow

**Design what your conversation does when it stops going well, then use the agent to argue that it should have carried on.**

You will use Copilot Studio's topic authoring surface, with a fallback topic and an escalation path. Steps 1 to 4 are executable in the maker portal; step 6 needs only the exported YAML.

**Step 1 — write the stop conditions before the handling.** Not what you will do. What must be true for this conversation to stop being the right conversation. Most designers write three and discover two of them are the same condition.

**Step 2 — separate the two failure classes explicitly.** *Did not understand* and *could not complete* are different, and their handovers differ: the first needs the utterance preserved, the second needs the error and the attempted action.

**Step 3 — name the recipient and what they receive.** A handover to "a human" is a handover to nobody. Name the queue, the role, and the payload.

```yaml
# escalation.yaml — illustrative shape only. Copilot Studio's authoring schema
# changes; verify against current documentation. Rich cards on this path are
# constrained to an older Adaptive Cards schema, which bounds what a handover
# can carry visually.
kind: AdaptiveDialog

# CASE 1: did not understand. The utterance is the evidence. Preserve it.
fallback:
  kind: OnUnknownIntent
  actions:
    - kind: SetVariable
      variable: Global.UnrecognisedCount
      value: =Global.UnrecognisedCount + 1
    - kind: ConditionGroup
      conditions:
        - condition: =Global.UnrecognisedCount >= 2
          actions:
            # Do NOT paraphrase. The exact words are the finding.
            - kind: SendActivity
              activity: "I'm not following this. I'm passing it to someone who can help."
            - kind: BeginDialog
              dialog: handover_to_specialist
    # First time only: ask, then stop. No options, no examples.
    - kind: Question
      prompt: Can you say that again in your own words?
      variable: Global.LastUtterance

# CASE 2: could not complete. The error is the evidence, not the utterance.
onError:
  kind: OnError
  actions:
    - kind: SendActivity
      activity: "That didn't work on my side. Handing over with what I tried."
    - kind: BeginDialog
      dialog: handover_to_specialist

handover_to_specialist:
  recipient: "Tier-2 platform queue"        # a queue, a role — never 'a human'
  carries:
    - Global.LastUtterance                  # verbatim, for CASE 1
    - Topic.AttemptedAction                 # what was tried, for CASE 2
    - Topic.ErrorDetail
    - Global.ConversationTranscript
  # NOT carried, deliberately: the agent's own guess at what the user meant.
  # A guess recorded as context becomes the recipient's starting assumption.
```

**Step 4 — test it with a participant who is not confused but is unconvinced.** Answer as the sceptic: *we tried this three years ago and it did not work.* Watch what the design does. Most designs treat it as a misunderstanding and try again, which is the conversational equivalent of answering the objection.

**Step 5 — write down what your design does with the sceptic, and whether that is right.** Not understanding, not working and not agreeing are three cases and your design almost certainly has two.

**Step 6 — attack your own escalation with the agent.** Give it your design and one instruction: build the strongest case that every one of these stop conditions is premature and the conversation should have continued. Then ask it which of your conditions could fire when nothing is actually wrong. Then ask the question that transfers to the room: **what does this design throw away at the moment it hands over?**

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That stopping is a decision with a condition, a recipient and a payload — three separable things you have been fusing. You will also watch the transferable failure happen in miniature: **a design that routes disagreement to the same place as confusion has decided that a sceptic is a malfunction**, which is the thing you will do in the room forty minutes in, and you will do it faster because it works.
## What good looks like

- The sceptic is **recruited, not converted.** You validated the history, asked what was different last time, asked what would need to be true now, and then asked them to be the person who explains the obstacles. You did not argue.
- Silence is treated as **information about the conditions**, not as a property of the silent person. You changed something about the room before you changed anything about them.
- The question you could not answer is parked visibly, with an owner and a date, and you did not guess.
- Running behind produced a **stated cut with a named decider**, rather than a compression that quietly removed the part where the customer talks.
- Your record contains at least one situation you handled badly while keeping the prohibition. **That is a pass.** The prohibitions exist for the moments when your judgement is degraded, which is exactly when you will most want to override them.
- Your escalation design distinguishes not understanding from not working, and neither of them from not agreeing.
- The handover names a recipient and a payload, and states one thing it deliberately does not pass on.

## How it goes wrong

- **Marking yourself on recovery** — you judge the handling by whether the room came back, so any intervention that worked is retrospectively correct. *Warning sign:* your account of the moment contains the word "eventually" and no account of what you were tempted to do.
- **Converting the sceptic** — you answer their objection, well, with evidence. They stop objecting. They also stop telling you what will actually block this. *Warning sign:* the room got easier and you learned nothing new.
- **Reading silence as agreement** — nobody objected, so the proposal is accepted. *Warning sign:* your notes record consensus and cannot name who expressed it.
- **Answering the unanswerable** — you produce a plausible answer to a question outside your knowledge, because not knowing feels like a failure of the competence you were selected for. *Warning sign:* you are speaking in the conditional and nobody has written anything down.
- **The silent cut** — you are behind, so you shorten the exercise where the customer works and keep the part where you present. *Warning sign:* the schedule slipped and your own speaking time did not.
- **The dominating participant as the customer** — one voice answers everything, and your notes become that person's view of their organisation. *Warning sign:* your consolidated finding at [S018](/wiki/seminars/S018.md) traces to one informant.
- **Disagreement routed as malfunction** — your escalation treats a sceptic the same way it treats a misunderstanding, and tries again. *Warning sign:* your design has two failure classes and the room has three.
- **The handover with no recipient** — the conversation stops and passes to "a human", or the record says the situation was escalated and cannot say to whom. *Warning sign:* the same sentence would be true if nobody received it.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed here.** One property is assessed at [S015](/wiki/seminars/S015.md): whether the prohibitions survived the assigned difficulty, or the breach was recorded by you rather than found by the assessor.

**1 · The rehearsal.** Situations are assigned, not chosen, and are drawn from the ordinary catastrophes: the person who answers everything, the person who answers nothing, the sceptic with history, the private conversation, the failed environment, the disagreement between two participants who both report to somebody present, the drift, the overrun, the late arrival, the exercise that does not land, and the question you cannot answer. Run each for a few minutes. **The observer marks against the prohibitions only.**

> **Scoring criterion.** A pass is a prohibition kept. A learner who handled the situation clumsily and kept the prohibition passes; a learner who rescued the room by breaking one does not, however well it went. **If nobody in your group breaks a prohibition, the participants are being too kind and the rehearsal is not testing anything.**

**2 · Recruit a sceptic without answering them.** Two minutes. Your partner opens with *we tried this three years ago and it did not work.* You may not present evidence, may not describe what is different about your approach, and may not use the word "but".

> **Model answer.** Validate the history as history. Ask what was different then — what they had, who was involved, what stopped it. Ask what would need to be true now for it to go differently. Then ask them to be the person who names the obstacles for the rest of the room. **The warning sign that you got this wrong is that the room got easier and you learned nothing new.**
>
> **And write down the falsifier.** The corpus attaches to this entry a claim that sceptics who feel heard often become the practice's strongest advocates. Write down what you would have to observe for it to be false. **A claim you cannot falsify is one you should not repeat**, and this one is comfortable precisely because it converts an inconvenient person into a future asset.

**3 · List what speaking would cost.** For the silent participant in your assigned rehearsal, list five things it might cost them to speak. Then choose the intervention that changes one of those costs rather than addressing the person.

> **Model answers.** The costs are usually structural: their manager is present; the topic implicates a decision they made; they lack the data they think an answer requires; the loud participant is senior to them; they were not told why they were invited. **The correct intervention changes the room, not the person** — ask a factual question they own, by name and by expertise; move the manager out of the segment; split the room; put the question in writing. Telling somebody their view matters changes nothing about what expressing it costs.

**4 · Answer the unanswerable.** Your partner asks a technical question you genuinely cannot answer. Handle it in three sentences.

> **Model answer.** Say you do not know. Name who would know or how you would find out. Park it visibly with an owner and a date. **Do not guess in the conditional** — the plausible answer is the expensive one, because it enters somebody's notes as fact and you will never see it again. The option that costs least later is the one that costs most now, which is why item 3 of the opening is hard before the session and easy after it.

**5 · Route the three cases.** Using your escalation design, feed it three inputs: an utterance it cannot parse, an action that errors, and a clear, well-formed disagreement. Record where each one goes.

> **What to notice.** Most designs send all three to the same place, and the third is the one that matters. **A design that treats disagreement as a malfunction has decided the customer is wrong before anybody has looked**, and it will do that consistently, at scale, without anybody noticing — which is the same failure you will make in the room, only slower.

**6 · Go back to your own item 5.** Find what you wrote about which situation you would rather handle badly than handle by breaking a rule. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference.

> **Criterion.** A passing answer names a situation, names the specific prohibition it would tempt you to break, and says what that break would cost after the session rather than during it. "I'd rather not lose the room" fails: it names no prohibition and no later cost. The strongest answers name the unanswerable question or the sceptic, because in both cases the intervention that fixes the moment is the one that destroys the finding.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer beside their own produced no benefit, and only the group **instructed to compare them** learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference is. **Its limits: school, university and laboratory evidence, with no adult-professional study in its reference list.**

**7 · Read one rehearsal record aloud** — the one where you handled it worst.

> **What to notice.** The room's only question is: **did you break a prohibition?** Not whether you recovered. If the answer is no, that record is the strongest thing in your file. **A cohort in which nobody volunteers a bad record has not understood the marking scheme**, and that is itself the diagnostic.

## What this does not establish

Nothing here shows that any prescribed handling produces a better session than any other.

The catalogue this session teaches is **codified experience with no evaluation attached.** No handling in the corpus is reported to have been tried and compared. The prohibitions are the most valuable part precisely because they are the part that cannot be justified by a good outcome, and they are also the part with the least support.

**The claim about silence is class 2** — direction and mechanism only, from a trade book read in full for this repository, whose underlying empirical paper has not been read here. No magnitude is asserted. The claim that the psychological conditions for speaking are a property of the group rather than of the organisation comes from that same book.

The sceptic-becomes-advocate claim is **unwarranted and is taught as a specimen of a convenient belief**, not as a technique.

**The negotiation material is a practitioner framework and reports no controlled comparison of anything.** Its historical cases illustrate mechanism without isolating causes, and no effectiveness claim for teaching it is warranted by it. Its own later editions concede that positional bargaining is economical in narrow cases and that cultural patterns are clues for inquiry rather than predictions about an individual.

**The error-management material does not support anything about facilitation.** Every effect in it is relative to another training method — there was no no-training control anywhere in the pool — and **no study measured on-the-job performance**; all outcomes are laboratory transfer tasks, almost all software training, mostly with students. The variant closest to this rehearsal, in which learners study others' errors rather than making their own, was excluded from that literature by definition and its effectiveness is an open question its authors raise and do not answer. **The application to rehearsal is this session's inference.**

**The platform documentation describes what a product does and evidences no outcome.** It was read through search excerpts rather than full page fetches, several tables were truncated, it compares Microsoft options only with each other, and its product names are in active flux. **Nothing in it says what a well-designed escalation achieves**, only what the surface permits — and the distinction between not understanding, not working and not agreeing is this session's framing rather than the vendor's.

**No effect size is asserted anywhere in this session.** Direction and mechanism only.
