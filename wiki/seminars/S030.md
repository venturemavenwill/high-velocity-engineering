## The situation

This is an assessment occasion and it is the stage's class boundary.

Every previous assessment in this programme told you what to produce. **This one does not.** You choose the scope at which your design is written, and the scope you choose determines the standard you will be marked against.

Answer these in writing before you begin, and submit them.

1. What decision does this design commit you to, and for how long?
2. At what scope should it be written — a task, a feature, a milestone, or an investigation?
3. **Name the heading your chosen scope requires that the scope below it does not.**
4. Who runs this after it exists?
5. What in your design is still open, and are you allowed to leave it open?

**Items 3 and 4 are the same question.** The published convention this session uses escalates from feature scope to milestone scope by adding exactly one heading, and that heading is about operation. A design that leaves the scope of a single piece of work **acquires a runtime owner**, and that acquisition is the whole content of the escalation.

## What you must produce

- **One design document**, at the scope you chose, following that scope's heading list.
- **A one-paragraph defence of the scope choice**, submitted with it.
- **The constraint register from [S026](/wiki/seminars/S026.md)**, attached as the design's frozen inputs.

The document is marked as in progress while it is in progress, and the mark is removed when it is not.

## What good looks like

These are the assessed elements. Each is checked as present and adequate, or not.

1. **The scope is chosen, defended, and correct for the decision the design carries.** A design that commits somebody to running something is written at the scope that has an operational heading. One that does not, is not — and writing at a larger scope than the decision requires is failed for the same reason as writing at a smaller one.
2. **Non-functional requirements appear as inputs**, with the measurements and verification methods from [S022](/wiki/seminars/S022.md) and [S023](/wiki/seminars/S023.md) carried through unchanged. A threshold that changed between the requirement and the design is either an error or a decision, and if it is a decision it has a record.
3. **The frozen inputs are attached, and each names its authority** or is explicitly marked as having none.
4. **Open questions are present and actually open.** Each names who would close it and what would close it. A question with neither is not open; it is abandoned.
5. **Risks name mitigations, and at least one risk is carried unmitigated with that stated.** An honest unmitigated list is worth more than a complete mitigated one.
6. **The in-progress marking is correct at the moment of submission.** Trivially checkable and routinely wrong.
7. **Where the design meets an existing system, the boundary decision from [S027](/wiki/seminars/S027.md) is named and its standing cost is stated.**

**Nothing else is assessed.** Not the quality of the architecture, not the technology chosen, not the length of the document, not whether anybody accepted the design, not whether the design is one an assessor would have produced, and **not whether the scope you chose is the scope an assessor would have chosen** — only whether you defended it against the decision the design actually carries.

## How it goes wrong

- **The essay at every scope** — one document shape for all decisions, so a task-sized choice arrives with a milestone's ceremony and a milestone-sized choice arrives with a task's. *Warning sign:* your defence of the scope choice is about the size of the document.
- **The milestone design with no operational owner** — the design commits somebody to running the thing and nobody is named. This is the failure the escalation exists to catch and it is the commonest one. *Warning sign:* item 4 was answered with a team that has not agreed.
- **The open question that is a decision nobody wants to take** — it is listed as open because listing it is easier than resolving it, and it names nobody who could close it. *Warning sign:* the same question was open at [S024](/wiki/seminars/S024.md).
- **The fully mitigated risk register** — every risk has a mitigation, several of the mitigations are the word *monitor*, and nothing is carried. *Warning sign:* nothing in your design worries you.
- **The perpetual draft** — the in-progress marking has been on the document for weeks and has stopped meaning anything. *Warning sign:* somebody asks whether it is finished.
- **The design with unauthored constraints** — the frozen inputs are attached and none of them has an authority, so the design is built on the register's weakest entries. *Warning sign:* the attached register is the first version, unchanged since [S026](/wiki/seminars/S026.md).

## Do it

**Choose the scope first and write the defence before you write the design.** Doing it in that order is the only protection against choosing a scope because it suited the document you had already begun.

Then write the design.

Then run one check that is entirely mechanical and catches most of what is wrong: **go through the constraint register entry by entry, and for each frozen input point at the place in the design where it is accommodated.** Entries with nowhere to point are either constraints your design violates, or constraints that were never real. Both are findings, and both belong in the document.

## Show it

Hand the design to somebody who would have written it at a different scope from the one you chose, and ask them which headings they missed.

**What they name is the scope argument**, and it is the argument you are defending. If they miss nothing, either the scopes are closer than you thought or you wrote at the larger one and did not notice.

## What this does not establish

Nothing shows that any of these four scopes is the right granularity, or that four is the right number of them. **They are one organisation's published convention, read on 2026-07-30**, and the publication offers no comparative evidence for any of its practices and does not claim to.

**The claim this session is built on — that leaving feature scope adds exactly one obligation, and that obligation is an operational owner — is precise, attractive, checkable in minutes against any design document, and is a convention rather than a finding.** It is taught because a claim you can check against your own artefacts is worth more than a claim you cannot, not because anybody has shown it holds.

The wider material this stage draws on **reports no engagement in which a design was found to have been written at the wrong scope**, because it reports no engagement that went badly at all. There is no observed instance of this failure anywhere in the sources, only the specification that would catch it.
