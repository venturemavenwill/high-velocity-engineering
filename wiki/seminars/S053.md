# S053 · Mapping Controls Without a Junk Drawer

**Module:** [M11](/wiki/modules/M11.md) · **Quarter:** [Q6](/wiki/quarters/Q6.md) · **Complexity class:** 4
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** none
**Whitepaper:** [WP-053](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-053.md)
**Retrieves from:** S024, S029, S051, S052
**Entry state:** this-programme-only

## The situation

You have a bound threat registry from [S052](/wiki/seminars/S052.md) and you are about to attach controls to it. This is the step where a threat model becomes useless while looking finished.

The failure has a name. **The junk drawer** — a set of broad hardening measures attached to everything, so that every threat has a control, every control has a threat, and the coverage summary is complete. It is the characteristic failure of coverage-maximising practitioners, it is produced by good intentions, and it is invisible in every summary the artefact will ever generate.

Answer these before instruction.

1. Take one control you already intend to apply. Name a threat in your registry it does **not** stop.
2. Your control and your threat mention the same component. What has that established?
3. A control protects the channel. The threat is in the payload. Is it credited?
4. When is it cheap to be over-inclusive, and when is it catastrophic?
5. If a threat in your plan has no control mapped to it, what does a reader assume?

**Item 4 has two correct and opposite answers**, and knowing which situation you are in is the session's actual content. **Item 5's honest answer is that they assume it is handled**, because an unmapped threat still appears in the plan and still reads like every other entry.

## What you must produce

A **mapping** from threats to controls, with **three-way accounting** that must reconcile.

- **The mapped set** — threat identifier, control identifier, and the reason the control addresses that threat.
- **Unmapped threats**, each with a reason.
- **Unmapped controls**, each with a rationale for keeping it.

Plus a **domain selection record**: which security domains you considered, **which you skipped, and why you skipped them.** The skipped list is not optional and it is not a formality.

And one paragraph the source material has nowhere: **where your own switch happens** — the point at which you stop being generously over-inclusive and start being ruthlessly precise.

## What good looks like

- **Every mapping states why**, in terms of the mechanism. *Both concern authentication* is a failed entry.
- **No control appears against more than a small share of your threats** without a specific defence of each attachment. A control mapped everywhere has been mapped nowhere.
- **Three tests are applied before any credit and are visible in the reasoning:** layer, direction, bypass.
- **Unmapped threats are stated as unmapped in the plan itself**, not merely in an appendix. A reader must not be able to mistake absence for coverage.
- **The skipped-domain list has justifications you would defend**, not *not applicable*.
- **The selection rule is written once and used everywhere.** If you selected domains twice by slightly different reasoning, say so and reconcile.
- **No mitigation is generic.** *Apply appropriate controls* is worse than an empty cell, because an empty cell can be noticed.

## How it goes wrong

- **The junk drawer** — broad hardening attached to everything, coverage complete, nothing addressed. *Warning sign:* your coverage summary has no gaps.
- **The name match** — a control and a threat share a component name and are joined. *Warning sign:* your reason restates the two entries instead of connecting them.
- **The wrong-layer credit** — the control protects the channel and the threat is in the payload; or it protects ingress and the threat is egress; or it protects the data plane and the threat is in the management plane. *Warning sign:* the reason works equally well in both directions.
- **The bypassed control** — credited, real, and defeated by a path your own documentation describes. *Warning sign:* you never looked for a second route to the same component.
- **The generic mitigation** — unactionable, uncheckable, unfalsifiable, and occupying the place a real one would have gone. *Warning sign:* it would fit any system.
- **The silent unmapped threat** — it is in the plan, it has no control, and nothing marks it. *Warning sign:* your unmapped list lives somewhere the reader will not reach.
- **The drifting selector** — you chose domains once during enumeration and again during mapping, by rules that were nearly the same. *Warning sign:* you cannot produce the rule as a single sentence.

## Do it

**First, select domains over-inclusively and record what you skipped.** Here over-inclusion is cheap and under-inclusion is catastrophic: a domain you never considered produces threats you never enumerated and a gap nothing downstream can detect. **Write the skipped ones down with justifications**, because a skipped domain with no recorded reason is indistinguishable from one you forgot.

**Second, write the selection rule as one sentence and use it everywhere.** In the material behind this session a near-identical selection heuristic appears in several places and drifts between them. **Two stages selecting domains by slightly different rules will select different domains for the same system**, and the plan will then be graded against controls a different stage never fetched. **Nothing detects it.** One sentence, one rule, used twice.

**Third, switch instinct, deliberately and on the record.** From here on, over-inclusion is the enemy. **Component overlap is not relevance and a shared name proves nothing.** Prefer leaving a threat unmapped to attaching something that does not stop it, and write your switch point down so somebody can argue with it.

**Fourth, apply three tests before crediting anything.**

- **Layer.** Does the control act where the threat acts — channel or payload, transport or content?
- **Direction.** Does it act in the direction the threat travels — ingress or egress, inbound or outbound?
- **Bypass.** Is there a documented alternative path to the same component that this control does not sit on? **A credited control that a documented path bypasses is not a control at all.**

**Fifth, map using the compact indexes only.** Give the joining work — yours or an agent's — the projections from [S051](/wiki/seminars/S051.md) and [S052](/wiki/seminars/S052.md), not the full entries. The diet is the point: a join wants keys and a short reason, and full text invites the name match.

**Sixth, reconcile the accounting.** Mapped, unmapped threats, unmapped controls. The three must add up, and the reconciliation is what stops a threat quietly leaving the artefact.

Then notice what you have just built. **You have instrumented yourself heavily against over-mapping and not at all against under-mapping**, which is exactly what the source material does — and an under-mapped threat is the one that will hurt you, because it survives into the plan reading like everything else.

## Show it

Two questions to a reviewer holding your mapping.

**First: find a control credited to a threat it does not stop.** Give them the three tests. Most mappings contain one and most authors cannot see their own.

**Second: which unmapped threat reads as handled?** They should be able to answer by scanning the plan. If they can, your unmapped marking is decoration, and the fix is in the plan's presentation rather than in the mapping.

## What this does not establish

**Nothing here measures whether a mapping is good.** The source material instruments precision elaborately — signal hierarchies, semantic gates, anti-junk-drawer rules, a stated preference for leaving a threat unmapped — and instruments recall not at all. This session inherits both the discipline and the asymmetry, and names the asymmetry rather than pretending to have solved it.

**The three tests are practitioner judgement carried from experienced people and are not validated anywhere.** They are stated as distinctions practitioners routinely collapse, which is an observation about practice, not a measurement of consequence.

**The switch point is this programme's invention.** The source holds both instincts — over-inclusion cheap at domain selection, catastrophic at mapping — and **never says when to change.** Requiring the learner to write their own switch down does not answer the question; it makes the answer visible so it can be argued with. That is the whole claim.

**And the accounting machinery is a symptom.** In the source, two adjacent stages are given opposite uncertainty policies — retain when uncertain, then drop when uncertain — which guarantees a large unmapped tail. **The three-way accounting exists because two policies pull in opposite directions: a structural problem treated as a reporting problem.** Your reconciliation will be tidy. It is not evidence that anything was resolved.

| Durable claim | Perishable instance |
|---|---|
