# S053 · Mapping Controls Without a Junk Drawer

**Module:** [M11](/wiki/modules/M11.md) · **Quarter:** [Q6](/wiki/quarters/Q6.md) · **Complexity class:** 4
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** Microsoft Purview DSPM for AI beside Defender AI-SPM — data posture and workload posture as two distinct control surfaces with different owners
**Whitepaper:** [WP-053](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-053.md)
**Retrieves from:** S024, S029, S051, S052
**Entry state:** this-programme-only

## The situation

You have a bound threat registry from [S052](/wiki/seminars/S052.md) and you are about to attach controls to it. This is the step where a threat model becomes useless while looking finished.

The failure has a name. **The junk drawer** — a set of broad hardening measures attached to everything, so that every threat has a control, every control has a threat, and the coverage summary is complete. It is the characteristic failure of coverage-maximising practitioners, it is produced by good intentions, and it is invisible in every summary the artefact will ever generate.

Answer these before instruction.

1. Take one control you already intend to apply. Name a threat in your registry it does **not** stop.
2. Your control and your threat mention the same component. What has that established?
3. A control protects the channel. The threat is in the payload. Is it credited?
4. When is it cheap to be over-inclusive, and when is it catastrophic?
5. If a threat in your plan has no control mapped to it, what does a reader assume?

**Item 4 has two correct and opposite answers**, and knowing which situation you are in is the session's actual content. **Item 5's honest answer is that they assume it is handled**, because an unmapped threat still appears in the plan and still reads like every other entry.

## What you must produce

A **mapping** from threats to controls, with **three-way accounting** that must reconcile.

1. **The mapped set** — threat identifier, control identifier, and the reason the control addresses that threat, stated in terms of the mechanism.
2. **Unmapped threats**, each with a reason.
3. **Unmapped controls**, each with a rationale for keeping it.
4. **A control inventory** in which every control carries a **surface** (data posture or workload posture), a **named owner**, and a **verification method** — how anybody would establish that it is actually in place.
5. **A domain selection record**: which security domains you considered, **which you skipped, and why you skipped them.** The skipped list is not optional and it is not a formality.
6. **One paragraph the source material has nowhere:** where your own switch happens — the point at which you stop being generously over-inclusive and start being ruthlessly precise.

## The material

### The junk drawer, and why it is invisible from every angle

The practitioner judgement this session is named for is blunt: **when mapping controls to threats, the instinct to be over-inclusive is a disaster — the junk drawer of broad hardening attached to everything is the characteristic failure of coverage-maximising practitioners** ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-agentic-threat-modelling.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-agentic-threat-modelling.md)). That source is internal and access-controlled; it is paraphrased here, quoted nowhere, and **supplies no external warrant for anything.**

What makes the junk drawer dangerous is not that it is wrong. Every control in it is real, every threat it touches is real, and every individual pairing is arguable. **What makes it dangerous is that it produces a complete coverage summary**, and a complete coverage summary is the artefact everyone above you actually reads.

The test is one you can apply to your own work today. **A catalogue in which everything maps to *governance* or *monitoring* is a catalogue that has stopped discriminating.** Those two words are the drawer. They are broad enough to be true of almost any control and almost any threat, and a mapping that leans on them has produced coverage without producing an answer to a single question about the system.

The same source supplies the discriminating tests, all three as distinctions practitioners routinely collapse.

- **Layer.** Channel against payload. A control that protects the transport does not address a threat carried inside a well-formed message on that transport.
- **Direction.** Ingress against egress. A control on what arrives does not address what leaves.
- **Plane.** Data plane against management plane. A control on requests does not address the path by which the system is reconfigured.

And a fourth, which is not a distinction but a search: **a credited control that a documented alternative path bypasses is not a control at all.** The word *documented* is doing work — the bypass is usually written down in your own architecture, and nobody looked.

**Component overlap is not relevance; a shared name proves nothing.** That is the whole content of the name-match failure, and it is the single most common defect in machine-produced mappings, because a shared string is exactly what a naive join finds.

### Two posture surfaces, two owners, and why the split is the lesson

Here is where the platform stops being an illustration and becomes the argument.

The vendor ships **two separate products for what a junk drawer would file as one thing** ([research/06-microsoft-platform/purview-dspm-for-ai-and-defender-ai-spm-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/purview-dspm-for-ai-and-defender-ai-spm-docs.md)). **Data security posture management for AI** governs what sensitive information flows into and out of AI tools, who is oversharing what, and which interactions are captured. **AI security posture management** governs which AI applications exist in the estate at all, what they are built from, how they are misconfigured, and what an attacker could chain together.

**They answer two different questions — what is our data doing in AI systems, and what AI systems do we even have — and the fact that these require separate products is itself the lesson.** They have different administrative surfaces, different licensing, and in most organisations different owners: one sits with information protection and compliance, the other with cloud security operations.

So when your mapping attaches *governance* to a threat, ask which of those two it means. If the answer is *both* or *neither*, you have found your junk drawer, and you have found it using a distinction the vendor's own product estate refuses to collapse.

The data-side argument is the strongest reasoning on those pages and belongs in your head regardless of tooling. Generative AI **"amplifies the problem of oversharing data"** by proactively surfacing content that is obsolete, over-permissioned or lacking governance controls. Oversharing is not new — organisations have had over-permissioned document libraries for two decades, and the reason it caused little damage is that nobody read them. **A retrieval system reads all of them, instantly, on behalf of anyone who asks. Deploying capable retrieval converts every dormant permissions error in an organisation into a live one.** That is why a permissions fix is a *data-posture* control and an endpoint hardening is a *workload-posture* control, and why crediting one against a threat that belongs to the other is a wrong-surface credit — a fourth test, alongside layer, direction and plane.

The same material carries the governance dilemma in one sentence. Among the item-level remediation actions is removing a sharing link, and the documentation flags it as **"an action to be used sparingly because it could prevent people from accessing the item legitimately."** **The safest action is the most disruptive, and somebody has to own that trade-off.** A control with no named owner is a control nobody will ever be willing to fire.

**Read that source for structure and not for assurance.** First-party and definitional. **No detection rate, no false-positive rate and no coverage measurement is published — assert none, because none exists.** One of the two pages is explicitly a superseded classic version; the other was read through search excerpts rather than fetched. Its operational bounds — how much of an estate is assessed, on what cadence, with what delay — are paraphrased here as **a bounded sample, on a periodic cadence, with a latency measured in days**, because those bounds are the resolution of the instrument and the specific numbers will be wrong before you need them.

### Where a control lives is part of what the control is

A control is not only a mechanism. It is a mechanism **at a place**, and the place determines what it can and cannot address.

The gateway documentation is the clearest available demonstration ([research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md)). Prompt moderation implemented as a gateway policy **guarantees uniform enforcement across every consumer** and simultaneously **centralises a decision some applications might legitimately want to make differently.** That is a real trade-off with no correct answer, and it is invisible in a mapping that records only *content safety applied*.

The same page carries a control-class distinction worth memorising. **Managed identities authenticate to services "so you don't need API keys for authentication"** — which is **the elimination of a long-lived secret, not merely its better storage.** Those are different controls addressing different threats. A key in a vault is still a key; it can be read, copied and used by whoever holds it. Crediting a secrets-management control against a threat that requires secret *elimination* is a layer error wearing a plausible label.

The workload-posture material supplies four infrastructure-as-code checks that together are a minimal secure baseline for any hosted model endpoint: **private endpoints; restricted endpoints; managed identity; identity-based authentication.** **Three of the four are about eliminating keys and network exposure**, which tells you where the vendor thinks the tractable risk actually is. Use them as a starting inventory, not as a coverage claim.

**The gateway documentation's limits:** first-party, definitional, with a commercial frame. It argues for a gateway and does not discuss the latency, cost or operational burden the gateway itself adds. **No benchmarks, no latency figures, no cost measurements and no comparison against other products.** Capability availability varies by service tier, so any statement about what is available is conditional on a tier you must check. Its architectural reasoning is sound and **asserted rather than demonstrated.**

### A control that cannot name its verification method has not been written

The openly published engineering handbook carries a non-functional-requirement form with several fields, and its most useful property is which of them are load-bearing: **a requirement that cannot name how it would be verified has not been written** ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md), pages read 2026-07-30). Import that wholesale into your control inventory. Every control gets a verification method, and the method is a thing somebody could do rather than a thing somebody could claim.

That handbook also demonstrates, accidentally, why this matters. **It contradicts itself in at least a dozen places without noticing** — secrets in a key vault on one page and encrypted into the repository on another; mocking frameworks recommended and discouraged; three different branch-naming conventions. None is an error of fact; all are the residue of many hands over years with no reconciliation pass. **A control catalogue accumulates the same way, and nothing detects it.** Its threshold catalogue carries no source at all, which the note records explicitly: **usable as an example bank, never as a benchmark.** And it **reaches for a magnitude in at least four places and warrants none of them; those four are recorded in this repository as specimens and reproduced nowhere.**

### Why your coverage summary will look fine either way

One structural warning before you start, from a literature with no product to sell. **"A model with good performance 'on average' may still exhibit failure modes that are not apparent by looking at an aggregate metric"**, from which the requirement to slice follows, together with the observation that **which slices matter is a domain question rather than a statistical one** ([research/02-technical-foundations/crowe-machine-learning-production-systems.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/crowe-machine-learning-production-systems.md)).

Apply that directly. A mapping with excellent aggregate coverage can be uniformly blind to one surface, one layer or one direction, and **no summary of it will show that.** Slice your own mapping by surface and by test outcome before you believe any total.

**Its limits are heavy:** practitioner guidance from a commercial publisher, no study design, no comparison group, three of four authors at one large vendor, some claims resting on anonymous practitioner testimony, and every quantity a property of one system after one run. **Mechanism transfers; no magnitude does, and none appears here.**

### The selector that drifts, and the accounting that hides a structural problem

Two final findings from the source design, both of which you are about to reproduce unless you act.

**A category-selection heuristic appears in several separate places in near-identical form, and drifts between them.** Two stages selecting domains by slightly different rules will select different domains for the same system — and the plan will then be graded against controls a different stage never fetched. **Nothing detects it.** Hence: write the rule once, as one sentence, and use it in both places.

And the accounting. **Two adjacent stages in that design are given opposite uncertainty policies** — retain when uncertain in one, drop when uncertain in the next. Each is defensible alone; together they guarantee a large unmapped tail, which the design absorbs with a dedicated output section and mandatory three-way accounting. **The accounting machinery exists because two policies pull in opposite directions: a structural symptom treated as a reporting problem.** Your reconciliation will balance. That is arithmetic, not resolution.

## The workflow

**Build a control inventory with two surfaces and named owners, map on compact indexes only, then attack the mapping.**

**Step 1 — select domains over-inclusively and record the skips.** Here over-inclusion is cheap and under-inclusion is catastrophic: a domain never considered produces threats never enumerated, and a gap nothing downstream can detect. **Write the skipped domains down with justifications**, because a skipped domain with no recorded reason is indistinguishable from one you forgot.

**Step 2 — write the selection rule as one sentence, in the file, and use it in both places.**

**Step 3 — build the inventory with the surface split forced.** Create `security/model/controls.yaml`. Every control carries a surface, an owner and a verification method — and the schema refuses the words that make a junk drawer:

```yaml
# security/model/controls.yaml   <- SHAPE, NOT SCHEMA.
selection_rule: >
  Consider a domain if any characteristic marked present in characteristics.yaml
  implicates it; record every domain not considered, with the characteristic
  whose absence excused it.

controls:
  - id: CTL-04
    name: "Managed identity on the model endpoint"
    surface: workload-posture        # data-posture | workload-posture
    owner: "platform-security (named person, not a team alias)"
    verification: "Read the resource's identity block; confirm no key exists
                   in configuration. Repeatable by anyone with reader access."
    class: secret-elimination        # NOT 'secret-management' - different control
    acts_on:
      layer: management-plane        # channel | payload | management-plane
      direction: inbound             # inbound | outbound | both
    # FORBIDDEN as a surface, class or reason: "governance", "monitoring",
    # "best practice", "hardening". If one of these is the honest answer,
    # the control has not been identified yet.
```

**Step 4 — switch instinct, deliberately and on the record.** From here on, over-inclusion is the enemy. **Component overlap is not relevance and a shared name proves nothing.** Prefer leaving a threat unmapped to attaching something that does not stop it, and write your switch point down so somebody can argue with it.

**Step 5 — map using the compact indexes only.** Give the joining work — yours or an agent's — the projections from [S051](/wiki/seminars/S051.md) and [S052](/wiki/seminars/S052.md), not the full entries. **The diet is the point:** a join wants keys and a short reason, and full text invites the name match.

**Step 6 — apply four tests before crediting anything.** Layer. Direction. Plane. Surface. Then search for the bypass: is there a documented alternative path to the same component that this control does not sit on?

**Step 7 — read both posture surfaces and place your existing controls.** Open the data-posture recommendations and the workload-posture recommendations separately, and for each control already in your inventory, say which surface it belongs to and who owns it there. **Expect the uncomfortable finding: several of your controls belong to a surface whose owner you have never met.** That is not a documentation gap. It is the actual organisational fact your plan depends on, surfaced for the first time.

**Step 8 — reconcile the accounting.** Mapped, unmapped threats, unmapped controls. The three must add up. Then slice the mapped set by surface, by layer and by direction, and look for a slice with no entries.

**Step 9 — attack your own mapping.** One prompt to the agent, with the compact indexes and your mapping attached: *for each mapping, argue that the control does not stop the threat, using layer, direction, plane and surface — and name the documented path that bypasses it.* Keep what beats your reasoning; record what you rejected. **An agent asked to check a mapping will confirm it, because the mapping is internally consistent. An agent asked to break one has to find the layer error.**

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That the word *governance* covers two products, two administrative surfaces and two owners — and that your mapping was using it to mean all of them at once. You will also observe that the reconciliation balances whether or not the mapping is any good, which is the clearest available demonstration that arithmetic is not assurance.

## What good looks like

- **Every mapping states why**, in terms of the mechanism. *Both concern authentication* is a failed entry.
- **No control appears against more than a small share of your threats** without a specific defence of each attachment. A control mapped everywhere has been mapped nowhere.
- **Four tests are applied before any credit and are visible in the reasoning:** layer, direction, plane, surface — plus the bypass search.
- **Every control names a surface, a living owner and a verification method** somebody other than the author could perform.
- **Unmapped threats are stated as unmapped in the plan itself**, not merely in an appendix. A reader must not be able to mistake absence for coverage.
- **The skipped-domain list has justifications you would defend**, not *not applicable*.
- **The selection rule is written once and used everywhere.** If you selected domains twice by slightly different reasoning, say so and reconcile.
- **No mitigation is generic.** *Apply appropriate controls* is worse than an empty cell, because an empty cell can be noticed.

## How it goes wrong

- **The junk drawer** — broad hardening attached to everything, coverage complete, nothing addressed. *Warning sign:* your coverage summary has no gaps.
- **The two-word catalogue** — every control classified as governance or monitoring. *Warning sign:* you cannot say which of the two posture surfaces a control belongs to.
- **The name match** — a control and a threat share a component name and are joined. *Warning sign:* your reason restates the two entries instead of connecting them.
- **The wrong-layer credit** — the control protects the channel and the threat is in the payload; or ingress against egress; or data plane against management plane. *Warning sign:* the reason works equally well in both directions.
- **The wrong-surface credit** — a workload hardening credited against a data-exposure threat, or the reverse. *Warning sign:* the control's owner could not fix the threat if they wanted to.
- **The bypassed control** — credited, real, and defeated by a path your own documentation describes. *Warning sign:* you never looked for a second route to the same component.
- **The generic mitigation** — unactionable, uncheckable, unfalsifiable, and occupying the place a real one would have gone. *Warning sign:* it would fit any system.
- **The silent unmapped threat** — it is in the plan, it has no control, and nothing marks it. *Warning sign:* your unmapped list lives somewhere the reader will not reach.
- **The drifting selector** — you chose domains once during enumeration and again during mapping, by rules that were nearly the same. *Warning sign:* you cannot produce the rule as a single sentence.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed today.** The mapping, the three-way accounting and the skipped-domain list are assessed at [S055](/wiki/seminars/S055.md) as constituents of the plan.

**1 · Count your drawer.** Go through your control inventory and count how many entries you would classify as *governance* or *monitoring* if forced to choose one word.

> **Scoring criterion.** Any control for which those words are the honest answer has not been identified yet, and the repair is to name the mechanism instead. **A control is a thing that happens to a request, a document, a credential or a configuration.** If you cannot say which, you have a policy rather than a control — which may be entirely appropriate, and belongs in the plan labelled as one.

**2 · Sort every control onto a posture surface, then find its owner.** Data posture or workload posture, and a person.

> **What you should find.** Two things. That a few controls genuinely straddle — a sensitivity label drives both a data policy and a retrieval behaviour — and that straddling controls are where the plan is most likely to assume somebody else is handling it. And that **at least one surface has an owner you have never spoken to.** Record that in the plan. **An unowned control is a control that will never be fired, and the most disruptive remediations are exactly the ones that need an owner willing to accept the disruption.**

**3 · Go back to your own item 1, then break it.** Find what you wrote for the opening item — the threat your intended control does *not* stop. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then produce a proper negative claim for that control: the class of threat it structurally cannot address, and the test that shows it.

> **Criterion.** A negative claim passes if it names one of the four tests and applies it. *This control acts on the channel; the threat is in the payload, so it is not credited.* *This control acts on ingress; the threat travels outbound.* *This control belongs to the workload surface and the threat is a data-posture threat whose owner is in a different organisation.* It fails if it says the control is *not sufficient* or *does not fully address* the threat — both of which are hedges, and both of which will be read as partial credit by the next person who touches the artefact.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Two questions to a reviewer holding your mapping.**

> **First: find a control credited to a threat it does not stop.** Give them the four tests. **Most mappings contain one and most authors cannot see their own.** *Second: which unmapped threat reads as handled?* They should be able to answer by scanning the plan. If they can, your unmapped marking is decoration, and the fix is in the plan's presentation rather than in the mapping.

**5 · Apply the four infrastructure checks to something you have actually deployed.** Private endpoints, restricted endpoints, managed identity, identity-based authentication.

> **What you should find.** That three of the four are about eliminating keys and network exposure, and that the one you are most likely to have got wrong is the distinction between *managed identity* and *a key held somewhere safer*. **They are different controls addressing different threats**, and a mapping that credits secret storage against a threat requiring secret elimination has made a layer error with a plausible label on it. **If you cannot run these checks against a real deployment, record that as the finding** — a control you cannot verify is a control whose verification method you have not written.

**6 · Write your switch point, then have somebody attack it.** One paragraph: at what point in your process does over-inclusion stop being cheap and start being catastrophic?

> **Scoring criterion.** A pass names an artefact boundary rather than a feeling — *at the moment I stop selecting domains and start attaching controls to threat identifiers*. A fail says *when I have enough coverage* or *once the important ones are done*. **The source design holds both instincts and never says when to change**, and this exercise does not answer the question either. It makes your answer visible so somebody can argue with it, which is the whole claim.
>
> **Then compare.** Look at your answer to opening item 4 — when is it cheap to be over-inclusive, and when catastrophic. Did you name a *moment* or a *feeling*? That is the difference between a rule somebody else could apply and a habit only you can.

## What this does not establish

**Nothing here measures whether a mapping is good.** The source material instruments precision elaborately — signal hierarchies, semantic gates, anti-junk-drawer rules, a stated preference for leaving a threat unmapped — and instruments recall not at all. This session inherits both the discipline and the asymmetry, and names the asymmetry rather than pretending to have solved it.

**The four tests are practitioner judgement carried from experienced people and are validated nowhere.** They are stated as distinctions practitioners routinely collapse, which is an observation about practice rather than a measurement of consequence. The fourth — surface — is this programme's addition, derived from a product split rather than from any finding.

**The switch point is this programme's invention.** The source holds both instincts — over-inclusion cheap at domain selection, catastrophic at mapping — and **never says when to change.** Requiring the learner to write their own switch down does not answer the question; it makes the answer visible so it can be argued with. That is the whole claim.

**The accounting machinery is a symptom.** In the source, two adjacent stages are given opposite uncertainty policies — retain when uncertain, then drop when uncertain — which guarantees a large unmapped tail. **The three-way accounting exists because two policies pull in opposite directions: a structural problem treated as a reporting problem.** Your reconciliation will be tidy. It is not evidence that anything was resolved.

**The posture products' effectiveness is unpublished and unassertable.** **No detection rate, no false-positive rate and no coverage measurement exists for either surface**, so nothing in this session establishes that either finds what is there. One page behind the note is explicitly superseded and its replacement was not read; the other was read through search excerpts. The operational bounds are the resolution of the instrument and are paraphrased rather than reproduced. **Agent-level capability moves to a separate licence during the life of this material**, which will change what a learner can even see.

**The gateway documentation argues for a gateway.** It is first-party with a commercial frame, offers no benchmarks, no latency figures and no comparison against alternatives, and does not discuss the cost or operational burden the gateway itself adds. Its capability availability varies by service tier. **Its architectural reasoning is sound and asserted rather than demonstrated.**

**The verification-method requirement comes from a handbook that cannot be dated as a whole**, contradicts itself in at least a dozen places without noticing, and asserts four magnitudes it never warrants. Its own threshold catalogue carries no source — **usable as an example bank, never as a benchmark.**
