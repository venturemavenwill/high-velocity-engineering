## What this day is for

You are about to spend three years inside a design that will repeatedly feel less efficient than the alternative. You will be tested on material before it is taught. You will be made to revisit things you believe you already know. You will be given problems interleaved rather than blocked, so that no session ever produces the smooth sense of mastery a blocked session produces.

If you do not hold a model of why, you will experience all of this as bad teaching, and you will route around it — by blocking your own study, by re-reading instead of retrieving, and by trusting the fluency your own study produces. That is a rational response to an unexplained aversive design, and it is the single most likely way this programme fails you.

So the first day is about the design itself, and it is taught by putting you through a compressed version of it and letting the result do the arguing.

It is also the day the instrument arrives. **You configure GitHub Copilot agent mode and the Microsoft Learn MCP Server before anyone tells you what either is good for, and the day's second thesis is that an instrument is characterised before its outputs are trusted.** The two theses are the same thesis. Your own sense of how well a study session went is an instrument reading, and so is an agent's confident paragraph, and neither is evidence until someone has checked it against an outcome.

## Key terms

| Term | What it means here |
|---|---|
| **Retrieval practice** | Pulling something out of memory without looking. The act of retrieving changes what you will remember later; it is not a neutral measurement of what you already know. |
| **Spacing** | Leaving a gap between encounters with the same material. The gap is the mechanism, not the inefficiency. |
| **Interleaving** | Mixing problem types within a session instead of doing all of one kind, then all of the next. |
| **Blocked practice** | The opposite of interleaving: all of one type together. It feels better and it is the thing this programme deliberately refuses. |
| **Pretesting** | Being asked questions before you are taught the answers. The failed attempt does work that reading the answer first does not. |
| **Generation** | Producing an answer yourself — wrong is fine — before the canonical answer arrives. |
| **Fluency illusion** | The feeling of understanding produced by material passing smoothly in front of you. It tracks ease of processing, not durability of learning. |
| **Cognitive load** | How much your working memory is holding at once. It is finite, and different parts of a task compete for it. |
| **Desirable difficulty** | A difficulty introduced on purpose because it slows performance now and improves retention later. Not all difficulty is desirable; the distinction matters. |
| **Jagged frontier** | The uneven boundary of what an AI system does well, which does not follow how hard a task feels to a person. |
| **Calibration** | The match between how confident you are and how right you are. It is trainable, and it is the programme's central professional skill. |

## The core ideas

**This section is the chapter. Read it on your own; nothing in it needs a room, a group or an instructor.** It teaches the six mechanisms this programme is built on, then two claims about the instrument you are about to configure. Everything after it is the session plan for the day.

### 1. Why the session that felt good is the one to be suspicious of

Begin with something uncomfortable. **The study session that feels productive and the study session that produces durable learning are frequently not the same session.** Not occasionally. Frequently, and in a predictable direction.

Think about what happens when you re-read a chapter. The second pass goes faster than the first. The third goes faster still. Sentences that stopped you the first time now slide past. You finish with a distinct sense that the material has settled — that you *have* it.

Almost none of that feeling is about learning. It is about **processing fluency**: your brain is recognising the shape of text it saw twenty minutes ago, and recognition is cheap. The subjective signal you are reading — *this is going smoothly, therefore I understand it* — is a real signal about a real thing. It is just not a signal about whether you will be able to produce the material next month with the book shut.

Here is the trap in its sharpest form. **The techniques that produce the strongest feeling of learning and the techniques that produce the most learning are close to opposites.** Re-reading feels excellent and does relatively little. Trying to recall with the book closed feels like failure — because it partly *is* failure, out loud, in front of yourself — and does a great deal.

> **Worked example.** Two students prepare for the same assessment. Ana re-reads the chapter four times over one evening and finishes confident. Ben reads it once, then over four evenings attempts to write down what he remembers, checking only afterwards. On the fourth evening Ben still cannot recall two of the six concepts and feels behind. Ana feels ready. **If you tested them that night, Ana would very likely win.** If you tested them in a month, the ordering typically reverses — and, crucially, *neither student's confidence would have predicted it.* Ana's confidence was high and wrong. Ben's was low and also wrong, in the other direction.

This is the **fluency illusion**, and it is the most important single idea on this page, because it is the reason you cannot use your own sense of how a session went as evidence about that session. **Your confidence is an instrument reading, and this instrument is known to be optimistic about itself.**

**The common misreading:** *"So I should feel bad while studying."* No. Discomfort is not the goal and is not the mechanism — plenty of miserable study is also useless. The claim is narrower and stranger: **the feeling is uninformative, so stop using it to steer.** Use an outcome instead. Which is what the rest of these mechanisms are for.

### 2. Retrieval is an event, not a measurement

Most people carry a model in which **studying causes learning and testing measures it**. Input, then read-out. It is a clean model and it is wrong in a way that changes how you should spend every hour.

The act of retrieving something from memory — source closed, effortfully, possibly failing — **is itself among the most powerful learning events available.** A test is not a thermometer dipped into what you know. It is closer to exercise: the effort of the retrieval is the thing that changes you.

The mechanism is worth holding, because it explains the details. Retrieving is not replaying a recording; it is **reconstructing**, and reconstruction strengthens and reorganises the route you took to get there. Which is why the *difficulty* of the retrieval matters. A retrieval that costs you nothing — the fact you read four seconds ago — changes little. A retrieval that is hard but succeeds changes a lot. **The effort is not a side-effect of the method. It is the method.**

> **Worked example.** You have one hour and one chapter. Option A: read for sixty minutes. Option B: read for forty minutes, then close the book and write down everything you can for twenty. Option B produces a page that is visibly incomplete, with gaps you can see, and it feels like you have wasted twenty minutes you could have spent covering more ground. **Option B is generally the better hour**, and the visible gaps are the most valuable thing it produced, because they tell you where to look — information Option A never generates at any point.

**This is what phase 2 is, on every day of this programme except this one.** Before anything new arrives, you retrieve material from earlier days. It is not revision and it is not a warm-up quiz. It is the day's first learning event.

**The common misreading:** *"So I should test myself at the end to check."* That converts a learning event back into a measurement event and throws away most of the benefit. **Retrieve early, retrieve when it is still hard, and retrieve before you feel ready** — the discomfort of retrieving something you have not quite consolidated is where the work happens.

### 3. The gap is the mechanism, not the cost

Meet the same material twice and the interval between encounters changes how much survives. Longer gaps produce more forgetting *between* the encounters and better retention *afterwards*.

Read that again, because the shape of it is counterintuitive. **The forgetting is not a price paid for spacing. It is closer to the reason spacing works.**

The mechanism follows directly from the previous section. If retrieval strengthens memory in proportion to the effort of retrieving, then an encounter that arrives while the material is still fresh is a cheap retrieval that buys little. An encounter that arrives after partial forgetting is an expensive retrieval that buys a lot — provided it still succeeds. **Spacing is a way of manufacturing difficulty in retrieval without manufacturing confusion.**

> **Worked example.** You have four hours for a topic and a fortnight before you need it. Massing means four hours on Saturday. You would finish knowing the material well — genuinely well, that evening. Spacing means one hour on four separate days. On the second day you would arrive having lost some of the first day and have to rebuild it, which feels like going backwards. **The rebuild is the mechanism.** The massed version optimises for how much you know at the end of the session. The spaced version optimises for how much you know at the end of the fortnight, and those are different quantities that people routinely confuse.

There is a limit worth stating, because "longer gaps are better" is not true without bound. If the gap is so long that retrieval **fails completely**, you get little — a failed retrieval that never resolves is close to a fresh encounter. The useful region is *hard but successful*, and finding it for yourself is part of what the six-week schedule in phase 5 asks you to reason about.

**This is why material you thought was finished reappears.** A day closed at S001 returns at S011 and again later, and it will feel like the programme has not noticed you already did it.

### 4. Interleaving: the version that feels worse and works better

**Blocked practice** means twenty problems of type A, then twenty of type B. **Interleaved practice** mixes them.

Blocked practice feels dramatically better and the reason is instructive. By your fifth problem of type A you have stopped deciding *which method this is* — they are all type A — and you are only executing. Execution gets faster. Accuracy within the block climbs. The session produces a clear, satisfying sense of a skill improving.

**That climb is the problem.** You have practised executing a method under conditions where the choice of method was given to you for free. **The real task almost never gives it to you for free.** A production incident does not arrive labelled *this is a concurrency problem*. A customer's complaint does not arrive labelled *this is a requirements problem, not a performance problem*. The hard part — and the part blocked practice never touches — is the discrimination.

Interleaving forces that discrimination on every single item. Performance during practice is worse and feels worse. Performance later, on a mixed set where nobody tells you the type, is better.

> **Worked example.** Two people learn to identify four failure modes. One studies them in four blocks. The other sees them shuffled. The blocked learner leaves the session able to describe all four crisply and would say they know them well. The interleaved learner leaves feeling muddled, having confused two of them repeatedly. **Hand both a real unlabelled failure a month later and it is generally the muddled one who classifies it correctly** — because confusing two things repeatedly is exactly the process by which you learn what distinguishes them.

**Almost nothing in this programme is blocked.** When a session feels scattered, when today's material seems to sit oddly against yesterday's, that is usually the design rather than a failure of it. **This is also the mechanism you are most likely to undo on your own**, by reorganising your private study into tidy blocks because the tidiness feels like control.

### 5. Generation and pretesting: failing first, deliberately

Phase 1 hands you five questions on material nobody has taught you. You will get most of them wrong. **That is the design, not a diagnostic and not a trick.**

The finding is genuinely odd: **attempting retrieval before instruction improves later retention of the tested material, even when every attempt fails.** Not "even when it's hard" — even when you get it wrong.

The leading explanation is that the failed attempt does something reading the answer first cannot. Trying and failing forces you to *notice which distinctions matter*. You discover you do not know whether the answer depends on one thing or another; you generate a wrong candidate and feel it not fit. When the correct answer then arrives, it lands as **a resolution to a question you actually held** rather than as a definition you were handed. A definition slides past. A resolution sticks, because there was a gap shaped like it.

The same logic runs at larger scale in phase 3. You get three real cases with the outcomes attached and the rationale removed, and you must explain the difference before you have the concepts that explain it. **Your account will be incomplete, and the cases are constructed so that the obvious account fails on the third one.** You are not expected to arrive at the canonical answer. You are expected to arrive at the point where you can feel that your answer does not cover the third case — and that feeling is what phase 4 then resolves.

**The common misreading:** *"So the point is that struggle is good."* The point is narrower. **The generation attempt must be followed by the canonical account, and reasonably soon.** Failure that is never resolved teaches very little and can teach the wrong thing. This is why phase 3 is always followed by phase 4 and never left hanging — a rule the programme applies to itself and states in [Learning Science Foundations](/wiki/program/02-Learning-Science-Foundations.md).

### 6. Cognitive load: the one thing that goes down

Every mechanism so far has made something harder. Here is the one that does not — and it is what stops all of this from collapsing into *make everything difficult*, which is not the claim and would be a bad design.

Working memory is small. Estimates vary and none of them are large. When a task requires holding many interacting elements at once, your capacity gets consumed by the *holding*, and there is nothing left over to do the learning with. **Difficulty that eats capacity without producing discrimination is not desirable difficulty. It is just difficulty.**

So the programme separates two things that are constantly confused, and the separation is the single most useful sentence on this page:

> **The programme raises difficulty in retrieval and selection, and lowers it in representation and attention.**

**Raised, deliberately.** You must produce answers rather than recognise them. You must decide what kind of problem you are looking at rather than being told. Material returns after you have partly lost it.

**Lowered, equally deliberately.** Worked examples rather than unguided flailing at a new procedure. Diagrams with their labels integrated, so you are not holding a legend in your head while reading a figure. Nothing decorative. No material split across two places that must be mentally joined. No unexplained jargon where a plain word exists.

> **Worked example of the distinction.** Being asked to recall the four mechanisms from memory is desirable difficulty — the effort is the learning. Being asked to read a diagram whose labels are in a separate table on another page is *undesirable* difficulty — the effort is pure overhead and buys nothing. **They feel similar from the inside.** Telling them apart is a skill, and one worth developing, because you will design things for other people to learn from.

**This is the sentence to have at two in the morning in week nine**, when the programme feels perverse and you are wondering whether the difficulty is the point or the problem. Ask which kind it is. If it is retrieval or selection, it is the design. If it is representation or attention, it is a defect — and this programme treats it as a defect and wants to hear about it.

### 7. The instrument in your hands is jagged

Now the second thesis. You are about to configure an AI coding agent, and you will use one for three years.

The intuition nearly everyone brings is that such a system is **good at easy things and struggles with hard ones**, so its reliability can be estimated from how hard a task looks. This intuition is wrong, and it is difficult to unlearn because it is so reasonable.

Capability is **jagged**. The boundary between what the system handles well and what it does not is uneven, and it does not follow human-perceived difficulty. Tasks that feel trivial to you can fall outside it. Tasks that feel demanding can sit comfortably inside. The frontier has a shape, but it is not the shape of your intuitions about effort.

**The consequence that matters is not that it fails. It is *how* it fails.** Outside the frontier you do not get an error, a refusal, or a visible breakdown. **You get a persuasive wrong answer, delivered in exactly the same confident register as the correct ones.** There is no signal in the output that distinguishes the two.

Put that together with the fluency illusion from section 1 and you have the day's real problem. **You now have two instruments that both report confidence unreliably: your own sense of understanding, and the agent's fluency.** Neither is evidence. The entire programme is built on your becoming able to say what each of them is wrong about.

> **Worked example.** You ask an agent to explain why three timetables produced different outcomes. It produces a fluent, well-organised, plausible account. You attach a documentation server to ground it, expecting accuracy to improve. It barely changes — because the documentation server contains vendor product documentation and the question is about learning science, so **there is nothing in the corpus to ground against.** The tool is not wrong here; it is *silent*, while the agent remains exactly as fluent. **Grounding makes an answer accurate within a corpus and silent outside it**, and nothing in the output tells you which side of that line you are on. You will run this exact experiment in phase 3, and most rooms do not predict the result.

**This programme asserts the direction and the mechanism, and no magnitude.** The finding is grounded at [research/04-professional-formation/dellacqua-jagged-technological-frontier.md](/research/04-professional-formation/dellacqua-jagged-technological-frontier.md), which is **a note written from an abstract rather than a full text**, and no number from it is usable here. You will be told this repeatedly and it is not pedantry: a claim's shape and a claim's size have different evidential requirements, and this repository has the shape and not the size.

Since the boundary cannot be worked out from an armchair, it must be **mapped**. Which is what you will spend three years doing, one frontier statement per build — *one thing it did faster than I could, one thing it did badly that I caught.*

**The common misreading:** *"So the agent is bad at hard things."* It is not ordered by difficulty at all. That is precisely why the frontier must be tested rather than assumed — **and why a tool that has never failed in front of you has not yet been tested.**

### 8. Defaults are opinions, and thresholds are policy

Before you type anything today, a series of decisions will already have been made on your behalf. Which model answers your question. Whether a tool you connected is actually switched on. When you are asked to approve an action and when you are not. Which files the system can reach, and which commands it can run.

**None of these is a law of nature.** Each is a product decision. Each is documented, if you go and look. Each is defensible — the defaults are mostly sensible, chosen by people who thought about it. And **you remain accountable for the behaviour that results from a default you never chose.**

The specific defaults for today's tools are enumerated in phase 4, and they are **perishable**: they belong to one version of one product and are re-verified before every offering of this programme. One of them changed during this design's own drafting, which is the example you get for why the register exists.

**The durable claim is the one to keep, and it generalises far past this tool:**

> An engineer who cannot state what was chosen for them, and why, does not understand their own system.

This is not a claim about AI tooling. It is a claim about compilers, frameworks, cloud services, database isolation levels, retry policies and every library you have ever imported. **The tool in front of you today is simply the most convenient place to acquire the habit**, because its defaults are unusually consequential and unusually well documented.

**One subtlety worth noticing now**, because it recurs all year: a stated restriction may bound one capability and not another. Today's agent can only touch files inside your open project — a real and reassuring restriction. **The terminal commands it runs are not bounded by that restriction at all**; they run with the permissions of the editor process. Two different boundaries, one of which is the one most people picture. **When you read a safety claim, read exactly what it is a claim about.**

## Phase 1 · Pretest and prediction (25 min)

You receive a short set of items on material nobody has taught you: two on program tracing, two on probability, one on measurement. You will get most of them wrong. **That is the point** — see [core idea 5](#5-generation-and-pretesting-failing-first-deliberately).

Before you attempt them, record two predictions:

- **P1.** How many of the five items will I get right?
- **P2.** Of the material taught today, how much will I be able to reproduce in six weeks?

Both predictions are sealed and stored. P2 is opened at S011.

**The Copilot prediction, sealed with the rest.** You also record, in two sentences:

- **P3.** Given the same five pretest items, how many will GitHub Copilot in agent mode get right — and **on which one will it be wrong, and why?**

P3 is checked at phase 8 against a live run. The first sentence is a number and is the easy part. **The second sentence is the day's real instrument, and most people will not attempt it** — which is itself the finding: **if you cannot say where a tool will fail, you are not yet in a position to use its output.** P3's cohort accuracy is the first entry in the programme's running frontier map.

**Warrant.** Pretesting: attempting retrieval before instruction improves later retention of the tested material, even when the attempt fails. Calibration: the prediction is the instrument that makes the later confrontation possible. Instrumentation rule 8 binds P3 to phase 1 — asked after phase 4 it would measure recall of what the day had already shown.

## Phase 2 · Cumulative retrieval set (20 min)

There is nothing to retrieve. This phase is replaced, once only in the programme, by a demonstration.

You are given a passage of technical prose, and the room is split into two conditions without being told the conditions differ:

- **Condition A** re-reads the passage three times.
- **Condition B** reads once, then attempts free recall twice with the passage face down.

Condition A reports higher confidence. Both conditions are told to expect a test at S003. The result is not revealed today.

**Warrant.** The testing effect combined with the metacognitive illusion that accompanies it. The delayed reveal is essential: revealed immediately, the re-reading group typically wins, which is exactly the trap.

---

## Phase 3 · Contrasting cases and generation (45 min)

**Three timetables, one question, and four executions of it.**

*First, 20 minutes, unchanged.* Your group is given three real timetables from three real programmes, with outcome data attached but the design rationale removed. You must produce an account of **why the outcomes differ**, and commit to it in writing.

The cases are constructed so that the naive account — more contact hours produces better outcomes — fails on the third case. You do not yet have the concepts of spacing, interleaving or retrieval practice, so you will generate something incomplete. **That is the design; see [core idea 5](#5-generation-and-pretesting-failing-first-deliberately).**

*Then, 15 minutes.* Your group runs **one** of four configurations of the same request — *account for the outcome difference across these three timetables* — in GitHub Copilot agent mode:

- **Case A.** Agent mode with no MCP server enabled and no instructions file.
- **Case B.** Agent mode with the Microsoft Learn MCP Server enabled.
- **Case C.** Agent mode with the Learn MCP Server enabled and an instructions file requiring every claim to carry a citation to a fetched source in the same sentence as the claim.
- **Case D.** Agent mode asked to *design a fourth timetable* that would outperform all three, rather than to explain them.

*Then, 10 minutes.* Groups report. The four agent outputs go on the board alongside your accounts, undifferentiated and unlabelled as to origin.

**The payload is case B, and it is the case the room does not predict.** Attaching a documentation server to the agent changes the answer very little, because learning science is not Microsoft documentation and the Learn MCP Server contains publicly available Microsoft Learn documentation and nothing else. The room's day-one expectation is that grounding makes an answer accurate. What it does is make an answer accurate **within a corpus**, and outside that corpus the tool is silent rather than wrong — while the agent remains exactly as fluent and exactly as confident. Case C is what makes this legible: an instruction requiring an in-sentence citation forces the absence into view, because there is nothing to cite.

Case D is included because it is the case most groups expect to fail and it does not obviously fail. The agent produces a defensible fourth timetable. Whether it is defensible *for the right reasons* is not answerable today and is not answered.

**Warrant.** Productive failure. The generation attempt fails to produce the canonical account and is not meant to; it produces the differentiated knowledge that makes the canonical account, when it arrives, land as a resolution rather than as a definition. Contrasting cases: four executions differing in exactly one configuration decision each, against one invariant question, is the configuration under which the effect of the decision becomes noticeable without being named.

## Phase 4 · Consolidating instruction (50 min)

The canonical account, delivered directly and efficiently, mapped explicitly onto what your group generated — naming which part of your account was right, which was incomplete, and which was the thing you could not have got to. **This is [core ideas 1 to 6](#the-core-ideas) delivered live; if you have read them, this phase is a second pass rather than a first.**

Content, in order:

1. **Retrieval practice.** Retrieving is a learning event, not a measurement event.
2. **Spacing.** Why the gap is the mechanism and not the cost.
3. **Interleaving.** Why mixed practice feels worse and transfers better.
4. **Generation and pretesting.** Why phase 1 was a set of questions they could not answer.
5. **The illusion.** Why fluency during study predicts almost nothing, and why their own sense of how well a session went is not evidence.
6. **Cognitive load.** The one thing the programme will *reduce* rather than increase, and why that is not a contradiction.

Then the reconciliation, stated plainly because it is the sentence you will need at 2 a.m. in week nine: **the programme raises difficulty in retrieval and selection, and lowers it in representation and attention.**

Then two points about the instrument, indexed to phase 3.

7. **The jagged frontier.** Capability is uneven in ways that are not predictable from how hard a task looks to a person, and a worker's intuition about where the tool will help — formed from human-difficulty priors — is an unreliable guide. The finding also runs in the unwelcome direction: on a task outside the frontier, assisted performance can fall *below* unassisted performance, because the failure mode is the adoption of a persuasive wrong answer rather than a visible breakdown. **This programme asserts the direction and the mechanism and no magnitude.** The single figure students are given is that they are about to spend three years locating this boundary empirically, because it cannot be located from the armchair. Source: [research/04-professional-formation/dellacqua-jagged-technological-frontier.md](/research/04-professional-formation/dellacqua-jagged-technological-frontier.md).

8. **Defaults are opinions and thresholds are policy.** Four decisions were made for the room before anyone in it typed anything, and all four are documented.

   - **The model was chosen by the tool.** The default `Auto` model selection lets Copilot choose a model for each step dynamically; choosing a specific model is what the documentation recommends when predictable steps matter. Nobody in the room chose a model, and the room's four cases may not have run on the same one.
   - **The MCP server's tools arrived switched off.** Tools contributed by an MCP server are not automatically enabled; their checkboxes are cleared by default and must be selected. Case A and case B differ by a checkbox.
   - **Connection is not invocation.** The Learn MCP Server's own getting-started documentation recommends writing an instructions file for the situation where *the agent does not use the tool when you expected it to*. A configured server is an available tool, not a used one.
   - **The approval boundary is not where you think it is.** Agent mode asks for confirmation before running a terminal command or a tool that is not built in. It can manipulate only files in the open solution directory and its subdirectories, and cannot reach files excluded from it — **but terminal commands run with the same permissions as the editor process and are not bounded by that restriction.** The file boundary and the command boundary are different boundaries, and only one of them is the one most people picture.

   **An engineer who cannot state what was chosen for them and why does not understand their own system.** Every one of these four is perishable. The sentence is not.

   *Perishable, marked as such:* the model-selection default, the tool-enablement default, the approval-mode default and the file-visibility rule are properties of one version of one product and are verified before each offering. *Durable:* that a platform has chosen for you, that the choice is defensible, and that you are still accountable for it.

**Warrant.** Cognitive load management. This phase is explicitly the low-load phase; worked, integrated, no split attention, no decorative material. Points 7 and 8 are placed after the reconciliation because both are claims about instruments, and the students' own study fluency is the instrument the preceding six points characterised. Scope rule, strong form: point 8's four defaults are safe as an account of the tool in front of the student today and unsafe as an account of any other version, which is stated in those words.

## Phase 5 · Whole-task learning task (90 min)

**The build: a configured repository, and a schedule produced with it.**

You design a six-week study schedule for a body of material you must actually learn — the Q1 material — and must justify every scheduling decision against the mechanisms just taught.

Constraints supplied:

- A fixed total of 40 study hours.
- Four topic areas with stated interdependence.
- A stated assessment date.
- A stated competing obligation in week three.

**The instrument is built first, in the first 30 minutes.** You produce a repository containing:

1. **An instructions file** at `.github/instructions/<name>.instructions.md` with an `applyTo` front-matter glob, stating the rules you want the agent to follow when drafting a schedule. Matching instructions are injected into every request automatically; no slash command invokes them.
2. **The Microsoft Learn MCP Server configured** at `https://learn.microsoft.com/api/mcp`. It requires no authentication and carries no charge, which is why everyone can hold one.
3. **A verification.** You send one prompt and inspect the GitHub Copilot Chat output channel to confirm the instructions actually appear in the request payload. The documentation calls this debug view the source of truth. **An instructions file you have not observed in a payload is a file, not a specification.**

The remaining 60 minutes produce the schedule, directed through the agent.

Deliverable: a schedule plus a one-page justification, plus one **falsifiable prediction** about their own performance that the schedule implies — and three further elements:

- **The prompt record.** What was asked, in order, and why — including the instructions abandoned. A schedule produced in one prompt and one produced in eleven are different engineering events and the record is what distinguishes them.
- **The intervention record.** Every correction made to the agent, and what it was. An empty record fails, unless the artefact shows the design could have caught an intervention-worthy error — which on this day means the verification step at item 3 above.
- **The frontier statement.** One thing the agent did faster than you could have, and one thing it did badly that you caught. Both specific to this build.

The schedule is real. You follow it. The prediction is checked at S011. The repository is kept and is the same repository you use on every subsequent day in the programme.

**Warrant.** Whole-task practice from the first day. This is a genuine instance of the target task class — decide under constraint and justify — at class-1 difficulty because the method is given. The instructions file is a specification in executable form, and the programme assesses specifications from S006; introducing the object before the word costs nothing and means that S006 has something to name rather than something to define.

## Phase 6 · Error clinic (40 min)

The programme's first error clinic, and the one that establishes whether the mechanism is real.

Format: the instructor goes first, and describes a specific, genuine, non-trivial error of their own — a design decision in this programme that produced a bad outcome for a previous cohort, named concretely and without self-protective framing.

Then, voluntarily, you describe a study habit you now believe is ineffective and why you kept it. **Nothing said here is recorded, referenced, or transmitted to any assessment process.** That protection is stated explicitly, in these words, and it is stated again at every subsequent error clinic.

**The agent case, third and last.** The instructor shows one output from a phase 3 case — selected in advance, from a previous cohort or from staff preparation — in which the agent stated something confidently and wrongly about the timetable data, and in which the group that ran it reported the output to the room without catching it. The analysis is not of the agent. **It is of why review did not catch it**, and the answer is always the same shape: the output was well organised, it used the vocabulary the reader was expecting, and nothing in its form marked the false sentence as different from the true ones. That is the mechanism behind the jagged-frontier result at phase 4 point 7, seen once, on day one, in the room's own work.

**Warrant.** Error management training and psychological safety. Modelling by the higher-status participant is the precondition; the disclosure protection is the mechanism. The agent case is placed last so that the room has already established that error is safe to look at before the first error they are asked to look at is one they made an hour ago.

See [research/04-professional-formation/](/research/04-professional-formation/collected-materials.md).

---

## Phase 7 · Peer articulation (30 min)

Pairs. You explain one mechanism from phase 4 to a partner, who then attempts to **break it** — to construct a case where the mechanism would predict badly.

At least three pairs report a break attempt to the room. Two of these will be genuine objections. **The instructor concedes the genuine ones rather than defending them**, and adds them to the programme's open-questions list, which you can see.

**Warrant.** The interactive tier of the ICAP framework — knowledge is constructed in the exchange, not transmitted. And a demonstration, on day one, of what the programme means by conceding rather than hedging.

---

## Phase 8 · Calibration and commitment (20 min)

- Predictions P1 and P2 are compared against the day's actual phase-1 performance. P1 is almost always an overestimate.
- **P3 is resolved.** The five pretest items are run through the agent live, in front of the room. The count is compared against the cohort's predicted counts, and — the part that matters — the *named failure* is compared against the actual one. The two rarely coincide. The distribution is recorded as the first point on the cohort's frontier map, and the map's reader is stated: next year's cohort.
- You record what you will now do differently.
- You receive your expanding-interval retrieval schedule for the Q1 material, with the first repetition set for the following morning.
- The phase-2 condition assignment is revealed but the outcome is not.

## Between-day protocol

- **Next morning:** first retrieval repetition, 10 minutes, on the phase-4 mechanisms.
- **Day 3:** second repetition, interleaved with CF101's first problem set.
- **Day 5:** written reflection, minimum one sleep cycle after the second repetition, on one thing from the day you now think is wrong.
- **Ongoing:** the self-designed schedule from phase 5 is in force.

## Assessment

None for entrustment. One specification item in PE101: the phase-5 artefact, assessed on whether the justification is sound against the taught mechanisms, never on whether the schedule was followed.

Checked, in binary:

1. The schedule and its one-page justification against the taught mechanisms.
2. The falsifiable prediction about your own performance.
3. The repository: an instructions file with an `applyTo` glob, a configured Learn MCP Server, and the payload verification showing the instructions were injected.
4. **The prompt record** — instructions given, in order, with reasons, including abandoned ones.
5. **The intervention record** — every correction made to the agent. An empty record fails unless item 3's verification is present and shows the design could have detected an intervention-worthy error.
6. **The frontier statement** — one thing the agent did faster than you could, one thing it did badly that you caught, both specific to this build.

**Nothing else is assessed.** In particular the quality of the agent's output is not assessed, the number of prompts is not assessed, and no judgement is made about whether you should have used the agent more or less.

*Annotation at the point of change.* The [Assessment Architecture](/wiki/program/03-Assessment-Architecture.md) records that specifications for S001 to S040 are not retrospectively amended and do not carry the closed-list sentence. That boundary stands for everything above item 3. Items 3 to 6 did not exist when the boundary was drawn, and a new element class introduced after a rule exists is governed by the rule. The pre-existing items are listed here for completeness and their standard is unchanged.

**Durable — assessed.** That an instrument is characterised before its outputs are trusted. That fluency is not evidence, whether the fluency is a student's own or a tool's. That capability is uneven in ways not predictable from perceived task difficulty. That a platform makes choices for you and you remain accountable for them. That a specification whose conformance is checked by execution is still a specification.

**Platform instance — not assessed.** GitHub Copilot agent mode as it behaves in the version installed this term; the `Auto` model-selection default; the cleared-by-default state of MCP tool checkboxes; the approval boundary and the file-visibility rule; the `.github/instructions/*.instructions.md` path and the `applyTo` front-matter key; the Learn MCP Server endpoint, its three tool names and the absence of authentication. **What this register cost.** Before the platform anchor, this day carried no perishable content at all and was the only day in the programme of which that was true — a fact worth telling students, and now false. The trade is recorded rather than quietly dropped: the day bought an operable instrument and a checkable specification, and it paid with the one page in the wiki that needed no re-verification.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The assessed work for this day is listed under [Assessment](#assessment) and is unchanged. These are for you, alone, with the page shut where it says so.

**1. Test the illusion on yourself.** Pick a page of technical material you have not read. Read it twice. Then close it and write down everything you can recall. Now score yourself against the source. Before you look — write your predicted score. **The gap between your prediction and your score is the fluency illusion, measured on you.** Keep the number; you will want it in week nine.

**2. Find the blocked practice in your own habits.** Write down how you last prepared for something that mattered. Was it blocked or interleaved? Massed or spaced? Re-reading or retrieval? No judgement — most people's honest answer is blocked, massed and re-reading, because all three feel the best.

**3. Predict the frontier before you test it.** Write down three tasks you are confident an AI agent will do well and three you are confident it will do badly. Rank all six by *how hard they feel to you*. Then run them. **The exercise works whether or not your predictions hold** — if the ranking by human difficulty predicted the outcome, say so; that is a finding about your six tasks, not about the tool.

**4. Name a default you did not set.** Open any tool you used this week. Find one setting you never chose that changed what it did. State what it decided on your behalf, and whether you would have chosen the same. *Repeat this until it becomes automatic; it is the habit behind half of what this programme teaches.*

**5. Write the sentence.** In your own words, without copying from above, explain to somebody who has not read this page why a study session that feels bad might be the better session. **If you cannot do it without looking, you have just demonstrated the day's thesis.**

## What to take from this day

**The claim to hold.** **An instrument is characterised before its outputs are trusted — and you are one of the instruments.** Your sense that you have understood something is a reading, taken with a device that is known to be optimistic about itself. So is the agent's fluency. Neither is evidence, and the whole programme is built on your being able to say what each of them is wrong about.

**What the sources license, and what they do not.** The psychological-safety and error-management material behind phase 6 is verified in this repository and supports **direction and mechanism only** — that disclosure is a condition of learning from error, not how much. The jagged-frontier claim at phase 4 is grounded in a note written from an abstract rather than a full text, which is recorded at [research/04-professional-formation/dellacqua-jagged-technological-frontier.md](/research/04-professional-formation/dellacqua-jagged-technological-frontier.md); **the shape of the finding is usable and no number from it is, and this day asserts none.** *Defaults are opinions* is this repository's own synthesis of vendor documentation, not something a vendor claims.

**Guidance for your own work.**

- **Write the prediction before you look.** What will the agent produce, and where will it be wrong? A prediction made after seeing the output is a memory of agreeing with it.
- **Locate the frontier by testing, not by guessing from difficulty.** Capability is uneven in ways that do not track how hard a task feels to you.
- **Name one default you did not set** in anything you ship this week, and say what it decided on your behalf.
- **Classify every conclusion you draw about a tool** into the five classes on the [Agent Configuration Standard](/wiki/program/13-Agent-Configuration-Standard.md) — configured control, executable check, observed result, outcome evidence, unsupported inference. Today almost everything you can honestly say is in the first three, and noticing that is the point of starting here.

**Where this is easy to get wrong.** Reading the frontier as *the agent is bad at hard things*. It is not ordered by difficulty, which is precisely why it has to be mapped rather than assumed — and why a tool that has never failed in front of you has not yet been tested.

## Further reading

**Start here, in this repository.** [Learning Science Foundations](/wiki/program/02-Learning-Science-Foundations.md) is the full treatment of every mechanism summarised above — fifteen of them, each with its design consequence and the state of its evidence. **It is the single most useful page in the programme for a student who wants to know why any of this is done.**

**On the instrument.** [research/04-professional-formation/dellacqua-jagged-technological-frontier.md](/research/04-professional-formation/dellacqua-jagged-technological-frontier.md) for the jagged frontier, and [research/04-professional-formation/metr-ai-assisted-productivity-study.md](/research/04-professional-formation/metr-ai-assisted-productivity-study.md) for the finding that matters most on this day: experienced developers can be **slowed** by AI assistance while believing they were sped up. That is the fluency illusion and the jagged frontier arriving in the same result. **Read the verification status in both notes before quoting either** — one rests on an abstract.

**On failing first.** [research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md) explains why phase 3 asks you to produce an account you do not yet have the concepts for. Note its own boundary conditions — the effect reverses in some populations, and the note says so.

**On error and disclosure.** [research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md](/research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md) for why the programme treats an error as material rather than as a fault. **Two limits are recorded in that note and matter:** every effect is relative to another training design rather than to no training, and nothing in it reaches on-the-job performance.

**On what your own conclusions are worth.** [Agent Configuration Standard](/wiki/program/13-Agent-Configuration-Standard.md) — the five classes of conclusion. Today almost everything you can honestly say about your agent falls in the first three, and noticing that is the point of starting here.

**A caution about all of the above.** Every claim in this programme carries a recorded verification depth, and several of the sources behind this day were read only as abstracts. **No effect size is asserted anywhere in this wiki for an unverified source, including on this page.** Direction and mechanism only — and if you find a magnitude quoted here, it is a defect and should be reported.
