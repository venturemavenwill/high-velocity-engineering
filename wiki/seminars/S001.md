## The situation

You have been asked to help. Somebody has described what they want built, and they were specific about it. There is a date, a budget of sorts, and a room of people who expect you to start.

Nothing in that description tells you whether the work is worth doing, whether it can be done on the platform they have named, or whether the person describing it is the person who has the problem.

**Answer these in writing before reading further.** They are not rhetorical. You will be held to your answers at [S005](/wiki/seminars/S005.md), and item 2 is the one this session is actually about.

1. A customer describes a solution in unusual technical detail. What does that specificity tell you?
2. What would have to be true for the right answer to be **do not build this**?
3. Who in the room loses if this succeeds?
4. The brief assumes a platform capability exists. How would you check, in under ten minutes, without asking the customer?
5. What could you learn today that would stop the engagement?

Most people answer 1, 3 and 4 readily and produce nothing concrete for 2. That gap is the finding, and it is why this programme opens on qualification rather than on delivery.

## What you must produce

**An engagement position** — one page, four parts:

1. **The problem**, stated as something a named person experiences, not as a technology.
2. **The feasibility check** — one claim in the brief that you tested against first-party documentation, what you found, and the date you checked.
3. **What you would need to learn** to be confident, naming a person or an observation rather than a document.
4. **A disqualifying condition** — the specific thing that, if true, would stop the work.

## The material

### Why qualification is the highest-leverage act, and why almost nobody writes it down

Forward-deployed engineering places engineers inside the customer's context rather than behind a product boundary. The role's own definitional literature is blunt that this is a different job from product engineering: the practitioner writing at Palantir describes a division between engineers who build the platform and engineers who deploy it against a specific customer problem, with the second group's productivity depending on how much of the first group's work they can lean on ([research/05-fde-craft/palantir-fde-practitioner-writing.md](/research/05-fde-craft/palantir-fde-practitioner-writing.md)).

**That note carries a warning worth taking seriously before you use anything in it: it is a recruiting artefact and says so in its first and last lines.** Its account of the role is the company's own, written to attract applicants. Read it for the structural distinction — platform work versus deployment work — and not as evidence about outcomes.

Now the uncomfortable part. This programme's largest single source is a body of internal practice material from a customer-embedded engineering organisation inside a large technology vendor, recorded in this repository as an original paraphrase at [research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md). It is experienced, detailed, and in places genuinely wise.

**It contains no worked example of declining an engagement.** Its case studies all succeed. Its single failure narrative is a synthetic composite, fictional in its particulars by its own admission. It states no exclusion criteria — no domain limit, no scale limit, no regulatory limit, and no description of a customer who would be unsuitable. Its readiness rubric is composed almost entirely of customer dispositions.

There is one recorded refusal in the whole corpus: a proposal to replace human reviewers with AI in a legal-review process was judged irresponsible, because those reviewers catch issues with legal consequences. That refusal is real, it is correct, and it is a single instance.

**So this stage is written past its source.** That is a class 3 claim — design reasoning with no external warrant — and it is stated in every session in the stage. The argument is that a practice which cannot refuse work cannot be said to exercise judgement. It is an argument, not a finding.

### The customer's account of the problem is weak evidence

The interviewing literature is unambiguous that asking people about their problems produces unreliable data, for a reason that has nothing to do with honesty. Fitzpatrick's central claim in *The Mom Test* is that people will lie to you — not maliciously, but because you asked a question whose answer they can see would please you ([research/05-fde-craft/fitzpatrick-the-mom-test.md](/research/05-fde-craft/fitzpatrick-the-mom-test.md)).

The repair is to ask about **past behaviour rather than future intention**, and about **specifics rather than generalities**. "Would you use a tool that did X?" is a question about a hypothetical self. "Walk me through the last time this went wrong" is a question about an event that either happened or did not.

The same note records three categories of bad conversational data and a repair for each: **compliments** (deflect and return to specifics), **generalities** (anchor to a concrete past episode), and **unbidden feature requests** (dig for the motivation beneath the request rather than accepting or refusing it). You will meet all three in your first hour of any engagement.

**Two limits on that source.** The book is a practitioner text, not a study; it reports no measurement of any kind. And the local copy this repository read carries no publisher, year or edition, so it cannot be cited by edition — the note records that explicitly.

### Specificity is a signal, not a specification

A request stated with unusual precision means thinking has already happened somewhere you cannot see. Someone chose that architecture. Someone rejected alternatives. The brief you received is the *output* of a decision process you were not part of, and the interesting content is the process rather than the output.

The practitioner judgement recorded in the accelerated-delivery corpus puts it directly: a request stated with unusual specificity signals that thinking has happened elsewhere, and the correct response is to ask what is driving it rather than to accept it at face value.

The related failure is more common and harder to see: **customers routinely arrive having chosen a solution without ever having articulated a problem**, and the real difficulty is often mentioned once, in passing, in the middle of a long conversation about something else. Some customers have genuinely lost track of what they were solving, because it moved across handoffs or because they moved on from it. Noticing the buried problem is the job.

### The platform decision has three axes, and you can check them today

Here is where qualification stops being a conversation and becomes engineering work.

Most briefs contain at least one assumed platform capability. The customer believes a thing is possible, or cheap, or available in their region, or compatible with their compliance posture. **That belief is checkable, and checking it is qualification.**

Microsoft's own Cloud Adoption Framework guidance on agent technology states the selection criteria plainly as **"your organization's technical capabilities, timeline, and control requirements"** ([research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md)). Capability, speed, control. That triple is the actual decision, and it generalises well beyond agent-building.

The same documentation family splits the build paths by service model: **Microsoft Foundry** as platform-as-a-service supporting both declarative and pro-code development with your choice of models; **Copilot Studio** as software-as-a-service for low-code development by business teams; and **GPUs and containers** as infrastructure-as-a-service for maximum flexibility.

Two published facts from that family are directly useful in qualification, and both are the kind of thing a brief silently assumes away:

- **Foundry's basic setup "lacks network isolation"** and is described as being for rapid prototyping when speed and ease of access are the priorities; the standard setup provides fine-grained control over data, security and networking. **Prototyping topology and production topology are different things, and the transition between them is a deliberate act** — not a configuration detail discovered at the end.
- **Copilot Studio is "designed for small-scale deployments, such as department-level solutions."** That is a scale claim published by the vendor about its own product. A brief that assumes enterprise-wide rollout on that tool is outside the design envelope, and the symptoms show up as governance and lifecycle problems rather than as functional failures.

**This is unusually candid vendor documentation and should be read closely for that reason.** It is also first-party product documentation with a selection-guidance purpose: its authority is definitional. It offers no adoption data, no failure-rate data, no cost comparisons and no evidence that its recommended mappings produce better outcomes. **Treat it as a well-constructed decision framework, not as validated findings.**

### Your instrument is unreliable in a specific direction

You will check that capability using an AI agent, and you need one warning before you do.

Capability in these systems is **jagged** — the boundary between what they handle well and what they do not is uneven and does not follow how hard a task feels to a person. Tasks that seem trivial can fall outside it; tasks that seem demanding can sit comfortably inside ([research/04-professional-formation/dellacqua-jagged-technological-frontier.md](/research/04-professional-formation/dellacqua-jagged-technological-frontier.md)).

**What matters is not that it fails but how.** Outside that boundary you do not get an error or a refusal. You get a persuasive wrong answer in the same confident register as the correct ones.

A separate study found experienced developers were **slowed** by AI assistance while believing they had been sped up ([research/04-professional-formation/metr-ai-assisted-productivity-study.md](/research/04-professional-formation/metr-ai-assisted-productivity-study.md)). Perceived and actual effect diverged, in the same direction as the confidence problem above.

**Both notes carry recorded limits.** The jagged-frontier note was written from an abstract rather than a full text, so the shape of the finding is usable and no number in it is. **This programme asserts direction and mechanism and never a magnitude**, here or anywhere.

The practical consequence for today: an agent that agrees with your engagement position has not been used as an instrument. Ask it to argue against you.

## The workflow

**Test one feasibility claim against first-party documentation, in under ten minutes, without asking the customer.**

You will use GitHub Copilot in agent mode with the Microsoft Learn MCP Server attached. The server is a remote streamable-HTTP endpoint, requires no authentication and carries no charge, which is why it is the first tool this programme uses — every learner can hold one regardless of licensing.

**Step 1 — configure the server.** Add the Learn MCP Server to your editor's MCP configuration. It exposes documentation search, documentation fetch, and code-sample search.

**Step 2 — confirm the agent will actually use it.** Attaching a server is not the same as invoking its tools, and when it goes wrong it goes wrong **silently**. The vendor's walkthroughs include *allow the agent to use the MCP server* as its own user step, and declining that trust means chat requests simply continue without the tools — no error, no notice. The server's own guidance opens its recommended-instructions section with the case *when the AI agent doesn't use the MCP tool when expected*, and its repository records that even tool-friendly models sometimes fail to call MCP tools by default. **A connected server is an available tool, not a used one.**

**Step 3 — write an instructions file that forces the absence into view.** Create `.github/instructions/qualify.instructions.md` with an `applyTo` glob covering your working folder:

```markdown
---
applyTo: "**/*.md"
---

# Feasibility checking

- Every claim about a platform capability MUST carry a citation to a fetched
  documentation page, in the same sentence as the claim.
- If no fetched source supports a claim, say "not found in the documentation"
  rather than answering from general knowledge.
- Always record the date the page was fetched.
```

**Step 4 — verify the instructions actually arrived.** Send one prompt, then inspect the Copilot Chat output channel and confirm the instruction text appears in the request payload. **An instructions file you have not observed in a payload is a file, not a specification.**

**Step 5 — run the check.** Ask the agent your feasibility question. Something like: *does the tool named in this brief support the deployment scale the brief assumes, and where is that stated?*

**Step 6 — read what came back for the thing that is not there.** The in-sentence citation requirement from step 3 is what makes the absence visible. If the corpus does not cover your question, a well-behaved agent now says so instead of producing a fluent paragraph. **Grounding makes an answer accurate within a corpus and silent outside it, and nothing in an ungrounded output tells you which side of that line you are on.**

**Step 7 — attack your own position.** Ask the agent to argue that the engagement should be declined. Record what you asked, what you kept and what you rejected.

## What good looks like

- The problem is stated as something a named person experiences, **not as a technology**.
- The feasibility check names a page, a finding and a date — or records honestly that the documentation does not settle it.
- At least one stated condition is genuinely disqualifying: it would stop the work, not merely complicate it.
- The learning plan names a person or an observation, not a document.
- **The position is falsifiable.** You can say what evidence would change it.
- The prompt record shows at least one instruction you abandoned.

## How it goes wrong

- **The solved problem** — the customer arrives having chosen the solution and never articulated the problem. *Warning sign:* the request names a product before it names a difficulty.
- **The buried problem** — the real difficulty is mentioned once, in passing, inside a conversation about something else. *Warning sign:* you leave the room with a clear brief and no surprise.
- **The absent problem** — the customer has genuinely lost track of what they were solving. *Warning sign:* nobody present can say who first raised it.
- **The unfalsifiable position** — you write a position no evidence could disturb. *Warning sign:* your disqualifying condition is something that could never occur.
- **The agreeing instrument** — you ask the agent to review your position and it approves. *Warning sign:* you did not ask it to argue the opposite.
- **The ungrounded answer accepted** — the agent answers a documentation question from general knowledge and you cannot tell. *Warning sign:* no citation, no date, and you did not notice either.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The assessed artefact is the engagement position, checked at [S005](/wiki/seminars/S005.md).

**1 · Rewrite three questions.** Take these and rewrite each to ask about past behaviour rather than future intention:

- *Would you use an agent that summarised these reports?*
- *Is this process painful?*
- *Do you think your team would adopt this?*

> **Model answers.** *When did you last need a summary of one of these reports — walk me through what you did.* · *Tell me about the last time this process went wrong.* · *What was the last tool this team was asked to adopt, and what happened?* The test is whether the answer is a memory or a prediction.

**2 · Find the scale claim.** Using the workflow above, find where the documentation states the intended deployment scale for one Microsoft agent-building tool. Record the page and the date.

> **What you should find.** The comparison guidance states that Copilot Studio is designed for small-scale deployments such as department-level solutions. **If you cannot find a scale statement for the tool you chose, that absence is the finding** — record it as an open question rather than inferring a limit.

**3 · Go back to your own item 2, then break it.** Find the answer you wrote to opening item 2 before you read anything. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it side by side with the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then write your condition properly, and write the scenario in which it fires. If you cannot write the scenario, the condition is decoration.

> **Criterion.** A condition passes if a reader can describe an observation that would trigger it. "If the customer is not committed" fails. "If no named person in operations will give two hours a week for six weeks" passes.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Run the agent twice.** Ask it once to improve your position, once to destroy it. Compare the two outputs for length, confidence and specificity.

> **What to notice.** The destructive run is usually more specific. A model asked to improve something tends to add; asked to attack it, it must locate a weakness, and locating is a harder operation than adding.

**5 · Name the three axes.** From memory, closed book: what are the three selection criteria the Cloud Adoption Framework names for choosing a build path?

> **Answer.** Technical capabilities, timeline, and control requirements.
>
> **Then compare.** Look back at your answer to opening item 4 — how you said you would check a platform assumption. Which of the three axes did you not think to check? That omission is more useful to you than the recall was.

## What this does not establish

**Nothing here shows that qualification improves outcomes.** No source behind this programme measures it. The practice corpus refuses work once and reports no engagement it declined; it publishes no outcome, evaluation, comparison or measurement of any kind.

**The platform facts are perishable and the framework is not validated.** The Microsoft guidance is first-party documentation with a selection purpose. Its authority is definitional — it tells you what the products are, not that choosing them this way works. It offers no adoption data and no failure-rate data.

**The instrument warnings are direction-only.** The jagged-frontier note rests on an abstract. No magnitude from it is usable and none is asserted.

**This stage is class 3 throughout** — design reasoning with no external warrant. It is included because a practice that cannot say no cannot be said to have judgement. Hold that as an argument.
