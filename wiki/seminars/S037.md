# S037 · The Prototype That Fails Immediately

**Platform anchor:** Microsoft Agent Framework evaluations run before the build, on purpose, so the first result is a failure with a cause

## The situation

You built the cheap version. You showed it. It broke in front of the sponsor.

Two people in the room now hold different accounts of what just happened. You think the exercise worked. They think it went badly. **Reconciling those two accounts is your job, it is not automatic, and nobody will do it for you.**

There is a version of this session in which you avoid the problem by making sure nothing breaks. That version is available to you, it takes about an hour, and it is the failure this day exists to prevent.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught. Nobody is going to help you with them, and that is deliberate.

1. Your prototype fails on the first real case. Write the sentence you say next.
2. Write what the sponsor is thinking while you say it.
3. What did the failure remove from the plan? Be specific — a design option, a cost, a week, an assumption.
4. You could fix it in an hour before the meeting. Give the reason not to.
5. A failure that costs a fortnight and a failure that costs an afternoon are both failures. What is different about how you report them?

**Item 2 is the session.** Most learners produce a good sentence for item 1 and have never thought about who is receiving it. A sentence written for an audience you have not modelled is a sentence written for yourself.

## What you must produce

1. **The design** — what you built the artefact to break on, chosen from your [S032](/wiki/seminars/S032.md) causes, and what you expected to happen.
2. **The instrument** — the evaluation you wrote **before** the artefact existed: the cases, the criteria, the split strategy, the threshold and its justification, and what difference the run could have detected.
3. **The break** — what actually happened, dated, with the case it happened on and who chose that case.
4. **The criteria diff** — your sealed pre-build criteria beside the ones you hold after looking at the outputs, with a reason for every change.
5. **The account** — what you said, to whom, and what they concluded. Including the cases where they concluded the wrong thing.

## The material

### Two accounts of the same event, and only one of them arrives for free

The engineer's account is that a cheap artefact removed an expensive option from the plan. The sponsor's account is that the thing does not work. **Neither is a misunderstanding**; they are different frames applied to the same event, and the sponsor's is the one that requires no effort, because in almost every other professional context a demonstration that fails is a demonstration that failed.

The practice corpus behind this stage states the claim in a line — a cheap prototype that fails immediately is to be treated as an instant win — and then adds the clause that makes it a skill rather than an attitude: **stakeholders are to be actively coached into reading it that way rather than as a setback** ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md)). The clause concedes that the reading is not natural, and it puts the conversion on the engineer, which is where it belongs — you are the only person in the room who knows what the failure cost.

**That corpus is access-controlled, is never quoted or named here, and has no evidence base of any kind.** It reports no engagement that went badly, so it cannot supply a single case in which the coaching was refused. **The framing is practitioner judgement. Its refusal rate is unknown because its refusals are unrecorded.**

The price is what makes the framing honest rather than rhetorical. Three things have to be in the sentence you say next: **what broke**, **what that removes from the plan** in terms the listener holds, and **what it cost to find out.** A failure reported without its price is just a failure, and a fortnight's work reported in the register of an afternoon's experiment is a lie told with correct facts.

### Evaluation written before the build is the engineering form of failing first

Here is the move that turns a disposition into a workflow. **You write the evaluation before the thing it evaluates exists**, and you run it first, so that the first result you ever see is a failure with a named cause rather than a demonstration that went well.

The Agent Framework's evaluation model is small enough to hold in your head, which is the reason it is usable this way. Three types: **`EvalItem`**, "a single item to evaluate — wraps the full conversation and derives query/response via a split strategy"; **`Evaluator`**, "a provider that scores items"; and **`EvalResults`**, aggregated pass/fail counts with per-item detail ([research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-framework-and-evaluations-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-framework-and-evaluations-docs.md)). The documentation's stated design principles include **"zero friction"**, and that is not marketing: the practical barrier to routine evaluation is setup cost, and **a harness that takes a day to stand up does not get used during development.** An evaluation that is not used during development is a release gate, and a release gate cannot produce a failure early enough to be cheap.

The two evaluator tiers matter for the same reason. **`LocalEvaluator`** "runs checks locally without API calls — ideal for inner-loop development, CI smoke tests, and fast iteration". **`FoundryEvals`** connects to the cloud service for LLM-as-judge scoring, defaulting to relevance, coherence and task adherence, and **automatically adding tool call accuracy when items contain tool definitions.** The two can be mixed in one run. **Defaults are opinions**, and that default says an agent with tools acquires an obligation an agent without them does not.

One decision in that API silently determines what every number you produce means, and the documentation says so plainly: **"Multi-turn conversations must be split into query and response halves for evaluation. How you split determines *what you're evaluating*."** Three strategies are provided — **last turn**, the default, for response quality at a point; **full**, treating the first user message as the query and everything after as the response, for task completion and overall trajectory; and **per-turn**, scoring each exchange independently with cumulative context. **An agent that recovers well from a bad first attempt scores well under full and badly under per-turn.** Neither score is wrong. They answer different questions, and a team that never chose is reporting an answer to a question it did not ask. **State the split strategy every single time you report a number.**

The tool-usage evaluators are the vocabulary this stage has been missing. Five distinct checks — `tool_selection`, `tool_input_accuracy`, `tool_call_success`, `tool_output_utilization` and the composite `tool_call_accuracy` — decompose what a single *did the tool work* check would conflate: choosing the wrong tool, calling the right tool with wrong arguments, the call failing technically, and ignoring or misreading a successful result. **Two of those produce a system that reports success and is wrong**, which is exactly the failure class you cannot see from the outside and exactly the class your sponsor will trust.

Two further details are worth taking. **`task_navigation_efficiency` takes `actions` and `expected_actions` and requires no model parameter** — it is the one deterministic agent-behaviour check in the set, and the only one whose result does not depend on a judge. And **repetitions** exist "to detect non-deterministic behavior", which is the documentation conceding what your sponsor will not: **one sample from a stochastic system is not a measurement.**

**The limits here are severe and must travel with every use.** This is first-party documentation, definitional rather than empirical. **No agreement rate with human judgement is published for any evaluator, no calibration data, and no rationale for where the default thresholds came from.** Most of these evaluators are LLM-as-judge; most agent evaluators are marked **preview**; one consulted page is a superseded **classic** variant; and the pages were read through search excerpts rather than full fetches. **Assert no evaluator's agreement with human judgement. None is published, and this programme states none anywhere.** The threshold is a policy choice separable from the measurement — move it and the pass rate changes while the system does not — so **a threshold must be justified, never inherited.**

### The criteria you write before the build will be wrong, and that is the deliverable

The obvious objection to writing the evaluation first is that you cannot know what to measure until you have seen what the thing produces. **That objection is correct**, it has a name, and the name is the reason the exercise is structured as it is.

Shankar and colleagues call it **criteria drift**: "users need criteria to grade outputs, but grading outputs helps users define criteria" ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/shankar-evalgen.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/shankar-evalgen.md)). It is a genuine circularity rather than a workflow inefficiency. The same work reports that some criteria appear **dependent on the specific outputs observed** rather than independently definable in advance, which "raises serious questions for approaches that assume the independence of evaluation from observation of model outputs".

So the pre-build criteria are not a prediction you are expected to get right. **They are the artefact against which your drift becomes visible**, and the diff between the sealed version and the version you hold after reading outputs is the most informative page you will produce today. A learner whose criteria did not move either wrote them so vaguely that no output could disturb them, or did not read the outputs.

**Two limits.** The evidence for criteria drift is **qualitative, from a small study of a research prototype**; it establishes that the phenomenon is real and consequential and does not quantify it. And it creates a problem it does not solve: if criteria are revised after seeing outputs, **those same outputs can no longer serve cleanly as a held-out set.** Freeze and hold out fresh cases before any claim you intend somebody to act on.

The practitioner literature supplies the ladder this sits on. Husain's three levels — cheap assertions, human and model review, live experimentation — are ordered by cost, and the ordering dictates cadence ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/husain-your-ai-product-needs-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/husain-your-ai-product-needs-evals.md)). Two of his rules bear directly on today. **"Pass rate is a product decision"** — importing the all-green convention from software unit testing is a category error, and today you want a red result. And an LLM judge is **"a meta-problem within your larger problem"** requiring its own mini-evaluation, validated against your own labels and reported as precision and recall, because **raw agreement is misleading when classes are imbalanced.** Given that the vendor publishes no agreement data at all, that validation is not optional decoration; it is the only source of the number. **The limits: a single case study at one company, no control condition, no measured improvement, and tooling recommendations dated to early 2024.**

### A failure is only informative if the instrument could have caught it

An evaluation is an experiment, and it inherits every limit of one. The part that bears hardest today is **power**: doing the analysis before the run turns *we tested and found nothing* into a statement about the system rather than about the test ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md)). **A run that could not have detected the difference it was looking for has established nothing, and nothing in the output will tell you that.**

Two further disciplines transfer directly. **Compare paired rather than separately** where two variants meet the same cases, because they agree about which cases are hard and the pairing removes that shared variation for free. And **never suppress variance by changing a setting whose purpose is something else** — turning the sampling temperature down to make a demonstration look cleaner changes the thing you were measuring. **That source is analytical rather than empirical, its illustrative magnitudes rest on stylised assumptions, and no number from it appears here.**

### What this session is doing to you, and the condition that makes it work

You are being run through the professional form of this programme's own teaching design, and it is worth naming because the mechanism is the same one you are about to use on a sponsor.

The design puts the attempt before the explanation deliberately. Its author's summary is **"Fail First, Learn Later"**, and its central mechanism is the failed generation effect — attempting an answer before instruction improves later learning *even when the attempt is wrong* ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Two of its stated conditions bind this session. **Scaffolding the generation phase backfires**: manipulating guidance during the problem-solving phase reduced the failure rate and reduced the learning with it, which is why nobody stopped you building the wrong thing. And **the consolidation phase must explicitly prompt comparison**: presenting a correct answer beside a learner's wrong one achieved nothing on its own, and only the condition instructed to compare them learned more. **Doing the comparison in your head does not count**, and that is the source's finding rather than this programme's preference.

**Its evidence is school, university and laboratory evidence, and the author's claim that the cycle works in corporate and adult settings appears in his own voice with nothing in his reference list behind it.** Its use here is an extrapolation. He also concedes that designed failure is **sufficient but not necessary** for the awareness it produces.

The independent convergence comes from error-management training, and it carries the boundary condition that explains why you built the harness first. Learners should meet the difficulty before the resolution, and the payoff shows on transfer rather than during the session — the advantage is **absent while training and present afterwards** ([research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md](/research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md)). But the method **assumes the task answers back.** Where the task's own feedback cannot be interpreted, the effect was not significant, and the authors describe that boundary as receiving **only limited support**, on the variable with the weakest agreement in their own coding.

**An agent is an unclear-feedback task by default.** It completes a run and reports success while having selected the wrong tool, passed a malformed argument, or ignored what the tool returned. **The evaluation harness is the instrumented feedback path that makes the whole error-management rationale apply to this exercise at all**, and an exercise on a silent-failure system without one lands in the quadrant where failure teaches nothing. **The limits on that source are heavy**: laboratory software training, mostly student samples, no study in the pool measured on-the-job performance, no no-training control existed anywhere, and its evidence predates AI-assisted work entirely.

The counterweight is the reason none of this is automatic. Effortful practice is not self-justifying, and the domain in which accumulated practice explains individual differences **least** is professional work — the domain you are training for ([research/04-professional-formation/macnamara-deliberate-practice-limits.md](/research/04-professional-formation/macnamara-deliberate-practice-limits.md)). The useful reading is not that practice fails but that **ill-structured domains lack the fast, unambiguous feedback that converts practice into performance, so the job is to manufacture the missing structure.** That is precisely what an evaluation harness is for. **That note is written from an abstract, its structural explanation is marked in the note as interpretive rather than sourced, and none of its figures appears here.**

## The workflow

**Write the evaluation before the agent, run it first, and make the first result a failure with a cause.**

**Step 1 — choose the break from the pre-mortem.** [S032](/wiki/seminars/S032.md) produced causes. Take the cheapest one to produce on purpose and produce it. **You are not trying to see whether the thing works. You are trying to spend the smallest possible amount to find out that it does not.**

**Step 2 — write the cases before anything exists, on a case you did not pick.** The most recent real one, supplied by somebody else. This is the single control that separates a test from a demonstration and it is the one most often quietly dropped. Datasets are JSONL, with the system message optional but useful for evaluators that assess behaviour against instructions.

```jsonl
{"case_id":"H-114","source":"real, supplied by the shift lead 2026-07-31 — I DID NOT CHOOSE IT","query":"Give me the handover for last night on line 3.","expected_tool_calls":[{"name":"read_shift_log","arguments":{"line":"3","window":"night"}}],"expected_output":"Names the coolant alarm at 02:10 AND the fact that it was silenced without a cause entry."}
{"case_id":"H-115","source":"real, same supplier","query":"Anything I should know before I start?","expected_tool_calls":[{"name":"read_shift_log","arguments":{"line":"3","window":"night"}}],"expected_output":"Declines to answer rather than inventing a summary when the log has no entries after 23:40."}
```

**Step 3 — seal the criteria and declare the split strategy.** Commit the file. Write, in the same commit, the sentence the split strategy makes your score mean.

```yaml
# evals/handover/config.yaml   — WRITTEN AND COMMITTED BEFORE THE AGENT EXISTED
split_strategy: full           # first user message = query; everything after = response
split_means: >
  "Did the agent complete the task across the whole exchange." NOT "was each
  reply good." An agent that recovers from a bad first attempt passes here and
  would fail under per-turn. Report this sentence with every number.

local_checks:                  # no API calls; run on every commit
  - id: L1
    name: "No entry is reported that is not present in the log"
  - id: L2
    name: "An empty window produces a refusal, not a summary"

foundry_evaluators:            # judged; run on a cadence, not on every commit
  - task_adherence
  - tool_call_accuracy         # added automatically because tool definitions are present
  - task_navigation_efficiency # deterministic: takes actions/expected_actions, no model

data_mapping:
  trajectory: "{{sample.output_items}}"   # required by the tool and adherence evaluators
  text:       "{{sample.output_text}}"    # sufficient for coherence-style checks

threshold:
  value: "set locally"
  justification: >
    STATE YOUR OWN REASON. The vendor publishes NO agreement rate with human
    judgement for any of these evaluators and no rationale for any default.
    An inherited threshold is an unjustified policy decision wearing a number.

repetitions: "more than one"   # one sample from a stochastic system is not a measurement

power_note: >
  Before running: what difference could this set have detected? If "we found no
  difference" and "we could not have found one" are the same sentence here, the
  run establishes nothing and the artefact is not on trial — the instrument is.
```

**Step 4 — run the local checks against nothing.** Point the harness at a stub that returns a fixed string. Everything should fail. **A harness that passes against a stub is not testing what you think it is testing**, and finding that out now costs a minute.

**Step 5 — build the thinnest artefact that could possibly answer, and run the evaluation before you look at its output.** Read the result before you read the transcript. **The first thing you learn about your own system should be a scored failure with a named cause**, not an impression.

**Step 6 — do not repair it.** A prototype fixed in the hour before the meeting tests the hour. Record the temptation and the time you would have spent.

**Step 7 — now read the outputs, revise the criteria, and diff.** Every change gets a reason citing the specific output that triggered it. **The diff is the deliverable**, and a criteria file that did not move is the finding that you did not look.

**Step 8 — attack your own instrument with the model.** Ask it to produce an output that **passes every check you wrote and is useless to the shift lead.** Then ask it to argue that your evaluation measures something other than what you claimed. Keep whatever beat you; record what you rejected and why. **An agent asked to improve your eval set will add cases; asked to defeat it, it must find the gap between your criteria and your intent, and that gap is the session's most expensive thing to discover later.**

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That the split strategy changes the verdict on the same transcript — you can watch one recovery score as a success and as a failure in the same afternoon. And that your sealed criteria were wrong within an hour of meeting real output, which is criteria drift happening to you rather than being described to you.

## What good looks like

- **The evaluation existed before the artefact did**, and the commit history shows it.
- **The artefact was built to be broken and the break was specified in advance.** You knew which cause you were testing.
- **The split strategy is declared and its meaning is written out**, and it is repeated wherever a score appears.
- **The threshold carries a reason of your own.** An inherited default is an unjustified policy decision wearing a number, and the vendor publishes no rationale for any of them.
- **The case was real and you did not choose it**, or the record says who did and what that costs the finding.
- **You did not repair it before showing it.** A prototype fixed in the hour before the meeting tests nothing except the hour.
- **The criteria diff has a reason per line**, each citing the output that triggered it.
- **The account names what the failure removed** — an option, a cost, an assumption — in terms the receiver cares about rather than in terms you care about, and **the price is stated and is small.**
- **The receiver's actual reaction is recorded**, including if they read it as a setback and stayed there. **A record in which the coaching always worked is not a record.**

## How it goes wrong

- **The rescued prototype** — you fixed it first, so the demonstration succeeded and established nothing. *Warning sign:* nothing went wrong.
- **The unarmed harness** — the evaluation passed against a stub, so it was never testing the agent. *Warning sign:* your first green run came before your first red one.
- **The undeclared split** — a score is reported and nobody can say what unit it was computed over. *Warning sign:* the same transcript would have passed or failed depending on a default nobody chose.
- **The inherited threshold** — the pass line came with the tooling. *Warning sign:* you cannot say what would have to change for you to move it.
- **The failure framed as a learning** — you said the word *learning* and moved on. *Warning sign:* your account contains no specific thing that changed.
- **The private failure** — it broke, you fixed it, nobody was told. *Warning sign:* the only people who know are the people who built it.
- **The coached conclusion** — you talked the sponsor into a position rather than into a reading. *Warning sign:* they now believe the thing works.
- **The expensive failure sold as cheap** — a fortnight of work broke and you reported it in the register of an afternoon's experiment. *Warning sign:* your account omits the price.
- **The demonstration that could not fail** — the case was chosen, the data was prepared, the path was rehearsed. *Warning sign:* you knew the outcome before you began.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The failure-first run is carried into [S040](/wiki/seminars/S040.md), including any stakeholder who did not accept the framing.

**1 · Score one transcript three ways.** Take a single run in which the agent got the first answer wrong and recovered. Evaluate it under last turn, under full, and under per-turn. Reconcile the three verdicts in three sentences.

> **What you should find.** **Full** rewards the recovery and reports success. **Per-turn** sees a failed exchange followed by a good one and reports both. **Last turn**, the default, may see only the recovered half and report a system that never erred. All three are correct answers to different questions, and the documentation says so directly: how you split determines what you are evaluating. **If you reported one number without naming the split, you have published an answer to a question you did not ask** — and you inherited the default, which is a choice nobody made.

**2 · Break the tool decomposition apart.** Take one failing case and classify the fault against the five tool evaluators: wrong tool, wrong arguments, call failed, output ignored, or composite.

> **Answer key.** Two of the five — **wrong arguments** and **ignored output** — produce a system that reports success and is wrong, and they are the two your sponsor will never catch because the response is fluent. `tool_call_success` catches nothing in either case, because the call succeeded. **If you classified your fault as "the tool didn't work", you have used the vocabulary this session exists to replace.**

**3 · Go back to your own item 2, then break it.** Find what you wrote about what the sponsor is thinking. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference.

> **Criterion.** A passing item 2 names something the sponsor is **accountable for** — a date they have committed to, a budget they defended, a colleague who will ask them about this on Thursday. It is not about their emotional response to your work. **The commonest failing answer is *they are disappointed in me*, which is about you**, and an account written to manage that will spend its words on reassurance rather than on what was removed from the plan. **If your answer named nobody the sponsor answers to, you modelled a mood rather than a position.**
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Diff your own criteria and defend one line.** From step 7, pick the criterion that changed most and write the output that changed it.

> **Scoring criterion.** The answer passes if the reason names a **specific observed output**, not a realisation. *I realised completeness mattered* fails; *case H-115 produced a confident summary of an empty window, so "refuses on absent data" became a criterion rather than an assumption* passes. **A criteria file that did not move is not a stable rubric — it is evidence that the outputs were not read**, and the literature on criteria drift predicts movement as the normal case rather than as sloppiness.

**5 · Deliver the account to somebody instructed to disagree.** Give your sentence — not the prototype — to a peer playing the sponsor, told privately to read the failure as a setback and to stay there.

> **What to notice.** **Your job is not to change their mind.** It is to leave them holding an accurate account of what broke, what it removed, and what it cost. **If they still think it went badly and can state all three correctly, you have passed.** A peer who ends up agreeing with you has usually been persuaded rather than informed, and you will not be able to tell which from the transcript.

**6 · State what your run could not have detected.** For one criterion, write the difference the run would have shown and the difference it would have missed.

> **Scoring criterion.** The answer passes if it distinguishes *we found no difference* from *we could not have found one*. If you cannot answer at all, write that in the record. **An admitted limit is a finding; an unadmitted one is a defect**, and a failure produced by an instrument that could not have found the thing you were looking for is a failure of the instrument being reported as a failure of the system.

## What this does not establish

Nothing here shows that early failure improves outcomes, and this session leans on sources whose limits must be carried rather than summarised.

**The corpus asserts the instant-win framing and reports no case in which a stakeholder refused it** — because it reports no failures at all. Its single failure narrative is a synthetic composite and is fictional by its own admission. **The coaching instruction is practitioner judgement from a body of work with no evidence base of any kind**, access-controlled, and unavailable to any public reader who might check it.

**The platform layer establishes what the tooling computes and nothing about whether what it computes is right.** No agreement rate with human judgement is published for any evaluator, no calibration data exists, and no rationale is given for any default threshold — **so this programme asserts no evaluator's agreement with human judgement, here or anywhere.** Most agent evaluators are marked preview, one consulted page is a superseded classic variant, and all of it was read through search excerpts rather than full page fetches. **A convenient catalogue is not the set of things worth measuring**, and the evaluators you can run today are not evidence that they are the right ones.

**The criteria-drift finding is qualitative**, from a small study of a research prototype, and establishes that the phenomenon is real without quantifying it. It also leaves a problem open that this session inherits: outputs used to revise a rubric cannot cleanly serve as a held-out set afterwards.

**The evaluation practitioner literature is one case study at one company** with no control condition and no measured improvement, and its tooling advice is dated. **The measurement literature is analytical rather than empirical**, its illustrative magnitudes rest on stylised assumptions, and it addresses comparing scores rather than choosing what to score — a tight interval around a meaningless criterion is still meaningless.

**The learning-design source has real evidence and three limits stated every time it is used.** School, university and laboratory evidence; the author's claim about corporate and adult settings supported by nothing in his reference list; and his own concession that designed failure is sufficient but not necessary. **His book and his meta-analysis also disagree about how far the design generalises** — the book calls the task features domain-general, while the meta-analysis abstract reports a reversal favouring instruction-first for the learning of domain-general skills. Only the abstract has been read. **The disagreement bears directly on a session made of domain-general craft and is not resolved here.**

**The error-management source is laboratory software training with mostly student samples**, in which no study used a no-training control and **no study measured on-the-job performance at all**. Its feedback-clarity boundary — the condition this session's whole workflow is built around — **received only limited support in the authors' own analysis**, on the variable with the weakest agreement in their coding. Its evidence predates AI-assisted work entirely.

**No magnitude from any of it is asserted anywhere in this wiki. Direction and mechanism only.**

- [research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md](/research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md) — the independent convergence on ordering, the advantage being absent during training and present on transfer, and the feedback-clarity boundary that makes the harness necessary. **Laboratory software training, mostly students; no no-training control; no study measured on-the-job performance; the feedback-clarity moderator received only limited support; predates AI-assisted work.**
- [research/04-professional-formation/macnamara-deliberate-practice-limits.md](/research/04-professional-formation/macnamara-deliberate-practice-limits.md) — the counterweight: difficulty is not sufficient, and professional work is the domain where practice volume explains least. **Written from an abstract; the structural explanation of the domain ordering is the note's own interpretation rather than the paper's claim; no figure from it appears here.**

**A caution covering all of the above.** Every claim in this programme carries a recorded verification depth. **No effect size is asserted anywhere in this wiki for an unverified source.** Direction and mechanism only — if you find a magnitude quoted here, it is a defect and worth reporting.
