# S037 · The Prototype That Fails Immediately

**Module:** [M08](/wiki/modules/M08.md) · **Quarter:** [Q4](/wiki/quarters/Q4.md) · **Complexity class:** 3
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** none
**Whitepaper:** [WP-037](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-037.md)
**Retrieves from:** S032, S036
**Entry state:** this-programme-only

## The situation

You built the cheap version. You showed it. It broke in front of the sponsor.

Two people in the room now hold different accounts of what just happened. You think the exercise worked. They think it went badly. **Reconciling those two accounts is your job, it is not automatic, and nobody will do it for you.**

Answer these in writing before anything is taught.

1. Your prototype fails on the first real case. Write the sentence you say next.
2. Write what the sponsor is thinking while you say it.
3. What did the failure remove from the plan? Be specific — a design option, a cost, a week, an assumption.
4. You could fix it in an hour before the meeting. Give the reason not to.
5. A failure that costs a fortnight and a failure that costs an afternoon are both failures. What is different about how you report them?

**Item 2 is the session.** Most learners can produce a good sentence for item 1 and have never thought about who is receiving it.

## What you must produce

A **failure-first run**, in three parts.

- **The design** — what you built the artefact to break on, chosen from your [S032](/wiki/seminars/S032.md) causes, and what you expected to happen.
- **The break** — what actually happened, dated, with the case it happened on and who chose that case.
- **The account** — what you said, to whom, and what they concluded. Including the cases where they concluded the wrong thing.

## What good looks like

- **The artefact was built to be broken and the break was specified in advance.** You knew which cause you were testing.
- **The case was real and you did not choose it**, or the record says who did and what that costs the finding.
- **You did not repair it before showing it.** A prototype fixed in the hour before the meeting tests nothing except the hour.
- **The account names what the failure removed** — an option, a cost, an assumption — in terms the receiver cares about rather than in terms you care about.
- **The receiver's actual reaction is recorded**, including if they read it as a setback and stayed there. **A record in which the coaching always worked is not a record.**
- **The price of the failure is stated**, and it is small. If it was not small, the record says why the exercise was run anyway.

## How it goes wrong

- **The rescued prototype** — you fixed it first, so the demonstration succeeded and established nothing. *Warning sign:* nothing went wrong.
- **The failure framed as a learning** — you said the word *learning* and moved on. *Warning sign:* your account contains no specific thing that changed.
- **The private failure** — it broke, you fixed it, nobody was told. *Warning sign:* the only people who know are the people who built it.
- **The coached conclusion** — you talked the sponsor into a position rather than into a reading. *Warning sign:* they now believe the thing works.
- **The expensive failure sold as cheap** — a fortnight of work broke and you reported it in the register of an afternoon's experiment. *Warning sign:* your account omits the price.
- **The demonstration that could not fail** — the case was chosen, the data was prepared, the path was rehearsed. *Warning sign:* you knew the outcome before you began.

## Do it

**Choose the break from the pre-mortem.** [S032](/wiki/seminars/S032.md) produced causes; this session takes the cheapest one to produce on purpose and produces it. **You are not trying to see whether the thing works. You are trying to spend the smallest possible amount to find out that it does not.**

**Then run it on a case you did not pick.** The most recent real one, supplied by somebody else. This is the single control that separates a test from a demonstration, and it is the one most often quietly dropped.

**Then do the coaching, deliberately and out loud.** The corpus this stage draws on states that a cheap prototype that fails immediately is to be treated as an instant win, and — this is the part that matters — that **stakeholders are to be actively coached into reading it that way rather than as a setback.** The instruction concedes that the reading is not natural. Your sentence has to carry three things: what broke, what that removes from the plan, and what it cost to find out.

**Then let them disagree.** A sponsor who says *this is a setback* has not misunderstood you; they have applied a different and entirely reasonable frame, in which a thing that does not work is a thing that does not work. Record the disagreement rather than closing it. **A record in which every stakeholder was successfully converted is a record of a conversation that did not happen.**

**Then notice what this session is doing to you.** This programme's own teaching design puts the attempt before the explanation, deliberately, on the argument that being stuck is what makes the subsequent explanation land — and that the phase which converts the failure into learning is the explicit, prompted comparison between what you produced and what turned out to be right. **You have just been run through the professional version of that.** The source for the design also reports that helping people towards the answer during the attempt reduces both the failure and the learning, which is why nobody stopped you building the wrong thing.

Write down the difference between what you did and what you would now do, side by side, in two columns. **The comparison is the artefact. Doing it in your head does not count**, and that is the source's own finding rather than this programme's preference: showing people the right and wrong versions together achieves nothing unless they are explicitly required to compare them.

## Show it

Give your account — the sentence, not the prototype — to a peer playing the sponsor, who has been told privately to read the failure as a setback and to stay there.

**Your job is not to change their mind.** It is to leave them holding an accurate account of what broke, what it removed, and what it cost. **If they still think it went badly and can state all three correctly, you have passed.**

## What this does not establish

Nothing here shows that early failure improves outcomes, and this session leans on a source whose limits must be carried rather than summarised.

**The corpus asserts the instant-win framing and reports no case in which a stakeholder refused it** — because it reports no failures at all. Its single failure narrative is a synthetic composite and is fictional by its own admission. **The coaching instruction is practitioner judgement from a body of work with no evidence base of any kind.**

**The learning-design source behind the comparison step has real evidence and three limits this session states every time it uses it.** Its evidence is school, university and laboratory evidence; the author's claim that the cycle works in corporate and adult settings is asserted in his own voice and supported by no citation anywhere in his reference list. **This programme's use of it on adult professionals is an extrapolation and is recorded as one.** Its author concedes that designed failure is sufficient but not necessary for the awareness it produces. And **his own book and his own meta-analysis disagree about how far the design generalises** — the book claims the design principles are domain-general and names business and writing among its applications, while the meta-analysis abstract reports a reversal favouring instruction-first for the learning of domain-general skills. Only the abstract has been read here. **The disagreement is unresolved, it bears directly on a session made of domain-general craft, and it is not resolved by this page.**

**No magnitude from any of it is asserted anywhere in this wiki. Direction and mechanism only.**
