# S042 · Scope That Grows, and the Backlog Item That Does Not

**Module:** [M09](/wiki/modules/M09.md) · **Quarter:** [Q5](/wiki/quarters/Q5.md) · **Complexity class:** 3
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** The APIM AI gateway policy as the place where scope growth becomes visible as token cost rather than as sentiment
**Whitepaper:** [WP-042](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-042.md)
**Retrieves from:** S024, S025, S041
**Entry state:** either

---

## The situation

You are building the slice. Halfway through, you find something adjacent that obviously ought to be done, and doing it now would take less time than writing it down.

That sentence is the whole session. Every engineer has said it, most of them were right about the time, and the aggregate effect across an engagement is a piece of work nobody can describe the boundary of.

The open playbook's response is mechanical and it is one of the few rules on that site with no judgement in it at all: **new work becomes a new backlog item, never a bigger old one.** Scope is not managed by intention. It is managed by refusing to let the container grow.

Answer these before instruction.

1. What did you add to the last thing you built because stopping to record it felt more expensive than doing it?
2. Who decides whether a change belongs to the work in front of you?
3. What is the difference between a request you decline and one you defer, from the requester's point of view?
4. If every piece of discovered work became a new item, what does your backlog look like at the end of the engagement, and is that bad?
5. What does it cost when this rule is applied wrongly?

**Item 4 is where the rule stops being free.** A mechanical rule produces a mechanical consequence, and a backlog that records everything and refuses nothing is a different failure with the same cause.

## What you must produce

A **scope ledger** covering one working period on your slice.

Each entry: what was discovered, how it was discovered, and its disposition — **absorbed into the current work**, **recorded as a new item**, or **refused** — with the reason, and with the sentence you would actually say to the person who asked for it.

The ledger closes with two lines: what your ledger would add to the engagement if every recorded item were built, and which of them you would decline if somebody asked today.

## The material

### The rule is mechanical, and that is its entire value

The openly published playbook states it in the general-guidance list with no hedge and no judgement attached: **never add scope to an existing backlog item; create a new one** ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md), pages read 2026-07-30). It sits beside two neighbours that are usually quoted separately and belong together: *"Make the simple thing work now"*, and the work-items rule that *"Each Task should be sized so that they can be completed within a day."*

What makes the scope rule unusual on that site is that it contains no judgement at all. It does not ask whether the discovered work is important, or urgent, or cheap. It asks only whether the container in front of you already existed, and if it did, the new work goes somewhere else. **A rule with no judgement in it cannot be argued with, which is exactly why it survives pressure** — there is nothing to negotiate about at the moment when negotiating would be easiest.

It is also the only scope mechanism in that document that leaves a trace. An item that grew is visible in its own history; an item that was created is visible in the backlog. **The rule's warrant is that it is checkable, which is a property of the rule and not a claim about outcomes**, and the playbook supplies no evidence that following it produces better software, in common with every other practice on the site.

Two review rules bracket the same boundary from either side, and the pairing is worth holding together. From the reviewer: **"Don't block the current PR due to issues that are out of scope"**. From the author: **"If the requested change is within the scope of the task, 'I'll do it later' is not an acceptable reason!"** Two rules, one boundary, and **neither of them tells you where the boundary is.** That omission is the session.

**The playbook's limits travel with every use.** Pages carry footer dates spread across four years, roughly half the site is stub, link list or version-pinned recipe, it contradicts itself in at least a dozen places without noticing, and it asserts four unwarranted magnitude claims that this repository records as specimens and reproduces nowhere. Cite it by page and by the date that page was read.

### Two kinds of growth, and only one of them is a failure

This is the distinction the session exists to install, and it is not in any source.

**Scope grows because the problem was understood better.** You discover that the case types are not two but five; that the identity model the customer described does not match the one running; that the thing you were told was a lookup is a workflow. That growth is *learning arriving late*, and the correct response is not to refuse it. It is to notice that the design it invalidates was a design, and to send it to a decision record at [S024](/wiki/seminars/S024.md) rather than to the backlog. **A discovery that changes what you are building is not a backlog item. It is a decision somebody has to take, and filing it as work quietly takes that decision on their behalf.**

**Scope grows because nobody said no.** Somebody asked, the asking was reasonable, the work was small, and declining would have been socially expensive. This is the growth the rule is for, and it has a signature: each individual addition is defensible and the aggregate is indescribable. Nobody can say what the work in front of them contains, only that it is nearly done.

The two are indistinguishable in the moment and trivially distinguishable afterwards, which is why the ledger must be kept live. **A ledger written at the end of the period is a justification of the period**, and it will classify everything as the first kind.

### Cheap production is what erodes the rule, and you cannot feel it happening

The rule was written for a world in which writing the adjacent thing down was cheaper than building it. That is no longer reliably true, and the session's honest position is that the rule is now under a pressure its authors never modelled.

The most careful available evidence on that pressure is a randomised trial in which experienced maintainers worked on their own real backlog items in their own repositories, with tooling access randomised per task. **The measured direction was that they were slower with the tooling, and both they and expert forecasters had predicted the opposite.** The finding that matters most here is the one about perception: after having actually done the tasks, the developers still believed the tooling had made them faster. **Direct personal experience did not correct the misperception** ([research/04-professional-formation/metr-ai-assisted-productivity-study.md](/research/04-professional-formation/metr-ai-assisted-productivity-study.md)).

The limits on that study are severe and must travel with it. The developer sample is very small; the population is expert maintainers working in codebases they know intimately, which is close to a worst case for assistance and is not representative of software work generally; the outcome is self-reported time; the tooling is fixed to a particular early generation; **the authors publish an explicit list of things their result does not show and that list should be cited alongside the headline**; and **the research group itself now labels the result out of date** and has redesigned the study, because participants increasingly decline to work without the tooling at all. A later run pointed the other way and the group judges its own signal unreliable. **No magnitude from any of it is reproduced here, and none may be.**

What survives all of that is a mechanism, and the mechanism is what bears on scope. **If the felt cost of adding the adjacent thing is not the real cost, then the judgement that the addition is free is being made by an instrument that is known to be miscalibrated in that exact direction.** *It was quicker to do it than to write it down* is a report about a feeling, and the feeling has been measured, in a narrow population, and found not to track the clock.

### The gateway is where the second kind of growth becomes a number

A gateway in front of model endpoints exists to make multi-tenant concerns visible, and the documentation is explicit that this is an extension of an ordinary API gateway rather than a new category of thing: the AI gateway **"extends API Management's existing API gateway"** and **"it's not a separate offering"** ([research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md)).

The argument that makes it a scope instrument is the noisy-neighbour argument, stated cleanly enough to teach on its own. Tokens are the scarce resource; providers assign quota per minute; that quota has to be distributed "across your model consumers, such as different applications, developer teams, or departments within the company"; and with more than one consumer **"you need to make sure that one app doesn't use the whole TPM quota and block other apps from accessing the backends they need."** Generalised: **a shared finite resource with no per-consumer accounting will be consumed by whichever consumer is least careful.**

The mechanisms follow. The **token limit policy** enforces a limit or quota "over a specified period, such as hourly, daily, weekly, monthly, or yearly", keyed on "any counter key, such as subscription key, originating IP address, or an arbitrary key defined through a policy expression". It can **precalculate prompt tokens at the gateway**, "minimizing unnecessary requests to the AI service backend if the prompt already exceeds the limit" — a fail-fast design, rejecting at the edge rather than paying to discover the rejection downstream. And `llm-emit-token-metric` attaches custom dimensions to emitted metrics.

**The dimension is the part that makes this a scope instrument rather than a billing one.** Total token consumption tells you the bill. Token consumption per consumer tells you who to talk to. **If you key the counter on the slice rather than on the subscription, the gateway becomes an instrument that reports scope growth as a number, on a schedule, without anybody having to feel anything about it.** That is not what the documentation proposes it for, and it is the durable idea underneath what the documentation does propose.

One further published mechanism is a scope trap rather than a scope instrument. **Semantic caching** reuses prior completions "by comparing the vector proximity of the prompt to prior requests", and the similarity threshold is a correctness dial: set it loosely and the system returns an answer to a question that was not asked. **The source does not discuss that risk at all** — the caution is this repository's own analysis, not the documentation's — and the failure is silent, which is the worst combination for anything sitting on a delivery path.

**The limits are heavy.** Product documentation with a commercial frame, arguing for a gateway and not discussing the latency, cost or operational burden the gateway itself adds; **capability availability varies by service tier**, so any lab must confirm what the available tier supports; the Foundry integration and the unified model API are **preview** and should not be built on for anything durable; **no performance or cost-saving data of any kind appears** — the benefits described are plausible mechanisms rather than measured outcomes; policy names, attributes and tier boundaries drift between revisions; and **the privacy implications of logging prompts and completions are not addressed anywhere in the source**, despite logging being recommended for billing and auditing.

### The organisation that cannot perceive scope except as cost

This is why the anchor was chosen, and it is a claim about organisations rather than about gateways.

In most delivery settings, scope growth is perceived as sentiment. Somebody feels the work is getting away from them; somebody else feels it is fine; the disagreement is about temperament and is settled by whoever is more senior or more tired. **A ledger converts that into a written disagreement, which is progress. A per-slice token counter converts it into a number that arrives whether or not anybody raises it, which is a different kind of progress.**

The caution is symmetrical and important. A number is not more true than a sentiment; it is only harder to ignore. The delivery corpus behind this stage is a worked demonstration of what happens when that difference is forgotten: **its cost side is engineered and its return side is elicited** — consumption forecast by engineers and refined against telemetry from their own system, benefit chosen by the customer from a menu and measured with instrumentation the customer may not possess ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md), **internal, access-controlled, paraphrased and never quoted**). **A ratio whose denominator is well instrumented and whose numerator is not is not a well-instrumented ratio, but it will feel like one.**

The same corpus contains the sharpest available warning about arranging your own evidence. Its prepared answer to the objection that the tooling improved and delivery did not speed up reattributes the shortfall to the customer's own organisational constraints — so **a disappointing result confirms the thesis rather than challenging it.** Read that structure once and you will recognise it in your own ledger: a scope entry whose disposition is *absorbed* and whose reason is *the customer was not ready* has done the same thing on a smaller scale.

The measurement discipline that closes this is stated plainly in the practitioner literature: **cost and latency belong in the set of criteria an application defines up front**, alongside quality, rather than being discovered at operation ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/huyen-ai-engineering.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/huyen-ai-engineering.md), **a practitioner book, first edition December 2024, whose strongest evaluation claim its author marks as a belief rather than a finding**). A ledger that records what was added and not what it costs to run has recorded half of the growth.

## The workflow

**Instrument the slice so that scope growth arrives as a number you did not have to ask for.**

**Step 1 — put the gateway in front of the model endpoint before you need it.** One backend, one API, one subscription key. Do this on the first day of the working period, because a gateway introduced after the growth has happened measures the growth's aftermath.

**Step 2 — key the counter on the slice, not on the application.** This is the whole trick and it is one attribute. The documented counter key accepts "an arbitrary key defined through a policy expression", so give each slice from [S041](/wiki/seminars/S041.md) its own key and let the quota be per slice.

**Step 3 — write the policy, with the limit set to what the slice was scoped for rather than to what the backend will bear.**

```xml
<!-- policies/inbound/first-slice.xml  -- one policy per slice, keyed on the slice -->
<inbound>
  <set-variable name="sliceId"
                value="@(context.Request.Headers.GetValueOrDefault("x-slice-id","unattributed"))" />

  <!-- The budget is the SLICE's, not the backend's. If this trips, scope grew. -->
  <llm-token-limit counter-key="@((string)context.Variables["sliceId"])"
                   tokens-per-minute="500"
                   estimate-prompt-tokens="true"
                   remaining-tokens-variable-name="remainingTokens" />

  <!-- Dimensions are what make a metric answerable.
       Total spend tells you the bill; spend per slice tells you which boundary moved. -->
  <llm-emit-token-metric namespace="slice-scope">
    <dimension name="Slice"     value="@((string)context.Variables["sliceId"])" />
    <dimension name="API ID"    value="@(context.Api.Id)" />
    <dimension name="Exclusion" value="@(context.Request.Headers
                                          .GetValueOrDefault("x-excluded-item","none"))" />
  </llm-emit-token-metric>
</inbound>
```

**Step 4 — tag every call that serves something on your exclusion list.** The `x-excluded-item` header is not production hygiene; it is the ledger's instrument. **A call that carries an exclusion name is scope growth caught at the moment it happened**, and it will be in the metric before it is in anybody's memory.

**Step 5 — leave prompt-token precalculation on and watch what it rejects.** Rejecting at the edge is fail-fast: you find out that a slice has outgrown its budget without paying the backend to tell you. Record each rejection in the ledger with a disposition.

**Step 6 — reconcile the ledger against the metric at the end of the period, in that order.** Write the ledger first, from memory and notes; then open the metric. **The gap between the two is the session's real output.** Entries you absorbed and did not record will appear as spend against a slice you thought was closed.

**Step 7 — use the agent to find your absorptions, then argue with it.** Give it your slice definition and your diff, and instruct it to list everything present in the change that the definition does not require. Then instruct it to argue that your three most defensible ledger entries were absorptions dressed as discoveries — that the problem was not understood better and you simply did not want to have the conversation. **The value is in the entries you disagree with**, because those are the ones where your boundary is doing work you had not articulated. **An agent that agreed with your dispositions was given your dispositions.**

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That scope growth has a cost curve and that the curve is visible before anybody is willing to say so out loud. A team can disagree for a fortnight about whether the work is getting away from them; the same team, looking at spend against a slice whose budget was set on day one, has a shorter disagreement. **You will also discover which of your exclusions were never exclusions at all** — because they will have traffic.

## What good looks like

- **Every entry has a disposition and none of them is blank.** An undisposed discovery is an absorbed one.
- **At least one entry is refused rather than recorded.** A ledger with no refusals is a queue.
- **The absorbed entries are justified against the slice definition** from [S041](/wiki/seminars/S041.md), not against convenience.
- **The sentences are sayable.** A refusal you would not deliver in the room is a refusal you will not make.
- **The decision-rights question is answered.** The ledger names who decides, and it is not always you.
- **Where a discovery invalidates the design rather than extending it, the ledger says so** and points at a decision record from [S024](/wiki/seminars/S024.md) rather than at an item.
- **The two kinds of growth are distinguished per entry**, and the entries classified as better understanding outnumber neither the refusals nor the records by an accident nobody can explain.
- **The ledger and the meter disagree, and the disagreement is written down.** A ledger that matches the instrument perfectly was written after looking at it.

## How it goes wrong

- **The growing item** — the work in front of you quietly becomes everything discovered near it. *Warning sign:* you cannot say when the current work will be finished, only that it is nearly done.
- **The graveyard backlog** — the rule is followed perfectly, nothing is ever refused, and the record becomes an inventory of obligations nobody will honour. *Warning sign:* item 4 felt like a virtue.
- **The silent absorption** — the addition is real, it is small, it is not recorded, and it is invisible at review. *Warning sign:* your change contains something you would have to explain.
- **The scope argument inside the review** — the boundary is litigated in the comments on the change rather than settled before it. *Warning sign:* the longest thread on your change is about whether something belongs in it.
- **The deferral that is a refusal** — you record an item you have no intention of building, because recording is easier than declining. *Warning sign:* your ledger has no refusals and a very long record.
- **The costless addition** — production is cheap enough that adding the adjacent thing takes almost no effort, so the rule feels like bureaucracy. *Warning sign:* your justification is about how little it cost rather than about whether it belonged.
- **The relabelled absorption** — an addition nobody wanted to refuse is entered as *the problem was understood better*, which is the one disposition that requires no defence. *Warning sign:* every entry in your ledger is learning and none is pressure.
- **The instrument nobody looks at** — the meter is configured, the dimensions are right, and no human has opened it since the day it was built. *Warning sign:* your metric has a slice dimension and your ledger has no reconciliation line.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The ledger with its refusals intact is carried into [S044](/wiki/seminars/S044.md), where the boundary is enforced by somebody else, and into [S045](/wiki/seminars/S045.md), where the refusals are checked for having survived assembly.

**1 · Classify six discoveries, then say what each costs to record.** For each, choose absorbed, recorded or refused, and say which of the two kinds of growth it is.

- *A second case type, discovered because the first one's data had it embedded.* · *A logging field the platform team asks for.* · *A retry the model needs because the backend is rate-limiting you.* · *An export the sponsor mentioned in passing.* · *An identity model that turns out not to be the one you were told about.* · *A tidier prompt that costs nothing and improves nothing measurable.*

> **Model answers.** The second case type and the identity model are the first kind of growth — the problem was understood better — and **neither belongs in the backlog.** Both invalidate the design rather than extending it, so both go to a decision record at [S024](/wiki/seminars/S024.md), where somebody with authority decides. The retry is absorbed: it is not new work, it is the work you already agreed to, failing. The logging field and the export are the second kind — recorded as new items, and the export is a candidate for refusal because nobody has said who would use it. **The tidier prompt is the trap.** It costs nothing, improves nothing you can name, and is the entry most people absorb silently. **If it is not worth an item it is not worth doing**, and your instrument will show it as spend against a slice that was supposed to be closed.

**2 · Say the three hardest sentences out loud.** Take your three hardest ledger entries and say to one colleague the sentence you would actually say to the person who asked. Their only job is to tell you whether it declines, defers or concedes.

> **What you should find.** **Most engineers discover their refusals are deferrals.** *We will put it on the list* is a concession that has been formatted as a decision, and the person who asked will hear it as a yes with a delay. A refusal names the thing, gives one reason, and does not offer a date. The discovery is more useful in this room than in the customer's, which is the only reason the exercise exists.

**3 · Go back to your own item 4, then break it.** Find what you wrote for opening item 4 — what your backlog looks like at the end of the engagement if every discovery becomes an item, and whether that is bad. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference.

> **Criterion.** The rule guarantees that nothing is lost. It guarantees nothing about anything being built. **A backlog that records everything and refuses nothing is an inventory of obligations nobody will honour**, and it is worse than losing the items would have been, because a customer reading it believes those things are coming. The honest answer to item 4 is that a mechanical rule produces a mechanical consequence, and that the consequence has to be paid for by a second act — refusal — which the rule does not supply and the playbook does not mention. **If you wrote that a complete record is obviously good, you named a property of the record and not a property of the engagement.**
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is. **School, university and laboratory evidence; no adult-professional study in its reference list.**

**4 · Key the counter on the slice and make it trip.** Run the workflow, then deliberately serve one item from your exclusion list and watch the metric.

> **What you should find.** That an exclusion with traffic against it is not an exclusion, and that the instrument says so without anybody having a conversation. Note two published properties while you are there. **Prompt-token precalculation rejects at the edge**, so you learn a slice has outgrown its budget without paying the backend to tell you. And the counter key is documented as accepting an arbitrary policy expression — **the platform does not know what a slice is, and it does not need to.** That is the reusable part; the policy element name is not. **The documentation publishes no performance or cost-saving data of any kind, so nothing here shows that gateways reduce anything.**

**5 · Reconcile the ledger against the meter, in that order.** Write the ledger from memory and notes first. Then open the metric. Report the gap.

> **Scoring criterion.** The answer passes if the gap is non-zero and each discrepancy has an explanation that is not *I forgot*. **A perfect match is the finding that should worry you**, because it usually means the ledger was written after the metric was consulted, which makes it a description of the instrument rather than a record of the fortnight. The reconciliation is also where you will meet the session's uncomfortable claim first-hand: additions that felt free were not free, and the feeling has been measured in a narrow population and found not to track the clock.

**6 · Argue with the machine about your own dispositions.** Run workflow step 7, then record which of its accusations you accepted.

> **What to notice.** The instrument cannot tell whether the problem was understood better — it has no access to your understanding — so everything it flags is a hypothesis about your motives. **That is the useful property, not a defect**: it will accuse you of absorbing things you genuinely learned, and defending those entries is how you find out which of your reasons are written down and which are in your head. If it accepted every disposition, check what you gave it. **An instrument pointed at your own conclusions returns your own conclusions.**

## What this does not establish

Nothing here shows that scope discipline improves delivery.

The playbook states the rule as a standing commitment and **supplies no evidence for it**, in common with every other practice on the site. The internal corpus contains **no failed engagement and no cost side**, so it cannot supply a case in which scope growth caused a delivery to fail, because it reports no delivery that failed.

**The rule's real warrant is that it is checkable**, which is a property of the rule rather than a claim about outcomes: an item that grew is visible in its own history, and an item that was created is visible in the backlog. That is why this session teaches it. Whether checkability produces better software is not established anywhere in the material behind this stage.

The ledger itself — the three dispositions, the sayable sentence, the refusal requirement — is **design reasoning with no external warrant.** No source proposes it. **So is the distinction between the two kinds of growth**, and so is the use of a per-consumer token counter as a scope instrument: the documentation proposes that mechanism for fairness and billing, and says nothing about scope.

**The gateway documentation is definitional and contains no measurement.** It states what the platform does, not how well it performs: no benchmarks, no latency figures, no cost-reduction data and no comparison against other products. Its architectural reasoning is sound and asserted rather than demonstrated. **Capability availability varies by service tier**, two of the capabilities it describes are **preview**, and the correctness risk in semantic caching is not discussed in the source at all — that caution is this repository's analysis. **The privacy surface created by logging prompts and completions is likewise not addressed**, despite the source recommending that logging for billing and auditing.

**The productivity evidence establishes a direction in one narrow population and nothing more.** Very small developer sample; expert maintainers in codebases they know intimately, which is close to a worst case for assistance; self-reported time; one fixed early tooling generation; an explicit non-claims list published by the authors that must be cited alongside the headline; and **the research group itself now labels the result out of date and has redesigned the study**, because participants will no longer reliably work without the tooling. A later run pointed the other way and its own authors judge the signal unreliable. **No magnitude from any of it appears here.** The claim this session carries from it is only that felt speed and measured speed can diverge, and that experience alone did not correct the divergence.
