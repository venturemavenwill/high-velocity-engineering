# S042 · Scope That Grows, and the Backlog Item That Does Not

**Module:** [M09](/wiki/modules/M09.md) · **Quarter:** [Q5](/wiki/quarters/Q5.md) · **Complexity class:** 3
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** none
**Whitepaper:** [WP-042](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-042.md)
**Retrieves from:** S024, S025, S041
**Entry state:** either

---

## The situation

You are building the slice. Halfway through, you find something adjacent that obviously ought to be done, and doing it now would take less time than writing it down.

That sentence is the whole session. Every engineer has said it, most of them were right about the time, and the aggregate effect across an engagement is a piece of work nobody can describe the boundary of.

The open playbook's response is mechanical and it is one of the few rules on that site with no judgement in it at all: **new work becomes a new backlog item, never a bigger old one.** Scope is not managed by intention. It is managed by refusing to let the container grow.

Answer these before instruction.

1. What did you add to the last thing you built because stopping to record it felt more expensive than doing it?
2. Who decides whether a change belongs to the work in front of you?
3. What is the difference between a request you decline and one you defer, from the requester's point of view?
4. If every piece of discovered work became a new item, what does your backlog look like at the end of the engagement, and is that bad?
5. What does it cost when this rule is applied wrongly?

**Item 4 is where the rule stops being free.** A mechanical rule produces a mechanical consequence, and a backlog that records everything and refuses nothing is a different failure with the same cause.

## What you must produce

A **scope ledger** covering one working period on your slice.

Each entry: what was discovered, how it was discovered, and its disposition — **absorbed into the current work**, **recorded as a new item**, or **refused** — with the reason, and with the sentence you would actually say to the person who asked for it.

The ledger closes with two lines: the total your ledger would add to the engagement if every recorded item were built, and the ones you would decline if somebody asked today.

## What good looks like

- **Every entry has a disposition and none of them is blank.** An undisposed discovery is an absorbed one.
- **At least one entry is refused rather than recorded.** A ledger with no refusals is a queue.
- **The absorbed entries are justified against the slice definition** from [S041](/wiki/seminars/S041.md), not against convenience.
- **The sentences are sayable.** A refusal you would not deliver in the room is a refusal you will not make.
- **The decision-rights question is answered.** The ledger names who decides, and it is not always you.
- **Where a discovery invalidates the design rather than extending it, the ledger says so** and points at a decision record from [S024](/wiki/seminars/S024.md) rather than at an item.

## How it goes wrong

- **The growing item** — the work in front of you quietly becomes everything discovered near it. *Warning sign:* you cannot say when the current work will be finished, only that it is nearly done.
- **The graveyard backlog** — the rule is followed perfectly, nothing is ever refused, and the record becomes an inventory of obligations nobody will honour. *Warning sign:* item 4 felt like a virtue.
- **The silent absorption** — the addition is real, it is small, it is not recorded, and it is invisible at review. *Warning sign:* your change contains something you would have to explain.
- **The scope argument inside the review** — the boundary is litigated in the comments on the change rather than settled before it. *Warning sign:* the longest thread on your change is about whether something belongs in it.
- **The deferral that is a refusal** — you record an item you have no intention of building, because recording is easier than declining. *Warning sign:* your ledger has no refusals and a very long record.
- **The costless addition** — production is cheap enough that adding the adjacent thing takes almost no effort, so the rule feels like bureaucracy. *Warning sign:* your justification is about how little it cost rather than about whether it belonged.

## Do it

Keep the ledger live rather than reconstructing it. A ledger written at the end of the period is a justification of the period.

**First, settle decision rights before you need them.** Write down, in one line, who decides whether a discovery is in scope. If the answer is *me*, say what would have to be true for it to be somebody else. The playbook's review guidance closes this loophole from both sides and the pairing is worth holding together: an out-of-scope issue **may not block** the change in front of you, and an in-scope change **may not be deferred** by its author. Two rules, one boundary, and neither of them tells you where the boundary is.

**Second, notice that the rule is mechanical and the judgement it defers is not.** *Is this in scope?* is exactly the question the rule does not answer. Two people can apply the rule perfectly and disagree about every entry in your ledger, and the session's position is that this is a feature: the rule converts an invisible disagreement into a visible one, at a point where somebody can settle it.

**Third, use an agent to find your absorptions.** Give it your slice definition and your change, and ask it to list everything present in the change that the definition does not require. Then argue with the list. **The value is in the entries you disagree with**, because those are the ones where your boundary is doing work you had not articulated.

## Show it

Take your three hardest entries to one other person and say the sentence out loud — the one you would say to the person who asked.

They have one job: to tell you whether the sentence declines, defers or concedes. **Most engineers discover their refusals are deferrals**, and the discovery is more useful in this room than in the customer's.

## What this does not establish

Nothing here shows that scope discipline improves delivery.

The playbook states the rule as a standing commitment and **supplies no evidence for it**, in common with every other practice on the site. The internal corpus contains **no failed engagement and no cost side**, so it cannot supply a case in which scope growth caused a delivery to fail, because it reports no delivery that failed.

**The rule's real warrant is that it is checkable**, which is a property of the rule rather than a claim about outcomes: an item that grew is visible in its own history, and an item that was created is visible in the backlog. That is why this session teaches it. Whether checkability produces better software is not established anywhere in the material behind this stage.

The ledger itself — the three dispositions, the sayable sentence, the refusal requirement — is **design reasoning with no external warrant.** No source proposes it.
