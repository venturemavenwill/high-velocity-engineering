## The situation

You have a handover, a withdrawal trigger, a dependency disposal record and a rehearsal in which somebody could not do something.

Today the recipient runs the whole stack in front of you — deploys a change, exercises the gateway policy that governs it, runs the evaluation that says whether it still works, and reads the posture report that says whether it is safe. **You may not speak.** Then you decide whether to leave, and you defend the decision to somebody whose job is to argue that the recipient is not ready.

This session is not a specification check. **It is a judgement about you** — whether you can be trusted to decide when a running system may be left with other people — and it is formed from everything observed across this stage rather than from what you produce this morning.

**Answer these in writing before reading further.** Item 4 is the session's core.

1. What is your decision: leave, leave with a named condition attached, or do not leave yet?
2. What observation is that decision resting on? Not which document — which observation, of whom, doing what.
3. What did you see in the rehearsal that argues against your decision, and why did you decide anyway?
4. If you are asking the recipient to sign, what is the signature evidence of?
5. What would have made you say no, and did you look for it?

**A signature records that somebody was willing to accept something. Only the rehearsal records whether they can operate it.** A handover with a signature and no rehearsal has transferred blame rather than a system.

## What you must produce

**A transfer decision**, with the acceptance record the recipient is being asked to sign, and a defence of the decision.

1. **The decision** — leave, leave with a named condition, or do not leave yet — and the observation it rests on.
2. **The acceptance record**: what is accepted, by whom, with what authority, on what date.
3. **What the signatory is not accepting** — the unowned lines, the dependencies documented rather than disposed, the acts the rehearsal showed they cannot yet perform. This section is usually missing and is the one the recipient will read first.
4. **The supervision level you are claiming**, at sub-level resolution, not merely *supervised* or *unsupervised*.
5. **The standard your evidence actually meets** — how many observers, on how many occasions, from how many evidence types — stated on the face of the record rather than implied.
6. **The evidence against your own decision**, presented by you before the challenger finds it.
7. **The defence**, short and live.

## The material

### A signature is a claim, not a receipt

Start with the sentence the whole session turns on. **The recipient signs because signing is a claim they are making, not a receipt you are collecting.**

That distinction changes what the document has to contain. A receipt records that something arrived. A claim records what its author is asserting, and a claim can be wrong — which means it must be specific enough to be wrong. *We accept the system* is a receipt. *We accept operation of the retrieval pipeline at the standard demonstrated on the fourteenth, excluding the threshold-review act, which we cannot yet perform and which remains with the supplier until March* is a claim.

**The not-accepting section is what converts one into the other**, and it is the section that gets deleted, because it is the only part of the document that creates work for somebody.

Asking a recipient to sign a receipt when you needed a claim is the commonest way a handover transfers blame instead of a system. **They signed, so it is theirs.** The signature is then quoted for years by people who were not present, against a document that recorded nothing about capability.

### What entrustment is, and what today's evidence actually licenses

The framework this session is built on comes from clinical education, and its central move is to make the object of judgement a unit of work rather than a person ([research/08-assessment-epas/ten-cate-epa-framework.md](/research/08-assessment-epas/ten-cate-epa-framework.md)).

An **entrustable professional activity** is a bounded piece of practice with a beginning and an end that a supervisor could delegate whole. Entrustment answers *would I let them do this unsupervised, and what would I still want to see?* — and it is revisable in both directions.

The part this session needs most is the scale, because a handover has more resolution than *supervised* or *unsupervised* and throwing that resolution away is how an honest observation becomes a vague claim. Five levels run from present and observing, through executing with a supervisor in the room, executing with a supervisor quickly available, working unsupervised, to supervising others. **The sub-levels are where a real transfer sits:** level 3 splits into all findings double-checked, key findings double-checked, and a distant supervisor with prompt review; level 4 splits into remote monitoring and none. **The distance between "I check everything you did" and "I check the important parts" is the whole of a support taper**, and a record that cannot express it has not described the arrangement it is proposing.

The evidence standard is where today's exercise falls short, and the shortfall must be on the record rather than in a footnote. A **summative** entrustment decision — one with "the nature of certification", taken "for the future responsibilities of the learner from that moment on" — **"should include an agreement between multiple observers that this entrustment is justified, to avoid subjective bias, and multiple observations"**, drawing on four evidence types: direct observation, longitudinal observation, case-based discussion, and product evaluation. **Ad-hoc** entrustment is the continuous informal judgement made when a supervisor feels the skills match the complexity and the risk is acceptable.

**One observer, one occasion, one evidence type is ad-hoc. Say so.**

The primer also names the five things that actually drive an ad-hoc judgement, and they will operate on you today whether or not you acknowledge them: perceived features of the learner; **the supervisor's own propensity to delegate**; the complexity of the activity; the context; and the nature of the relationship between the two parties. **Two of those five are about you and one is about your relationship**, which is three-fifths of a judgement you are about to describe as being about them.

And the mechanism for probing understanding is the direct ancestor of today's defence. An **entrustment-based discussion** is a short focused conversation, usually after the activity, that "should probe the student's deep understanding of what was done, but should include 'what-if' questions, to grasp whether the learner would know what to do what unexpected or familiar situations would arise." **The what-if requirement is what distinguishes it from a walkthrough**, and the reason is stated plainly: "an entrustment decision implies the acceptance of risk, as it can never be fully predicted what the student will encounter."

The primer records one more property nobody writes down: **entrustment expires** where the activity has not been practised for a long time.

**Its limits:** a clinical model with patients, legally responsible supervisors and a licensure endpoint, so a course must construct its stakes artificially and the construction is the hardest part of the transfer; guidance from the concept's originator, reporting adoption rather than effectiveness; and a full evidence standard expensive enough that **any course adopts a reduced version and should say so.** This one is reduced.

### Why the decision is written as narrative and not as a score

The assessment literature that studies what assessors actually do is unusually direct about this, and its central finding inverts the usual assumption ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/govaerts-narrative-assessment.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/govaerts-narrative-assessment.md)).

When assessors read real competency portfolios while thinking aloud, **"assessors mainly relied on narrative feedback to inform their judgment, because this provided meaningful and detailed information about the student's development, strengths and weaknesses... Grades and qualifications were merely used to confirm impressions based on narratives."** The numbers were not the evidence. They were a check on a conclusion already formed from the prose.

Three further findings bear directly on how this session is run, and each is uncomfortable.

**Rater variability is structural rather than a training deficiency.** The related study of workplace-based assessment reports that idiosyncratic rater effects account for a substantial share of the variance in performance ratings, and — the part that forecloses the obvious fix — that **"levels of rater idiosyncrasy were substantial and unrelated to rater expertise."** Attempts to standardise instruments and train raters "have met with mixed success at best."

**Assessors anchor.** They "were inclined to stick to their initial judgments even when confronted with seemingly disconfirming evidence", and their final judgements were more elaborate than their preliminary ones but **not substantially different**. Additional reading produced better articulation of the same conclusion, not revision of it. The same study found that **"most raters started to develop person schemas the moment they began to observe trainee performance"** — the impression forms during the first minute and the rest is elaboration.

**Agreement on the outcome conceals disagreement in the reasoning.** Assessors "reached similar final (pass–fail) judgments of students' professional competence" and "differed in their information-processing approaches and the reasoning behind their judgments", with the divergence driven by their beliefs about which evidence is trustworthy, their private theories of what competence is, and the inferences they drew from the same observation. **Two assessors can both pass you for incompatible reasons, and the scores will not show it.**

The remedy the authors recommend is not a better rubric. It is that high-stakes decisions **"should not be made individually, but should result from group discussions"**, and that assessors should **"thoroughly substantiate their judgments"** — because substantiation is what makes divergent reasoning visible and therefore discussable.

**That is why this session records a level with a mandatory narrative and no mark.** A mark would hide exactly the information the decision consists of.

**Its limits:** two studies from one research group and one institution; think-aloud methods cannot capture fast or automatic processing and risk participants confabulating explanations for the researcher; the portfolio study used constructed mock portfolios; and several of the group's most-cited works are paywalled and were not consulted. **The magnitudes it reports are recorded in the research layer and are not asserted here.**

### Why the defence is oral, and what that changes

A defence is not a presentation with questions afterwards. It is the assessment.

The clearest publicly documented implementation of this pattern in a computing course makes the reasoning explicit ([research/08-assessment-epas/cmu-oral-defense-and-token-economy-patterns.md](/research/08-assessment-epas/cmu-oral-defense-and-token-economy-patterns.md)). Its labs are graded in-session by "showing source code, demoing executions, and verbally answering a few questions to demonstrate your understanding", and collaboration on those labs is permitted **including outright copying**, because "you will have to present and explain your solution to the TA on your own."

**Once the assessment is oral, the artefact stops being the evidence and policing its provenance stops being necessary.** That is the design's real economy, and it is why this programme's tool policy can be permissive: the same course tells students it is "open to using AI tools" and "actually strongly recommend\[s\] it for many tasks", with exactly two prohibitions — examinations, and **"live conversations with course staff"**, where using any form of AI to generate answers is cheating.

The same source supplies the warning that connects tool use to the defence: **"Using code generation tools without understanding the generated code will likely create challenges when answering TA questions."** The defence is where comprehension debt becomes visible, and it is the only place in the whole stage where it does.

**Its limits:** course policy documentation from an offering that has run for several years, not a study of assessment. **No effectiveness data is published** — no grade distributions, no comparison against the exam-based version it replaced, and no measurement of whether oral verification detects understanding that written work misses. Treat it as well-tested practice, which is a real form of evidence, and not as an evaluated intervention.

### The recipient has to be able to say no, and so do you

The acceptance record only works if the person signing it can decline to.

What is being asked of the recipient today is to state, in front of their supplier and possibly their own management, that there are things they cannot do. That is an interpersonal risk before it is an administrative act, and the literature on such risks is specific about what people do with them: "No one wakes up in the morning excited to go to work and look ignorant, incompetent, or disruptive. These are called interpersonal risks, and they are what nearly everyone seeks to avoid, not always consciously" ([research/04-professional-formation/edmondson-psychological-safety.md](/research/04-professional-formation/edmondson-psychological-safety.md)). The learned response is "Don't want to look incompetent? Don't admit to mistakes or weaknesses."

**A recipient who cannot afford to say *we cannot do that* will sign a record that says they can.** And that record will be quoted at them.

The quantitative synthesis of the same literature tests whether the construct explains anything beyond adjacent ones such as positive leader relations and work engagement, and ranks its antecedents rather than listing them ([research/04-professional-formation/frazier-psychological-safety-meta-analysis.md](/research/04-professional-formation/frazier-psychological-safety-meta-analysis.md)). **That incremental-validity test is the sceptical question, and it is why the construct is kept here rather than assumed.**

**The limits on both are real:** the book read is a trade press statement by the construct's originator, with case material drawn almost entirely from secondary sources and a narrative rather than systematic review; **the 1999 paper that defined the construct empirically is unread in this repository and the book does not verify it**; the meta-analysis note rests on its abstract. **Direction and mechanism only.**

The same condition applies to you. **Under a challenger instructed to press, the cheapest available move is to concede** — and a reversal that introduces no new information is not a change of mind, it is a change of subject. The efficacy literature supplies the mechanism to watch for in yourself: under taxing demand, those with a low sense of efficacy in a domain "become more and more erratic in their analytic thinking and lower their aspirations" ([research/04-professional-formation/bandura-self-efficacy.md](/research/04-professional-formation/bandura-self-efficacy.md)). **The degradation is cognitive, not merely confidence-related**, which is why a defence rehearsed once is a different performance from a defence attempted cold.

**Its limits:** a conference collection edited by the field's founder, with no disconfirming study in his own chapter; settings in schools, families, clinics, migration and addiction treatment; **no study of adult professionals learning a technical craft**; self-report throughout; the 1977 paper unread here.

### The whole stack, and why running it end to end is the only honest test

The four acts in today's demonstration are chosen because each belongs to a different surface, and because a recipient can plausibly hold three and not the fourth.

**Deployment** exercises whether they can put a change into the running system on their own credentials — and the capacity documentation supplies the trap most likely to fire afterwards rather than today: dedicated capacity is held **"whether or not requests are being made"**, and **"Having PTU quota doesn't guarantee that capacity is available"** ([research/06-microsoft-platform/provisioned-throughput-for-foundry-models.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/provisioned-throughput-for-foundry-models.md)).

**Gateway policy** exercises whether they can change the rule that governs consumption. The gateway documentation's own framing is that this is where multi-tenant concerns live — authenticating access, load balancing across endpoints, monitoring and logging interactions, managing token usage and quotas across applications, and enabling self-service ([research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md)). Its scarcity argument is the one a recipient must be able to reproduce: tokens are the scarce resource, quota is assigned per minute, and it must be distributed "across your model consumers" so that **"one app doesn't use the whole TPM quota and block other apps"**. **A recipient who cannot change a token limit cannot respond to their own noisy neighbour**, and that is a capability, not a configuration detail.

**Evaluation** exercises whether they can say what the system's quality signal means — which split strategy produced it, where the threshold came from, and how many repetitions stand behind it, since "how you split determines *what you're evaluating*" and the threshold is policy rather than measurement ([research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-framework-and-evaluations-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-framework-and-evaluations-docs.md)).

**Posture check** exercises whether they can find out what the system is exposing. The governance documentation states the mechanism that makes this urgent in one sentence: **"Because of the power and speed AI can proactively surface content that might be obsolete, over-permissioned, or lacking governance controls, generative AI amplifies the problem of oversharing data"** ([research/06-microsoft-platform/purview-dspm-for-ai-and-defender-ai-spm-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/purview-dspm-for-ai-and-defender-ai-spm-docs.md)). **Deploying a capable retrieval system converts every dormant permissions error in an organisation into a live one**, and the recipient inherits that conversion.

**Read the operational limits of the posture tooling as the resolution of the instrument rather than as its accuracy.** The assessment described runs on a cadence over a sample of the estate, with stated per-location item ceilings, stated site limits for item-level scanning, and multi-day latencies before results appear. **A tool that scans a sample weekly with multi-day latency gives a posture estimate, not a real-time control.** One default is a trap worth naming in the acceptance record: without an explicit policy, auditing events are captured for some Copilot surfaces **but not the prompts and responses** — knowing an interaction happened is not knowing what was said.

**All four platform sources carry the same limits and they are severe.** First-party documentation whose authority is definitional; **no adoption data, no failure-rate data, no cost comparison, no evidence that any recommended mapping produces a better outcome.** The evaluation and posture pages were read through search excerpts rather than full fetches in part; **the fetched governance page is explicitly labelled a classic version that has been replaced**, and one licensing boundary on the posture side moves on a stated future date. **No detection rate, no false-positive rate and no evaluator agreement figure is published anywhere in this family, and none is asserted here or anywhere in this wiki.**

### The source that has no exit criteria at all

The practice corpus behind this programme mechanises the teardown of its own assets to the level of a register state change — and contains **no handover, no named receiving owner, no exit criteria, no acceptance, no support taper, and no evidentiary requirement that the teardown was performed at all** ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md)).

**Its question is *is our house clean?*, not *can they run it without us?*** This session exists because only the second is an engineering problem, and it is written past its source rather than from it.

**Its limits:** access-controlled internal material, paraphrased and never quoted, with no reference, footnote, study or dataset anywhere across roughly ninety documents. **Its case studies all succeed and it reports no engagement where capability transfer failed** — an absence of looking rather than evidence of success.

## The workflow

**The recipient runs deployment, gateway policy, evaluation and posture check, in that order, on their own credentials. You may not speak.**

**Step 1 — agree the silence and its one exception, in writing, before anybody sits down.** You may not speak, ask, prompt, point or make a noise. The single exception is your written intervention condition from [S079](/wiki/seminars/S079.md), and it fires only on consequence outside the exercise. **If you speak, the run is over and the record says why.**

**Step 2 — deployment.** They take one change from branch to running, unaided, and then state what they did *not* deploy. Record every access refusal separately. **A refusal is a result, not a delay.**

**Step 3 — gateway policy.** They change the token limit that governs one consumer and demonstrate that it took effect. The policy fragment is short and the question afterwards is shorter:

```xml
<!-- inbound policy on the model-facing API -->
<llm-token-limit counter-key="@(context.Subscription.Id)"
                 tokens-per-minute="500"
                 estimate-prompt-tokens="false"
                 remaining-tokens-variable-name="remainingTokens" />
```

**Ask them, afterwards, what the counter key means and what happens when a second application arrives.** A recipient who can edit the number and cannot answer that has learned a control panel, not a system.

**Step 4 — evaluation.** They run the suite and then answer two questions without looking anything up: which split strategy is in force, and where the threshold came from. **A run they cannot interpret is a command they executed.**

**Step 5 — posture check.** They produce the current posture report and answer one question: what does this instrument *not* see? The expected shape of a good answer names the sampling, the cadence, the latency before results appear, and — if it applies to their estate — that prompt and response content is not captured without an explicit policy.

**Step 6 — write the acceptance record, and write the half everybody deletes.** The recipient reads it aloud before signing. If they will not read it aloud, it is a receipt:

```yaml
# handover/acceptance.yml   <- a CLAIM the recipient is making, not a receipt you collect.
#                              Every value is a fact about ONE date and ONE demonstration.
demonstration:
  date: "<YYYY-MM-DD>"
  operated_by: "<recipient's name>"        # if this is the author's name, stop.
  credentials: "<recipient's own principal>"
  author_spoke: false                      # if true, this record is void and says so
  observers: ["<name>"]                    # count them. one is ad-hoc.
  occasions: 1
  evidence_types: ["direct_observation", "case_based_discussion"]
  standard_met: "ad-hoc"                   # NOT summative: that needs multiple observers,
                                           # multiple occasions, and four evidence types

accepted:
  - act: "deploy a change from branch to running, unaided"
    supervision_level: "3c"                # distant supervisor, findings promptly reviewed
    demonstrated_on: "<YYYY-MM-DD>"
    expires: "<YYYY-MM-DD>"                # entrustment lapses through disuse
  - act: "change a token limit and demonstrate its effect"
    supervision_level: "3b"                # key findings double-checked

not_accepted:                              # THE SECTION THAT GETS DELETED. It is the record.
  - act: "review evaluator thresholds and decide whether the system has degraded"
    reason: "attempted on <date>; could not state what the score was about"
    stays_with: "<supplier> until <date>"
    who_agreed: "<named person on the supplier side>"
  - dependency: "DEP-002 capacity commitment"
    reason: "documented, not disposed; bills while idle; term expires <date>"
    decision_owed_by: "<named person>"
    decision_due: "<YYYY-MM-DD>"

against_this_decision:                     # presented by the AUTHOR, before the challenger finds it
  - "<the rehearsal observation that argues the other way, and why I decided anyway>"

signature:
  records: "willingness to accept the above, on this date"
  does_not_record: "demonstrated capability beyond the acts listed under 'accepted'"
  read_aloud_by_signatory: true
```

**Step 7 — defend it live, and be asked the standing question.** The artefacts are read beforehand; this is not a presentation. Whatever else is asked, you will be asked: **what would you have needed to see to decide the other way?**

**Step 8 — attack your own record before the challenger does.** One prompt: *here is my acceptance record and my rehearsal record. Find every act listed under "accepted" that the rehearsal record does not support, and every line under "not accepted" that is really an accepted risk with no owner. Then write the three hardest what-if questions a receiving organisation would ask about the acts I claimed.* Read it for the act you claimed and did not observe. Keep what is better than your own reasoning and record what you rejected. **Record separately that this instrument was not independent of the system it audited** — and note that using any such tool during the live defence itself is prohibited, for the same reason the defence exists.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** Three things arrive in one morning that no document produces. **The access wall**, in the first minutes. **The comprehension gap**, at steps 3, 4 and 5, where a recipient who can operate every control and explain none of them becomes visible in a way no runbook review ever makes them. And **your own silence**, which is harder than it sounds and which is the only direct evidence in the whole stage about whether you can let go.

## What good looks like

- The decision names the observation it rests on, and the observation is of a person performing an act, not of a document existing.
- **The evidence that argues against the decision is presented by you**, before it is found by the challenger.
- The signature is described accurately, in the recipient's hearing: it records acceptance, not competence, and the record says so on its face.
- The not-accepting section is real and specific. **A record that says the recipient accepts everything is a record that has not been read by them.**
- **The supervision level is stated at sub-level resolution.** *Supervised* and *unsupervised* are not levels; they are the two ends of a range, and every real handover sits between them.
- **The standard your evidence meets is stated, not implied.** One observer on one occasion is ad-hoc, and saying so costs nothing and prevents everything.
- The recipient can explain, not merely operate: what the counter key means, what the score is about, and what the posture instrument does not see.
- If the decision is *do not leave yet*, it names the condition, who observes it, and what happens to the fixed end date — because saying not yet is only a decision if it has a cost attached.
- Under challenge, you distinguish what you observed from what you inferred, and you do not defend an inference as though it were an observation.

## How it goes wrong

- **The signature as proof** — the recipient signs, and the learner treats the signature as evidence of capability. *Warning sign:* your defence begins with the fact that they agreed.
- **The suppressed rehearsal** — the incapacity observed at [S079](/wiki/seminars/S079.md) is not in the acceptance record, because including it would invite an argument. *Warning sign:* your acceptance record is shorter than your rehearsal record.
- **The overclaimed standard** — one observation on one morning is written up as though it certified something. *Warning sign:* the words *ad-hoc*, *one observer* and *one occasion* appear nowhere in your record.
- **The control panel operator** — the recipient performed all four acts correctly and can explain none of them. *Warning sign:* every demonstration succeeded and no question was asked.
- **Departure dressed as transfer** — the decision is *leave*, and every reason given is about your side: the date, the budget, the next engagement. *Warning sign:* no sentence in your defence is about what the recipient can do.
- **The refusal with no cost** — the decision is *do not leave yet*, and nothing about it is expensive, so it is not a decision. *Warning sign:* your condition has no observer and no date.
- **Conceding under pressure** — challenged hard, you reverse a decision you had good grounds for. *Warning sign:* your reversal introduced no new information.
- **The broken silence** — you spoke during the demonstration, and the record does not say so. *Warning sign:* you remember helping and your record does not.

## Assessment

**This is an entrustment decision, not a specification check.** There is no checklist here, and nothing in this section should be read as one. What is recorded is an ordinal supervision level with a mandatory narrative, against a single judgement:

> **Can this learner be trusted to decide when a running system may be left with somebody else?**

**How the level is formed.** From accumulated observation across [S071](/wiki/seminars/S071.md)–[S079](/wiki/seminars/S079.md), by more than one assessor, on more than one occasion, with each assessor recording level and narrative independently before any discussion. The artefacts supply the evidence; today's defence tests it. Divergence between assessors of more than one sub-level is investigated rather than averaged. See [Assessment Architecture](/wiki/program/03-Assessment-Architecture.md) for the scale and the rater rules.

**Why independent recording before discussion is mandatory rather than tidy.** The assessment literature this programme's architecture rests on found that assessors reaching the same outcome frequently reached it by incompatible reasoning, and that the divergence is invisible in the outcome ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/govaerts-narrative-assessment.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/govaerts-narrative-assessment.md)). It also found that assessors anchor early and that further evidence elaborates rather than revises the initial judgement. **Discussion before independent recording converts two judgements into one**, and the one it converts them into is whichever was spoken first.

**What the assessors are reading for**, none of which is a threshold to be met:

- Whether the learner's account of the recipient's readiness matched what was observable in the rehearsal record — in either direction. Overstating and understating are the same defect.
- Whether the learner presented the evidence against their own decision before being shown it.
- Whether they held the line between a signature and a demonstrated capability under pressure, in front of somebody who wanted the line blurred.
- Whether the standard claimed on the acceptance record matched the evidence actually held.
- Whether a decision to leave was grounded in the recipient's state rather than in the delivery organisation's calendar.
- Whether they could say what would have changed their mind, and whether they had gone looking for it.

**A narrative is mandatory and names a specific observed action.** A level recorded without one is rejected by moderation rather than by the assessor.

**What may not enter the judgement.** Nothing the learner surfaced about their own error in a rehearsal debrief may be used against them here. A learner who reports that they intervened outside their written condition has produced the most useful evidence available in [S079](/wiki/seminars/S079.md), and a programme that penalises that report will not receive it again.

**No AI tool may be used during the live defence.** That prohibition is narrow and deliberate: tools are recommended everywhere else in this programme, and the reason they are excluded here is the same reason the defence exists. **Once the assessment is oral, the artefact stops being the evidence** ([research/08-assessment-epas/cmu-oral-defense-and-token-economy-patterns.md](/research/08-assessment-epas/cmu-oral-defense-and-token-economy-patterns.md)), and the only thing being assessed is what the learner holds.

## Exercises

**These are not assessed.** They are preparation for a defence that is. The distinction matters here more than anywhere else in the programme: nothing you write below enters the entrustment judgement, and everything you learn from it will.

**1 · Turn four receipts into claims.** Rewrite each acceptance line so that it could be wrong:

- *The customer accepts the system.*
- *Operations has been trained on the runbook.*
- *The evaluation harness has been handed over.*
- *Security has reviewed the deployment.*

> **Model answers.** *Operations accepts running the retrieval pipeline at level 3c — distant supervision with prompt review — demonstrated on the fourteenth, and does not accept the threshold-review act, which stays with the supplier until March.* · *Two named operators completed the runbook to termination unaided on separate occasions; a third has not attempted it.* · *Operations accepts running the suite and reading a failing check; it does not accept changing thresholds, which requires a justification nobody currently holds.* · *Security reviewed the deployment configuration on the ninth against the four infrastructure checks; it has not reviewed the retrieval permissions, which is where the oversharing risk sits.* **The test in every case is whether a reader six months later could establish that the line was false.**

**2 · Place five arrangements on the sub-level scale.** For each, say which level it is and what would move it one step:

- You review every change they make before it goes out.
- You review the ones touching the retrieval configuration.
- You are contactable and see a weekly summary.
- You see nothing and hear nothing.
- They review a more junior colleague's work.

> **Model answers.** All findings double-checked; key findings double-checked; distant supervision with prompt review; unsupervised without monitoring; supervising others. **The exercise is not the mapping.** It is noticing that four of these five are routinely described by the same word, and that a handover expressed in that word has not described the arrangement anybody actually agreed to. **A support taper is a sequence of sub-levels with dates against them**, and if you cannot write it as one, you do not have a taper — you have an end date and a hope.

**3 · Go back to your own item 4, then break it.** Find what you wrote for opening item 4 — what the signature is evidence of. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference.

> **Criterion.** A pass says the signature records **willingness to accept**, on a date, for a listed set of acts — and says explicitly that it records nothing about capability beyond those acts. **Anything of the form *that they are ready*, *that they understand*, or *that the handover is complete* fails**, and it fails for a reason worth carrying: every one of those is a claim about the recipient made by you and signed by them, which is the exact inversion of what the document is for. **A signature is a claim they are making, not a receipt you are collecting.**
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Ask three what-if questions about one act, then answer them yourself.** Take an act you are about to record as accepted, and write the three counterfactuals a receiving organisation would ask — the unexpected situations the demonstration did not contain.

> **Model answer shape.** For *deploy a change unaided*: what if the deployment fails halfway and leaves the previous version serving? What if the capacity you deployed onto is not available next Tuesday? What if the person who did it is on leave and the change is urgent? **The what-if requirement is what distinguishes an entrustment discussion from a walkthrough** — retrospective explanation shows the recipient understood the case they met, and only counterfactual questioning is evidence about the cases they did not. **If you cannot answer your own three, you are not entitled to record the act as accepted.**

**5 · Run the whole stack once yourself, silently, with a partner as recipient.** Using the workflow, have another learner execute deployment, gateway policy, evaluation and posture check while you say nothing. Then write the acceptance record together.

> **What you should find.** That the silence is much harder than the record-keeping, that the comprehension gap appears at the gateway or the evaluation rather than at the deployment, and that your first draft of the not-accepting section is empty. **If your partner completed all four acts and you have nothing under *not accepted*, ask the what-if questions from exercise 4 before concluding it.** The commonest cause of an empty not-accepting section is not a capable recipient; it is a demonstration that contained no unexpected situation.

**6 · Write the narrative an assessor would write about you, then check it against the rule.** In three sentences, record a supervision level for yourself on the judgement in the Assessment section, and name one specific observed action supporting it.

> **Scoring criterion.** A pass names an action — something you did, on a date, that somebody could have watched. **A fail names a quality**: *thorough*, *balanced*, *considered*. The rule that a narrative must name a specific observed action is not a stylistic preference; it exists because the assessment literature finds that assessors reason from prose and confirm from scores, and that prose without a concrete referent conceals divergent reasoning rather than exposing it. **A narrative made of adjectives is a score in disguise.**

## What this does not establish

**That the learner would make this decision the same way when it is real**, and the gap is not small.

Here, the recipient cannot be harmed, the system carries no consequence into next month, the challenger is playing a part, and the learner's own livelihood does not depend on the engagement continuing. Every pressure that makes this decision hard in practice — the commercial interest in remaining involved, the sponsor who wants it finished, the colleague already assigned elsewhere, the recipient who will be blamed either way — is absent or simulated.

**The entrustment machinery is the best-warranted thing in this session and its warrant is clinical.** It comes from a setting with patients, legally responsible supervisors and a licensure endpoint. This programme constructs its stakes artificially, and the construction is the hardest part of the transfer. **The full evidence standard the framework specifies — multiple trained observers, multiple observations, four evidence types, deliberated — is expensive, and this session's version is reduced.**

**The narrative-assessment findings come from two studies by one research group at one institution**, using think-aloud methods that cannot capture fast or automatic processing and that risk participants confabulating explanations. Several of the group's most-cited works are paywalled and were not consulted. **The magnitudes it reports are not asserted here.**

**The oral-defence pattern is well-tested practice and not an evaluated intervention.** No effectiveness data is published for it — no grade distributions, no comparison against what it replaced, and no measurement of whether oral verification detects understanding that written work misses.

**The safety literature is read at two removes**, and its foundational empirical paper is unread in this repository. **A session cannot manufacture the conditions under which a recipient can safely say no**, and this one does not claim to.

**The platform documentation is definitional, single-vendor and perishable.** One governance page consulted is explicitly labelled a superseded classic version; one licensing boundary moves on a stated future date; several evaluators are marked preview. **No detection rate, no false-positive rate, no evaluator agreement with human judgement and no adoption figure is published anywhere in that family, and none is asserted here.**

**No source behind this programme reports whether any delivered system survived its delivery team.** There is no sustainment evidence anywhere in the corpus, no post-deployment record for any case, and therefore no basis for claiming that a well-made leaving decision produces a better outcome than a badly made one.

What an entrustment level records here is narrow and worth stating in the words it should be read in: **this learner, observed on these occasions, reasoned about leaving in a way that would be defensible to a colleague who was not present.** That is all. It is not a prediction.

**Direction and mechanism only. No magnitude, no rate, no proportion.**
