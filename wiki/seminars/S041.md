# S041 · The Minimal Valuable Slice

**Module:** [M09](/wiki/modules/M09.md) · **Quarter:** [Q5](/wiki/quarters/Q5.md) · **Complexity class:** 3
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** Deployable rather than demoable — the Microsoft 365 Agents Toolkit's three lifecycle stages beside Foundry's smallest deployable unit and the Foundry Control Plane's setup tiers, delivered on a prototyping topology you have agreed in advance to throw away
**Whitepaper:** [WP-041](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-041.md)
**Retrieves from:** S021, S026, S039, S040
**Entry state:** either

## The situation

Somebody has committed to the thing. The proof is priced, the unproven list is written, and the engagement now starts spending.

The room you are in on Monday morning contains four engineers, a subscription provisioned on Friday, a customer architect who has already sent you a diagram, and a sponsor who has told their own leadership that something will be visible before the end of the month. Every one of those is a pressure toward the same first increment — the one that can be started immediately.

The first question is not what to build. It is what to build **first**, and the honest answer is almost never the answer that arrives naturally, because the answer that arrives naturally is the part that is easiest to start.

**Answer these in writing before reading further.**

1. What is the smallest thing you could put in front of somebody that would change what they believe?
2. Who is the recipient of your first increment — a role, or better, a person?
3. What were you about to build first because it was easy to begin rather than because it was worth having?
4. Which frozen part of the problem space forces the shape of your slice, and which part only looks frozen?
5. If the engagement stopped the day after this increment, what would remain that was worth the money?

**Item 5 is the one the design cares about.** Most learners answer it with a list of things that would have been *learned*, which is a proof answer rather than a delivery answer. [Q4](/wiki/quarters/Q4.md) is over. Something is supposed to remain, and it has to be something other than your own understanding.

## What you must produce

A **slice definition** on one page. Six parts, in this order.

1. **The increment**, stated as something a named recipient can do that they could not do before. Not a component. Not a capability. A thing a person does.
2. **The belief it is intended to change**, and whose belief it is.
3. **The exclusion list** — what is deliberately not in it, each item with the reason and **the date the exclusion was decided**.
4. **The frozen constraints that determined its shape**, separated from the fluid ones you chose not to move and could have.
5. **The residual-value statement**: what survives if the engagement ends the day after, expressed as an artefact or a capability rather than as a lesson.
6. **The domain sentence** — the increment in one sentence, using only words the customer's own experts already use, with any word you had to invent marked as invented.

## The material

### Everything short of realised value is activity, and that includes your architecture

The delivery practice this stage draws on carries one claim that is genuinely uncomfortable to put in front of engineers, and it is the best thing in the corpus. **A delivery can satisfy every agreed success criterion, use excellent architecture, follow textbook engineering fundamentals, ship to production — and still produce nothing of value.** Everything short of realised benefit is reclassified as activity rather than impact, and the reclassification is deliberately made to include the things engineers are proudest of: the team, the architecture, the fundamentals and the deployment ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md)).

Two limits travel with that claim and neither is optional. The corpus is **internal and access-controlled, is not publicly reachable, is not citable externally, and is paraphrased here rather than quoted.** And it fails its own test: of roughly thirty quantitative assertions across the delivery material, almost all measure activity — elapsed time, headcount, proportion of code generated, minutes to add a feature — rather than outcome. **A body of work whose central claim is that only measurable benefit counts supports itself almost entirely with measures of activity, and none of its figures appears anywhere in this wiki.**

The claim survives its own author's failure to meet it, because it is closer to a definition than to a finding. An increment nobody outside the delivery team can receive cannot change anybody's belief; an increment that changes nobody's belief has tested nothing; and a fortnight spent producing something that tested nothing is a fortnight of activity however good the code is.

The openly published playbook names the same unit and treats it as a standing commitment — **the minimal valuable slice, "the smallest increment that delivers customer value"** — and its own page on slices carries one instruction and only one: *"Do NOT work independently on your task without providing any updates to your team."* ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md), pages read 2026-07-30.) **The playbook offers no comparative evidence for that practice or for any other on the site.** Its pages carry footer dates spread across four years, roughly half of it is stub, link list or version-pinned recipe, it contradicts itself in at least a dozen places, and it asserts four unwarranted magnitude claims that this repository records as specimens and reproduces nowhere. Cite it by page and by the date that page was read, never as a whole.

### A slice is valuable when it is meaningful in the domain's own language

This is the test that stops a slice definition from being a work breakdown, and it comes from the design literature rather than from the delivery corpus.

The founding text of domain-driven design is organised around one instruction about language: build the model from the domain experts' own jargon, but **"cleaned up," to have sharper, narrower definitions** — and then hold the experts' veto open, because "the domain experts should object if these definitions diverge from the meanings accepted in the field" ([research/05-fde-craft/evans-domain-driven-design.md](/research/05-fde-craft/evans-domain-driven-design.md)). The diagnostic that follows is the one this session borrows: **"If sophisticated domain experts don't understand the model, there is something wrong with the model."** Note both hedges, because they are usually dropped when the line is quoted — the test is against *sophisticated* experts, and technical components of the design are explicitly exempt.

The same book supplies the sharpest available statement of what a first increment is for. On distillation: **"Make the CORE small."** On where to start: "except when the team has proven skills and the domain is very familiar, the first-cut system should be based on some part of the CORE DOMAIN, however simple." And on the elegant slice nobody asked for: **"Don't introduce a 'deeper model' that you couldn't convince a domain expert to use, no matter how elegant it seems."**

The failure case is worked in the book as a first-person account, and it is exactly the failure this session exists to prevent. A syndicated-loan project built its design around an object called *loan investment* — a term that "was not a banking term", which the business experts had said several times they did not understand, and which they had accepted anyway because "they had deferred to our software knowledge and assumed it was useful to the technical design." The experts noticed. They said so. They were overruled by politeness. **A slice whose central noun is a word your customer cannot use in a sentence has already lost its recipient**, and the domain sentence exists to make that visible in a minute rather than in a fortnight.

**Two limits.** The book **declares prerequisites** and makes no claim without them — "Development is iterative" and "Developers and domain experts have a close relationship", stated flatly as "prerequisites for applying the approach in this book." And this repository's note records that its publisher and date were **inherited from the reading register and not verified**, because the copy read carries no package metadata. It is a professional pattern book, not a study; its own conclusion follows up five of the author's projects and reports one as a partial failure.

### Minimal is a claim about the exclusion list, not about size

Here is the move this session installs, and it is the design's own rather than any source's.

A description of what will be built is compatible with almost any subsequent behaviour, because anything added later can be presented as having been part of the plan. **A written exclusion converts a later addition into a visible departure.** That is the whole mechanism, and it is why the assessed artefact is the list of what is absent.

The consequence is that *minimal* stops being a size adjective. A slice is not minimal because it is small; it is minimal because everything adjacent to it has been named and refused. Two increments of identical size, one with an empty exclusion list and one with a long dated one, are different artefacts: the first is a description, the second is a decision. **[S045](/wiki/seminars/S045.md) assesses exactly this distinction**, and it does so by asking on what day each exclusion became an exclusion.

The list also has to hurt. Without a requirement that at least one exclusion is painful, it fills with things nobody wanted, which is the increment written backwards in a different font.

### The freeze is half decision and half rumour, and you cannot tell by looking

[S026](/wiki/seminars/S026.md) installed the frozen-and-fluid classification, and this is where it gets spent.

The rule attached to it in the practice corpus is a rule against learned helplessness: even in an almost entirely frozen problem space there is always something that can be changed, and **constraint is not an excuse for doing nothing** ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md), **internal, paraphrased, with no outcome data of any kind**). The difficulty is that the two kinds of constraint are indistinguishable in their content. A decision somebody made and an assumption everybody inherited arrive in the same sentence: *we cannot do that here.*

They differ in exactly one observable property. **A decision has an author who could reverse it. An inherited assumption does not, and nobody can name one.** The question is therefore *who would have to agree to change this* — asked of colleagues rather than of the customer, and asked diagnostically rather than confrontationally, because you are not requesting the change. You are finding out whether there is anybody to request it from.

An untested freeze is a rumour, and a slice shaped by a rumour is smaller than it needed to be for a reason nobody can name.

### The order of work, and why the technical shape comes last

The delivery standard behind this stage makes an ordering claim rather than a methodological one, and the ordering is the substance: understand the customer's situation before configuring anything; establish the working environment before writing requirements; write business-level requirements before technical ones; **derive the technical specification from the business one rather than authoring the two in parallel** ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-fde-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-fde-delivery-practice.md), **internal, paraphrased, never linked; a plan for how work will be done, reporting no outcome, no evaluation and no comparison against any other way of working**).

The transferable part is the diagnosis rather than the sequence. A crew that writes the technical specification first has not merely reordered its documents. **It has committed to an architecture before it understood the problem, and the ordering rule exists so that the commitment becomes visible rather than arguable.** Your slice definition is the business-level artefact; the agent configuration you build below is derived from it, and if you find yourself writing the configuration first, that is the finding.

### The platform's smallest deployable unit, and the honest account of what it excludes

Foundry publishes two agent shapes and the line between them is not a capability line, it is a compute line. **Prompt agents** use "declarative config in portal or code", are "managed by Foundry with **no agent compute**", and are documented as suiting "agents that don't include custom code or require custom hosting". **Hosted agents** run "your code (containerized)" on managed container compute ([research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md)).

**That makes the prompt agent the smallest thing this platform will deploy for you**, and it is the right instrument for this session precisely because of what it will not do. It will not run your code. It will not hold logic you cannot express declaratively. Anything your slice needs that falls outside configuration is, on this platform, an argument for a different hosting shape — which is a decision, not a detail, and belongs on the exclusion list with a date.

Two further published facts bear directly on the slice. The **basic setup** is documented as being "for rapid prototyping and individual development when speed and ease of access are priorities" and, in the vendor's own words, it **"lacks network isolation"**; the standard setup provides "fine-grained control over data, security, and networking". Stating that plainly is good documentation, and the lesson is not *always choose standard*. It is that **prototyping topology and production topology are different things and the transition between them is a deliberate act** — which is an exclusion your definition should carry explicitly rather than discover at handover. And the options are **"not mutually exclusive"**: a component built one way can expose tools that a component built another way consumes, so the real question is frequently *which shape for this part* rather than *which platform for this system*.

**The limits are heavy and must travel with every use.** First-party selection guidance whose authority is definitional; **no adoption data, no failure-rate data, no cost comparison and no evidence that its recommended mappings produce better outcomes**; single-vendor by construction; **read through search excerpts rather than full page fetches, with several comparison tables truncated**; and **highly perishable**, with product names and capability boundaries actively changing.

### Deployable is a lifecycle, and two directories of it were written by somebody else

The difference between a slice that is deployable and one that is merely demoable is not code quality. **It is whether provisioning, deploying and publishing are three separate acts with three separate owners, or one gesture nobody can repeat.**

The vendor's own toolchain models exactly that separation and is worth reading for the model rather than for the commands. Its lifecycle is declared in a file with **three stages — provision, deploy, publish** — each a named list of actions, with sibling files for the local and sandbox targets, and the command-line interface maps onto the stages one to one ([research/06-microsoft-platform/agents-toolkit-and-sdk-family.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/agents-toolkit-and-sdk-family.md)). The generated project holds five directories: editor debugging configuration, the manifest templates, the environment files, **infrastructure templates for provisioning Azure resources**, and the source.

**Two of those five contain things the developer did not write and will be asked to own** — the infrastructure templates, and the identity configuration the provision stage performs on their behalf. That is the first honest observation the material supports, and it is a slice observation rather than a tooling one: **the smallest deployable unit is never as small as it looks, because deploying it creates objects somebody has to keep.** Naming an owner for each is the difference between an increment and a liability.

Three things in the published command surface are statements about the job rather than about the tool. A **collaborator** command exists to check, grant and list who may access and manage the app and its identity registration — the tooling models the fact that an app has more than one owner. An **uninstall** command exists to clean up the resources associated with an environment — estate hygiene as a first-class act, which is rarer than it should be. And an **upgrade** command exists to bring a generated project into line with the current tooling, **which is an admission, in the command list, that a project can fall out of compatibility with the thing that generated it.**

The environment model is the strongest idea in the family and the one that survives the product. Environments are files with placeholder substitution resolved at provision time; **secrets live in a separate per-target file that is never committed**, with the pipeline injecting them at run time. **Promotion is then a flag rather than an edit** — the same commands with a different environment named. The published claim built on that generalises well beyond this vendor: **target environment and agent version are the same problem and take the same mechanism**, because a different app identifier makes two installable apps from one manifest, and the platform treats apps with different identifiers as separate installations however much code they share. **Variant definitions are then files in the repository reviewed like any other source, with no runtime feature flags required.**

**The limits are heavy and every one of them matters more here than usual.** Six pages were fetched and read in full; roughly thirty more were reached only through search-returned excerpts, **so no command list or requirement list in that note is exhaustive.** **Every principal name in this area has changed at least once**, one package has changed name twice, and one predecessor carries a dated end of support that falls inside the useful life of the note. Part of the material is not published documentation at all but a first-party artefact shipped inside an editor extension, cited by extension version because it has no public URL and no currency guarantee. **And the ergonomics claims are marketing** — streamlines, dramatically simplifies, enhanced productivity, rapid — asserted without measurement anywhere in the material. **There is no measure, anywhere, of how much any of this reduces time to a first working agent: no baseline, no comparison, no study.** Take the lifecycle separation and the environment model, which are reasoning. Take none of the adjectives.

### A topology you have already agreed to throw away

The exclusion list's hardest entries are the ones that are true today and will be wrong later, and the platform supplies the cleanest available instance.

The setup choice is a three-way rather than a two-way, and the third column is the one people discover late ([research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-control-plane-and-governance.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-control-plane-and-governance.md)). **Basic setup** gets you started without managing resources and gets nothing else in the published comparison. **Standard setup with public networking** puts conversation history, files and vector stores in **your own** Storage, AI Search and Cosmos DB, and adds customer-managed keys. **Standard setup with private networking** adds bring-your-own-network isolation on top.

The distinction that decides your exclusion list is stated in the table's own note and is routinely misread. **Private network isolation refers to *outbound* agent communication and is standard-only. Inbound protection can be applied to any setup**, by adding a private endpoint and disabling public inbound access. **Basic setup can be given a private front door. It cannot be given a private back door.** A brief that says *it must be private* has not yet said which direction, and the answer changes which topology you may start on.

The practical residue is the kind that eats a day and is all published: outbound agent traffic uses container injection into a subnet **you** delegate, at or above a stated minimum size; **private endpoints to the connected data resources are not created for you**; a private endpoint stays pending until somebody with the right role approves it; some network-isolated configurations are **not configurable through the portal at all** and require the command line or an SDK; and there is **no automatic cross-region failover** — multi-region means separate resources with routing handled in your own application layer.

**And the move is a re-provision, not a toggle.** Standard setup requires you to bring your own resources so that agent data stays in your tenant, which means the transition from the topology you started on to the topology you operate is a rebuild with a migration attached. **That is exactly what an exclusion list exists to hold**: not *we did not do networking*, but *we took a prototyping topology on a stated date, we know what leaving it costs, and here is the person who owns the decision to leave.*

**The limits.** Seven pages were fetched and read in full and roughly twenty more read only through search excerpts, so their structure is reconstructed and their tables may be truncated. **This is vendor documentation about vendor governance**: it argues that centralised oversight is desirable and never discusses what the oversight layer costs in latency, permissions overhead or organisational friction. **It is definitional, not evidential — no adoption, failure-rate, performance, cost-saving or outcome data appears anywhere in it.** Roles are actively renaming and the pages carry the notice inline. **Preview features are load-bearing throughout, and a governance argument that depends on a preview feature is a governance argument with no service-level agreement.** Classic and new surfaces are both live, so a search result can silently be the wrong one. **Every number in it is a dated platform parameter and two of its interpretive thresholds are asserted with no stated basis at all; none is reproduced here.**

### Deciding how you will know, before you build

One discipline from the measurement strand belongs here rather than three sessions later. **"Before investing time, money, and resources into building an application, it's important to understand how this application will be evaluated"** — named by its author *evaluation-driven development*, by analogy with writing tests before code ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/huyen-ai-engineering.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/huyen-ai-engineering.md)).

The same author immediately concedes the objection in her own voice, and the concession is what makes the idea usable rather than doctrinaire: focusing only on applications whose outcomes can be measured "is similar to looking for the lost key under the lamppost (at night). It's easier to do, but it doesn't mean we'll find the key." **A slice chosen because it is measurable is the same failure as a slice chosen because it is startable**, wearing better clothes. The correct output is a slice chosen because it is valuable, together with an honest statement of how much of its value you will be able to see.

**Limits:** a practitioner book, first edition dated December 2024, whose strongest claim about evaluation its author explicitly marks as a belief rather than a finding. Do not upgrade the hedge.

## The workflow

**Deploy the smallest thing the platform offers, and let the deployment tell you what your slice definition was hiding.**

**Step 1 — write the slice definition first, in the customer's words, and commit it before you open a portal.** The order is checkable afterwards from your own commit history, and the checkability is the point.

**Step 2 — provision a project and choose the setup deliberately, in the direction the brief actually cares about.** Take the basic setup if you are prototyping, and **write the words "no outbound network isolation" into your exclusion list on the same day**, with the date. You have not made a mistake. You have taken a prototyping topology on purpose, and the exclusion list is where that fact goes so that somebody else can find it.

**Then ask the question the brief did not answer: private in which direction?** Inbound protection can be added to any setup; outbound isolation is standard-only, and standard means bringing your own storage, search and database so that agent data stays in the customer's tenant. **Write into the same entry that the move is a re-provision rather than a toggle**, and name the person who owns the decision to make it. An exclusion whose cost is unstated is an exclusion nobody will ever price.

**Step 3 — define the prompt agent declaratively, and put the exclusions in the file itself.** Keep the configuration in the repository next to the slice definition rather than only in the portal, so that a change to it is a reviewable diff.

```yaml
# agents/first-slice.agent.yaml
# Derived from docs/slice-definition.md. If this file was written first, that is the finding.
name: adjudication-drafter
kind: prompt-agent            # declarative; Foundry-managed; NO AGENT COMPUTE
recipient:
  role: "Senior case officer, benefits team"
  named_person: "the officer who runs Tuesday triage"   # a person who can refuse this
  can_refuse: true
belief_to_change: >
  That a first-pass draft can be produced from the case file without a person
  re-reading the whole file in order to trust it.
domain_sentence: >
  Drafts a recommendation for a straightforward case, citing the paragraphs it relied on.
  # invented words: none. If you invent one, list it here and expect to lose it.
instructions_file: ./instructions/adjudication-drafter.md
tools:                        # Tool catalog / MCP / OpenAPI are the published routes
  - type: openapi
    spec: ./tools/case-file-read.yaml     # read only, on purpose
excludes:                     # THE ARTEFACT. Each entry dated when it was decided.
  - item: "Writing back to the case system"
    reason: "A recipient cannot refuse a system that has already acted."
    decided: 2026-07-31
  - item: "Non-straightforward cases"
    reason: "Frozen: the escalation path is set by policy, not by us."
    decided: 2026-07-31
    freeze_tested: true
    who_could_move_it: "Head of adjudication policy"    # a person, or it is a rumour
  - item: "Network isolation"
    reason: "Basic setup taken deliberately for speed; prototyping topology, not production."
    decided: 2026-07-31
  - item: "Anything requiring our own code to run"
    reason: "A prompt agent has no agent compute. Needing it is an argument for a hosted agent."
    decided: 2026-07-31
    painful: true             # this is the one we wanted and are not building
residual_value: >
  The instructions file and the read-only tool contract survive us and are usable
  by the customer's own engineers without this agent existing.
```

**Step 4 — test exactly one freeze.** Take the constraint that most restricts the slice and find out who would have to agree to change it. Record the name in `who_could_move_it`. **If you cannot fill that field, the constraint is a rumour and your slice is smaller than it needed to be.** You are not asking anybody to change anything.

**Step 5 — deploy it and put it in front of the named recipient, with nobody narrating.** Hand it over and be quiet. An increment that needs you in the room to be understood is a demonstration, and a demonstration is a different artefact with a different audience.

**Step 6 — use the agent against your own slice, twice, and let it decide nothing.** First: give it the slice definition and the commitment from [S039](/wiki/seminars/S039.md) — **not the exclusion list** — and instruct it to argue that the increment is not valuable and only appears to be; that the recipient will not use it; that the belief will not move; and that a different increment would have moved it. Second: give it the exclusion list alone and ask which exclusion it would attack first if it were the customer's architect. Keep whatever is better than your own reasoning and record what you rejected and why. **An agent that congratulated you was not used as an instrument.**

**Step 7 — ask the corpus's own better question, in its own form.** Not *how do I produce this faster*, but **where does the recipient's system currently lose time**. The characteristic mistake is asking how to generate more code instead of asking where the work is actually lost, and an instrument will answer whichever question you put to it.

**Step 8 — make the increment deployable by somebody who is not you, and find out what provisioning created on your behalf.** A slice that only you can put in front of a recipient is a demonstration with a longer setup. **Declare the lifecycle as three stages against named environments, promote by naming an environment rather than by editing a file, and then account for every object the provision stage created that you did not write.**

```yaml
# deploy/lifecycle.yaml   <- SHAPE, NOT SCHEMA. Stage and action names are this term's;
# verify them against the tooling version you are running and record which you checked.
# Committed beside docs/slice-definition.md. If this file came first, that is the finding.

environments:                 # promotion is a flag, never an edit
  - name: dev                 #   provision --env dev   /   deploy --env dev
  - name: pilot               #   the SAME commands, a different name
secrets_live_in: "the per-target user file, never committed; the pipeline injects them"

stages:
  provision:                  # CREATES THINGS. Each one needs an owner, today.
    creates:
      - object: "identity registration for the agent"
        owner: "UNASSIGNED"                 # <- this blank is the exercise
      - object: "infrastructure templates under infra/, which I did not write"
        owner: "UNASSIGNED"
        read_before_first_demo: false        # <- and so is this
      - object: "the basic-setup project itself"
        owner: "me, until handover"
        topology: "prototyping"
        agreed_throwaway: true               # AGREED ON A DATE, WITH A NAMED PERSON
        decided: 2026-07-31
        leaving_costs: "a re-provision with bring-your-own storage, search and database"
        who_owns_leaving: "NAME REQUIRED, or this is a rumour like any other freeze"
  deploy:
    from: "the definition and instructions files in this repository"
  publish:
    excluded: true
    reason: "Distribution route not yet decided; foreclosed routes are S026's artefact."
    decided: 2026-07-31

rehearsal:
  performed_by: "a colleague, from a clean clone, on their own machine"
  observer_said_nothing: true                # the silence is the measurement
  what_was_missing_first_attempt: ""         # fill this in. It is never empty.
```

**The rehearsal line is the assessed part.** Have somebody else provision your increment from a clean clone while you watch and say nothing, and write down what was missing on their first attempt. **It is never nothing, and what is missing is almost always a credential or a file that lives only on your machine** — which is precisely the class of thing a slice definition conceals and a handover discovers. This is the cheap, early form of the test [S079](/wiki/seminars/S079.md) runs for real.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That the boundary of a declarative agent is a real boundary and not a stylistic one: the moment your slice needs logic you cannot express in configuration, the platform stops, and the stop is visible in an afternoon rather than arguable in a meeting. **You will also find out, usually at step 5, whether your recipient was a person or a role you wrote down to satisfy the template** — because a role cannot be handed something, and a person can refuse it.

## What good looks like

- **The recipient is somebody who could refuse the increment.** A slice whose only audience is your own team is a milestone, not a slice.
- **The exclusion list is longer than the inclusion list**, or the definition argues why it is not.
- **At least one exclusion is painful** — something you wanted to build, that you can justify leaving out.
- **The slice is defensible as valuable rather than as feasible.** *We could get this done* is not a reason to do it first.
- **Frozen and fluid are distinguished**, and at least one thing classified as frozen has been tested rather than assumed.
- **Every exclusion carries the date it was decided.** An undated exclusion is an outcome wearing the clothes of a decision, and [S045](/wiki/seminars/S045.md) marks the difference.
- **The domain sentence uses only words the customer already uses**, and any word you had to invent is marked as invented.
- **The residual-value statement names an artefact or a capability**, not a lesson.

## How it goes wrong

- **The foundation slice** — infrastructure, scaffolding and a data layer, with nothing anybody outside the team can touch. *Warning sign:* your recipient is a future version of yourself.
- **The demonstration slice** — it shows beautifully and nobody can use it. *Warning sign:* the increment has a script.
- **The easy slice** — chosen because it was startable, defended afterwards as valuable. *Warning sign:* you wrote item 3 blank.
- **The whole-thing slice** — the smallest increment that anyone would find valuable turns out, on inspection, to be the entire system. *Warning sign:* your exclusion list is empty.
- **The frozen-space surrender** — the constraint is real, the slice is therefore trivial, and the triviality is somebody else's fault. *Warning sign:* your definition explains more than it proposes.
- **The unverified freeze** — a constraint everybody repeats and nobody has tested. *Warning sign:* you cannot name who would have to agree to move it.
- **The measurable slice** — chosen because its outcome could be observed rather than because it mattered, which is the lamppost failure in delivery clothing. *Warning sign:* you can say precisely how you would evaluate it and vaguely why anybody wanted it.
- **The invented noun** — the slice is organised around a term that exists only inside your team, which the customer's experts have politely accepted. *Warning sign:* your domain sentence needs a gloss.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The slice definition is carried into [S042](/wiki/seminars/S042.md), where the ledger is kept against it, and into [S045](/wiki/seminars/S045.md), where every departure from it is marked and dated.

**1 · Sort six candidate first increments into slice and not-a-slice.** For each, name the recipient who could refuse it, and if there is none, say so.

- *A schema and a loader for the case data.* · *A read-only draft recommendation for one case type, in front of one officer.* · *An end-to-end walkthrough for the sponsor's leadership review.* · *An authentication integration with the customer's identity provider.* · *A single tool contract the customer's own engineers can call without our agent existing.* · *A retrieval layer over the policy corpus.*

> **Model answers.** Only the second and fifth have recipients who could refuse them. The draft recommendation is a slice because a case officer can decline to use it and the decline is information; the tool contract is a slice because it is receivable and survives you, which is the residual-value test. The schema, the authentication integration and the retrieval layer are foundation work — necessary, defensible in every particular, and receivable by nobody outside the team. **The walkthrough is the dangerous one**, because it has an audience, produces enthusiasm and cannot be refused: nobody declines a demonstration, so nobody's belief is tested. If you classified it as a slice, you classified attention as value.

**2 · Write the domain sentence, then take it to somebody who is not an engineer.** One sentence, using only words your customer's experts already use. Ask them to say back what the thing does.

> **Scoring criterion.** It passes if their restatement contains no word you had to explain, and if it names the same recipient you had in mind. **Every place their description differs from your intent is a defect in the sentence, not in their reading.** The founding source's test is that sophisticated domain experts should understand the core of the model, with technical components explicitly exempt — so the exemption is available to you, and using it to cover the whole sentence is how you fail.

**3 · Go back to your own item 5, then break it.** Find what you wrote for opening item 5 — what would remain that was worth the money if the engagement stopped the day after. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference.

> **Criterion.** A residual-value answer names an artefact somebody else can hold or a capability somebody else now has: *the tool contract and its tests, which their own engineers can call*; *a case officer who can produce a draft without us*; *an instructions file that encodes the policy reading and is theirs*. **If you wrote that the customer would have learned whether the approach works, you wrote a proof answer**, and proofs were [S040](/wiki/seminars/S040.md). If you wrote that they would have a working prototype, ask who is allowed to run it after you leave.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is. **The evidence is school, university and laboratory work; there is no adult-professional study in its reference list.**

**4 · Deploy the prompt agent and find the wall.** Run the workflow to step 5, then extend your slice by one requirement that needs code to run. Record what happened at the platform boundary and how long it took to find.

> **What you should find.** That the boundary is published and real — a prompt agent is documented as having **no agent compute**, so the requirement does not degrade, it stops. **The engineering lesson is that a hosting choice looks like a detail until it is load-bearing, at which point it is an architecture decision taken weeks earlier by whoever provisioned first.** Note also that the vendor states the options are not mutually exclusive, so the honest answer is often a second component in a different shape rather than a migration of the first. **The documentation publishes no adoption, cost, failure-rate or outcome data of any kind**, so nothing here tells you whether either shape works better. It tells you where the wall is.

**5 · Test one freeze and name the person.** Take the constraint that most restricts your slice. Find out who would have to agree to change it. Do not ask them to change it.

> **Scoring criterion.** The answer passes if it contains a name, or a specific role held by a specific person, or an explicit statement that no such person could be found. **A constraint with no locatable author is an inherited assumption, and the correct entry in your slice definition is "untested" rather than "frozen".** Learners routinely discover that a constraint everybody repeated was set for a reason that has expired, and equally routinely discover that one they assumed was negotiable is written into somebody's contract. Both are wins. **The only failure is leaving the field blank and continuing to call it frozen.**

**6 · Attack your own exclusion list with the instrument, then with a person.** Run workflow step 6, then read the list to one colleague whose only job is to say what they expected to be on it and is not.

> **What to notice.** Anything they name that is *inside* your slice is a candidate for removal. **The list is the artefact and the increment is downstream of it** — a slice defined only by what it contains has not been cut, it has been described. Compare what the instrument attacked with what the person attacked: the instrument will generally attack your reasons, and the person will generally attack your absences. **Only one of those two failure modes is visible in the written artefact, which is why both passes are required.**

**7 · Have somebody else deploy your slice, and count what provisioning created.** Run workflow step 8. List every object the provision stage produced that you did not write, and put a name against each.

> **What you should find.** Two things, both uncomfortable. First, that **the list is longer than you expected and at least two entries are infrastructure or identity** — the two areas a developer is least likely to have read and most likely to be paged about. Second, that the first attempt from a clean clone fails on something living only on your machine. **Neither is a tooling defect. Both are properties of the slice**, and they are what separates *deployable* from *demoable*: a demonstration needs you in the room, and so does an increment whose provisioning steps only you can perform. **Note that the toolchain publishes no measure of how much faster any of this is than doing it by hand** — no baseline, no comparison, no study — so the finding here is the ownership list and never a claim about speed.

**8 · Say which direction "private" meant, and price leaving the topology you started on.** Take the brief's networking requirement and classify it as inbound, outbound, or unstated. Then write what it would cost to move.

> **Scoring criterion.** It passes if the direction is named and the move is priced as a **re-provision with bring-your-own storage, search and database** rather than as a setting. **Most briefs say "it must be private" and mean inbound**, which any setup can be given; a brief that means outbound has eliminated the fastest starting topology before anybody opened a portal. **The failure is an exclusion list entry reading "networking to be addressed later"**, which prices nothing, names nobody and will be read at handover as an oversight rather than as a decision. **The published table is a decision aid and not evidence** — it is definitional vendor documentation with no adoption, failure-rate or outcome data anywhere in it, several of its features are preview, and its parameters are dated and are not to be memorised.

## What this does not establish

Nothing here shows that small increments produce better outcomes than large ones.

The open playbook names the minimal valuable slice and treats it as a standing commitment, and **offers no comparative evidence for it**, which is true of every practice on that site and is stated plainly in this repository's reading. The internal corpus reports **no failed engagement, no cost side and no post-deployment record of any kind**, so it cannot supply a case in which slicing rescued a delivery, because it cannot supply a delivery in trouble.

**The claim this session actually rests on is narrower and closer to a definition:** an increment that nobody outside the delivery team can receive cannot change anybody's belief, and an increment that changes nobody's belief has not tested anything. That is an argument.

**The design's central addition — that the exclusion list is the real artefact — is design reasoning with no external warrant and appears in no source.** So is the requirement that exclusions carry dates, and so is the domain sentence as an artefact, although the language test underneath it belongs to the design literature.

**The pattern language supplying that test is a professional book, not a study.** It declares its own prerequisites and makes no claim without them; its publisher and date were inherited rather than verified for this repository; and its author's own conclusion reports one of his five followed-up projects as a partial failure with a named cause. It offers no comparative evidence that projects built this way outperform projects that are not.

**The platform material is first-party selection guidance with no outcome data**, read through search excerpts rather than full page fetches, with several comparison tables truncated, and it is the most perishable material in this programme. It tells you where the boundary between a prompt agent and a hosted agent lies. It does not tell you that either choice produces better software.

**The evaluation-driven-development idea is a practitioner's recommendation, and its author marks her strongest version of it as a belief.** Her own objection to it — the lamppost problem — is reproduced here because a course that teaches the idea without the objection is teaching a heuristic as a rule.
