# S041 · The Minimal Valuable Slice

**Module:** [M09](/wiki/modules/M09.md) · **Quarter:** [Q5](/wiki/quarters/Q5.md) · **Complexity class:** 3
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** none
**Whitepaper:** [WP-041](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-041.md)
**Retrieves from:** S021, S026, S039, S040
**Entry state:** either

## The situation

Somebody has committed to the thing. The proof is priced, the unproven list is written, and the engagement now starts spending.

The first question is not what to build. It is what to build **first**, and the honest answer is almost never the answer that arrives naturally, because the answer that arrives naturally is the part that is easiest to start.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught.

1. What is the smallest thing you could put in front of somebody that would change what they believe?
2. Who is the recipient of your first increment — a role, or better, a person?
3. What were you about to build first because it was easy to begin rather than because it was worth having?
4. Which frozen part of the problem space forces the shape of your slice, and which part only looks frozen?
5. If the engagement stopped the day after this increment, what would remain that was worth the money?

**Item 5 is the one the design cares about.** Most learners answer it with a list of things that would have been *learned*, which is a proof answer rather than a delivery answer. [Stage 4](/wiki/quarters/Q4.md) is over. Something is supposed to remain.

## What you must produce

A **slice definition** on one page.

- The increment, stated as something a named recipient can do that they could not do before.
- The belief it is intended to change, and whose belief it is.
- **The exclusion list** — what is deliberately not in it, each item with the reason.
- The frozen constraints that determined its shape, and the fluid ones you chose not to move and could have.
- The residual-value statement: what survives if the engagement ends the day after.

## What good looks like

- **The recipient is somebody who could refuse the increment.** A slice whose only audience is your own team is a milestone, not a slice.
- **The exclusion list is longer than the inclusion list**, or the definition argues why it is not.
- **At least one exclusion is painful** — something you wanted to build, that you can justify leaving out.
- **The slice is defensible as valuable rather than as feasible.** *We could get this done* is not a reason to do it first.
- **Frozen and fluid are distinguished**, and at least one thing classified as frozen has been tested rather than assumed.
- **The residual-value statement names an artefact or a capability**, not a lesson.

## How it goes wrong

- **The foundation slice** — infrastructure, scaffolding and a data layer, with nothing anybody outside the team can touch. *Warning sign:* your recipient is a future version of yourself.
- **The demonstration slice** — it shows beautifully and nobody can use it. *Warning sign:* the increment has a script.
- **The easy slice** — chosen because it was startable, defended afterwards as valuable. *Warning sign:* you wrote item 3 blank.
- **The whole-thing slice** — the smallest increment that anyone would find valuable turns out, on inspection, to be the entire system. *Warning sign:* your exclusion list is empty.
- **The frozen-space surrender** — the constraint is real, the slice is therefore trivial, and the triviality is somebody else's fault. *Warning sign:* your definition explains more than it proposes.
- **The unverified freeze** — a constraint everybody repeats and nobody has tested. *Warning sign:* you cannot name who would have to agree to move it.

## Do it

Write the slice, then attack it in this order.

**First, name the recipient out loud.** If you cannot name a person or a specific role, you do not have a slice — you have a work package. The corpus behind this stage is unambiguous that everything short of realised value is activity, and an increment nobody outside the delivery team receives is activity with better packaging.

**Second, test one freeze.** Take the constraint that most restricts the slice and find out who would have to agree to change it. You are not asking them to change it. You are establishing whether the freeze is a decision somebody made or an assumption everybody inherited. **Constraint is not an excuse, and an untested freeze is not a constraint — it is a rumour.**

**Third, use an agent against the slice rather than for it.** Give it the slice definition and the commitment from [S039](/wiki/seminars/S039.md), and ask it to argue that the increment is not valuable and only appears to be — that the recipient will not use it, that the belief will not move, and that a different increment would have moved it. Record what you kept and what you rejected.

Then ask it the corpus's own question in its own form: **not how to produce this faster, but where the recipient's system currently loses time.** The characteristic mistake is asking how to produce more code instead of asking where the work is actually lost, and an agent will answer whichever question you put to it.

## Show it

Read your exclusion list to one other person, and give them one job: to say what they expected to be on it and is not.

Anything they name that is in your slice is a candidate for removal. **The list is the artefact — the increment is downstream of it** — and a slice defined only by what it contains has not been cut, it has been described.

## What this does not establish

Nothing here shows that small increments produce better outcomes than large ones.

The open playbook names the minimal valuable slice and treats it as a standing commitment, and **offers no comparative evidence for it**, which is true of every practice on that site and is stated plainly in this repository's reading. The internal corpus reports **no failed engagement, no cost side and no post-deployment record of any kind**, so it cannot supply a case in which slicing rescued a delivery, because it cannot supply a delivery in trouble.

**The claim this session actually rests on is narrower and closer to a definition:** an increment that nobody outside the delivery team can receive cannot change anybody's belief, and an increment that changes nobody's belief has not tested anything. That is an argument. The design's addition — that the exclusion list is the real artefact — is design reasoning with no external warrant and appears nowhere in any source.
