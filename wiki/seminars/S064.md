## The situation

The system runs, and somebody pays for it every day. That somebody is not you. After [Stage 8](/wiki/quarters/Q8.md) it is definitely not you, and it is not your organisation either.

**The cost side of this practice is the part it does well.** Consumption is forecast by engineers, instrumented by engineers, and refined against telemetry from the system itself. That is a real method and this session teaches it as content rather than as specimen.

Two things are missing from it, and both are assessed here because nothing else in the programme assesses them. **The first is that the corpus's own value claims have no cost side at all** — staffing, compute, the cost of the discovery activity itself and opportunity cost are absent, and every claim offered is a gross benefit. **The second is that the model is never handed over.** The one place the right instinct appears — that the customer should be left holding the cost-modelling capability, because they will need it after the team disengages — sits as a reflection inside a narrative rather than as a step in any procedure. Nobody is required to transfer it and nobody is required to check that it runs.

**Answer these in writing before reading further.** Item 2 is the one this session is really about, and most people discover they cannot answer it.

1. What does one unit of your system's work cost, and what is the unit?
2. Which design decision in your system is the most expensive to run, and was it chosen for that reason?
3. What does your observability cost, and against which signals?
4. What happens to the bill if usage grows and nothing else changes?
5. Who will hold this model after you leave, and can they run it?

Item 1 is what separates a cost model from a bill: a figure per compute hour is a bill, and a figure per unit of the customer's work is a model. **Item 2 is where the session's durable claim lives.** Cost in these systems is a **design property**, not an operational surprise — because the architecture determines the token path, and the token path is the bill. Almost nobody chose their most expensive decision for that reason, and saying so honestly is worth more than a retrofitted justification.

## What you must produce

**A cost model a customer engineer can run without you.**

1. **The unit**, expressed as a unit of the customer's work — per claim processed, per document reviewed, per site surveyed — never per unit of infrastructure.
2. **The token path**, stated as a design fact: what an average request sends, what it generates, what is cached, and which architectural choices set each of those.
3. **The capacity posture**: shared, dedicated, or a mixture, with the reason — and, where anything is committed, the evidence that capacity was confirmed before the commitment was made.
4. **Instrumentation**, with the instruments named and pointing at signals that survived [S061](/wiki/seminars/S061.md), and every remaining estimate marked as an estimate and dated.
5. **Observability as a cost line**, not as overhead.
6. **The cost-bearing design decisions named**, each with the alternative that was not taken, what that alternative would have cost, and whether cost was a criterion at the time.
7. **The growth behaviour**: what the bill does as usage rises, and which term dominates first.
8. **The delivery cost in the same document as the running cost.**
9. **A named person on the customer's side who has run it**, with the date they ran it.

## The material

### The architecture determines the token path, and the token path is the bill

The reason cost is a design property rather than an operational one is mechanical, and it is worth understanding at the level of the machine rather than the invoice.

**Inference is not one operation.** The reference treatment of transformer inference separates **prefill**, where the input prompt is processed and many tokens are handled in parallel, from **decode**, where output tokens are produced one at a time ([research/02-technical-foundations/pope-scaling-transformer-inference.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/pope-scaling-transformer-inference.md)). The two have different bottlenecks: prefill is compute-bound and can keep an accelerator busy, while **generation is dominated by moving weights and cache through memory rather than by arithmetic.** That is why raw arithmetic capability is a poor predictor of token latency, and why a long prompt and a long answer have different cost structures even when the token counts match.

The same work makes the design consequence explicit. **The key-value cache, not the parameter count, is what limits long-context serving**, and the number of key-value heads is the lever — which is the memory argument that made shared-key-value attention variants standard. And it reports its results as a **Pareto frontier** rather than as a single figure, because latency and utilisation trade against each other: **there is no universally optimal deployment, and the right configuration is selected from the application's requirements.** A team that has not stated its latency requirement has not selected a configuration; it has accepted one.

The serving side supplies the other half. **Throughput requires batching many requests together, so batch size is the primary throughput lever** — and what caps batch size is memory occupied by the key-value cache, which grows and shrinks dynamically and whose size is not known in advance because the number of output tokens is not known in advance ([research/02-technical-foundations/kwon-pagedattention-vllm.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/kwon-pagedattention-vllm.md)). Naive allocation wastes memory through **fragmentation** and through **redundant duplication** where requests share a prefix, and both forms of waste reduce how many requests fit, which reduces achievable throughput. The remedy borrowed from operating systems — non-contiguous fixed-size blocks with a table mapping logical positions to physical ones — turns sharing into a mapping problem and bounds the waste.

**Read those two together and the durable claim falls out.** Prompt length, output length, context window, shared prefixes, batching behaviour and cache reuse are all **architectural decisions**, and each of them sets a term in the bill before any traffic exists. **Cost is therefore decided at design time and merely observed at run time.**

**Limits on both.** The inference paper's concrete results are specific to one accelerator family, one model and one context length, and it predates several now-standard techniques; **the reasoning transfers and the coefficients do not.** The serving paper's headline comparison is against systems of its own period at matched latency, and paging introduces its own overheads; **it is a serving-efficiency paper and says nothing about model quality, evaluation or safety.** **No figure from either appears here.**

### The memory, recall and cost trade is explicit in the literature — and it is a choice, not a default

Retrieval makes the same point with a cleaner surface, because the trade is a parameter rather than an emergent property.

**Memory, not computation alone, is the binding constraint at scale.** Compressing a vector into a short code is what makes large-scale similarity search fit in memory at all, and the compression is achieved by splitting a vector into parts, quantising each against its own small codebook, and treating the effective codebook as the product of the small ones ([research/02-technical-foundations/jegou-product-quantization.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/jegou-product-quantization.md)). **Code length, sub-space count, codebook size and the number of candidate lists probed are the parameters that define the operating point** — shorter codes mean more vectors in memory and a coarser distance approximation, therefore lower recall. **A vector index is designed against a budget rather than selected from a catalogue**, and the recall cost of aggressive compression is real and easy to under-notice because it degrades results gradually rather than failing visibly.

**The storage tier dictates the algorithm rather than merely configuring it.** Moving an index from memory to solid-state storage changes what the cost metric is: on disk the binding quantity is random reads, so minimising traversal hops matters more than minimising distance computations, and the design response is a hybrid — compressed representations in memory for navigation, full-precision data on disk for accuracy ([research/02-technical-foundations/subramanya-diskann.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/subramanya-diskann.md)). The same work names the metric that actually determines cost at scale and that vendors characteristically omit: **density, meaning how many indexed items a single machine can serve.** Recall and latency without density is an incomplete claim, because machine count dominates the bill.

**Limits, and they are heavy on one of the two.** The compression paper's **full text was not retrieved** for this repository — the deposit endpoint returned an automated challenge and the publisher version is paywalled — so its account here is reconstructed from established secondary knowledge, **no experimental result of any kind is asserted**, and it should be obtained and checked before any assessed use. The disk-index paper is open and read, but its results are from a particular benchmark and hardware era, its baselines have all advanced since, and **no figure from it appears here.**

### Quota is permission; capacity is availability; a reservation is neither

This is where the mechanics meet the invoice, and it is the most transferable material in the session.

A provisioned deployment **holds a fixed amount of processing capacity exclusively for your deployment's use, whether or not requests are being made** ([research/06-microsoft-platform/provisioned-throughput-for-foundry-models.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/provisioned-throughput-for-foundry-models.md)). Everything follows from that clause. Capacity held whether used or not must be paid for whether used or not, **which is why the billing unit changes from tokens to time** — and why the decision stops being a configuration setting and becomes a capacity-planning commitment, since a wrong estimate now costs money continuously rather than in proportion to traffic.

The unit of capacity has four properties that are individually unremarkable and jointly consequential. It is **model-independent** — you do not buy it for a specific model. It is **region-specific** — an allocation in one region does not carry to another. Its **throughput varies by model**, so a heavier model needs more of it to serve the same traffic. And each model has a **minimum deployment size**, which puts a floor under the cost of using a large model at all. **What you buy is machine, not service**, and how much service it yields depends entirely on what you run on it.

The distinction the session turns on is stated by the documentation in a call-out. **Quota** is a policy limit enforced by the platform, scoped per subscription, region and deployment type, and **it has no associated cost**. **Capacity** is the amount actually available to deploy right now. And: **"Having PTU quota doesn't guarantee that capacity is available."** Two facts sharpen it. Capacity **changes throughout the day based on customer demand**, so the answer to *can I deploy this* is time-dependent. And releasing capacity offers **no guarantee the same capacity is available** if you re-create or scale up later — **so releasing is a one-way door in practice even though it looks reversible.**

Sizing is presented as an inference from a traffic model rather than a price lookup, and the three inputs are exactly the architectural terms above. **Request shape** — requests per minute, average input tokens, average output tokens. **Output-to-input ratio**, capturing that **output tokens require more processing capacity than input tokens**. And **cache rate**, where **cached tokens do not consume capacity**, so a higher cache rate lowers the requirement. These combine into a normalised throughput figure divided by a per-model divisor. **This is a capacity model, not a pricing model**, and every simplification in the traffic description propagates into the answer.

Three further mechanisms belong in the artefact. The **routing variants** trade residency against availability on a visible gradient, which makes a compliance requirement into a directly costed availability decision. **Spillover** redirects overflow requests from a saturated dedicated deployment to a shared one, triggered by an error response rather than by prediction — textbook graceful degradation, with the honest consequence that the user-visible behaviour changes when the overflow is served. And **hourly billing** charges per unit per hour regardless of tokens consumed, with the documentation explicitly advising against using provisioned deployments as an autoscaling mechanism, for two stated reasons: **capacity might not be available when you need to scale back up**, and continuous hourly billing at high utilisation typically exceeds reservation pricing.

**Then the sentence this session exists to install.** A reservation is **a financial discount applied to the billing meter, not to individual deployments** — and **"Reservations don't guarantee capacity. First create deployments to confirm that capacity is available, then purchase the reservation to lock in the discounted rate."** **Deploy, verify, then commit.** The intuitive sequence — commit, then deploy — is exactly wrong, and a commitment purchased before the shape of the workload is known is a bet dressed as procurement.

**Its limits:** first-party product documentation, fetched and read in full, definitional rather than empirical; **perishable** in names, defaults, ratios, minimums and portal paths; single-vendor, with an obvious commercial framing and a choice architecture that is the vendor's; and every concrete number — per-model ratios, minimum sizes, prices, default allocations — lives on companion pages that were not fetched and **none is asserted here.**

### A shared finite resource with no per-consumer accounting will be consumed by whoever is least careful

The gateway supplies the fairness half, and its reasoning is the sharpest on the page. Tokens are the scarce resource; providers assign quota per unit time; that quota must be distributed across applications, teams or departments. With one application a limit on the deployment suffices. **As the portfolio grows you must make sure that one application does not consume the whole quota and block the others** ([research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md)). That is the noisy-neighbour problem stated cleanly enough to serve as a general lesson.

The mechanism is a **token limit policy** enforcing a rate or a longer-period quota, keyed on **any counter key** — a subscription, an originating address, or an arbitrary expression. The detail worth teaching is **prompt-token precalculation**: input tokens can be estimated at the gateway so that an over-limit request never reaches the backend. **That is a fail-fast design — reject at the edge rather than paying to discover the rejection downstream.**

Three more capabilities bear on cost. **Semantic caching** reuses prior completions selected by vector proximity of prompts rather than exact match, with claimed benefits of fewer backend calls, better response times and lower cost — and with a correctness risk the documentation does not discuss at all: **a similarity threshold is a correctness knob, and set loosely it returns an answer to a question that was not asked, silently.** **Priority load balancing** exists specifically to saturate dedicated endpoints first, which is **the direct operational consequence of having bought capacity you pay for whether or not you use it.** And the documentation attaches a caveat that prevents the obvious misreading of scaling: adding gateway capacity does not scale the model backends, which must also be scaled and co-located. **Scaling the front door does not widen the corridor behind it.**

**Its limits:** first-party documentation with a commercial frame, fetched and read in full; capability availability varies by service tier; parts are preview; **no performance or cost-saving data of any kind** — the benefits are plausible mechanisms rather than measured outcomes; and it does not discuss the operational burden or latency the gateway itself adds.

### What the practice corpus does well, and the two things it leaves out

**The cost side of this practice is the part it does well**, and this session teaches it as content rather than as specimen. Consumption is forecast by engineers, instrumented by engineers, and refined against telemetry from the system itself.

Two omissions are assessed here because nothing else in the programme assesses them. **The corpus's own value claims have no cost side at all** — staffing, compute, the cost of the discovery activity itself and opportunity cost are absent, and every claim offered is a gross benefit. Its structural diagnosis, in this repository's own words, is that **the cost side is engineered and the return side is elicited**: consumption is instrumented by engineers and refined against their own telemetry, while return is chosen by the customer from a menu, measured with instrumentation the customer may not possess, over a period nobody specifies, with causation assumed. **A ratio whose denominator is well instrumented and whose numerator is not is not a well-instrumented ratio, but it will feel like one.**

**And the model is never handed over.** The one place the right instinct appears — that the customer should be left holding the cost-modelling capability because they will need it after the team disengages — sits as a reflection inside a narrative rather than as a step in any procedure. **Nobody is required to transfer it and nobody is required to check that it runs.** **Its limits:** access-controlled, paraphrased and never quoted, with no outcome data, no sustainment evidence and **no figure reproduced anywhere in this repository.**

### Sizing describes one session; the bill is sizing multiplied by concurrency

Everything above prices tokens. This subsection prices the thing that runs, and it inverts an intuition almost every engineer arrives with.

A hosted agent is not a service with replicas. **It scales per session**: each session gets its own isolated sandbox with a persistent filesystem, there is no replica count to configure and no warm pool to size ([research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-agent-build-and-runtime.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-agent-build-and-runtime.md)). You choose a compute tier — a processor and memory allocation — and the documentation states the consequence in its own voice: **the CPU and memory you set describe a single session, not the aggregate footprint of the agent**, and billing accrues across all active sessions.

**So the sizing dial is a multiplier, not a total.** Choosing a generous tier because it is only a little more expensive than the modest one is a decision multiplied by every conversation happening at once. **The most expensive mistake available on this surface is a generous default**, and it is invisible at the moment it is made because a single session on a generous tier costs approximately nothing.

The published right-sizing procedure is the honest part and it is worth adopting whole. **It is empirical rather than analytical**: run a representative workload, read processor use, available memory, request rate and average request duration in the connected telemetry resource over the tested range, and compare observed peaks against the allocation. **There is no formula, because there could not be one.** Which means the answer to *how much will this cost to run* is genuinely unavailable until something exists to measure — **and this session's whole premise is that cost is decided early.** Both are true. The resolution is not to guess a number; it is to write down the concurrency assumption, mark it as unmeasured, and name the observation that will replace it. **An assumption on the page can be corrected. An assumption in somebody's head becomes an invoice.**

Three further shapes follow from per-session scaling and belong in the model. **Session lifetimes are bounded** — there is an idle timeout after which compute is deprovisioned and state persisted, and a maximum lifetime after which the session ends, both published as platform constants. **Disk is allocated per session**, with a stated proportion reserved for system use and neither visible nor available to your code. And **platform ceilings exist** on revisions per agent and on agents per instance. **None of those numbers is reproduced here and none should be memorised**; what matters is that each is a constant somebody must look up on the day, and that a design which assumes any of them is unbounded will meet a wall it did not model.

**The note's limits travel with all of it.** **The most perishable material in this repository, and it perished visibly while being read**: four pages read in full, roughly thirty reached only through search excerpts with tables frequently truncated, several consulted pages explicitly superseded and still rendering, preview status pervasive. **Definitional and not evidential** — an accurate account of what the platform is configured to do, and no evidence that doing so produces any outcome. **The thresholds in its own right-sizing guidance are heuristics with no evidence attached**, and every number in it is a dated platform parameter reproduced nowhere here.

### The bill scales with people, and there is no hard spend cap

Two facts here, and each one breaks a different assumption a cost model usually rests on.

**The first is that governing an agent estate is licensed per human, not per agent.** The estate-governance licence covers a person who manages, sponsors or is served by agents ([research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-365-and-agent-identity.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-365-and-agent-identity.md)). **Adding agents does not move that line. Adding people does.** A team that models governance cost as a function of estate size has modelled the wrong variable, and will produce a forecast that is wrong in a direction nobody checks — because it is wrong in the direction of *cheaper as you scale*, which nobody queries.

**The reframing is the useful part and it is not commercial.** If the licence does not constrain how many agents you may run, something else does, and the honest candidate is governance rather than money: **the binding constraint on estate size is how many agents one sponsor can credibly attest for.** That is a limit with no published value, no vendor guidance and no tooling, dressed for most of a procurement conversation as a pricing question. **Naming it as a governance limit is the finding**, and it belongs in the model as a stated assumption with an owner, not as an omission.

**The second fact is stated affirmatively by the platform rather than merely absent, which is rare and worth dwelling on.** **There is no hard spend cap.** Budgets alert; they do not stop ([research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-control-plane-and-governance.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-control-plane-and-governance.md)). A notification is not a control, and the difference matters most in exactly the situation where you would want a control — an unattended loop, a retry storm, an abusive consumer — because all three are fastest at three in the morning.

**So the cap is something you build**, and the place you build it is the gateway that already appears in this session's anchor. The token limit policy in step 5 is not only a fairness mechanism; **it is the only stopping mechanism in the design.** A team that has budgets configured and no per-consumer token limit has an alarm and no brake. **Write that sentence into the model**, because it is the one a commercial reader will need and the one no product page will supply.

Two further reconciliation facts belong beside it. The platform's own cost estimate **excludes several categories that will appear on the invoice** — among them non-agent workloads, dedicated capacity and any discount arrangement — so an estimate and a bill are different documents and reconciling them is work somebody must own. And **fine-tuned model deployments can bill while carrying no traffic at all**, which is a hygiene item rather than an architecture one and belongs on the register [S074](/wiki/seminars/S074.md) will build.

**The limits on both sources are heavy and different.** The governance note is **vendor documentation about vendor governance, definitional and not evidential**, renaming its own roles mid-documentation, with preview features load-bearing and classic and current surfaces both live; **two interpretive thresholds are asserted in it on no stated basis and are reproduced nowhere in this wiki.** The estate note is **newer than anything else in this research set and extremely perishable**: general availability within months of the reading, substantially preview, three announced capabilities carrying a future date and unverified, an announcement page that arrived fragmented with capability tables reduced to headers, and several central pages not read. **The price it records is a price on a date and appears nowhere in this session.** Neither publishes any efficacy or adoption figure, and none is asserted.

## The workflow

**Model the token path first, then the money, then hand it over and watch.**

**Step 1 — pick the unit and defend it.** It has to be something the customer already counts. If they do not count it, you have chosen the wrong unit or found a discovery gap, and either way that is the finding.

**Step 2 — write the token path before you write a price.** Average input tokens, average output tokens, cache rate, context length, whether prefixes are shared. **Then name the architectural decision that sets each one.** This is the step that makes cost a design property rather than a monthly surprise, and it is the step teams skip.

**Step 3 — size the capacity from the traffic model, not from a price list.** Combine request shape, the output-to-input ratio and the cache rate into a throughput requirement, then convert. **Vary the cache rate and the output ratio and watch the requirement move**; the sensitivity is the point, because it tells you which architectural decision is worth revisiting.

**Step 4 — decide the capacity posture, and if you commit, commit in the documented order.** Deploy to confirm that capacity exists. Verify. **Then** buy the reservation. Record the sequence you actually followed, including if you did it in the wrong order, because that record is what a successor needs.

**Step 5 — put per-consumer accounting at the gateway before the second consumer appears.** A token limit policy keyed on a consumer identity, with prompt-token precalculation so that over-limit requests are rejected at the edge.

```xml
<!-- APIM inbound policy fragment. SHAPE, NOT SCHEMA.
     Policy names, attributes and tier availability are version-bound.
     Verify against current documentation and record the date you checked. -->
<inbound>
  <base />
  <!-- Fairness under contention: one consumer must not be able to exhaust the shared quota. -->
  <llm-token-limit
      counter-key="@(context.Subscription.Id)"
      tokens-per-minute="<your derived figure>"
      estimate-prompt-tokens="true"          <!-- fail fast: reject at the edge, not downstream -->
      remaining-tokens-variable-name="remainingTokens" />
</inbound>
```

**Step 6 — write the model, with the token path and the capacity posture inside it rather than beside it.**

```yaml
# cost/model.yaml   <- SHAPE, NOT SCHEMA. Verify any product keys and units separately.
unit: "one claim triaged end to end"        # a unit THE CUSTOMER ALREADY COUNTS

token_path:                                  # DESIGN FACTS, not observations
  input_tokens_avg: "<measured>"
  output_tokens_avg: "<measured>"
  cache_rate: "<measured>"                   # cached input tokens consume no dedicated capacity
  context_window: "<chosen>"
  shared_prefix: true                        # a system prompt reused across requests
  set_by:
    input_tokens: "retrieval returns k passages; k chosen at S041"
    output_tokens: "the response template; nobody costed it"
    context_window: "chosen to avoid a chunking change; THIS IS THE EXPENSIVE ONE"

capacity_posture:
  shape: "dedicated for the predictable base, shared for the tail"
  overflow: "spillover on error response"     # graceful degradation, NOT prediction
  user_visible_change_on_overflow: "<state it, because there is one>"
  commitment:
    purchased: true
    sequence_followed: "deploy -> verify capacity -> commit"   # THE DOCUMENTED ORDER
    evidence_capacity_confirmed: "<deployment id + date>"
    note: "quota is permission; capacity is availability; a reservation is a discount on a meter and guarantees neither"

fairness:
  per_consumer_accounting: "token limit policy keyed on subscription"
  why: "a shared finite resource with no per-consumer accounting is consumed by whoever is least careful"

lines:
  - name: observability
    basis: "signals retained at S061"
    note: "THIS IS SPEND. If it is not a line, nothing in the model ever argues against collecting more."
  - name: delivery
    basis: "engineering time, discovery activity, assurance work in Stage 6"
    note: "a gross benefit is not a result"

cost_bearing_decisions:
  - decision: "context window sized to avoid re-chunking"
    alternative_not_taken: "re-chunk and halve the average prompt"
    alternative_cost: "<estimate>"
    was_cost_a_criterion_at_the_time: false     # SAY SO. It usually was not.

growth:
  behaviour: "<what the bill does as usage rises>"
  dominant_term_first: "<which term grows fastest, and when it overtakes>"

estimates_still_unrefined:
  - field: cache_rate
    dated: "2026-07-15"

transfer:
  customer_side_owner: "<named person>"
  has_run_it: true
  date_they_ran_it: "<date>"
  where_they_got_stuck: "<verbatim>"          # this is the runbook S072 will need
```

**Step 7 — put observability in and let it argue back.** Take the signal list from [S061](/wiki/seminars/S061.md) and attach a cost to it. Some of what survived deletion will not survive this, and **that is the session working rather than a contradiction between the two.**

**Step 8 — find the cost-bearing decision and say whether cost was a criterion.** Walk the architecture and identify the choice whose running cost dominates. **Often cost was not a criterion, and saying so is more useful than pretending otherwise** — the same honesty [S029](/wiki/seminars/S029.md) demanded of a trade study that was never run.

**Step 9 — write the concurrency assumption down and mark it unmeasured.** If anything in your design runs per session rather than per replica, the sizing you chose is a multiplier. State the tier, state the concurrency you assumed, and state that you assumed it. Then name the observation that will replace the assumption — the representative workload you will run, and the four things you will read off the telemetry when you do. **You are not required to know the number. You are required to stop it being invisible.**

**Step 10 — name the brake, separately from the alarm.** Budgets alert; they do not stop. Write down, in one line, what actually halts spend in your design and who can pull it. If the answer is a gateway token limit, say so and say who owns the policy. **If the answer is nothing, write *nothing*** — that is a finding a commercial reader can act on, and a blank is not.

```yaml
# cost/model.yaml   <- appended to the block in step 6. SHAPE, NOT SCHEMA.

per_session_compute:                 # only if anything here scales per session
  tier_chosen: "<processor and memory allocation>"
  describes: "ONE SESSION. Not the agent's aggregate footprint."
  concurrency_assumed: "<number>"
  concurrency_status: assumed        # <- NOT measured. say so.
  bill_shape: "tier x concurrent sessions"
  right_sizing_plan:
    method: empirical                # there is no formula, and the vendor does not offer one
    workload: "<the representative workload you will run>"
    read_from_telemetry: [processor_use, available_memory, request_rate, avg_request_duration]
    compare: "observed peaks against allocation"
    scheduled_for: "<date>"
    owner: "<named person>"
  platform_constants_to_look_up_on_the_day:
    - idle timeout before compute is deprovisioned
    - maximum session lifetime
    - disk per session, and the proportion reserved and invisible
    - revisions per agent, and agents per instance
    # values deliberately not recorded here. they are this quarter's.

governance_licensing:
  billed_per: human                  # NOT per agent. adding agents does not move this line.
  therefore: "estate growth does not drive this cost; headcount does"
  real_binding_constraint: "how many agents one sponsor can credibly attest for"
  that_value_is: "unpublished, untooled, and OURS to state"
  our_stated_assumption: "<number, with the name of who accepted it>"

stopping:
  alarm:
    mechanism: "budget alerts"
    stops_spend: false               # stated affirmatively by the platform: NO HARD SPEND CAP
  brake:
    mechanism: "<gateway token limit policy, or 'NOTHING'>"
    owner: "<named person>"
    scope: "<per consumer? per subscription? estate-wide?>"
    tested_on: "<date somebody actually tripped it>"
  note: >
    a notification is not a control. the difference matters most at 03:00,
    which is when an unattended loop, a retry storm and an abusive consumer
    are all at their fastest.

reconciliation:
  estimate_excludes: "<the categories the platform's own estimator leaves out>"
  owner_of_reconciliation: "<named person>"
  idle_paid_deployments_swept_on: "<date>"   # fine-tuned hosting can bill with no traffic
```

**Step 11 — use the agent to attack the model, not to build it.** One prompt: *for each figure I claim is measured, argue that it is an estimate wearing a measurement's clothes, and name the telemetry that would settle it.* Then a second: *find the term that dominates at ten times this volume and tell me why my model hides it.* Then a third, for the block above: *argue that my brake does not stop the specific failure that would cost the most, and name the failure.* Keep what beats your own reasoning and record what you rejected. **An agent that agreed your model was sound was not used as an instrument.**

**Step 12 — hand it over and watch.** Sit with somebody on the customer's side while they run it. Do not touch anything. **Record where they got stuck**, because that is the runbook [S072](/wiki/seminars/S072.md) will need and you will not otherwise discover it.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That changing one architectural parameter — the cache rate, the output length, the context window — moves the capacity requirement more than any procurement decision will, and that you can watch it move in a spreadsheet before any traffic exists. **You will also observe the deploy-verify-commit ordering the hard way if you get it wrong**, because a reservation bought against unavailable capacity is a discount on a meter nothing is running through.

## What good looks like

- **The unit is a unit of the customer's work.** They can read the model and tell what one of their own transactions costs them.
- **The token path is stated as design facts with their causes named.** Input length, output length, cache rate and context window each carry the architectural decision that set them.
- **The capacity posture is a decision, not a default**, and where anything is committed, the model carries evidence that capacity was confirmed **before** the commitment was made.
- **Per-consumer accounting exists before the second consumer does.** A shared quota with no counter key is a starvation waiting for a colleague.
- **The model is refined against telemetry rather than assembled from estimates.** Every input that is still an estimate is marked as one and dated.
- **Observability appears as spend.** [S061](/wiki/seminars/S061.md) deleted signals for a reason and this is where the reason becomes visible.
- **At least one design decision is identified as cost-bearing**, with its alternative, the alternative's cost, and an honest statement of whether cost was a criterion at the time. This is a trade study in a smaller container and the standard from [S029](/wiki/seminars/S029.md) applies.
- **The growth behaviour is stated.** What the bill does as usage rises, and which term dominates first.
- **Somebody on the customer's side has run the model and their name and date are in it.** Not "will be trained to" — has.
- **The delivery cost is in the document.** **A gross benefit is not a result**, and a document with one column is a proposal rather than a model.

## How it goes wrong

- **The gross benefit** — a return offered with no cost beside it. *Warning sign:* your value document has one column and it is the good one.
- **The infrastructure unit** — cost per node, per hour, per instance, per token. *Warning sign:* the customer cannot tell what one of their own transactions costs.
- **The commitment before the confirmation** — a reservation purchased before a deployment established that capacity exists. *Warning sign:* your procurement date precedes your first successful deployment, and nobody noticed that a reservation discounts a meter rather than holding a machine.
- **Dedicated capacity used as autoscaling** — scaling a provisioned deployment up and down with traffic to stay on hourly billing. *Warning sign:* your plan assumes the capacity will be there when you scale back up, and the documentation says twice that it may not be.
- **The token path nobody wrote down** — a model priced from a rate card with no statement of what a request actually sends and generates. *Warning sign:* you can quote a unit price and cannot say what your average prompt costs.
- **The shared quota with no counter key** — one application consumes the whole allowance and the others are starved. *Warning sign:* an incident report that names a colleague's team.
- **The cache set loosely** — semantic caching tuned for hit rate, returning an answer to a question that was not asked. *Warning sign:* your cache configuration has a threshold and nobody can say what it costs when it is wrong.
- **The forecast that never met telemetry** — a model built once from estimates, never refined against what actually happened. *Warning sign:* every input is an estimate and none is dated.
- **Free observability** — telemetry treated as overhead rather than as spend, so nothing in the model ever argues against collecting more. *Warning sign:* it is not a line.
- **The optimisation nobody measured** — a change adopted because a tool labelled it an efficiency, with no measurement of the thing it optimised. *Warning sign:* your justification is the name of a feature. **The openly published handbook demonstrates this failure against itself**, in a checklist recommending a set of efficiency measures that its own sibling page argues may each be net negative.
- **The model you take with you** — the capability leaves when the team does. *Warning sign:* the model is a spreadsheet on your machine, and the handover plan says "we will share it".
- **The dominated term nobody found** — the model is accurate today and wrong at scale, because nothing states which term grows fastest. *Warning sign:* item 4 was answered with "it scales".

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The cost model is assessed at [S065](/wiki/seminars/S065.md), on whether it is expressed per unit of the customer's work, carries observability as a line, and names a customer-side person who has run it with a date.

**1 · Sort four claims by whether they are about permission, availability or price.** For each: *we have quota for this deployment* · *we bought a reservation* · *the deployment was created successfully* · *the meter is running*.

> **Model answers.** Quota is **permission** — a policy limit with no cost attached, and the documentation states in a call-out that having it does not guarantee that capacity is available. A reservation is **price** — a discount applied to the billing meter, not to a deployment, and the documentation says plainly that it does not guarantee capacity. A successful deployment is the only one of the four that is **availability**, and it is availability *at that moment*, since capacity changes through the day with demand. The meter running is price again. **The general rule: permission, availability and price are three different things that a procurement conversation collapses into one, and the correct sequence is deploy, verify, then commit.**

**2 · Move one architectural parameter and watch the capacity requirement move.** Take your token path and vary the cache rate, then the output-to-input ratio, then the context window. Record which one moves the requirement most.

> **What you should find.** That the sensitivity is large and asymmetric, and that the parameter with the biggest effect is usually one nobody treated as a cost decision. **Output tokens require more processing capacity than input tokens, and cached input tokens consume no dedicated capacity at all**, so a change in either moves the sizing before it moves the bill. Record the date you verified those two properties against current documentation; they are stated by the vendor and they are perishable. **Do not carry any per-model ratio out of this exercise** — those live on companion pages this repository did not fetch, and none is asserted anywhere in this programme.

**3 · Go back to your own item 2, then break it.** Find what you wrote for opening item 2 — the most expensive design decision and whether it was chosen for that reason. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then write the entry properly, for the decision you now think dominates.

> **Criterion.** A pass names the decision, names the alternative that was not taken, estimates what the alternative would have cost, and states honestly whether cost was a criterion at the time — including **no**, which is the commonest true answer and the one most learners edit out. It fails if the decision is named without an alternative, because a cost with no counterfactual is a number rather than a decision. **The reason the honest "no" is worth more than a retrofitted "yes" is that it locates a class of decision your team makes without a cost input**, which is actionable, whereas a retrofitted justification is not.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Starve a colleague on purpose.** Point two consumers at one shared allowance with no per-consumer limit and run them together. Then introduce a token limit policy keyed on consumer identity and run them again.

> **What you should find.** That without accounting, the allowance is consumed by whichever consumer is least careful rather than by whichever is most important, and that nothing in the failure identifies the culprit unless a counter key was chosen in advance. **This is fairness under contention, and it is a design decision rather than an operational response.** Note also the fail-fast property: input tokens can be estimated at the edge so that an over-limit request never reaches the backend — **rejecting before you pay to discover the rejection.**

**5 · Price your own observability, then delete something.** Take the signals that survived [S061](/wiki/seminars/S061.md) and attach a cost to each.

> **Scoring criterion.** A pass produces at least one signal that survived the question-and-asker test and does not survive the price, with the deletion recorded and what will now be missed stated. **If nothing changes, either your estate is unusually lean or your costs are not real figures**, and the second is far more common. The rule this makes concrete is the one [S061](/wiki/seminars/S061.md) borrowed from a published standard — collect what you need rather than what you can — and this session is where "need" acquires a denominator.

**6 · Hand it over and sit on your hands.** Give the model to a customer engineer and ask them to answer one question with it: what would it cost to double the volume of one thing they care about? You may not help. You may watch.

> **What to notice.** Whatever they could not do is the model's real specification, and **the gap between a model you can run and a model they can run is the entire content of this session.** Record where they stopped verbatim. The practice corpus this programme is built on **mechanises the teardown of its own assets to the level of a register-triggered state machine and leaves the transfer of capability as an unrecorded aspiration** — so there is no procedure to copy here, and what you write is the beginning of one.

**7 · Price the same agent twice — once as one session, once at your real concurrency.** Take a component that scales per session rather than per replica. Cost it at a single session on the tier you would instinctively choose. Then multiply by the number of conversations you expect to be live at once. Then repeat both at the tier below.

> **What you should find.** That the single-session figure is small enough to make the generous tier look free, and that the difference between the tiers becomes the largest line in the model once concurrency is applied. **The sizing you set describes one session, not the agent**, and billing accrues across all active sessions — so **the tier is a multiplier and every argument that ends "it is only a little more" is wrong by a factor you have not written down.** Then do the honest part: **you do not know your concurrency.** The published right-sizing procedure is empirical — run a representative workload, read processor use, available memory, request rate and duration off the telemetry, compare peaks against allocation — which means the number arrives after the thing exists. **Write the assumption on the page and mark it unmeasured, with the observation that will replace it and the date somebody will make it.** An assumption on the page can be corrected; an assumption in somebody's head becomes an invoice.

**8 · Find the brake, and find out that there isn't one.** For your design, answer two questions in writing: what alerts when spend rises, and what *stops* it. Then check the second answer by asking who can pull it and whether anybody ever has.

> **Scoring criterion.** A pass distinguishes the alarm from the brake and names each. **The platform states affirmatively that there is no hard spend cap** — budgets notify and do not stop — so a design whose only answer is a budget alert has an alarm and no brake, and the pass records that in those words. The strongest answer identifies the gateway token limit from step 5 as the brake and notices it was introduced as a *fairness* mechanism, then says who owns the policy and on what date somebody last tripped it deliberately. **A fail writes "budgets are configured" and stops**, which is the sentence this exercise exists to catch. **And if the honest answer is that nothing stops it, write *nothing*** — a blank reads as an oversight and a stated *nothing* reads as a decision somebody can overrule.

## What this does not establish

**Nothing here shows that a cost model changes a design decision, or that a customer who holds one uses it.** No source available to this programme reports an outcome for either. The practice corpus records no cost side anywhere in its value claims, no failure, no post-deployment record and **no evidence that any delivered system survived, was maintained by the customer, or was still running months later.**

**The transfer requirement in this session is written past the source.** The corpus mechanises the teardown of its own assets to the level of a register-triggered state machine and leaves the transfer of capability as an unrecorded aspiration. **There is no procedure to teach**, so this session assembles one from a single reflective sentence and marks it. That is design reasoning, and [S075](/wiki/seminars/S075.md) inherits it as the same class of claim.

**The platform material is definitional and perishable, and it is not evidence about anything.** The capacity documentation states how one vendor's capacity model behaves; it makes no performance claim that could be checked independently and offers no comparison against any other vendor's model. Its concrete numbers — per-model throughput ratios, minimum deployment sizes, prices, default allocations, regional availability — live on companion pages that **were not fetched**, and **none is asserted anywhere in this programme.** Its choice architecture is the vendor's, and the trade-offs it presents are real trades presented inside a commercial frame. The gateway documentation is the same in kind: **no performance or cost-saving data of any kind**, benefits stated as plausible mechanisms, availability varying by service tier, and parts of the surface in preview.

**The inference and serving literature is where the mechanism is warranted and the magnitudes are not.** The inference results are specific to one accelerator family, one model and one context length, and predate several now-standard techniques. The serving comparison is against systems of its own period. **The reasoning transfers, the coefficients do not, and no figure from either appears here.**

**One of the retrieval sources was not read.** The compression paper's full text could not be retrieved — the deposit endpoint returned an automated challenge and the publisher version is paywalled — so its account in this session is reconstructed from established secondary knowledge of the method. **No experimental result, dataset, code length or recall figure from it is asserted**, and it should be obtained and checked before any assessed use. The disk-index paper is open and read, but its results belong to a particular benchmark and hardware era whose baselines have all since advanced.

**The efficiency warning is verified and narrow.** The openly published handbook's sustainability checklist recommends a set of measures that its own disclaimers page immediately undercuts row by row, arguing that each may be net negative in context and that optimisation tooling does not guarantee the saving it names. **That is a specimen of a contradiction inside one document, not a finding about any particular technique**, and this session uses it as the former. The same handbook asserts four unsupported magnitude claims that are recorded elsewhere in this repository as specimens and **reproduced nowhere.**

**The claim this session is entitled to make is narrower than the one it would like to make.** It is not that costing improves outcomes. It is closer to a definition: **the architecture sets the token path, the token path sets the bill, and a commitment purchased before the shape of the workload is known is a bet.** The vendor's own instruction — deploy, verify, then commit — is the concrete instance, and it is the strongest thing on this page precisely because it is somebody else's documented guidance rather than this design's argument.

**The per-session and licensing material establishes shapes, not amounts, and the shapes are all this session claims.** That sizing describes one session and bills across concurrency is documented; **what your concurrency is, is not knowable before the system exists, and the vendor offers a procedure rather than a formula because there could not be one.** The thresholds inside that procedure are heuristics with no evidence attached and are reproduced nowhere. That governance licensing is per human rather than per agent is documented; **that the real binding constraint is sponsor attestation capacity is this repository's own reading, has no vendor warrant, and carries no published value.** That there is no hard spend cap is stated affirmatively by the platform; **no measure exists of how often the absence of one has cost anybody anything**, and none is asserted.

**Both new sources are definitional, single-vendor and among the most perishable material here.** One perished visibly while being read — four pages read in full, roughly thirty through excerpts with tables truncated, several consulted pages explicitly superseded and still rendering, preview status pervasive. The other reached general availability within months of the reading, is substantially preview, rests partly on a page that arrived fragmented with capability tables reduced to headers, and has several central pages unread. **Neither publishes any efficacy, adoption or outcome figure. Every number in either is a dated platform parameter, and none — including the licence price — appears anywhere in this session.**
