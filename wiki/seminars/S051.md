# S051 · The System Model Before the Threat

**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** none
**Whitepaper:** [WP-051](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-051.md)

**Entry state:** this-programme-only

## The situation

You have a running increment from [Stage 5](/wiki/quarters/Q5.md) and somebody has asked for a threat model. Every instinct you have says to start naming threats.

Do not. **The threats are the easy part and they are worthless until there is something for them to attach to.**

Answer these in writing before any instruction, and keep the answers.

1. Name a dataflow in your system. Now name it again, in a way another person would name identically.
2. Which of your components sit inside the same trust zone, and who told you?
3. For one flow, what is the transport, and did you read that anywhere or assume it?
4. What would happen to every later stage of an assurance process if this first artefact were wrong?
5. What in your system is currently a paragraph of prose that ought to be a numbered thing?

**Item 1 is the one that matters.** Two engineers describing the same flow in prose will produce two descriptions, and every downstream step that tries to join them will silently join the wrong things. Item 4 has one answer and it is the reason this session comes first.

## What you must produce

A **system model** in three parts, written so that a stranger could act on it.

- **A dataflow diagram.** Nodes carry a category and a trust zone. Edges are directed and **labelled with integers only** — never with protocol text, never with a description.
- **A dataflow attribute table**, one row per numbered flow, carrying transport, data classification from a closed list you define first, authentication, authorisation and purpose.
- **Two side-channels that travel with the table and are never folded into it:** an **inferred-values section** pairing every inference with the principle that supports it, and an **open-questions list**.

Then a **characteristics checklist**: the architectural traits your system does and does not have, each marked present or absent **with a named component or a flow number as its evidence.**

## What good looks like

- **Every flow has an integer and nothing else in its label.** Protocol, payload and purpose live in the table, not on the edge.
- **The data classification comes from a closed list**, and the list was written before the table was filled in.
- **Every inferred cell appears in the inferred-values section**, paired with the principle that produced it. A cell you assumed and a cell you read are indistinguishable in the table by design — the distinction lives in the side-channel, and the side-channel therefore has to survive.
- **No cell hedges.** *Probably TLS* is a failed cell. Uncertainty is relocated, never qualified in place.
- **The characteristics checklist cites components and flow numbers**, not adjectives.
- **The open-questions list is not empty**, and if it is, the model says why that is credible.

## How it goes wrong

- **The picture** — a diagram that communicates to a human and joins to nothing. *Warning sign:* your edges have words on them.
- **The premature threat** — threat names appear before the flows are numbered, and the model is then bent to fit them. *Warning sign:* you wrote a threat down in the first hour.
- **The complete-looking model** — no inferred values, no open questions, everything known. *Warning sign:* the model was easier to write than the system was to build.
- **The unevidenced checklist** — traits marked present because the system feels like the kind of system that has them. *Warning sign:* a tick with no component beside it.
- **The hedged cell** — uncertainty preserved politely, inside the table, where it will be read as a value. *Warning sign:* the words *typically*, *should be* or *generally* anywhere in a row.
- **The stale table** — you or your agent carried a table forward from earlier in the conversation instead of re-reading the file, and it drifted. *Warning sign:* you cannot say when the version in front of you was last read from disk.

## Do it

Work in an order that makes reversal cheap.

**First, name and number.** Enumerate components, assign trust zones, then draw the edges and number them. **Do not attribute anything yet.** The numbering is the act that converts your prose into something joinable, and it is the last moment at which renaming is free.

**Second, attribute one flow completely**, including its inferred-values entries, before attributing any other. A single finished row shows you whether your closed list is adequate; twenty half-finished rows show you nothing and are expensive to redo.

**Third, use an agent on the remaining rows, and give it a diet.** Hand it the numbered diagram and the closed list, and nothing else. Then read every row it produces and ask one question of each: **did it read this or infer it?** Every inference it made and did not declare is the thing this stage exists to catch, and you will find some.

**Fourth, command the re-read.** When you return to the model, re-open the file rather than working from the table already in front of you. A model — and a person — will confidently reuse stale context, and in a chained process the reuse is invisible.

Then set up your folder as a blackboard: one named artefact per output, each stage reading only what it is entitled to read. **You do not need an orchestration framework and you should not build one.** A folder with named artefacts and explicit refusals is a sufficient integration contract, and it has a property no graph gives you for free — **a missing prerequisite genuinely fails**, without any model's cooperation.

## Show it

Hand the model — and only the model — to somebody who did not build the system.

They have two jobs. **First, trace one flow end to end** and say what protects it. If they cannot, the model is a picture. **Second, point at any cell and ask whether it was observed or inferred.** If they have to ask you, your side-channel is not doing its work, and you have just seen the first step of the failure that [S054](/wiki/seminars/S054.md) is about.

## What this does not establish

Nothing here shows that a model built this way produces a better threat model, a safer system, or anything else.

**The pipeline this session is derived from reports no outcome evidence of any kind** — no comparison against a human-authored model, no defect-escape record, no coverage against a known vulnerability set. Its design was clearly shaped by observed failures: the instruction to re-read rather than trust context, and a writing strategy built for interruption, are not things anyone specifies prospectively. **The observations are nowhere recorded, so the strongest evidence that this ordering works cannot be cited.**

The claim that front-loading judgement and back-loading mechanics reduces error is **design reasoning with an argument behind it and no measurement behind it.** The argument is that an error at the front of a chain is not a local error, which is true of chains generally. Whether this chain benefits is untested.
