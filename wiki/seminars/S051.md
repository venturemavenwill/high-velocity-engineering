# S051 · The System Model Before the Threat

**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** Agentic threat modelling — components, flows and trust boundaries drawn and agreed before any threat is named
**Whitepaper:** [WP-051](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-051.md)
**Retrieves from:** S023, S026, S046, S047
**Entry state:** this-programme-only

## The situation

You have a running increment from [Stage 5](/wiki/quarters/Q5.md) and somebody has asked for a threat model. Every instinct you have says to start naming threats. Prompt injection. Data exfiltration. Supply chain. The vocabulary is available, it sounds like security, and producing it costs nothing.

Do not. **The threats are the easy part and they are worthless until there is something for them to attach to.**

Here is the shape of the failure you are about to be taught out of. A team produces a threat model that lists a dozen well-known attack categories against a system described in three paragraphs of prose. Every entry is true. Every entry is also true of every other system the team has ever built. Nothing in the document can be assigned to an owner, tested by a check, or closed on evidence, because nothing in it names a thing that exists. The document is filed, and the filing is mistaken for the work.

The corrective is not better threats. It is a model with parts that have names.

**Answer these in writing before any instruction, and keep the answers.** You will be sent back to item 1 in the exercises, and you will need what you actually wrote rather than what you now think you would have written.

1. Name a dataflow in your system. Now name it again, in a way another person would name identically.
2. Which of your components sit inside the same trust zone, and who told you?
3. For one flow, what is the transport, and did you read that anywhere or assume it?
4. What would happen to every later stage of an assurance process if this first artefact were wrong?
5. What in your system is currently a paragraph of prose that ought to be a numbered thing?

**Item 1 is the one that matters.** Two engineers describing the same flow in prose will produce two descriptions, and every downstream step that tries to join them will silently join the wrong things. Item 4 has one answer and it is the reason this session comes first in the module.

## What you must produce

A **system model** in three parts, written so that a stranger could act on it.

1. **A dataflow diagram.** Nodes carry a category and a trust zone. Edges are directed and **labelled with integers only** — never with protocol text, never with a description.
2. **A dataflow attribute table**, one row per numbered flow, carrying transport, data classification from a closed list you define first, authentication, authorisation and purpose.
3. **An inferred-values section**, travelling with the table and never folded into it, pairing every inference with the principle that produced it.
4. **An open-questions list**, likewise separate, carrying everything you could not establish.
5. **A characteristics checklist**: the architectural traits your system does and does not have, each marked present or absent **with a named component or a flow number as its evidence**.
6. **A one-line statement of what your model excludes** — the classes of harm this artefact is structurally unable to represent.

## The material

### An error at the front of a chain is not a local error

The design this module is built on comes from a customer-embedded engineering organisation within a large technology vendor, which built a chained pipeline of single-purpose agents that turns architecture documents into a security plan and then into work items in a live backlog. This repository holds an original paraphrase of it at [research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-agentic-threat-modelling.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-agentic-threat-modelling.md). **It is internal, access-controlled, quoted nowhere and attributable to nothing**; it can supply a design to examine and it cannot supply an external warrant for any claim.

The practitioner judgement that opens its first stage is the one worth carrying: an error at the front of a chain is not a local error. It silently corrupts everything downstream, which makes front-stage formatting discipline a security property rather than a stylistic one. **Every stage after this one is a join.** If the thing being joined on is ambiguous, the join is wrong and looks right.

The design's most transferable move follows directly. **It front-loads all semantic judgement and back-loads all mechanics.** Dataflows get integers at the first stage; every later stage refers to flows only by number. Threats and controls get prefixed identifiers as they are created. By the time the pipeline reaches the step where threats meet controls, the reasoning problem has been reduced to a join over two identifier sets, and by the assembly step to set resolution and text splicing. **Prose does not join. Identifiers do.**

### The closed list, and why the edge label is an integer

Two specification decisions in that design do more work than anything else in it, and both look pedantic until you have watched a model fail without them.

The first is that **edges carry integers and nothing else**. Protocol, payload, purpose and direction all live in the attribute table, keyed by the integer. The reason is not aesthetic. A label reading *HTTPS to the vector store* is a fact, an assumption and a purpose fused into one string, and no later stage can take it apart. Once flows are numbered, a threat can say *flow 7*, a control can say *flow 7*, and a reviewer can check whether they mean the same thing. Nothing about a prose edge label supports that check.

The second is that **data classification comes from a closed enumeration written before the table is filled in**. An open vocabulary produces *customer data*, *customer content*, *PII*, *personal data* and *sensitive* as five values for one class, and every count over that column is then wrong in a way no reader can see.

There is a third device in the same family that is easy to miss and worth stealing: the **compact index**. Each rich artefact also emits a deliberately lossy projection of itself — a few columns per entry — so that a downstream consumer can perform the join without loading the full text. The pipeline hands its mapping subagents the projections and forbids them the originals. **Imposing an information diet on a subagent is a design move, not a limitation**, and you will use it in the workflow below.

### Uncertainty is relocated, never qualified in place

The rule that produces the most argument in a room is this one: **hedging language is banned inside table cells.** Not discouraged — banned. *Probably TLS* is a failed cell. *Should be authenticated* is a failed cell. The stated reason is that a hedge inside a cell destroys machine consumption downstream, and that is correct as far as it goes.

But the rule only works because of what it is paired with. Uncertainty does not disappear; it is **relocated** into the inferred-values section, where each inference is recorded beside the principle that produced it, and into the open-questions list. The cell carries the value. The side-channel carries the provenance.

**That pairing is load-bearing, and it is where the module's central failure begins.** A design that bans the hedge and keeps the side-channel is honest. A design that bans the hedge and loses the side-channel has converted a guess into a stated fact, and it will not know it has. [S054](/wiki/seminars/S054.md) traces exactly that path through a real chain. Build the side-channel now, deliberately, and notice how weakly it is attached to the thing it explains.

### The checklist is a routing key, and evidence is a component or a flow number

The characteristics checklist — which architectural traits the system has — reads like documentation and is not. In the source design it is **the routing key for everything downstream**: which threat classes get considered, which control catalogues get fetched, which questions get asked at all. A trait wrongly marked absent removes a whole branch of the analysis silently, and no later stage can recover it, because no later stage knows the branch existed.

This is why every entry must cite a component or a flow number rather than an adjective. *Handles untrusted input: yes* is a mood. *Handles untrusted input: yes — component `ingest-worker`, flows 3 and 11* is a claim somebody can check and, more importantly, can falsify.

The same principle governs the whole genre. The design's own instruction for closure is that **evidence is a file path and a line, never an assertion that something is secure** — and its status stage enforces that by re-deriving a control's status from the workspace rather than accepting a tick. You will meet that mechanism properly at [S055](/wiki/seminars/S055.md). Here, the point is narrower: an artefact whose entries cannot be traced to a named thing is not an engineering artefact, whatever it looks like.

### Why the diagram is not the system, and two named reasons it drifts

A dataflow model is a static claim about a dynamic thing, and the production machine-learning literature names two properties that make the claim decay faster than you expect ([research/02-technical-foundations/crowe-machine-learning-production-systems.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/crowe-machine-learning-production-systems.md)).

**Entanglement** — "changing one thing changes everything" — means a change made in one region reappears in behaviour somewhere unrelated, so a boundary you drew last month may no longer be where the system's actual boundary is. **Configuration** is sharper still: hyperparameters, versions and feature lists live in configuration, and the slightest error there "can cause radically different model behavior that won't be picked up with traditional software tests." **A trust boundary implemented in configuration is a trust boundary that ordinary testing does not defend.** That is a first-class finding about your model and belongs in the open-questions list rather than in a footnote.

The same source supplies the framing that justifies doing any of this: observability is imported from control theory with the asymmetry that **"You can only control a system to the extent that you can observe it"**, operationalised as *alertability* — can a failure be made unambiguous — and *actionability* — is there enough signal to find the cause. A system model is the map that makes both possible. Without it, an alert names a symptom and no one can say which part of the system produced it.

**That source's limits are heavy and must travel with it.** It is practitioner guidance from a commercial publisher: no study design, no comparison group, no measurement that its practices outperform anything. Three of its four authors worked at one large vendor, so its default toolchain is that vendor's. Some of its foundational claims rest on anonymous practitioner testimony that cannot be checked. **Every quantity in it is a property of one system after one training run; mechanism and direction transfer, magnitudes do not, and none appears here.**

### What the genre looks like when somebody does it honestly

The openly published engineering handbook maintained by the same broad organisation carries a worked threat model, and it is the best available specimen of the finished genre ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md), pages read 2026-07-30). It models an edge system doing video capture, optical character recognition and text-to-speech, and it carries an architecture diagram, an asset table, a dataflow narrative, an enumerated threat list and a properties table mapping principle to threat to mitigation.

Two features of it are instructive rather than defective. **Its assumptions are stated separately from its threats** — the side-channel discipline, arrived at independently. And **every threat in it is marked "Not Mitigated"**, which is what an honest threat model looks like at the moment it is first written. Hold that; it is the module's closing argument and you meet it at [S055](/wiki/seminars/S055.md).

**Its limits are equally instructive.** The handbook's own threat-modelling page is a signpost rather than a treatment — four phases at about two sentences each, with the threat taxonomy linked rather than explained. The worked example's defects are clerical: duplicated row numbers, and several key artefacts held as images rather than as text, which means the most reusable parts of it cannot be joined on by anything. **The handbook also contradicts itself in at least a dozen places without noticing, cannot be dated as a whole, and reaches for a magnitude in at least four places while warranting none of them. Those four figures are recorded in this repository as specimens and are reproduced nowhere.**

### Inventory is a separate problem from analysis, and it is usually the missing one

One last thing your model needs and will not have: certainty that it is complete. The workload-posture documentation calls the target artefact a **generative AI Bill of Materials** — "application components, data, and AI artifacts from code to cloud" — and the reason it exists as a product feature is that organisations routinely cannot enumerate their own AI estate ([research/06-microsoft-platform/purview-dspm-for-ai-and-defender-ai-spm-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/purview-dspm-for-ai-and-defender-ai-spm-docs.md)).

The same material names **attack path analysis**, whose subject is "toxic combinations": individually acceptable configurations that compose into a breach. That idea belongs in this session rather than the next, because **a combination is a property of a model and not of a component.** You cannot see one in a list of parts. You can only see one in a graph — which is what you are drawing.

**Read that documentation for its structure and not for its assurance.** It is first-party and definitional: it states what the tooling does, and it publishes no detection rate, no false-positive rate and no coverage measurement of any kind. **Assert none, because none exists.** One of the two pages behind that note is explicitly superseded by a version this repository has not read, the other was read through search excerpts rather than fetched in full, and agent-level capability is mid-licence-migration. Its operational bounds — how much of an estate is scanned, how often, and how long results take to appear — are stated in the source as specific numbers and are paraphrased here as what they are: **a bounded sample, on a periodic cadence, with a latency measured in days.** An assessment with those properties gives you a posture estimate, not a control.

## The workflow

**Build the model as named artefacts in a folder, with the agent on an information diet, then attack the model rather than the system.**

**Step 1 — make the folder the contract.** Create `security/model/` in your increment's repository. One artefact per output, each with a fixed name: `components.yaml`, `dataflows.yaml`, `attributes.yaml`, `inferred.yaml`, `open-questions.md`, `characteristics.yaml`. Nothing writes outside its own file. **You do not need an orchestration framework and you should not build one** — a folder of named artefacts with explicit refusals is a sufficient integration contract, and it has a property no orchestration graph gives you for free: a missing prerequisite genuinely fails, without any model's cooperation.

**Step 2 — write the closed list before the table.** Put your data classification enumeration at the top of `attributes.yaml` and treat adding to it as a change that needs a reason.

**Step 3 — number the flows, and stop.** Enumerate components, assign trust zones, draw the directed edges, give each an integer. **Attribute nothing yet.** This is the last moment at which renaming is free.

**Step 4 — install the rule that makes hedging fail.** Create `.github/instructions/model.instructions.md` so the constraint reaches the executor rather than living in your head:

```markdown
---
applyTo: "security/model/**"
---

# System model authoring rules

- Edge labels are integers. No protocol, no payload, no purpose on an edge.
- `classification` MUST be one of the enum at the top of attributes.yaml.
- NEVER write a hedged cell. "probably", "typically", "should be", "generally"
  and "assumed" are forbidden inside any cell value.
- If a value is not established, write the value you infer AND add a matching
  entry to inferred.yaml naming the principle that produced it.
- If you cannot infer it, write `UNKNOWN` and add a line to open-questions.md.
- Re-read the file from disk before editing it. Do not reuse a table from
  earlier in this conversation.
```

**Step 5 — attribute one flow completely, by hand.** All columns, plus its `inferred.yaml` entries. One finished row tells you whether your closed list is adequate. Twenty half-finished rows tell you nothing and are expensive to redo.

**Step 6 — put the agent on a diet for the rest.** In GitHub Copilot agent mode, hand it `dataflows.yaml` and the enumeration only — **not your architecture documents, not the code.** Then read every row it returns and ask one question of each: *did it read this or infer it?* Every inference it made and did not declare is the defect this stage exists to catch, and there will be some.

**Step 7 — cross-check your inventory against a machine's.** If you have Defender for Cloud available, compare its discovered inventory of AI components against `components.yaml`. **Do the hand pass first.** The surprise is the point, and it runs both ways: things you own that you did not list, and things you listed that the scanner cannot see — a portal setting, a tenant policy, a person.

**Step 8 — attack the model, not the system.** One prompt to the agent, with the model attached: *for each trust boundary I have drawn, argue that it is in the wrong place, and name the flow number that proves it.* Then read what came back, keep what beats your own reasoning, and **record what you rejected and why**. An agent asked to review a model will approve it. An agent asked to relocate a boundary has to find one.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That the distinction between a value you read and a value you assumed is invisible in the artefact by construction, and survives only in a file that nothing forces anyone to open. You will have built, in an afternoon, the exact structure whose collapse [S054](/wiki/seminars/S054.md) is about — and you will have built it correctly, which is what makes the collapse worth watching.

## What good looks like

- **Every flow has an integer and nothing else in its label.** Protocol, payload and purpose live in the table, not on the edge.
- **The data classification comes from a closed list**, and the list was written before the table was filled in.
- **Every inferred cell appears in the inferred-values section**, paired with the principle that produced it. A cell you assumed and a cell you read are indistinguishable in the table by design — the distinction lives in the side-channel, and the side-channel therefore has to survive.
- **No cell hedges.** *Probably TLS* is a failed cell. Uncertainty is relocated, never qualified in place.
- **The characteristics checklist cites components and flow numbers**, not adjectives.
- **The open-questions list is not empty**, and if it is, the model says why that is credible.
- **The model states what it cannot represent.** A method that never declares its own scope exclusions invites the reader to assume it has none.

## How it goes wrong

- **The picture** — a diagram that communicates to a human and joins to nothing. *Warning sign:* your edges have words on them.
- **The premature threat** — threat names appear before the flows are numbered, and the model is then bent to fit them. *Warning sign:* you wrote a threat down in the first hour.
- **The complete-looking model** — no inferred values, no open questions, everything known. *Warning sign:* the model was easier to write than the system was to build.
- **The unevidenced checklist** — traits marked present because the system feels like the kind of system that has them. *Warning sign:* a tick with no component beside it.
- **The hedged cell** — uncertainty preserved politely, inside the table, where it will be read as a value. *Warning sign:* the words *typically*, *should be* or *generally* anywhere in a row.
- **The stale table** — you or your agent carried a table forward from earlier in the conversation instead of re-reading the file, and it drifted. *Warning sign:* you cannot say when the version in front of you was last read from disk.
- **The boundary drawn where the diagram is tidy** — trust zones follow the layout rather than the identity model. *Warning sign:* every boundary in your picture is a rectangle and none of them crosses a line.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The system model is carried into [S052](/wiki/seminars/S052.md), where every threat you write must bind to a component name or a flow number taken from it, and it is assessed as part of the plan at [S055](/wiki/seminars/S055.md).

**1 · Hand the model to a stranger and give them two jobs.** Somebody who did not build the system gets the model and nothing else.

> **What they must do.** First, **trace one flow end to end and say what protects it.** If they cannot, your model is a picture. Second, **point at any cell and ask whether it was observed or inferred.** If they have to ask you rather than reading the answer, your side-channel is not attached to anything — which is the first step of the failure [S054](/wiki/seminars/S054.md) is about, arrived at by yourself.

**2 · Break your own closed list.** Take your data classification enumeration and find one real value in your system that does not fit any of its members.

> **What you should find.** That the tempting repair is to add a catch-all — *other*, *mixed*, *internal* — and that the catch-all will absorb every hard case for the rest of the module. **A closed list with an escape hatch is an open list with extra ceremony.** The correct repairs are to split an existing member, or to record the value in the open-questions list until somebody with authority decides. Both are more expensive than the escape hatch and both preserve the column's meaning.

**3 · Go back to your own item 1, then break it.** Find the two namings you wrote for one dataflow before you read anything. **Do not rewrite them yet.** Put them beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then produce the flow properly, as a numbered edge with a completed attribute row and its inferred-values entries.

> **Criterion.** A flow naming passes if two engineers working independently from the same architecture would produce the same identifier for it, and if every property of the flow is recoverable from the table rather than from the name. It fails if the name carries information — *the encrypted call to storage* both names and asserts, and the assertion is unfalsifiable because it is welded to the identifier.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Run the agent on a diet, then run it fed.** Do workflow step 6 twice: once with the numbered diagram and the enumeration only, once with your whole repository attached. Compare the two attribute tables row by row.

> **What to notice.** The fed run usually produces more filled cells and fewer `UNKNOWN`s, and that is the finding rather than the improvement. **A model given more context declares less uncertainty**, because more of its inferences now feel supported. Count the entries each run put in `inferred.yaml`. If the better-informed run declared fewer inferences, you have watched provenance evaporate under exactly the conditions that make it hardest to notice.

**5 · Enumerate your own bill of materials by hand, then let a scanner disagree with you.** Run workflow step 7.

> **What you should find.** Disagreement in both directions. Components the scanner sees that you forgot are the easy half. **The instructive half is what neither of you can see** — a control that exists only as a tenant setting, a review that exists only as a person's habit, a dependency that arrives through a colleague's account. Record those in `open-questions.md`; they are the population that [S052](/wiki/seminars/S052.md)'s binding rule will exclude, and you will need the list.

**6 · Name your model's scope exclusions, closed book.** Without re-reading, write down three classes of harm your model structurally cannot represent.

> **Model answer.** Anything with no dataflow: insider action by an authorised person; physical and organisational controls; contractual and third-party obligations; supply-chain compromise of a build system that is not in your diagram; and the whole class of harms that arise from a correct system used for a purpose nobody sanctioned. The published design this module rests on **states no scope exclusions anywhere in its fourteen agent definitions**, which is the specific defect this exercise inoculates against.
>
> **Then compare.** Look at your answer to opening item 5 — what in your system was prose that should have been a numbered thing. Did any of it turn out to be unrepresentable rather than merely unwritten? That distinction is the one this exercise exists to install.

## What this does not establish

Nothing here shows that a model built this way produces a better threat model, a safer system, or anything else.

**The pipeline this session is derived from reports no outcome evidence of any kind** — no comparison against a human-authored model, no defect-escape record, no coverage against a known vulnerability set. Its design was clearly shaped by observed failures: the instruction to re-read rather than trust context, and a writing strategy built for interruption, are not things anyone specifies prospectively. **The observations are nowhere recorded, so the strongest evidence that this ordering works cannot be cited.** It is also internal and access-controlled, so nothing in it can be linked, attributed or externally verified by you or by anyone reading your work.

The claim that front-loading judgement and back-loading mechanics reduces error is **design reasoning with an argument behind it and no measurement behind it.** The argument is that an error at the front of a chain is not a local error, which is true of chains generally. Whether this chain benefits is untested.

**The observability framing is practitioner guidance, not research.** No study design, no comparison group, no measurement that its practices outperform alternatives; three of its four authors worked at one vendor whose toolchain is its default; some of its foundational claims rest on anonymous practitioner testimony. Every quantity in it is a property of one system after one run, and no figure from it appears here.

**The governance documentation is first-party, definitional and silent where it matters most.** It says what discovery and posture assessment do. It publishes **no detection rate, no false-positive rate and no coverage measurement**, so it is no evidence that what it finds is what is there. One of its two pages is explicitly superseded by a version not read here; the other was read through search excerpts rather than fetched. Its stated operational bounds are paraphrased in this session rather than reproduced, because a number lifted out of a perishable product page is the least durable thing you could carry away.

**The published worked example is a genre specimen and not a standard.** Its own handbook contradicts itself in at least a dozen places, cannot be dated as a whole, and asserts four magnitudes it never warrants. It is cited here for the shape of the artefact and for one honest habit — assumptions stated separately from threats — and for nothing else.
