# S043 · Tests as the Specification

**Module:** [M09](/wiki/modules/M09.md) · **Quarter:** [Q5](/wiki/quarters/Q5.md) · **Complexity class:** 3
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** none
**Whitepaper:** [WP-043](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-043.md)
**Retrieves from:** S022, S023, S041
**Entry state:** either

---

## The situation

The open playbook states two rules about tests that are unusual for their bluntness. Code is **incomplete** if it is not accompanied by tests. Tests are committed in the **same change** as the code they cover, and *I will add tests next* is recorded on that page as not acceptable.

Both are ordinary engineering hygiene and neither is why this session exists.

This session exists because of a single piece of practitioner testimony held in the internal corpus, in speaker notes, contradicting the collection's own headline. On a mature production codebase, the assistant **could not** be asked to add a feature end to end: the generated code reused outdated patterns and ignored architectural constraints. The workaround was to **make failing tests the specification and spend most of the human effort there.**

The same collection's customer-facing material describes end-to-end feature delivery as the model's signature achievement. **Neither document notices the other.**

Answer these before instruction.

1. Write down what *done* means for one behaviour in your slice, in a form something other than you could check.
2. Which of your architectural constraints could a test catch, and which could only a person catch?
3. Hand the implementation to somebody who has never seen this codebase. What do they get wrong first?
4. What does a test suite that passes on wrong code look like?
5. If the implementation becomes cheap, where does the human effort go?

**Item 5 is the session's centre of gravity.** The testimony's answer is *into the specification*, and that is a redistribution of effort rather than a saving. Nothing in the collection prices the redistribution, and neither does this session.

## What you must produce

**A failing specification** for one behaviour in your slice, written before the implementation exists.

- The behaviour, expressed as checks that currently fail for the right reason.
- **The untestable residue** — every constraint from item 2 that no check can express, each with the review lens that would catch it instead, and who would have to hold that lens.
- A statement of what the specification does **not** constrain, so that a reader knows what an implementation is free to do.
- One deliberate wrong implementation that passes your specification, and the check you added because of it.

## What good looks like

- **The checks fail before the implementation and fail for the stated reason.** A check that fails because the code does not compile has not specified anything yet.
- **At least one architectural constraint is expressed as an executable check**, not merely named.
- **The untestable residue is non-empty and each entry has an owner.** A specification with nothing outside it has not been examined.
- **The wrong implementation is genuinely wrong and genuinely passes.** Producing it is the assessed thinking, not a formality.
- **The specification is legible to somebody who has not seen the codebase**, and this is checked rather than asserted.
- **Nothing in the specification depends on who reads it.** A specification that only works when a human reads it is not a specification.

## How it goes wrong

- **The retrofitted test** — written after the implementation, shaped by it, incapable of failing on it. *Warning sign:* you have never seen it red.
- **The tautological check** — it asserts what the code does rather than what the behaviour requires. *Warning sign:* changing the requirement would not change the check.
- **The shape check** — it asserts that output has the right form and says nothing about whether it is right. *Warning sign:* your check would pass on a plausible-looking wrong answer.
- **The declared-absent constraint** — an architectural rule that cannot be expressed as a check is quietly dropped rather than moved to the residue. *Warning sign:* your residue is empty.
- **The specification only its author can read** — it encodes context nobody else has. *Warning sign:* explaining it takes longer than reading it.
- **The effort that vanished** — the implementation got cheap, the specification did not get more effort, and nobody noticed where the work went. *Warning sign:* the specification took less time than the implementation.

## Do it

**First, write the checks and watch them fail.** A specification you have not seen fail is a hypothesis about a specification. This is the same discipline [S022](/wiki/seminars/S022.md) installed for requirements, arriving here as executable form rather than as prose.

**Second, build the wrong implementation on purpose.** Take your specification and satisfy it badly — the least defensible code that goes green. You are looking for the gap between *passes* and *correct*, and the gap is where the untestable residue lives. **This is the session's most productive twenty minutes and the one learners skip**, because building something you intend to throw away feels like waste when the specification already passes.

**Third, delegate it and watch what happens.** Give the specification to an executor that has not seen your codebase — an agent is the convenient one, but a colleague from another team is a better instrument if you can get one — and let them implement against it without further explanation. **Record what they got wrong.** That record is the specification's actual quality measurement, and it is the only one available to you today.

Then read the testimony against your own result. The failure it describes is specific: outdated patterns reused, architectural constraints ignored. **If those appeared in your delegate's output, your specification did not carry them and your residue was incomplete.** If they did not appear, say what property of your codebase or your specification prevented it, because that property is the thing worth carrying to the next engagement.

## Show it

Hand your specification and nothing else to one other person, and ask them to describe the behaviour back to you.

Every place their description differs from your intent is a defect in the specification, not in their reading. **Fix the specification, not the explanation** — the explanation is not going to be in the room next time.

## What this does not establish

Nothing here shows that specification-first work produces better software, faster delivery or fewer defects.

The playbook states its testing norms as commitments and **supplies no comparative evidence**, and one of its pages reaches for a cost multiple attributed to an old study through a link that cannot be followed in any current browser. **That figure is not repeated here and is not a warrant for anything.**

**The practitioner testimony this session is built on is a single account.** It is the best-evidenced thing in its collection because it names a codebase property, a failure mechanism and a workaround that cost something — and it is still one engineer's experience, in speaker notes, with no measurement attached. It is treated here as a hypothesis worth teaching, not as a finding.

**What the session actually establishes is a conditional:** if you cannot state what *done* means in a form something other than you could check, you cannot delegate the behaviour and know whether you got it. That is close to a definition of delegation, and it is the strongest claim available.

The wrong-implementation exercise, the untestable-residue register and the requirement that residue entries carry an owner are **design reasoning with no external warrant.**

## Perishable content in this session

This session has no platform anchor, so the second column records organisational and tooling instances rather than a version-bound service.

| Durable claim | Perishable instance |
|---|---|
| A specification that only works when a human reads it is not a specification | The current capability of whatever executor is used to test that claim |
| A check that has never failed has specified nothing | Today's testing frameworks and what they make easy to assert |
| Some constraints have no executable expression and need a named holder | Which architectural constraints current tooling can and cannot check |
| Cheap implementation moves the human effort rather than removing it | The present cost of generating an implementation, which is what makes the redistribution visible |
