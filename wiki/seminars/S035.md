# S035 · Breaking a System That Appears to Work

**Module:** [M07](/wiki/modules/M07.md) · **Quarter:** [Q4](/wiki/quarters/Q4.md) · **Complexity class:** 3
**Strands:** SY · ME · PE · CF
**Whitepaper:** [WP-035](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-035.md)
**Depends on:** S009, S034
**Re-tests:** S028, S032, S033
**Platform anchor:** Bicep and the `what-if` operation as the review instrument · **MCP servers:** Azure MCP Server (write, disposable environments only) · Microsoft Learn MCP Server

---

## What this day is for

**This is the first day student changes reach production.**

The module has produced a specification, an evidence argument, a failure-handling decision and a contract — four documents about a component that has never been disturbed. Today the component is disturbed, on the live system, with real users on it.

The target is not the break. It is the question that precedes it: **what would count as this system working, and how would you know if it stopped?** A student who cannot answer that has nothing to observe, and the break becomes an event rather than an experiment.

The day's shape is therefore: state the criterion, predict the consequence, break it under controlled conditions, observe, repair, and report what actually happened — including the parts that were not predicted.

And it is the day the quarter's other sentence is discharged. Four days ago the agent began writing to Azure. **The half of that bargain nobody teaches is the review**, and the review instrument this platform supplies — `what-if`, which shows you what a deployment will change before it changes it — has documented false positives and documented false negatives. It is an instrument. [S029](/wiki/seminars/S029.md) said what to do with those. Today the room does it, on the tool it is about to stake a production change on.

---

## The production conditions

Stated before phase 1, and taken from the [Substrate and Infrastructure](/wiki/program/10-Substrate-and-Infrastructure.md) page:

- **A bounded window.** Ninety minutes, announced to users in advance as a maintenance period.
- **A staff-held rollback,** exercised only if a user is affected beyond the announced degradation. The student is not consulted before it is used, and its use is not held against them.
- **The criterion is committed in writing before the window opens.** A student without a committed criterion does not get a window.
- **One student change at a time,** sequenced, with the system returned to a known state between them.
- **A named staff member is accountable** for the substrate throughout and may decline any proposed break without appeal.
- **The agent does not apply the change.** Copilot may author the change, reason about its blast radius, run `what-if` and draft the report. **The application is a human act by the named student.** This is not a claim that agent-applied changes are unsafe in general; it is a claim that a day whose entire subject is consequence you cannot delegate must not delegate the consequence. Students who object to this are asked to make the argument at phase 6, where it is a good one.

## Phase 1 · Pretest and prediction (25 min)

Five items on the student's own component.

1. Is this component working right now? On what evidence?
2. What would you have to observe to conclude it had stopped working?
3. What will your break do that you have not predicted?
4. Predict: will the rollback be used today, for anyone in the room?
5. **Copilot prediction, sealed.** You will shortly ask the agent to express your break as a Bicep change and to tell you whether it is safe to deploy. Write down what it will tell you, and **name one thing that could be wrong with your change that its evidence is incapable of showing.** Sealed with items 1 to 4 and opened at phase 8.

Item 2 is the day's criterion demand. Item 3 asks for the unpredicted, which is not answerable and is asked anyway. Item 5 is the module's hardest sealed item and the design expects a low hit rate: it asks students to reason about the limits of an instrument they have not yet been told is an instrument.

**Warrant.** Pretesting on the working criterion before the criterion is discussed. Item 3's unanswerability is deliberate and is revisited at phase 8 against what actually occurred. Item 5 is sealed here per instrumentation rule 8, and its low expected hit rate is the point — it establishes, before phase 3 supplies the answer, that the cohort does not currently hold a model of what its review tool cannot see.

Free recall, no notes. The module's cumulative set.

- The S032 act-without-confirming distinction.

The final item is the module bounding itself, as at S030.

**Warrant.** Cumulative retrieval at gaps of one to four days, with a synthesis item and a boundary item.

## Phase 3 · Contrasting cases and generation (40 min)

**Executed, not read.** Groups receive **four break proposals** for a component like theirs, each with a stated criterion, a predicted consequence, and the Bicep change that implements it.

- **Proposal A** — the criterion is observable, the break is bounded, the prediction is falsifiable.
- **Proposal B** — the criterion is observable and the break is unbounded; the blast radius is not stated.
- **Proposal C** — the break is bounded and the criterion is unobservable, so nothing can be concluded.
- **Proposal D** — everything is well specified and the break tests something the contract does not promise.

Groups **run `what-if` against each of the four** on a disposable copy of the environment, read the output, and judge: would you run it?

Then the actual outcomes of all four are supplied — **all four were run on this system in previous years**, and one caused a real incident.

The adjudication defeats the room twice.

**The design payload is proposal D**, which most groups accept: a well-executed experiment against a promise nobody made is the module's characteristic waste, and identifying it requires yesterday's contract rather than today's tooling.

**The executed payload is that the proposal that caused the incident had the cleanest `what-if` output.** Its dangerous change sat inside a Bicep module, and the operation short-circuited — the documentation's own term — so every resource in that module was omitted from the analysis. The review was read as a clean bill of health. It was a blank page. On the tooling versions in use at the time, no diagnostic said so.

Task after: **for proposal D, state what it would have established and why that is worth nothing** — and, separately, **state what you would now require before trusting a `what-if` output.**

**Warrant.** Contrasting cases across break designs, adjudicated by real prior outcomes and now by a real review artefact. Running the tool rather than reading about it is what makes the second finding land: a group that has just read four outputs and formed a judgement from them is the right audience for the news that one of the outputs was silent. The second closing task is the day's bridge into phase 4 and is the [Copilot Practice Standard](/wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md)'s frontier discipline applied to a tool rather than to the agent.

## Phase 4 · Consolidating instruction (40 min)

1. **The criterion comes first.** Without a stated working criterion, a break produces an event and not evidence.
2. **Blast radius** as an explicit quantity: who is affected, how many, for how long, and what they experience. Estimated before, measured after.
3. **What to break:** the contract's guarantees, not the implementation's conveniences. Proposal D's error.
4. **Falsification applied to a running system** — S009, at scale and with consequences. The claim under test is the contract from S034.
5. **Observation before, during and after,** and why the before is the part students omit.
6. **Rollback as a decision made by someone else,** and why that is correct: a person inside an experiment is the worst-placed to judge when to stop it.
7. **The review instrument, characterised — and marked perishable.** `what-if` predicts the changes a deployment will make without making them, and it is available at resource-group, subscription, management-group and tenant scope. It is not an oracle and the documentation does not claim it is. **It has false positives:** properties that are set automatically as defaults during deployment are reported as deleted when they are absent from your file, which the documentation itself calls *noise*; and any property whose value comes from a `reference` expression is reported as changing every single time, because the operation compares a resolved value against an unresolved one. **It has false negatives, and they are the ones that matter:** a resource whose identifier or API version cannot be computed outside the deployment context *short-circuits*, and where a whole module short-circuits, **every resource in it is excluded from the results**; resources deployed through template links are not evaluated and do not appear at all; and when nested-template expansion exceeds its limits — five hundred nested templates, eight hundred resource groups, or five minutes — everything remaining is reported as `Ignore`. **A `what-if` output is not a list of what will change. It is a list of what the operation was able to work out**, and the difference between those two sentences is the whole of today's argument about agent-authored infrastructure.
8. **Defaults are opinions and thresholds are policy.** Three, and one of them determines whether you learn that your review was incomplete. **Diagnostics reporting an incomplete analysis are emitted only by recent tooling** — from Az PowerShell 13.1.0 and Azure CLI 2.75.0 — and the documentation is explicit that on earlier versions the same short-circuit occurs and *happens silently*. So whether you are told your review has a hole in it is a property of the version of a command-line tool on the machine that ran it. The validation level defaults to full provider validation including permission checks, which is the right default and is slower, and can be lowered to static template checking by a flag whose effect is invisible in the output. And `Delete` appears as a change type only for complete-mode JSON deployments, so **an output containing no deletions is not evidence that nothing will be deleted.**
9. **What the documentation does not say.** It states that noise occurs and does not say how much. It states that short-circuiting is *rare* and gives no rate, no dataset and no measurement. **There is no published figure anywhere for how often a `what-if` output is incomplete, and none is asserted here.** That is the instrument's characterisation: you know the failure modes exist, you know their direction, and you cannot know their frequency from the vendor. The available response is the one [S029](/wiki/seminars/S029.md) gave — do not rely on a single instrument for a claim you cannot afford to be wrong about — and the day's operational form of it is step 2's independently recorded before-state.
10. **Reporting what happened,** including the unpredicted. **The report's structure is stated: what was claimed, what was done, what was observed, what was not predicted, and what is now believed.** Five parts.
11. **What you owe the users** whose service you degraded — which is nothing, if the degradation was within the announced window, and something, if it was not.

**Warrant.** Consolidation before the production window rather than after, which is a departure from the assessment-day pattern and is necessary: the window is an execution, not an assessment. Points 7 to 9 are the day's platform content and satisfy the [Copilot Practice Standard](/wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md); they are placed at 7, immediately after the rollback point, because both concern **the same idea in two registers — that the judgement you most want to make yourself is the one you are worst placed to make.** Point 8 is the day's sharpest default, because it is a default about whether you are told the truth.

## Phase 5 · The production window (90 min)

**Class 3. The criterion, the break and the repair are all the student's.**

Sequenced, one change at a time, in a ninety-minute announced window.

1. **Commit the criterion and the predicted consequence** in writing. Reviewed by the accountable staff member, who may decline.
2. **Record the before-state** against the criterion, **independently of the tooling** — the observation you would trust if the review output were blank, because at phase 3 one of them was.
3. **Produce the change with Copilot, and review it.** The `what-if` output is attached to the artefact, **with a statement of what it does not cover for this particular change** — whether any module short-circuited, whether any reported deletion is default noise, whether any change is a `reference` artefact, and whether the tooling version in use emits diagnostics at all.
4. **Execute the break.** By hand, by the student, per the production conditions.
5. **Observe** — against the criterion, and for anything else.
6. **Repair,** and confirm the criterion is met again.
7. **Record the blast radius actually observed** against the estimate.

Where the rollback is used, steps 6 and 7 still occur and the day's artefact is unaffected.

**The artefact carries three further elements**, per the Copilot Practice Standard.

- **Prompt record.** What you asked the agent for, in order, including what you abandoned.
- **Intervention record.** Every point at which you corrected it. **An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless step 3's review statement demonstrates that your own procedure would have detected an intervention-worthy error — a recorded check for module short-circuiting is the accepted form and is the day's canonical example.
- **Frontier statement.** One thing the agent did faster than you could have, and one thing it did badly that you had to catch. **On this day the second half has a natural candidate and students should resist it**: "it could not tell me whether the change was safe" is a limitation of the tooling, not of the agent, and a frontier statement that confuses the two has misread the day.

**Warrant.** Whole-task at class 3 with real consequence. The commitment at step 1 is what makes the ninety minutes an experiment; the accountable staff member's veto is what makes it safe. Step 3 is the practical turn's contribution and the quarter's thesis in one artefact element: **the review is submitted alongside the change, and it is submitted with its own limits stated**, which is the only form in which an agent-authored infrastructure change can honestly be said to have been reviewed. Step 2's independence from the tooling is the countermeasure phase 3 earned.

## Phase 6 · Error clinic (45 min)

Extended, and **the assessed reporting has not yet happened**, which is deliberate.

Instructor first: a substrate change made by staff that had a larger blast radius than predicted, with attention to which part of the prediction was wrong and whether anyone would have caught it.

Then the agent case, which is the quarter's. A team asked Copilot to "tidy up the gateway Bicep and move the backend definitions into a module." It did, competently. The refactor was correct in every respect but one: a circuit-breaker rule that had been defined inline was not carried into the module. The team ran `what-if` before deploying, as their process required. **The output showed no change to the backend**, because the module had short-circuited and its resources were excluded from the analysis, and the tooling on the build agent was old enough not to emit a diagnostic. The change deployed. Eleven days later a model endpoint degraded, the priority pool did not fail over — for the reason [S033](/wiki/seminars/S033.md) point 8 gives — and the outage lasted forty minutes.

Attention goes to why review did not catch it. **The team did everything their process asked.** They reviewed the diff, they ran the preview, they read the output, and the output was clean. The failure is not in the agent's work and not in the reviewer's diligence; it is that **the artefact everyone treated as evidence of safety was an artefact of what the tool could compute**, and nobody had ever asked what it could not. That question is [S029](/wiki/seminars/S029.md)'s, it takes one sentence, and it is the difference between this outage and no outage.

Then students: what surprised you in the last ninety minutes.

**Firewall applies. Nothing said here enters the S035 specification decision.**

**Warrant.** Error management immediately after the highest-consequence activity in the programme so far, and before the report is written — so that the disclosure is not shaped by what the student intends to submit. The agent case is the [Copilot Practice Standard](/wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md)'s required addition and is the module's terminal one: it is chosen because **every human in it behaved correctly**, which is the only version of the write-access risk worth teaching. An agent failure that a careful person would have caught teaches carefulness. This one teaches instrument characterisation, which is the programme's actual answer.

## Phase 7 · Peer articulation (25 min)

Pairs. Each student states **what they did not predict**, and the partner's task is to say whether their own break would have exhibited the same thing.

The room's unpredicted observations are then collected on the board and grouped.

**Warrant.** Interactive-tier articulation on the day's most valuable output. Grouping the unpredicted across the cohort turns individual surprises into a class of things this system does that nobody models.

## Phase 8 · Calibration and commitment (20 min)

- Items 1 to 4 resolved. **Item 3's unanswerable question is revisited against what actually occurred**, and item 4's rollback prediction against whether it was used.
- **Item 5 opened.** The sealed predictions are read against what the agent said about the change and against what its evidence could actually have shown. The recorded measure is how many named a specific blindness — short-circuiting, an unevaluated template link, an expansion limit, default noise — rather than a general caution. **The design expects this rate to be the module's lowest, and a high rate would indicate the cohort held the instrument model before phase 3 supplied it**, which would make phase 3's second payload redundant.
- The blast-radius estimates shown against measurements, unattributed, as a distribution of ratios.
- **The S020 load-change comparison is revisited:** students are asked what their capacity prediction got wrong, now that they have seen this system under a deliberate disturbance.
- Each student writes one sentence for the substrate's documentation about the component they broke.
- Retrieval schedule: next morning, day 3, and the module set in the inter-module gap.

## Assessment

Specification item in SY201, and the module's terminal artefact.

Per the [Assessment Architecture](/wiki/program/03-Assessment-Architecture.md)'s rule 1, the checked elements are: presence of a committed criterion observable before the break; presence of a before-state record **recorded independently of the review tooling**; **presence of the `what-if` output together with a statement of what it does not cover for this change**; presence of an observation record; confirmation of repair; presence of both estimated and measured blast radius; a non-empty unpredicted section; **the prompt record; the intervention record; and the frontier statement.**

**Nothing else is assessed.** The quality of the break, the size of the effect, whether the rollback was used, and whether the `what-if` output turned out to be complete are all outside the specification — **a student who reported a limitation that did not bite has done the assessed work.**

**A report with an empty unpredicted section fails.** This is the day's sharpest clause. **An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless step 3's review statement demonstrates that the design could have detected an intervention-worthy error.

**Dispositions observed:** integrity, in whether the reported blast radius is the measured one.

## Perishable content in this day

| Durable claim | Platform instance, perishable |
|---|---|
| A criterion observable before the change is what makes a disturbance an experiment | The before-state record, taken independently of tooling |
| Blast radius is estimated and then measured | The ratio distribution across the cohort |
| Rollback belongs to someone outside the experiment | The accountable staff member's decision |
| **A review instrument has false positives and false negatives, and you must know its direction** | `what-if` noise and `reference` artefacts; short-circuiting, template links, `Ignore` at expansion limits |
| A default can determine whether you are told your evidence is incomplete | Diagnostics emitted only from Az PowerShell 13.1.0 and Azure CLI 2.75.0 |
| The five-part report, and the mandatory unpredicted section | — durable in full |

The substrate's specific component, the day's measurements, every tool version threshold, every expansion limit and every change-type name are **perishable** and are marked at points 7 to 9. The criterion-first principle, blast radius as an estimated and measured quantity, rollback as an external decision, the five-part report, **and the discipline of characterising a review instrument before relying on it** are durable and are what the specification assesses.

**Scope, strong form.** Treating `what-if` as a characterised instrument is **safe** for deciding how much weight to put on a deployment preview, for knowing which independent observation to take alongside it, and for reasoning about agent-authored infrastructure generally. It is **unsafe** as a model of resilience testing — the day teaches a bounded change to a single component under an announced window, and resilience engineering requires fault taxonomies, steady-state hypotheses over aggregate metrics and blast-radius control this day does not teach and this programme takes up nowhere, since [S080](/wiki/seminars/S080.md) works incidents rather than experiments. **Students should not leave believing they have learned resilience engineering.** It is also unsafe as a claim about any other platform's preview tooling, whose failure modes must be established rather than assumed.

## What to take from this day

**The claim to hold.** A criterion observable *before* the change is what makes a disturbance an experiment rather than an event. And a review instrument has false positives and false negatives whose direction you must know: **a preview is not a list of what will change, it is a list of what the operation was able to work out.**

**What the sources license, and what they do not.** Production disturbance practice, blast-radius reasoning and rollback discipline are grounded in [research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md); the criterion and observation discipline follows [research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md). **Direction and mechanism only; no magnitude is asserted.** The vendor states that noise occurs and does not quantify it, and calls short-circuiting rare without publishing a rate — so the instrument's failure modes are known, their direction is known, and their frequency is not obtainable.

**Guidance for your own work.**

- **Commit the criterion in writing before you touch anything**, and record the before-state independently of the tool you are about to rely on.
- **Submit the review with its own limits attached** — which module short-circuited, which reported deletion is default noise, and whether your tooling emits the diagnostic.
- **Place the preview in the right class** on the [Agent Configuration Standard](/wiki/program/13-Agent-Configuration-Standard.md). A clean output is an *observed result* about what the operation could evaluate; reading it as evidence that nothing will break is *unsupported inference*.

**Where this is easy to get wrong.** Treating a clean review as a clean bill of health. Every human in this case did what their process asked, and the artefact they trusted was an artefact of what the tool could compute — a blank page nothing marked as blank.
