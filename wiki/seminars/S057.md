# S057 · The Scope Test That Survives Agentic Delivery

## The situation

Every governance regime begins with a test that decides whether it applies. **That test is the most consequential sentence in the regime, and it is the one nobody reads.**

The regime this module is built on was found in two generations, and the change between them is the session.

**The older test asked, in effect, whether the team had built or fine-tuned a model.** An effort test. It made sense when the deliverable was a model and the effort was the risk.

**The newer test asks whether an artefact capable of AI behaviour crossed the boundary into the customer's control**, such that they can execute it, test it or operationalise it. A surface test.

Answer these before instruction.

1. Under an effort test, how much of your last delivery was in scope?
2. You configured an agent, wrote no model code, and handed it over. Effort test: in scope?
3. Where exactly is the boundary in your engagement? Name what is on each side.
4. Your test excludes something that can cause the harm the regime exists to prevent. What is it?
5. Your test includes something harmless. What does that cost?

**Item 2 is the whole argument.** Under an effort test, the fastest and most consequential work in modern delivery escapes governance entirely — you did no model work, so nothing applies, and the customer is now running something that acts. **Items 4 and 5 are the assessed work**, because a test whose author has found no fault in it has not been read by its author.

## What you must produce

**A scope test for your own engagement, written in the artefact-transfer form**, plus a classification of every one of your deliverables against it, plus two named failures of your own test.

- **The test**, in one paragraph. What has to have happened for the regime to apply.
- **The boundary**, described physically: who holds the credentials, who can change the thing, who can run it when you leave.
- **Every deliverable classified**, including the ones you would rather not classify.
- **One case your test wrongly excludes**, which could cause the harm the regime exists to prevent.
- **One case your test wrongly includes**, and what that costs.
- **What you will do about the excluded case anyway.**

## What good looks like

- **The test asks about a boundary crossing, not about effort.** If it can be satisfied by *we did not train anything*, it is the old test in new words.
- **The boundary is physical.** Credentials, control, the ability to run it after you leave. **A boundary you can only describe organisationally is a boundary you cannot apply.**
- **Every deliverable is classified**, and the awkward ones are classified rather than omitted.
- **The wrongly excluded case is concrete and consequential.** Not a hypothetical — something in this engagement or one adjacent to it.
- **The wrongly included case is priced.** Governance that catches everything is governance that gets routed around.
- **The excluded case has a plan anyway**, and the plan does not depend on the regime applying.

## How it goes wrong

- **The effort test in disguise** — the wording changed and the question did not. *Warning sign:* your test's answer depends on how much work you did.
- **The unlocatable boundary** — you can describe it in an organisational chart and not in credentials. *Warning sign:* you cannot say who can change the artefact next month.
- **The excluded configuration** — an off-the-shelf agent, configured by you, placed in a consequential workflow, and out of scope because you authored nothing. *Warning sign:* your test's line is *did we write it*.
- **The faultless test** — you found nothing wrong with your own test. *Warning sign:* you wrote it and then looked for confirmation.
- **The everything test** — it catches all your deliverables. *Warning sign:* you feel safe, and nobody will comply.
- **The orphaned exclusion** — you named the excluded case and then did nothing, because the regime did not require it. *Warning sign:* your answer to the last element is that it is out of scope.

## Do it

**First, write the test as a boundary question.** *Has an artefact capable of AI behaviour passed into the customer's control such that they can execute, test or operationalise it?* Adapt it to your engagement. **Do not soften the verb.** *Provided*, *shared* and *made available* all describe things a customer cannot necessarily run.

**Second, locate the boundary in credentials rather than in prose.** Who holds them. Who can change the artefact. Who can run it when your team is gone. **[S027](/wiki/seminars/S027.md) taught that extending what already runs means inheriting somebody else's boundary**, and this is where that inheritance becomes a governance fact rather than an architectural one.

**Third, classify everything, starting with what you would rather leave out.** The prompt file you handed over. The evaluation harness. The notebook somebody asked for. The configuration you exported so their team could reproduce it. **The awkward items are the reason the test exists.**

**Fourth, break your own test twice.** Find the case it wrongly excludes — the one that can do the damage the regime is for. **The regime this session is built on draws its own line at authored artefacts and explicitly excludes configuration using existing controls**, which is defensible on ownership grounds and indefensible on harm grounds: a carelessly configured off-the-shelf agent placed in a hiring workflow can do exactly the damage the regime exists to prevent, and it is out of scope. **Yours will have an equivalent hole. Find it.** Then find the case it wrongly includes, and price it, because **a regime that catches everything is a regime people route around** and an unenforced rule is worse than a narrow one.

**Fifth, plan for the excluded case anyway.** This is the element that separates a governance exercise from an engineering one. **A harm you have identified does not stop being a harm because your test did not catch it**, and what you do about it is not the regime's business — it is yours.

## Show it

Give your test and your unclassified list of deliverables to somebody else, and have them classify independently.

**The disagreements are the finding.** Every item you classified differently is an ambiguity in the test's wording, and the wording is the only part of a scope test that ever gets used. Rewrite for the disagreements rather than defending your reading of them.

Then ask them the question you cannot ask yourself: **which of these would you be uncomfortable being responsible for?** If that set and your in-scope set are different, one of them is wrong and it is usually the test.

## What this does not establish

**Nothing here establishes that a scope test of any shape catches harm.** No source available to this programme reports a case in which a scope determination prevented anything, and the regime it is taken from **publishes no evidence that any assessment it triggered ever changed a system.**

**The comparison between the two generations is the strongest available evidence and it is evidence about documents, not outcomes.** The newer test closes a real hole in the older one — that is a structural observation about coverage, made by reading both, and it is not a measurement of consequence.

**The authored-artefact line is a genuine unresolved conflict rather than an oversight.** Ownership and harm point in different directions and the regime chose ownership. This session does not choose; it requires the learner to find the case where the choice bites and to handle it outside the regime. **That is a workaround, not a fix**, and a graduate should expect to keep applying it.
