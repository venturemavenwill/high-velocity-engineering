# S057 · The Scope Test That Survives Agentic Delivery

**Module:** [M12](/wiki/modules/M12.md) · **Quarter:** [Q6](/wiki/quarters/Q6.md) · **Complexity class:** 4
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** Foundry prompt agents versus hosted agents — what changes about scope when the system gains its own code, compute and the ability to act without being asked
**Whitepaper:** [WP-057](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-057.md)
**Retrieves from:** S027, S050, S056
**Entry state:** this-programme-only

---

## The situation

Every governance regime begins with a test that decides whether it applies. **That test is the most consequential sentence in the regime, and it is the one nobody reads.**

The regime this module is built on was found in two generations, and the change between them is the session.

**The older test asked, in effect, whether the team had built or fine-tuned a model.** An effort test. It made sense when the deliverable was a model and the effort was the risk.

**The newer test asks whether an artefact capable of AI behaviour crossed the boundary into the customer's control**, such that they can execute it, test it or operationalise it. A surface test.

The reason the change is urgent rather than tidy is sitting in the vendor's own product taxonomy. There is a fork between an agent that is a configuration and an agent that is your code running on compute you are paying for, and a second fork between an agent that responds when spoken to and one that can **act without being asked.** Crossing either fork changes the architecture, the failure modes and the governance requirements together — and under an effort test, crossing both can be done without anybody writing a line of model code.

Answer these before instruction.

1. Under an effort test, how much of your last delivery was in scope?
2. You configured an agent, wrote no model code, and handed it over. Effort test: in scope?
3. Where exactly is the boundary in your engagement? Name what is on each side.
4. Your test excludes something that can cause the harm the regime exists to prevent. What is it?
5. Your test includes something harmless. What does that cost?

**Item 2 is the whole argument.** Under an effort test, the fastest and most consequential work in modern delivery escapes governance entirely — you did no model work, so nothing applies, and the customer is now running something that acts. **Items 4 and 5 are the assessed work**, because a test whose author has found no fault in it has not been read by its author.

## What you must produce

**A scope test for your own engagement, written in the artefact-transfer form**, plus a classification of every one of your deliverables against it, plus two named failures of your own test.

1. **The test**, in one paragraph. What has to have happened for the regime to apply.
2. **The boundary**, described physically: who holds the credentials, who can change the thing, who can run it when you leave.
3. **Every deliverable classified**, including the ones you would rather not classify, each carrying its **initiation model** — whether it can act without being asked.
4. **One case your test wrongly excludes**, which could cause the harm the regime exists to prevent.
5. **One case your test wrongly includes**, and what that costs.
6. **What you will do about the excluded case anyway.**

## The material

### An effort test was correct once, and stopped being correct without being changed

The older test asked about effort because effort was where the risk lived. When the deliverable was a trained model, the team that trained it had touched the data, chosen the objective, selected the evaluation and decided what counted as good enough. **Effort and risk were the same variable**, so measuring one measured the other.

That coupling has broken. A team can now assemble a consequential system by configuring things other people built, writing nothing that resembles model code, and the effort measure reads near zero while the surface measure reads high. **An effort test does not fail loudly when this happens. It returns *out of scope* and everybody moves on.**

**The newer test asks about a boundary crossing instead** — whether an artefact capable of AI behaviour passed into the customer's control such that they can execute, test or operationalise it ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md)). It is the single best governance idea in that collection, and the reason is that it asks about **where the thing ended up** rather than about **how hard it was to make**, and only the first of those survives a change in how systems are assembled.

**It then draws its own line at authored artefacts, and explicitly excludes configuration using existing controls.** That is defensible on ownership grounds and indefensible on harm grounds, and this session says both. A carelessly configured off-the-shelf agent placed in a hiring workflow can do exactly the damage the regime exists to prevent, and under this test it is out of scope.

**The corpus's limits are severe and travel with every use of it.** It is access-controlled and paraphrased here, never quoted. It contains no citation of any kind across approximately ninety documents. It contradicts itself in more than a dozen places. **It records no failure and states no exclusion criteria**, and comparing its two generations is a comparison of documents, not of outcomes — the newer test closes a hole that can be seen by reading, and nobody has measured what either test caught.

### The fork the platform makes visible: configuration, or your code on compute

The vendor's agent taxonomy is the cleanest available instrument for asking what actually crossed the boundary, because it separates the two things learners conflate.

**Prompt agents** use declarative configuration in a portal or in code, are managed by the platform with **no agent compute**, and suit agents that include no custom code and need no custom hosting. **Hosted agents** run **your containerised code** on managed container compute, and suit agents with custom code ([research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md)). **The line between them is whether you are running code, and therefore whether you are paying for and operating compute.**

That is a hosting distinction, and on its own it is not a governance one. **The governance fork is the other one**, and the same documentation family draws it sharply. **Declarative agents** are for focused scenarios, are designed to be used by individuals, are limited to the host's orchestrator and models, are hosted inside the productivity suite, and **do not support proactive interactions — they "rely on user-initiated interactions"**. **Custom engine agents** handle complex workflows, support group collaboration, are fully customisable including model choice and orchestration, are available inside and outside the suite, and **"enable agents to trigger actions automatically, even without direct user input"**.

Five axes are doing work there — orchestration control, model choice, initiation model, collaboration scope and hosting location — and **the initiation model is the one that is routinely overlooked and the one this session is built on.**

One more axis matters for a scope test and is easy to miss. **Owning the orchestrator is the fundamental fork**, because it determines model choice, extensibility and lifecycle tooling together. A team that took the host's orchestrator handed over a configuration; a team that brought their own handed over a system.

**The documentation's limits.** First-party and single-vendor, **read through search-returned excerpts rather than full page fetches with several comparison tables truncated**, so the feature lists are not exhaustive. **Highly perishable** — product names and feature boundaries are actively changing, and a limitation recorded today may be closed tomorrow. It publishes **no adoption data, no failure-rate data, no cost comparison and no outcome data of any kind**, and it compares the vendor's options only against each other. Read it for the axes; discard the products.

### Proactive messaging changes the failure model, not the feature list

**An agent that can act without being asked is a different system from one that cannot** — in its architecture, its failure modes and its governance requirements alike. The distinction is worth stating precisely because it is not obvious to somebody who has only built chatbots.

A reactive system fails in front of a person. Someone asked, someone read the answer, and the human in the loop is not a control anybody had to design — it is a property of the interaction. **A proactive system fails in an empty room.** The failure happens on a schedule or on a trigger, the output goes somewhere, and the first person to see it may be the person affected by it.

Every control this module has installed so far assumes a reader. **A proactive system removes the reader by design and does not announce that it has.** That is why the initiation model belongs in a scope test rather than in an architecture note: it is the single property that most changes what the regime has to cover, and it can be acquired by ticking a box.

### Alignment is trained behaviour, and it was not trained for this

The usual answer to *what stops it doing something bad* is that the model is aligned. That answer is real and it is much narrower than it sounds.

Instruction following is a **post-training property, not something that emerges from pretraining scale**. The paper that established the standard method is explicit that making models bigger does not inherently make them better at following a user's intent, and names the failure modes it set out to reduce: untruthful, toxic, or simply not helpful ([research/02-technical-foundations/ouyang-instructgpt.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/ouyang-instructgpt.md)). The method has three stages — demonstrations, a reward model trained on human comparisons, and optimisation against that learned proxy — and **each can fail independently.**

Two of its recorded limits bear directly on a scope test. **The result is defined relative to a particular labeller pool, a particular set of labelling instructions and a particular prompt distribution**, none of which is universal, and the paper documents this rather than hiding it. And **human preference is not the same as correctness**: optimising for preference can produce confident, agreeable, wrong answers. The authors state that their models still make simple mistakes and frame the work as a promising direction rather than a solution.

The successor method removes the reinforcement-learning stage by showing that the standard objective has a closed-form solution reachable with a simple classification loss on preference pairs ([research/02-technical-foundations/rafailov-dpo.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/rafailov-dpo.md)). Its own limits section carries the sentence a scope test needs: **it inherits every weakness of the preference data**, including annotator bias, sycophancy incentives and the gap between preference and correctness. And **its reported results are on controlled sentiment generation, summarisation and single-turn dialogue — multi-turn and agentic settings are outside the evaluated scope.**

**So: the behaviour you are relying on was trained by somebody else, against somebody else's preferences, and evaluated on settings that are not the one you are about to deploy into.** That is not an argument against using it. It is an argument for the artefact being in scope.

There is a harder version of the same point. The planning literature holds that auto-regressive models **cannot by themselves plan**, and cannot self-verify **"because self-verification is after all a form of reasoning"** ([research/02-technical-foundations/kambhampati-llms-cant-plan.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/kambhampati-llms-cant-plan.md)). **A system that cannot reliably plan, cannot check its own work, and can act without being asked is a combination worth naming out loud**, and no governance test that returns *out of scope* for it is doing its job. That paper is a position paper whose claims are arguments and have been contested, and it is scoped to auto-regressive models as of early 2024; the architectural discipline is what transfers.

### A chain that reads untrusted input and ends in an authenticated write

The most useful worked example available to this programme is a multi-agent pipeline built inside a customer-embedded engineering organisation to produce security plans. It is well designed — a folder used as a blackboard, early conversion of prose to stable identifiers, deliberately lossy indexes so a downstream agent can join without loading everything — and it fails in a way this session exists to make visible ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-agentic-threat-modelling.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-agentic-threat-modelling.md)).

**Every input stage reads untrusted repository content, feeds it to a model that writes files, and the chain terminates in an authenticated write to an external tracking system.** Nothing anywhere in it addresses content in the analysed repository attempting to influence the analysis. **For a pipeline whose entire subject is threat modelling, that is the sharpest available demonstration that the initiation and boundary properties of a system are not the properties its authors were thinking about.**

The second finding is the one a scope test has to internalise. **There is no approval gate anywhere.** Human decisions exist as inputs — which frameworks apply, what the scope is, where to write the results — and there are placeholders shaped like sign-off, with blanks for a reviewer and a date. **They are a form to fill in, not a precondition for anything.** The one place a person is unavoidably present is authenticating the write, **and that person is being asked for a credential, not for a judgement.**

**That is what an out-of-scope determination looks like from the inside**, and nobody in the chain did anything wrong.

**Its limits.** Internal and access-controlled, paraphrased here and never quoted, and **it cannot supply an external warrant for any claim.** No outcome evidence exists for any of it. The scar tissue in its prompts implies observed failures that are nowhere recorded. **The source contains operational attack detail that is not reproduced here and should not be.** Every concrete integration in it is perishable; the pattern is the durable part.

## The workflow

**Classify every deliverable against the platform's own agent taxonomy, then write the test that catches the ones that can act.**

**Step 1 — list every deliverable, including the ones you would rather not.** The prompt file. The evaluation harness. The exported configuration. The notebook somebody asked for. The connection that was set up so their team could reproduce your run. **The awkward items are the reason the test exists.**

**Step 2 — record the taxonomy and the date you read it.** Open the platform's agent-type material and write down the fork: declaratively configured with no agent compute, against your containerised code on managed container compute. Then find the second fork — whether the option supports proactive triggering or relies on user-initiated interaction. **Do not work from memory. Both tables move.**

**Step 3 — classify each deliverable on three axes, in writing.** Who owns the orchestrator. Whether it runs your code on compute somebody pays for. **Whether it can act without being asked.** The third is the one to get right, and the answer is frequently *not today, and the setting is one click away.*

**Step 4 — locate the boundary in credentials rather than in prose.** Who holds them. Who can change the artefact next month. Who can run it after your team is gone. **[S027](/wiki/seminars/S027.md) taught that extending what already runs means inheriting somebody else's boundary**, and this is where that inheritance becomes a governance fact rather than an architectural one. Note also that the platform's fast setup is documented as **lacking network isolation**, which means a boundary you believed existed in a prototype may simply not be there — prototyping topology and production topology are different things.

**Step 5 — write the test where the engagement's decisions live.**

```yaml
# governance/scope-test.yaml   <- SHAPE, NOT SCHEMA. Your regime's fields will differ.
test_form: artefact-transfer      # NOT effort. If the answer depends on how much work
                                  # you did, this is the old test in new words.
test: >
  The regime applies when an artefact capable of AI behaviour has passed into the
  customer's control such that they can execute, test or operationalise it.

boundary:
  credentials_held_by: "<who, today>"
  can_change_artefact: "<who, next month>"
  can_run_it_without_us: "<who, after we leave>"
  # If you can only answer these from an organisational chart, you have not found
  # the boundary. Answer them from an access control list.

deliverables:
  - name: "<artefact>"
    orchestrator_owner: "host | us | customer"
    runs_our_code_on_compute: false
    can_act_without_being_asked: false      # THE AXIS THAT CHANGES THE FAILURE MODEL
    one_setting_away_from_true: true        # record this separately. It usually is.
    crossed_boundary: true
    in_scope: true

known_failures_of_this_test:
  wrongly_excludes:
    case: "<concrete, from this engagement or one adjacent to it>"
    harm_it_could_cause: "<the harm the regime exists to prevent>"
    what_we_will_do_anyway: "<a plan that does not depend on the regime applying>"
  wrongly_includes:
    case: "<something harmless the test catches>"
    cost: "<compliance load, and the probability people route around it>"

non_conformance:
  # A regime with no legitimate way to say 'we cannot comply yet' gets quietly ignored.
  open_deviations: []      # stated, approvable, dated. Absence here is not compliance.
```

**Step 6 — break your own test twice.** Find the case it wrongly excludes and the case it wrongly includes. **A regime that catches everything is a regime people route around**, and an unenforced rule is worse than a narrow one.

**Step 7 — attack the test with the agent, on the one axis you are worst at.** One prompt: *given this list of deliverables and this test, name every item that could be made to act without being asked by changing a setting rather than by writing code, and say which of those my test lets through.* Then a second: *argue that my wrongly-excluded case is not actually excluded, so that I have to defend my own reading of my own test.* Keep what beats your reasoning; record what you rejected. **An agent that agreed your test was sound was not used as an instrument.**

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That the axis which most changes the failure model — whether the system can act unprompted — is usually a configuration choice rather than an engineering one, and is therefore invisible to any test that asks how much work you did. You will also observe how many of your deliverables sit **one setting away** from a different governance class, which is a number no architecture diagram shows and no effort test can produce.

## What good looks like

- **The test asks about a boundary crossing, not about effort.** If it can be satisfied by *we did not train anything*, it is the old test in new words.
- **The boundary is physical.** Credentials, control, the ability to run it after you leave. **A boundary you can only describe organisationally is a boundary you cannot apply.**
- **Every deliverable is classified**, the awkward ones are classified rather than omitted, and each carries its initiation model.
- **The one-setting-away column is populated honestly**, and it is not empty.
- **The wrongly excluded case is concrete and consequential.** Not a hypothetical — something in this engagement or one adjacent to it.
- **The wrongly included case is priced.** Governance that catches everything is governance that gets routed around.
- **The excluded case has a plan anyway**, and the plan does not depend on the regime applying.

## How it goes wrong

- **The effort test in disguise** — the wording changed and the question did not. *Warning sign:* your test's answer depends on how much work you did.
- **The unlocatable boundary** — you can describe it in an organisational chart and not in credentials. *Warning sign:* you cannot say who can change the artefact next month.
- **The excluded configuration** — an off-the-shelf agent, configured by you, placed in a consequential workflow, and out of scope because you authored nothing. *Warning sign:* your test's line is *did we write it*.
- **The initiation model unrecorded** — every deliverable is classified and none of the rows says whether it can act unprompted. *Warning sign:* your classification would read identically for a chatbot and for a scheduled trigger.
- **The alignment defence** — the artefact is out of scope because the underlying model is aligned. *Warning sign:* you cannot say whose preferences it was aligned to, or on what settings that alignment was evaluated.
- **The faultless test** — you found nothing wrong with your own test. *Warning sign:* you wrote it and then looked for confirmation.
- **The everything test** — it catches all your deliverables. *Warning sign:* you feel safe, and nobody will comply.
- **The orphaned exclusion** — you named the excluded case and then did nothing, because the regime did not require it. *Warning sign:* your answer to the last element is that it is out of scope.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The scope test is carried into [S058](/wiki/seminars/S058.md), which assumes the regime applies, and into [S060](/wiki/seminars/S060.md), where the assessor holds your test and nothing else.

**1 · Classify four deliverables.** For each, say whether it crosses the boundary under an artefact-transfer test, and whether it can act without being asked.

- *A prompt file you wrote and committed to their repository.* · *An evaluation harness you ran and then deleted from your own machine.* · *A configured agent in their tenant, using the host's orchestrator, triggered by a mention.* · *The same agent, with a scheduled trigger enabled.*

> **Model answers.** The prompt file crossed — they hold it, can change it and can run it. The harness turns on a question the list does not answer: did they get a copy? If not, nothing crossed, and **the correct output is a question rather than a classification.** The mention-triggered agent crossed and is reactive. The scheduled one crossed and is **proactive, and it is the same artefact** — which is the exercise. **A governance class that can be changed by a setting is a governance class that must be re-checked when settings change**, and that is what [S058](/wiki/seminars/S058.md) is about.

**2 · Find the fork in the vendor's own taxonomy.** Using the workflow above, locate where the documentation states which agent types support proactive interaction and which rely on user-initiated interaction. Record the page and the date.

> **What you should find.** That one family is documented as designed for individuals, limited to the host's orchestrator and models, and relying on user-initiated interactions; and that the other is documented as fully customisable, available inside and outside the productivity suite, and able to trigger actions automatically without direct user input. **Note what the documentation does not do: it presents this as a capability difference and does not draw a governance conclusion from it.** That conclusion is yours. Note also its recorded limits — first-party, single-vendor, read through search excerpts with tables truncated, **highly perishable**, and carrying no adoption, failure-rate or outcome data of any kind ([research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md)).

**3 · Go back to your own item 2, then break it.** Find what you wrote for opening item 2 — whether a configured agent, handed over with no model code written, is in scope under an effort test. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then classify one real deliverable of your own under both tests and record where they disagree.

> **Criterion.** An answer passes if it identifies that the effort test returns *out of scope* and that this is the failure rather than the answer — that the test is not wrong about the effort, it is asking about the wrong variable. **It fails if it argues the effort test would somehow catch it**, which requires reading a surface question into an effort test, or if it treats the two tests as agreeing.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Trade classifications with somebody else.** Give them your test and your unclassified deliverable list. Have them classify independently, then compare.

> **What to notice.** **The disagreements are the finding.** Every item classified differently is an ambiguity in the test's wording, and the wording is the only part of a scope test that ever gets used. Rewrite for the disagreements rather than defending your reading of them. Then ask the question you cannot ask yourself: **which of these would you be uncomfortable being responsible for?** If that set and your in-scope set differ, one of them is wrong and it is usually the test.

**5 · Retire the alignment defence.** Write down, in two sentences, what you actually know about the alignment of the model underneath your deliverable: whose preferences it encodes, and on what kinds of task that alignment was evaluated.

> **Scoring criterion.** A pass says something like *its instruction-following was trained on demonstrations and preference comparisons collected from a labelling pool and instruction set I do not have, and the published evaluations of methods in this family cover settings such as sentiment control, summarisation and single-turn dialogue rather than agentic operation* ([research/02-technical-foundations/ouyang-instructgpt.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/ouyang-instructgpt.md); [research/02-technical-foundations/rafailov-dpo.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/rafailov-dpo.md)). **A fail says the model is safe because it is aligned.** Note the limits that make this the right answer: alignment is a post-training property defined relative to a particular labeller pool and prompt distribution; **human preference is not correctness**, and optimising for it can produce confident, agreeable and wrong; and the successor method inherits every weakness of its preference data. **None of that is a reason not to use the model. It is a reason the artefact is in scope.**

## What this does not establish

**Nothing here establishes that a scope test of any shape catches harm.** No source available to this programme reports a case in which a scope determination prevented anything, and the regime it is taken from **publishes no evidence that any assessment it triggered ever changed a system.**

**The comparison between the two generations is the strongest available evidence and it is evidence about documents, not outcomes.** The newer test closes a real hole in the older one — that is a structural observation about coverage, made by reading both, and it is not a measurement of consequence. The corpus it comes from is access-controlled, carries no citation of any kind, contradicts itself in more than a dozen places, and **records no failure and no exclusion criteria anywhere.**

**The authored-artefact line is a genuine unresolved conflict rather than an oversight.** Ownership and harm point in different directions and the regime chose ownership. This session does not choose; it requires the learner to find the case where the choice bites and to handle it outside the regime. **That is a workaround, not a fix**, and a graduate should expect to keep applying it.

**The claim that a proactive system is a different governance object is this design's argument, not a finding.** The documentation records the capability difference and draws no governance conclusion from it. Nobody has measured whether systems that can act unprompted produce more harm, or whether teams that classify them separately catch more of it. **The reasoning is checkable; the consequence is untested.**

**The alignment material licenses less than it appears to.** It establishes that instruction following is trained rather than emergent, that the result is relative to a specific labelling pool and prompt distribution, that preference is not correctness, and that the published evaluations of these methods do not cover agentic operation. **It does not establish that any particular deployed model is unsafe**, and this session asserts nothing of the kind. It establishes only that the alignment defence is not available as a reason to put an artefact out of scope.

**The multi-agent pipeline is one specimen, read once, and its own author's observations are unrecorded.** Its scar tissue implies failures that are nowhere reported, so **the strongest evidence that the design works cannot be cited.** It carries no outcome evidence of any kind, and every concrete integration in it is perishable.

**The platform taxonomy is the most perishable content in this session.** Product names and feature boundaries are actively moving; a limitation recorded today may be closed tomorrow, and a course must not teach a limitation as permanent. **Cite the page and the date, never the product.**

**The one claim this session makes without hedging is definitional.** A test whose answer depends on how much work you did is measuring effort, and effort and risk stopped being the same variable when systems became assemblable from other people's components. That is true of the older regime, of your team's shortlist, and of whatever replaces both.
