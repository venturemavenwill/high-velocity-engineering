# S062 · Batching, Throughput and the Latency You Promised

**Module:** [M13](/wiki/modules/M13.md) · **Quarter:** [Q7](/wiki/quarters/Q7.md) · **Complexity class:** 5
**Strands:** SY · IS · ME
**Whitepaper:** [WP-062](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-062.md)
**Depends on:** S019, S034, S061
**Platform anchor:** The API Management AI gateway — `llm-token-limit`, priority-based backend pools, circuit breaker rules · **MCP servers:** Azure MCP Server (write) · Microsoft Learn MCP Server

---

## What this day is for

[S019](/wiki/seminars/S019.md) is the closest structural repeat in the programme, and the design says so out loud. A service, a curve, a knee, and an operating point that is a decision rather than a fact. Five quarters later the service is an inference path, the curve has a different mechanism underneath it, and the decision has the same shape.

What is new is that **the operating point is now reached by grouping requests together**, and grouping is not neutral. A request that waits to be batched is a request that took longer so that other requests could be served. [S061](/wiki/seminars/S061.md) explains why anyone would accept that trade: generating output moves the whole weight set to produce one token, and one movement can serve many requests at once. The movement is the expensive thing and it is shared.

So batching converts latency into throughput. The conversion rate is measurable. And the exchange is not made evenly — **every batching and scheduling policy decides which requests wait, and the decision is invisible in every aggregate the policy is judged by.**

**There is a second thing that is new, and it is uncomfortable.** On a managed deployment the batching policy is not yours. You did not choose it, you cannot inspect it, and you cannot change it. What you can choose is **admission** — who is allowed to send, how fast, with what budget, and what happens when they exceed it — and on this platform that choice is made in one place: a gateway policy, in a file, that the students will write today. The token limit policy is enforced against a **counter key you choose**, and *the counter key is the answer to who waits.* It is a distributive decision compiled into configuration.

The target: **a latency promise is a claim about a quantile, at a stated load, under a stated arrival pattern — and a promise about latency is simultaneously a commitment about throughput and about cost, whether or not the person making it knows that.**

## Phase 1 · Pretest and prediction (25 min)

Five items on the substrate's serving path, **sealed and opened at phase 8.**

1. The path at 50 per cent utilisation has a ninety-ninth-percentile latency of L. **Predict it at 90 per cent.**
2. You introduce batching with a bounded wait window. **Predict the effect on median latency and on ninety-ninth-percentile latency, separately** — and state whether your answer depends on the current load.
3. **Which class of request will an admission policy make wait longest, and who sends it?** One sentence.
4. Predict your item 1 answer's accuracy: within a factor of two, five, or worse.
5. **Copilot prediction, sealed.** You will shortly ask the agent, through the Azure MCP Server, to *"add failover so that when the primary deployment is busy, traffic goes to the second one."* Write down the configuration it will produce, and **name the one thing that will be wrong with it.** Sealed with items 1 to 4 and opened at phase 8.

Item 1 is a deliberate re-probe of the [S019](/wiki/seminars/S019.md) items 1 and 2 extrapolation at a five-quarter gap, and each student's answer is compared against their own from that day. Item 2 has no single answer: at low load a wait window strictly worsens both, and at high load batching improves both, and almost nobody predicts that the sign flips. Item 5's answer is at phase 3 and the design expects very few to reach it.

**Warrant.** Pretesting with a long-range re-probe of a named cohort baseline, per [WP-019](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-019.md) §9. **Item 3 is sealed here specifically so that the day's who-waits finding is measured against a prediction made before instruction** — the placement [S061](/wiki/seminars/S061.md) used for its binding-resource instrument, carried across. [WP-062](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-062.md) §8 argues the carry is partial and says what remains uncovered. Item 5 is the day's frontier-calibration datum and is administered here, per instrumentation rule 8.

## Phase 3 · Contrasting cases and generation (45 min)

**Executed, not read.** Groups have **one recorded traffic trace from the substrate and a gateway they can configure**, replayed under four admission and routing policies against the same deployments. Every group is a distinct consumer of the gateway with its own key, so the contention is the room's own.

| Case | Gateway configuration |
|---|---|
| **G1** | No per-consumer limit. One shared budget on the deployment, first come first served. |
| **G2** | A per-minute token limit keyed on the consumer, with prompt-token estimation **off**. |
| **G3** | As G2, with prompt-token estimation **on**, plus a token quota over a fixed period. |
| **G4** | A priority-based backend pool over two deployments with a circuit breaker rule on the primary, keyed on the consumer. |

Groups predict, for each: throughput, median latency, ninety-ninth-percentile latency, **and — this is the new column — who is refused, and with which status code.** They commit.

Then **aggregate measurements arrive.** Then, separately, **the per-consumer breakdowns.** The two-part reveal is [S053](/wiki/seminars/S053.md)'s construction, reused deliberately and named to the room as such after the second reveal.

The payload is **G4**, and it defeats expectation twice.

Every group configures it expecting overflow: the primary is busy, so traffic goes to the secondary. **It does not.** The documentation states that lower-priority backends are used only when *all* backends in higher-priority groups are unavailable **because circuit breaker rules are tripped** — and the portal's own worked example trips on server errors in the five-hundred range. A saturated model deployment does not return a server error. It returns a rate-limit response, which trips nothing, and **not one request moves.** The paid-for standby serves nothing while users are refused.

Then the groups fix it by adding the rate-limit code to the failure range and accepting the backend's own retry interval, which is what the documentation recommends for this backend. **And the second surprise arrives**, because the same documentation carries a caution that this backend's retry interval can be large — its illustration is a day. A rule that trips on a momentary throttle and then honours a retry interval of that size has removed the primary deployment for the rest of the week, and the primary is the one that was paid for.

Tasks after: **for each policy, name the class of requests that waits and say who sends it.** Then: **G1 refuses nobody and is still a distributive decision. Say what it distributes and to whom.** Then, third: **find the sentence that predicted G4, and record how long it took you to find it.** Both behaviours are documented — one on the routing page, one on the same page as a caution — and the group that has just been surprised twice goes and finds the two sentences with the Learn MCP Server. **The time is recorded and reported at phase 8**, because the day's real claim about documentation is not that it should be read but that it is *findable*, and a cohort that takes twenty minutes to locate a sentence that was always there has learned something about its own habits.

**Warrant.** Contrasting cases across four configurations with the trace, deployments and hardware held fixed, executed against a real gateway rather than replayed from supplied data. The staged reveal is the same device S053 used and the recognition is intended: the cohort met aggregate-hides-class with input taxonomies five weeks ago and meets it here with a population of users, which [WP-053](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-053.md) §6 identified as the transfer most likely to fail. **G4 is the payload because its real behaviour defeats the room's expectation and then defeats the room's repair**, and because both behaviours are documented rather than incidental — a group that reads the two pages before configuring will predict both, which is the reading discipline the day is trying to install.

## Phase 4 · Consolidating instruction (50 min)

1. **Batching converts latency into throughput.** A request that waits to be grouped took longer so that others could be served. The conversion rate is measurable and the trade is the whole subject.

2. **The sign of the effect depends on the load.** At low utilisation a wait window strictly worsens latency and buys nothing anyone needs. At high utilisation batching raises capacity, which moves the operating point back down the [S019](/wiki/seminars/S019.md) curve, and median and tail both improve. **Item 2 has two answers and the load selects between them.**

3. **The two phases batch differently, and you do not choose the batching.** [S061](/wiki/seminars/S061.md) point 1: input processing is already parallel across positions and gains comparatively little from grouping; output generation is bandwidth-bound and gains a great deal, because one movement of the weights serves every request in the group. **That asymmetry is why continuous-admission families exist.** On a managed deployment the family in use is the provider's decision, is not documented in the request, and is not yours — **so on this platform the whole of the batching literature is background and the whole of your control surface is admission.** Which family the provider currently runs is perishable and is marked so.

4. **A latency promise names three things:** a quantile, a load, and an arrival pattern. [S034](/wiki/seminars/S034.md)'s three-part contract with latency as the observable. A target stated without a load is a wish, and it will be read as a promise by whoever receives it.

5. **Every scheduling policy chooses who waits, and on this platform the choice has a field name.** Sorting by predicted work is a distributive decision. So is choosing a **counter key**. A limit keyed on the subscription treats a team as the unit; keyed on the caller's address it treats a machine as the unit; keyed on an arbitrary expression it treats whatever you wrote as the unit. **The people on the wrong side of that key are who waits, and nothing in the aggregate names them** — and the population it disadvantages is systematically the one least able to complain, because a user waiting for a long answer does not know what a short answer would have cost. This is [S053](/wiki/seminars/S053.md) point 6 with people as the class, and today it is one attribute in one file.

6. **Backpressure is the honest response to saturation; retry is the dishonest one.** [S033](/wiki/seminars/S033.md) point 3 named four consequences of retry at a single call site and stated that its list was unsafe as a basis for system-wide policy. **The system-wide part is here.** A retry is an arrival. Retries correlate with saturation, so retry is positive feedback on a queue that is already the problem. Client-side queueing compounds it by hiding the offered load from the server, so the load the server measures is not the load it faces. The remedies are admission control, load shedding with a signal the client can act on, and bounded retry with jitter — and the durable claim beneath all three is that **the server must be able to refuse, and refusing must be cheaper than the work.**

   **The platform supplies all three primitives and gives them distinguishable names.** A rate that is exceeded returns a rate-limit refusal; a budget over a period that is exhausted returns an authorisation refusal; a tripped circuit breaker returns a service-unavailable refusal from the gateway itself, without reaching the backend at all. **Three refusals, three meanings, three correct client behaviours** — and a client that treats them identically will retry into a wall on two of the three. Estimating the prompt's size at the gateway and refusing before the request is forwarded is the same idea taken one step further: refusing before you pay to be refused.

7. **Throughput is priced and latency is promised, and they are the same operating point.** Moving it changes both. This is [S019](/wiki/seminars/S019.md) point 8 with money attached, and it is the commitment [S064](/wiki/seminars/S064.md) will require you to sign.

8. **What the gateway actually guarantees, and what it only approximately does.** This is the day's perishable platform-specific content and every clause of it carries a date; all were verified for this offering on **2026-07-26**.

   - **Limits are approximate and the documentation says so.** Rate limiting is not exact because the enforcement is distributed; backend load balancing is approximate because gateway instances do not synchronise; circuit-breaker tripping is approximate for the same reason. **By how much is not published.** An engineer who quotes a limit as a guarantee has quoted something the vendor declined to guarantee.
   - **Concurrent requests can temporarily exceed the configured limit**, because the true token count is not known until the response returns.
   - **The remaining-budget figure the gateway hands back is an estimate** and may be larger than the truth.
   - **Streaming changes the enforcement silently.** When a response streams, prompt tokens are always estimated regardless of what you configured, and completion tokens are estimated too. **The policy you deployed is not the policy that runs for your streaming callers.**
   - **One circuit breaker rule per backend**, and while it is tripped the gateway returns service-unavailable to the caller.
   - **Scaling the gateway does not scale the backends.** They must be scaled and regionally co-located separately, and a team that adds gateway capacity to fix a backend problem has bought nothing.

9. **Defaults are opinions, and today they are attributes.** Four were chosen for you. *The failure range.* The worked example that most teams copy trips a circuit breaker on server errors, and a saturated model deployment does not produce one — phase 3's G4. *The retry interval.* Honouring the backend's stated retry interval is the documented recommendation and the documented caution is that this backend's interval can be large; accepting it is a decision about how long you are willing to lose your primary. *The estimation setting.* Whether prompt tokens are counted before or after the backend is a required attribute with no default, which means somebody chose it and the person reading the policy later will not know why. *And the cache partition.* If you add semantic caching and do not set the partitioning expression, **one user's answer is available to another** — the documentation's own remedy for cross-user access is an element you have to write. **An engineer who cannot state what was chosen for them does not understand their own system.**

10. **Scope, in the strong form.** The three-part latency promise and the who-waits analysis are **safe** for a single service under its own admission control, where the arrival process is user-driven and the service can refuse. They are **unsafe** in three conditions this day does not handle. Across a chain of services the promises do not compose — the end-to-end tail is dominated by the worst hop and by the correlation between hops, and per-service promises added together are not an end-to-end one. Where the arrival process is adversarial rather than user-driven, the whole analysis is a description of what an attacker will exploit rather than a plan; that case is [S077](/wiki/seminars/S077.md)'s. And **the analysis is unsafe wherever the limit you are reasoning with is one the vendor has described as approximate**, which on this platform is all of them: a plan whose safety margin is smaller than the unquantified error in the enforcement is a plan with no margin. **A student who carries this day's promise arithmetic into a multi-service path will over-promise, and the error grows with the number of hops.**

**Warrant.** Consolidation indexed to phase 3's four configurations. Point 2 is the mechanical content and the item 2 resolution. Point 5 is the retained content and the reason the day exists, and it is now reachable from the artefact the students configured rather than asserted, per instrumentation rule 7. **Point 6 is this day's discharge of the deferral [WP-033](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-033.md) §3.2 placed on it**, stated as a feedback argument rather than as a list of tactics, with the platform's three named refusals attached as the instance. **Point 8 is the day's perishable platform-specific point and point 9 is the day's defaults point**, both required by the [Copilot Practice Standard](/wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md). Point 10 is the scope statement in strong form and is the one most likely to be violated in a student's first job.

## Phase 5 · Whole-task learning task (90 min)

**Class 5. The promise, the policy, the refusal and the breaking load are the student's. This phase produces a build, and the build is in front of real traffic by the end of the day.**

Students work with Copilot in agent mode through the Azure MCP Server and **deploy a gateway policy** in front of the substrate's serving path. It is a file, it is reviewed, it is applied, and the room's traffic runs through it.

1. **State the latency promise your service currently makes** — quantile, load, arrival pattern — or state plainly that it makes none.
2. **Measure whether it currently holds,** per request class.
3. **Choose an admission and routing policy and deploy it.** State the operating point it places you at on the measured curve, and state **the counter key you chose and what it makes the unit of fairness.**
4. **State the throughput and cost consequence of the promise,** using your [S061](/wiki/seminars/S061.md) model. The promise comes first and its cost second, in that order.
5. **Name the class of requests your policy makes wait, and who sends it,** with the measured distributional evidence rather than an inference from the policy's design. **Then construct one further class after measurement, from the residuals:** group the requests that waited longest, characterise what they share, and report that class beside the ones you specified in advance.
6. **State the refusal, and implement it.** What the service does when offered load exceeds the policy's capacity: what it sheds, **which of the three refusals it returns and why that one**, what it signals, and what a well-behaved client does with that signal. The client behaviour is written down, not assumed.
7. **State the load at which your promise breaks and the leading signal for approaching it,** and commit both.

**The artefact carries three further elements**, per the [Copilot Practice Standard](/wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md).

- **Prompt record.** The instructions you gave the agent, in order, including the ones you abandoned.
- **Intervention record.** Every point at which you corrected it, and what the correction was. **An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that the design could have detected an intervention-worthy error — a test that drives the primary to refuse and asserts that traffic moved is the obvious example and is accepted, because it is exactly the check phase 3's payload defeats.
- **Frontier statement.** One thing the agent did faster than you could have, and one thing it did badly that you had to catch.

**Fading:** step 2's measurement harness is supplied and step 4's arithmetic is worked with the room. **Step 6 is unsupported.**

**Warrant.** Whole-task at class 5, now deploying rather than describing. Step 3's counter-key clause is what makes the day's distributive claim an artefact element rather than an observation. Step 4's ordering is the module's standing countermeasure against cost being reasoned forward and never backward: the student states a promise and is then required to derive what it costs, which is the direction they never take unprompted. Step 5 requires distributional evidence rather than reasoning from the policy, because a policy's intended distribution and its measured one differ and the difference is the finding. **Step 5's residual-derived class is the construction [WP-053](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-053.md) §8 identified and declined to build; under instrumentation rule 5 it is imported here rather than rediscovered**, because the failure it corrects — a catastrophe in a class nobody enumerated — recurs on this day in the same shape with users in place of input types. Step 6 is unsupported because nothing in phase 4 tells a student what *their* service should shed, and that judgement is the day's class-5 element.

## Phase 6 · Error clinic (40 min)

Instructor first: a substrate scheduling change that improved every number on every dashboard and produced a sustained complaint from one group of users. The complaint could not be reproduced, because the reproduction used a median request and the affected requests were not median. Attention is directed at two facts held together — **the dashboards were correct, and the change was an improvement by every measure anyone had agreed to watch.**

Then the agent case, which is phase 3's payload with a reviewer attached. A pull request generated by Copilot in agent mode from the instruction *"add failover to the second deployment."* It created a backend pool, put the primary in the higher priority group, attached a circuit breaker with a sensible failure count and a sensible interval, and set the failure range to server errors — which is what the vendor's own worked example does. **It was reviewed by two people, approved, merged, and it never moved a single request**, because the primary's failure mode is a rate-limit refusal and not a server error. The standby cost money for a month and served nothing.

Attention goes to why review did not catch it: **the configuration was correct against the documented example, and the example is not about this backend.** Both reviewers checked it against the pattern they knew, and the pattern is right for a backend that fails by breaking rather than by refusing.

Then students, in threes: a time you made something faster for most people, or a time you approved a configuration because it matched an example.

**Warrant.** Error management targeting points 5, 6 and 9. The instructor case contains no measurement error, no implementation error and no negligence, which is what makes it a lesson about what the measurement was of rather than about care. The agent case is the [Copilot Practice Standard](/wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md)'s required addition and is chosen because **it survives every review the cohort currently knows how to perform** — it matches the documentation, it passes lint, it deploys, and its failure is silent and expensive. It is also the case the room has already produced at phase 3, which means the clinic examines their own morning rather than a story.

## Phase 7 · Peer articulation (30 min)

Pairs. Each student presents **their step 1 promise and their step 5 waiting class.**

The partner takes the role of **a person who sends requests in the waiting class**, and has one question: *what am I getting in exchange?*

The student must answer with the measured distribution, not with a principle. "Overall latency improved" is not an answer to that question and the partner is instructed to say so.

Per rule 3, the outcome is recorded in the artefact. The role construction is [S019](/wiki/seminars/S019.md) phase 7's, with the counterparty changed from the person who pays to the person who waits; per rule 5 as amended at [WP-054](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-054.md) §8, **the reuse is recorded as contingent on S019's reported outcome.**

**Warrant.** Interactive-tier articulation with a role demand and an adversarial interest. The cohort defended an operating point to a payer at S019 and defends the same class of decision to its victim here, which is the pairing that makes point 5 a professional obligation rather than an observation.

## Phase 8 · Calibration and commitment (20 min)

- Sealed items opened. **Item 1's prediction against measurement, and against the same student's S019 answer five quarters ago.** The two errors are shown together; the question is whether the cohort's non-linear extrapolation improved or whether they merely learned the answer to one question.
- Item 2's median-and-tail predictions against G2's measured effect **at both loads**, with the sign flip stated plainly.
- **Item 3's sealed who-waits prediction against step 5's measured finding**, with the cohort agreement rate reported. This is the day's honest number.
- **Item 5 opened.** The sealed predictions of what the agent would configure are read against what it actually produced, and the cohort's hit rate is recorded. The design's specific interest is how many students named the failure-range problem rather than *some* problem, and whether anyone anticipated the retry-interval consequence of the repair.
- The room's phase 3 aggregate rankings against the per-consumer breakdown, with G4's position marked.
- Each student names **one promise they have made that did not name a load.**
- Retrieval schedule: next morning, day 2, week 1, week 3.

## Assessment

**One specification item**, in IS301 with SY301. Per [Assessment Architecture](/wiki/program/03-Assessment-Architecture.md) rule 1, the checked elements are: the current latency promise or its explicit absence; the per-class measurement of whether it holds; the deployed policy, its operating point and **the counter key with what it makes the unit of fairness**; the throughput and cost consequence derived from the S061 model; the waiting class with distributional evidence and its senders named; **the residual-derived class constructed after measurement**; the refusal, its shed criterion, **which of the three refusals it returns and why**, and its client signal; the committed breaking load and leading signal; the phase 7 outcome; **the prompt record; the intervention record; and the frontier statement.** **Nothing else is assessed.**

**A latency target stated without a load and an arrival pattern fails.** This is the day's single hard requirement and it is point 4 made operative.

**An artefact stating a promise without its throughput and cost consequence fails.** This is the module-level countermeasure and it applies at every day in M13.

**A policy that treats all three refusals as one condition fails.** A client instructed to retry on any refusal will retry into an exhausted period budget and into a tripped breaker, and point 6 is what the requirement enforces.

**An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that the design could have detected an intervention-worthy error.

**No gateway policy name, attribute or status code is assessed.** All are perishable under the [Durable and Perishable Register](/wiki/program/09-Durable-and-Perishable-Register.md) and appear only as instances; what is assessed is that the student named a unit of fairness, chose a refusal, and stated what the client does with it.

No entrustment judgement. The module's is at [S065](/wiki/seminars/S065.md).

**Discharge.** [WP-033](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-033.md) §3.2 deferred retry policy design at system scale to this day, and [S033](/wiki/seminars/S033.md) point 3 named retry's interaction with backpressure and with client-side queueing as omissions this day covers. **Phase 4 point 6 discharges both**, as a positive-feedback argument with three named remedies and a durable claim beneath them, and step 6 makes the refusal an assessed artefact element rather than a discussed one. The obligation is discharged.

**Instrumentation under rule 6.** Two instruments with named response occasions:

- **The item 2 median-and-tail prediction error**, reported separately for the low-load and high-load conditions, measures whether the cohort holds batching as a single-signed effect. **Response occasion: [S063](/wiki/seminars/S063.md), phase 5 step 6, where the student must report the tail effect of their admission policy per request class — a required artefact element, constructed so that the load condition the cohort got wrong is the one their own policy is measured under.**
- **The step 5 waiting-class statement, and its agreement with the sealed item 3 prediction**, measures whether students can anticipate the distributive consequence of a policy before choosing one. **Response occasion: [S065](/wiki/seminars/S065.md), phase 3, where the cost argument must name who bears the latency cost of the proposed operating point — a required element of the entrustment artefact and one of its named failure conditions.**

## Perishable content in this day

**Batching and scheduling strategies in production inference are semi-durable** per the [Durable and Perishable Register](/wiki/program/09-Durable-and-Perishable-Register.md) and will change within one cohort's passage; **gateway policy names, attributes and defaults are perishable outright.** The day's durable content is written as measurement obligations and as a trade, not as a recommended policy.

| Durable claim | Platform instance, perishable |
|---|---|
| Batching converts latency into throughput | A managed deployment's grouping policy, which you neither choose nor see |
| Every policy chooses who waits | The counter key on the token limit policy |
| The server must be able to refuse, cheaply | Three named refusals: rate, period budget, tripped breaker |
| Refusing before you pay to be refused | Prompt-token estimation at the gateway edge |
| A failover that never fires is not a failover | Priority pools that move traffic only when a breaker trips, on a range that excludes the actual failure |
| A resilience mechanism can be worse than the failure | Honouring a backend retry interval the vendor says can be very large |
| An enforced limit is not the same as a guaranteed one | Distributed enforcement described as approximate, by an unpublished amount |
| Configuration you did not write is still your decision | An unset cache partition that exposes one caller's answer to another |

- **Durable:** the latency-for-throughput conversion; the load-dependent sign of the batching effect; the three-part latency promise; every policy chooses who waits; backpressure against retry as positive feedback; the operating point as simultaneously a price and a promise; the non-composition of per-service promises across a chain; and the requirement to distinguish refusals that mean different things.
- **Perishable:** the four configurations as instantiated; every measured throughput and latency figure; every policy name, attribute name and status code; the continuous-admission family's current membership; the position of the substrate's knee; and every documented default. **No policy or attribute named in this day may appear in an assessment item.** All were verified for this offering on **2026-07-26**.
- **Local:** the substrate's traffic trace, its request classes and their senders, the identity of the waiting population, the phase 6 scheduling change, and the phase 6 pull request.

**Operated in full.** Everything in this day is run by the students against a real gateway and real deployments. **Nothing on this day is demonstrated rather than operated**, which makes S062 the module's most complete practical day and is the reason the gateway rather than provisioned capacity carries the module's operational weight.
