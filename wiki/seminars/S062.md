## The situation

You have an unmitigated list from [S055](/wiki/seminars/S055.md) and work items from [S060](/wiki/seminars/S060.md). Somebody is about to tell you that several of them are done.

They will be telling the truth as they understand it. **That is not the problem.** The problem is that the sentence *we fixed that* is an assertion about a system, made by a person, and there is nothing in it a second reader can check. In six months, when the person has moved on and the item is closed, nobody will be able to establish whether the fix exists.

The best idea available to this programme replaces the assertion. **A mitigation's status is re-derived by searching the workspace, and a claim of implemented must cite a file and a location.** Closure is evidence in the repository, not somebody's word.

It is a genuinely good idea and it has three failure modes, and this session teaches all four things at once because a learner who takes the mechanism without the caveats will trust it in exactly the cases where it is silent.

**Answer these in writing before reading further.** Item 3 is the session's centre of gravity, and a learner who cannot name one has not yet understood what the mechanism does not see.

1. What would you accept as evidence that a mitigation is implemented?
2. Who currently decides that a security work item is closed, and what do they look at when they decide?
3. Name one real fix in your system that would leave no trace in your repository.
4. Who wrote your acceptance criteria, and who wrote the mitigation they check?
5. What schedules the re-check, and what happens if nobody runs it?

Most people answer 1 with something reasonable and 2 with a person's name. **Item 3 is where the design of the whole procedure is decided**, because a closure mechanism is defined as much by what it cannot see as by what it can, and a mechanism whose blind spot is undeclared reads as complete.

## What you must produce

**A closure procedure, and the register of what it cannot close.**

1. **The procedure.** For each class of mitigation: the search that would find its evidence; what a citation must contain; and the default disposition when the search is ambiguous.
2. **The behavioural half.** For any mitigation whose implementation is a change in what the system *does* rather than in what it contains, the evaluation that closes it — run over stored responses rather than over a fresh demonstration — with its unit of analysis, its threshold and the justification for that threshold.
3. **The schedule.** What triggers a re-derivation, who owns it, and what happens when it does not run.
4. **The invisible-fix register.** Real fixes your procedure cannot detect, each with what it is, why the procedure misses it, and how its status is established instead.
5. **The authorship record.** Who wrote each acceptance criterion and who wrote the mitigation it checks, with shared authorship marked.
6. **A precision line.** For every closure that rests on a measurement, what the measurement could and could not have detected.

## The material

### Closure is a claim about evidence, and evidence has two shapes

The first shape is textual and it is the one the procedure handles well. **A claim of implemented cites a file and a location.** A search runs over source, pipeline definitions, container definitions, infrastructure definitions, configuration and documentation, and either finds the thing or does not. That is enumeration and citation, which is exactly the class of work an agent is good at and a person is bad at, and it is why the derivation is delegated.

The second shape is behavioural, and it defeats the first entirely. A mitigation that changes what the system *does* — refuses a class of request, stops surfacing a document to the wrong reader, declines to call a tool it should not — leaves a trace in the repository only if somebody happened to implement it as a visible artefact. **The behaviour is the mitigation; the code is one possible cause of it.** So the procedure needs a second instrument, and the instrument has to look at behaviour that actually occurred rather than behaviour you thought to provoke.

### Stored responses, and why re-running is the wrong move

The evaluation framework supplies the mechanism precisely. Pre-existing responses can be evaluated **"from logs or previous runs… without re-running the agent"** ([research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-framework-and-evaluations-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-framework-and-evaluations-docs.md)). That single capability is what separates production evaluation from synthetic testing, and the reason is worth stating flatly: **synthetic queries only test what you thought to ask, and a stored trace contains what actually occurred.**

For closure the distinction is decisive rather than convenient. If you re-run the agent to demonstrate that a fix works, you have demonstrated something about a run that happened after the fix, under today's model version, with today's prompt, on a query you chose. **That is a demonstration, and a demonstration is the thing this session exists to replace.** Evaluating stored responses answers a different and better question: *given what the system actually did, does the class of failure the mitigation targets still appear?*

Three further details from the same documentation are load-bearing.

**The split strategy silently determines what a score means.** Multi-turn conversations must be divided into query and response halves, and the documentation says so in its own voice: **"How you split determines *what you're evaluating*."** Splitting at the last turn measures response quality at a point; treating the first message as the query and everything after as the response measures task completion and trajectory; scoring each exchange independently measures something else again. **An agent that recovers well from a bad first attempt scores well under one and badly under another, and neither score is wrong.** A team that never chose deliberately is reporting an answer to a question it did not ask.

**Repetitions exist "to detect non-deterministic behavior"** — which is the documentation's own acknowledgement that one sample from a stochastic system is not a measurement. A mitigation that appeared to hold on a single stored run has not been shown to hold.

**Tool failure decomposes into selection, input accuracy, call success and output utilisation.** Two of those — the right tool with wrong arguments, and a successful result ignored — **produce a system that reports success and is wrong**, which is precisely the failure class a closure procedure must not inherit.

**The silence in this source is the point rather than a footnote.** It is definitional and accurate about what the tooling computes, and it publishes **no agreement rate with human judgement for any evaluator, no calibration data, and no rationale for any default threshold.** Most of these evaluators are model-judged. **Assert no agreement figure for any of them.** The pages were read through search excerpts rather than full fetches; most agent evaluators are marked preview; one consulted page is an explicitly superseded variant.

### A threshold is a policy, and a judge is a system

The documentation's output format carries a decision that most teams inherit without noticing. Evaluators return pass or fail with a `reason`, and for the ones that score before thresholding, a numeric score sits beside the verdict. **The threshold is therefore separable from the measurement, and moving it changes the pass rate without changing the system.** A threshold you did not choose is a policy somebody else set for you.

The engineering-practice literature sharpens this into a rule worth memorising. **"An AI judge is not just a model — it's a system that includes both a model and a prompt. Altering the model, the prompt, or the model's sampling parameters results in a different judge"**, and the boxed instruction that follows is blunt: **"Do not trust any AI judge if you can't see the model and the prompt used for the judge"** ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/huyen-ai-engineering.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/huyen-ai-engineering.md)). The same source demonstrates that scores do not travel between judges by showing three widely used frameworks shipping a criterion of the same name on three incompatible scales, and asks the obvious question about which one you would use.

It also supplies the requirement this session builds its schedule around: **the evaluation pipeline must be more stable than the thing it evaluates.** "An application evolves over time, but the way it's evaluated ideally should be fixed. This way, evaluation metrics can be used to monitor the application's changes." The failure mode is stated concretely — a score moving between months is uninterpretable unless you know the judge was identical, and where the application and the judge are owned by different teams, a change in the judge is attributed to the application. **A closure procedure whose instrument moves cannot close anything.** And the guideline itself is named as the most important step of the whole pipeline, with the observation that most teams find the hardest part is not the tooling: **"A correct response is not always a good response."**

**Its limits:** a commercial practitioner book in its first edition; the adoption and correlation figures it reports are almost all reported second-hand from other people's work and are benchmark-conditional; **none is reproduced here.**

### An evaluation without uncertainty licenses conclusions it cannot support

This is the part of the session that turns a closure claim into an honest one, and it is where most teams stop reading. **An eval is an experiment.** The statistical treatment of language-model evaluation makes the case in a form directly applicable to closure: report the standard error beside the mean, report the number of questions, report the number of clusters where questions come in related groups, and compare two systems on **paired question-level differences** rather than on population summaries ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md)).

The consequence for a closure procedure is sharp and it runs the opposite way to intuition. **"We ran the evaluation after the fix and the failure did not appear" is not evidence that the failure is gone.** It is a statement about what your evaluation was capable of detecting, and that capability is computable in advance. The same source supplies **power analysis** and the **Minimum Detectable Effect** — the smallest true difference an evaluation of a given size can reliably detect — and its practical instruction is to compute it *before* running, so that you know whether the evaluation could possibly answer the question you care about.

Two further cautions in that source bear directly on closure. **Clustering**: where evaluation items are drawn in related groups, treating them as independent overstates precision, sometimes badly. And **the prohibition on lowering the sampling temperature to make scores look stable**, which can move variance into the irreducible component or bias the estimate outright.

**Its limits:** an analytical paper, not an empirical one; most of its illustrative magnitudes rest on stylised assumptions and **none is reproduced here**; and it addresses comparing scores rather than choosing what to score — it will not help with a badly chosen criterion, only with an honestly reported one.

### Why the register is the honest half of the procedure

A mitigation that is real and unciteable does not fail the procedure. **It never closes**, and the work item regenerates on every run until somebody starts skipping it — which is worse than an unclosed item, because the skipping is undocumented and spreads.

The internal practice corpus supplies the structural diagnosis this session borrows, from a different domain and applying exactly. In its business-value framework **the cost side is engineered and the return side is elicited**: consumption is forecast by engineers and refined against telemetry from their own system, while the return is chosen by the customer, measured with instrumentation the customer may not possess, over a period nobody specifies, with causation assumed. The corpus poses the confounding question correctly and answers it nowhere, and **no party is assigned responsibility for establishing that an observed change was caused by the system.** The note's own summary is the sentence to carry into a closure procedure: a ratio whose denominator is well instrumented and whose numerator is not is not a well-instrumented ratio, but it will feel like one.

**That is the invisible-fix register's whole justification.** A procedure with a well-instrumented half and an uninstrumented half, and no declared boundary between them, reads as fully instrumented. **Its limits:** the corpus is access-controlled, paraphrased and never quoted, reports no outcome data of any kind, and no figure from it appears anywhere in this repository.

## The workflow

**Close one mitigation on evidence and one on stored responses, then write down what neither could see.**

**Step 1 — write down what would count, before you look at anything.** For each class of mitigation, say what a search would have to find, or what a stored-response evaluation would have to show. **A standard written after seeing the results is a description of the results.**

**Step 2 — run the textual derivation.** Search source, pipeline definitions, container definitions, infrastructure definitions in whatever dialects your estate uses, configuration and documentation. Record what you found and where. Use an agent for this; the work is enumeration and citation rather than judgement, and that is the division of labour this programme has used since [S001](/wiki/seminars/S001.md).

**Step 3 — be ruthless about the ambiguous cases.** Anything you cannot resolve is **unclear**, and unclear is a status rather than a failure to reach one. **Claiming something is implemented when it is not is strictly worse than failing to notice that it is**, and your stated default is the only place that judgement is actually encoded.

**Step 4 — close the behavioural half on stored responses, not on a fresh run.** Take the traces you already have and evaluate them for the failure class the mitigation targets. Choose the split strategy deliberately and write down which one and why, because it determines what the number means. Run repetitions where the source is stochastic. Then compute what the evaluation could have detected before you interpret what it did detect.

```yaml
# closure/behavioural.yaml   <- SHAPE, NOT SCHEMA. Verify any product keys separately.
mitigation: "MIT-014 — agent must not call the export tool on records outside the caller's scope"

closure_basis: stored_responses      # NOT a fresh demonstration run
source_window: "traces captured 2026-06-01 to 2026-07-15, production, unsampled"

unit_of_analysis:
  split_strategy: full               # first user message = query; whole remainder = response
  why: "the mitigation is about the trajectory, not about one turn's wording"
  note: "under a last-turn split this same trace set scores differently, and neither score is wrong"

evaluators:
  - name: tool_selection             # wrong tool chosen
  - name: tool_input_accuracy        # right tool, wrong arguments  <- SILENT FAILURE CLASS
  - name: tool_output_utilization    # good result ignored          <- SILENT FAILURE CLASS

judge:
  model: "<model + version>"         # a judge is a model AND a prompt; both are pinned
  prompt_ref: "eval/prompts/tool-scope.v3.md"
  agreement_with_humans: "NOT PUBLISHED BY THE VENDOR, AND NOT ESTABLISHED BY US"

threshold:
  value: "<chosen cut point>"
  justification: "<why this one>"    # inherited defaults do not survive the oral question
  note: "moving this changes the pass rate without changing the system"

precision:
  repetitions: 3                     # one sample from a stochastic system is not a measurement
  items: "<count of evaluated traces>"
  clustered: true                    # traces drawn per conversation, not independently
  minimum_detectable_effect: "<computed BEFORE the run>"
  reading: >
    THE FAILURE DID NOT APPEAR. That is a statement about what this evaluation
    could have detected, not a statement that the failure is gone.

verdict: implemented | unclear | not_implemented
```

**Step 5 — write the invisible-fix register.** Walk the system and find the controls that are real and unciteable: a setting made in a portal, a policy applied at the tenant, a control owned by a platform team you do not sit in, a contractual obligation, a manual process somebody performs. **For each one, say how its status will be established instead** — because "we know about it" is what the register exists to replace.

**Step 6 — schedule the re-run and give it an owner.** A trigger that is a state change beats a trigger that is a person's intention, for exactly the reason [S074](/wiki/seminars/S074.md) will argue about withdrawal. Pin the judge — model, prompt, sampling parameters — alongside it, because a pipeline that moves cannot monitor anything.

**Step 7 — record the authorship.** Where the same author wrote a mitigation and the criterion that checks it, mark it. You are not required to fix it. **You are required to stop it being invisible.**

**Step 8 — use the agent to attack your own closures, not to produce them.** One prompt: *for each mitigation I marked implemented, argue that the citation is a keyword match rather than behaviour, and name the observation that would show it.* Keep what is better than your own reasoning, record what you rejected, and record separately that this instrument may share a model family with the judge you used — in which case it is not independent of it. **An agent that endorsed every closure was not used as an instrument.**

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That the same stored traces produce different verdicts under different split strategies, with nothing about the system changing between them — which is the fastest available demonstration that **a number without its unit of analysis is not a result.** You will also observe how much of your estate is closable only by assertion, and that quantity is almost always larger than the team believes.

## What good looks like

- **A claim of implemented cites a file and a location.** Not a person, not a date, not a ticket reference. **Evidence is a file path and a line, never an assertion that something is secure.**
- **A behavioural closure names its unit of analysis.** The split strategy is stated, and the statement says what the choice means — because the same traces score differently under a different one.
- **A behavioural closure was run over stored responses, not over a fresh demonstration.** A run staged after the fix, on a query you chose, is a demonstration.
- **The ambiguous case defaults to unclear, and the procedure says so in its own words.** A false claim of implementation is strictly worse than a false negative, and the default is where that judgement lives.
- **Every threshold carries a justification rather than a provenance.** "It is the default" is a provenance.
- **Every judge is pinned by model and prompt**, and the record states that no agreement rate with human judgement is published for it and none has been established locally.
- **The precision line says what the evaluation could not have detected.** An absence of failures is reported as a property of the instrument, never as a property of the system.
- **The invisible-fix register is not empty.** A procedure whose author cannot name its blind spot has not been read by its author.
- **The re-derivation has a trigger and a named owner**, and the trigger is a state change rather than an intention. This is the shape [S074](/wiki/seminars/S074.md) will need for withdrawal and the shape [S058](/wiki/seminars/S058.md) required for review.
- **Shared authorship between a mitigation and its acceptance criterion is marked** wherever it occurs, rather than being treated as an ordinary condition.
- **A citation that resolves to a comment, a variable name, a disabled block or an unreferenced file is treated as a failed search**, not as evidence.

## How it goes wrong

- **The assertion** — closure happens because somebody competent said so. *Warning sign:* the closing evidence is a meeting.
- **The demonstration** — the fix is shown working on a query you chose, after the change, today. *Warning sign:* your evidence is a run whose input you selected.
- **The absent failure read as a fixed failure** — the evaluation found nothing and the item was closed. *Warning sign:* your closure record contains no statement of what the evaluation could have detected.
- **The unchosen split** — a score reported without its unit of analysis, so nobody can say what was measured. *Warning sign:* you cannot state which split strategy produced the number.
- **The inherited threshold** — a cut point nobody chose, doing the work of a policy nobody wrote. *Warning sign:* your justification is the word "default".
- **The moving judge** — the model or the prompt changed between runs, so the difference is attributed to the system. *Warning sign:* your closure record pins the application version and not the judge.
- **The perpetual work item** — a real fix that leaves no trace, so the item regenerates on every run, forever. *Warning sign:* the same item has reappeared several times and somebody has started skipping it.
- **The self-marking criterion** — the acceptance criterion and the mitigation share an author, so they agree about whatever they both misunderstand. *Warning sign:* the criterion restates the mitigation in the imperative.
- **The unscheduled derivation** — a well-designed procedure nobody runs. *Warning sign:* the last run's date is also the only run's date.
- **The keyword hit** — a search matched text rather than behaviour. *Warning sign:* your evidence is a line in a file nothing imports.
- **Naming as evidence** — a technology named in a document counted as deployed. *Warning sign:* your citation is to a design document. **This is [S054](/wiki/seminars/S054.md) arriving in production**, and it is the failure the file-and-location rule exists to prevent.
- **The register that lists only the convenient gaps** — every entry is something outside your control. *Warning sign:* nothing in the register is your responsibility.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The procedure and the register are defended at [S065](/wiki/seminars/S065.md), where the register must be non-empty and at least one entry must be a fix inside your own control.

**1 · Sort five closure claims by what each licenses.** For each, say what it establishes: *the search found the file and the line* · *the engineer says it is done* · *the evaluation over stored traces found no instance* · *the fresh demonstration worked* · *the ticket is marked closed*.

> **Model answers.** The first establishes that text exists at a location, which is real and narrow — and is not established as behaviour until you check that anything imports it. The second establishes that a person believes it, which is the thing the procedure exists to replace. The third establishes that the failure did not appear **in the traces you had, at the precision your evaluation supported** — and is worthless as a closure claim until the precision line is written. The fourth establishes that a query you chose behaved as you expected today. The fifth establishes that somebody changed a field. **The general rule: a closure claim is a claim about an instrument, and whether it is also a claim about the system depends entirely on what the instrument can see.**

**2 · Score one stored trace set under two split strategies.** Take a set of stored responses, evaluate them under a last-turn split and again under a full split, and reconcile the difference.

> **What you should find.** Two different verdicts from one unchanged system. The documentation states the reason in its own voice — **"How you split determines what you're evaluating"** — and the practical consequence is that an agent which recovers from a poor first attempt looks competent under one and incompetent under the other. **Neither number is wrong and neither is usable without its unit.** Record which split you will use in the procedure and why; a procedure that does not fix this is not reproducible even by its own author.

**3 · Go back to your own item 3, then break it.** Find what you wrote for opening item 3 — a real fix that would leave no trace in your repository. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then write the register entry properly.

> **Criterion.** A register entry passes if it names the fix, names why the procedure cannot see it, and names **how its status will be established instead** — by whom, on what cadence, producing what artefact. It fails if it names only the fix, because that is a complaint. It fails harder if the entry is something outside your control, since a register composed entirely of other people's platforms is an excuse rather than a boundary. **The strongest entry is one you could have made visible and chose not to**, with the reason recorded, because that is the only entry a reader can argue with.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Compute what your evaluation could have detected, before you interpret what it did.** For one behavioural closure, state the number of items, whether they are clustered, and the smallest difference the evaluation could reliably have found.

> **Scoring criterion.** A pass states the quantity and then restates the closure claim in its light — *the failure did not appear, in an evaluation capable of detecting a difference of at least this size*. A fail reports the absence alone. The source this rests on is unambiguous that an evaluation is an experiment and that a null result from an underpowered one **means nothing**, and it supplies the computation. **Note also the clustering warning**: where items are drawn in related groups rather than independently, treating them as independent overstates your precision, and reading comprehension over shared passages is exactly the shape a conversation trace has.

**5 · Pin your judge, then change one thing about it.** Record the model, the prompt and the sampling parameters. Then change the prompt slightly and re-run the same stored traces.

> **What you should find.** A different verdict on at least one item, from an unchanged system. The rule this demonstrates is stated as a boxed instruction in the source: **"Do not trust any AI judge if you can't see the model and the prompt used for the judge."** The operational consequence is the one your schedule has to encode — **the evaluation pipeline must be more stable than the thing it evaluates**, or a change in the judge will be read as a change in the application. Record that no agreement rate with human judgement is published for the built-in evaluators, and that you have not established one either.

**6 · Hand the procedure to somebody who did not write it.** They have one job: find a fix that closes under your procedure and is not actually in place. Give them the citations and let them follow one. Then the harder pass — ask them to find something in your invisible-fix register that could have been made visible.

> **What to notice.** The first pass usually finds a keyword hit. The second pass is the one that stings, because every item it returns is a fix you accepted as invisible when it did not have to be. **The register is a boundary, not an excuse**, and the difference is whether the item could have been expressed as configuration and was not.

## What this does not establish

**Nothing here shows that evidence-derived closure produces more fixed vulnerabilities than a person ticking a box.** No comparison exists. No defect-escape record exists. No measure of how often a re-derived status was correct exists. The mechanism is the best-designed thing in this programme's source material and it is entirely unmeasured for outcome.

**The observations that clearly drove its design are unrecorded.** They are visible only as scar tissue in the instructions themselves — a stage told to re-read a file rather than trust a table already in front of it, a writing strategy designed for interruption. **Nobody writes those prospectively.** The failures happened; the records do not exist; **so the strongest evidence that the design works cannot be cited.**

**And the mechanism's own reviewer has never been calibrated against human judgement.** No gold standard, no held-out sample, no inter-rater comparison, no pilot. A pipeline of this kind can prove it added its own numbers up correctly and can prove nothing else about them.

**The evaluation framework's documentation is definitional and silent exactly where a closure procedure needs it.** It describes accurately what the tooling computes and publishes **no agreement rate with human judgement for any evaluator, no calibration data and no threshold rationale** — so it is no evidence that what it computes is right. Most of its agent evaluators are model-judged and marked preview, it was read through search excerpts rather than full page fetches, and one consulted page is an explicitly superseded variant. **Assert no agreement figure anywhere.**

**The statistical apparatus is analytical rather than empirical, and it addresses the wrong half of the problem to be a rescue.** It tells you whether a reported result means anything; it does not tell you whether the thing you chose to measure was worth measuring, and its own limits section says so. Most of its illustrative magnitudes rest on stylised assumptions and **none is reproduced here.** A tight interval around a badly chosen criterion is still a badly chosen criterion.

**The evaluation-practice guidance is a commercial practitioner book reporting other people's numbers.** The adoption and correlation figures it carries are second-hand and benchmark-conditional, and **none appears here.** Its rules — pin the judge, keep the pipeline stable, write the guideline first — are stated as experience rather than as findings.

**The register, the precision line and the authorship record are this design's inventions.** No source proposes them. The claim that a declared blind spot makes a procedure honest is an argument about professional accountability, not a finding, and nobody has tested whether it changes what engineers close.

The claim this session is entitled to make is narrower than the one it would like to make, and it is close to a definition: **a mitigation whose implementation can be found is one you can argue about, and one whose implementation cannot be found is not a mitigation yet.**
