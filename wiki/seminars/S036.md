# S036 · Deliberate Low Fidelity

**Module:** [M08](/wiki/modules/M08.md) · **Quarter:** [Q4](/wiki/quarters/Q4.md) · **Complexity class:** 3
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** Copilot Studio's published limits beside the Microsoft 365 Agents Playground, which excludes declarative agents — choosing fidelity on a surface where the platform has withdrawn the cheap option and proposed no substitute
**Whitepaper:** [WP-036](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-036.md)
**Retrieves from:** S016, S031, S035
**Entry state:** either

---

## The situation

You have an idea and you want to know whether it is any good. So you make something to show people.

You have an hour before the session and a tool that will render the idea as a working conversational agent, in a Teams-shaped window, with the customer's terminology in it and a sensible-looking response for the three questions you can think of. It will take you less time to produce that than to draw the idea on a sheet of paper.

**Everything you do to that artefact between now and the meeting makes the answer you get worse.** The mechanism is specific enough to check rather than believe, and you will check it today.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught. They are a generation phase and nobody is going to help you with them.

1. Sketch your idea in one minute, by hand, on one sheet. Keep it.
2. List five details you left out. For each, write the conversation it would have started.
3. Which of those five conversations is about your idea, and which are about something else?
4. You show somebody a finished-looking artefact and they say it looks great. What did you learn?
5. Producing the refined version costs you nothing extra. Give the reason not to.

**Item 5 is the session's problem and it does not have a settled answer.** The argument for stripping detail was built in a world where detail had a price, and the price did the enforcing. It no longer does. Most learners produce a fluent answer to items 2 and 3 and something apologetic for item 5, and the shape of that gap is what the rest of the day is about.

## What you must produce

1. **A concept artefact at deliberately low fidelity** — one idea, in a form that can be altered while somebody is holding it, containing nothing that is not the idea.
2. **The same idea built once in Copilot Studio**, stopped deliberately at a stated fidelity ceiling, with the ceiling written down before you start.
3. **A stripping record.** One row per detail removed or refused, naming the detail, the discussion its presence would have provoked, and whether that discussion is about the idea or about something else. **The record is the argument; the artefacts are only its output.**
4. **Your position on item 5**, written as the sentence you would actually say to a colleague who has already produced the refined version for free and does not see the problem.

## The material

### The mechanism, and why it is a mechanism rather than a preference

The practice corpus behind this stage — the internal delivery material of a customer-embedded engineering organisation inside a large technology vendor, recorded as an original paraphrase at [research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md) — argues for deliberate low fidelity, and it argues rather than asserts. That is unusual enough in the collection to be worth saying: most of its claims are stated flat.

The argument runs like this. Adding detail to an early concept artefact is **actively harmful**, because the added detail drags the discussion onto the detail. Its own worked examples are the kind you will meet within a week of arriving anywhere: an argument about the wrong equipment type, an argument about an assumed attribute of the workforce, an argument about whether a device fits over the safety gear people already have to wear. **None of those arguments is about your idea, and the room only has so many minutes.**

The reason this works is not mysterious. A concrete detail is more discussable than an abstraction. Everybody in the room can hold an opinion about a device; far fewer can hold an opinion about whether the underlying idea is worth pursuing, and the first opinion is cheaper to voice. So the specifics you added for plausibility act as a conversational attractor, and the best question of the session ends up being about something you invented to fill a gap.

There is a second mechanism, independent of the first, and it is the one that survives when the first is handled. **Polish signals investment, and people do not attack investment.** A rough artefact invites alteration. A finished one invites praise. That is why the low-fidelity artefact has to be alterable while somebody is holding it: an artefact that takes longer to change than the conversation takes is already too finished, whatever it looks like.

**Carry the corpus's limits with every use of it.** It is access-controlled and cannot be linked, quoted or named. It contains **no citation, no study and no measurement anywhere** — its case studies all succeed, its single failure narrative is a synthetic composite and fictional by its own admission, and it records no cost side of any kind. It is a good argument from experienced people. It is not a finding.

### The research literature agrees, and is sharper about what fidelity actually is

The interviewing literature reaches the same place from a different direction, and it adds the distinction that makes the folk version usable.

Portigal's treatment of concept work is not concept testing. A deliberately **provocative, partial or non-viable concept** is used to reveal design criteria and the boundaries of desirability — you present it neutrally, take open reaction before prompting on planned topics, and **avoid signalling authorship or emotional investment** ([research/05-fde-craft/portigal-interviewing-users.md](/research/05-fde-craft/portigal-interviewing-users.md)). The last clause is the operative one for this session. A concept whose author is visible is a concept nobody will reject, and *made by me and I am pleased with it* is broadcast by finish more reliably than by anything you say.

The same source supplies the correction most engineers need: **fidelity is not one dial.** Appearance, behaviour, content and physical form are separate dimensions, and polish along any of them introduces unintended assumptions — about brand, about feasibility, about mobility, about completeness. This matters because it means *low fidelity* is not a synonym for *rough-looking*. You can and often should run a high-content, low-appearance artefact: real terminology from the customer's own domain, real edge cases, drawn on paper. The instinct to make everything equally rough is as unexamined as the instinct to make everything equally finished.

**Two limits travel with that note.** It is the **2013 first edition only**, and nothing from the later edition is used here. And it is a practitioner synthesis reporting **no controlled comparison of interview techniques, no sampling framework and no measured causal effect** — the note records that even its empathy claim rests on an analogy in a footnote rather than on a study of user interviewing.

The contextual-design method makes the artefact's purpose explicit in a sentence worth memorising. Rough paper prototypes are instruments for codesign, not demonstrations: paper **keeps attention on concept and structure, makes incompleteness obvious, and permits the prototype to be changed during the conversation**, with movable elements represented as separate pieces so the user and the designer can manipulate them together ([research/05-fde-craft/beyer-holtzblatt-contextual-design.md](/research/05-fde-craft/beyer-holtzblatt-contextual-design.md)). And the governing rule: **the goal is to discover failure, not to collect approval.**

That source also gives you the discipline for what to do with a reaction, which most teams get backwards. A reaction is traced to the **highest** relevant level and redesign proceeds from there downward — new information about practice may change the model, a mismatch in structure changes the structure, and only a local presentation problem changes the presentation. **Polishing an interface for a product structure that is about to change is the commonest waste in early design**, and a high-fidelity artefact invites exactly that, because it makes the presentation the most available thing to comment on. **The limits: a method book, second edition, whose effectiveness rests on practitioner experience rather than on comparative evidence.**

### Choosing a tool because of what it cannot do

Here the session stops being about drawing and becomes an engineering decision, because you are not going to hand your customer a sheet of paper for an agent.

Microsoft's own comparison guidance for Copilot Studio is unusually candid, and the candour is the point. On the advantage side it describes a low-code drag-and-drop interface usable **"without coding"**, **"hundreds of prebuilt connectors"**, and suitability for **"smaller, targeted agents that don't require large-scale enterprise governance"**. On the disadvantage side, stated equally plainly: **"limited ability to fine-tune API calls, actions, and response formatting compared with a pro-code environment"**; source control and lifecycle tooling **"less integrated than the project-based workflow in Agents Toolkit"**; Adaptive Cards **"limited to schema 1.6 and earlier"**; and slower feature availability. The same family positions the product as **"designed for small-scale deployments, such as department-level solutions"** ([research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md)).

Read that list again as a fidelity specification rather than as a complaint. **Every disadvantage in it is a ceiling on how finished you can make the thing**, and a ceiling is precisely what the mechanism asks for. You cannot fine-tune the response formatting, so you will not spend two days on response formatting. The card schema is pinned, so the visual richness stops somewhere you did not choose. The lifecycle tooling is thinner, so the artefact resists becoming a codebase you feel obliged to keep. **A published limit is an enforcement mechanism you do not have to supply yourself**, which is the only kind that survives the costless-polish problem.

Now the objection, and you must hold it, because a session that stopped at the previous paragraph would be selling you something. **The ceiling is on controllability, not on plausibility.** A Copilot Studio agent published into a chat surface looks entirely finished to the person using it — it has the customer's branding around it, it responds in fluent prose, and nothing about it says *this is a sketch*. The tool caps how far you can tune it and caps nothing about how convincing it looks. So choosing it is a real fidelity decision on one dimension and no decision at all on another, and if you use it without saying which, you have bought the mechanism's language without its effect.

**The limits on that documentation are heavy and go with every use.** It is first-party selection guidance whose authority is definitional. It offers **no adoption data, no failure-rate data, no cost comparison and no evidence that its recommended mappings produce better outcomes.** It compares one vendor's options against each other and not against alternatives. It was **read through search-returned excerpts rather than full page fetches, with several comparison tables truncated.** And it is **highly perishable** — product names and capability boundaries are actively changing, and a limitation recorded today may be closed before you next teach this.

### The platform removed the cheap option, and proposed no substitute

Everything so far has assumed you are choosing fidelity. **Sometimes the choice has already been made for you, and noticing that is a harder and more honest version of this session's problem than choosing well.**

Here is the case, and it is the sharpest asymmetry in the whole build surface. **Code-based agents get a sandbox** that removes the tenant, the tunnel and the application registration from the loop, mocks the surrounding context, and publishes a table of what it cannot emulate ([research/06-microsoft-platform/agents-toolkit-and-sdk-family.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/agents-toolkit-and-sdk-family.md)). **Declarative agents get none of it.** The sandbox's own documentation states it in one sentence — **"This is not supported for declarative agents"** — and the vendor's first-party editor material repeats it: the sandbox applies to code-based agents only, and declarative agents and their plugins **must be tested in the host product.**

The published tutorial confirms the shape of the alternative without ever naming it as a development cycle: author the definition, select **provision**, then open the live product surface, select your agent and ask it a question. **So for a declarative agent, `provision` is the inner loop.** There is no sandbox, no mock, no tunnel-free path and no offline substitute. **The cheapest available iteration requires a licensed tenant and a round trip through a live product.**

**The documentation states every fact that implies this and never states the implication.** No page consulted names the observed loop as the recommended development cycle, none discusses its cost, and **no substitute is proposed anywhere.** That is the finding, and it is bigger than a tooling inconvenience: **it is a substantial difference in the cost of iteration between two agent kinds, and it is a project cost that belongs in a plan rather than in a developer's afternoon.**

Now put it beside this session's argument and watch the argument get harder. **The whole of the low-fidelity case rests on there being a cheap, rough, alterable version available to choose.** On this surface there is not. You cannot hold the behaviour dial down by choosing a cheaper loop, because the cheaper loop does not exist; the only loop produces a provisioned agent inside a real product. **What remains yours is content, scope and what you decline to build** — and the honest act is to name which dial the platform took away rather than to describe the artefact you were forced into as a deliberate choice.

There is a second-order point here about the repair this session offered earlier, and it should not be skipped. **A discipline that rests on a vendor's published limits rests on somebody else's backlog.** Earlier that risk ran one way — a limit might be *closed*, removing your enforcement mechanism. **Here it runs the other way**: a capability has been *withheld* from one agent kind, which enforces a floor rather than a ceiling. **Both are the same dependency**, and a design that celebrates one while ignoring the other has not understood what it is depending on.

**The limits on that note travel with every sentence.** It is first-party product documentation, **definitional rather than evidential**: the vendor may state that the sandbox excludes declarative agents, and has published nothing measuring what that costs anybody. Roughly thirty pages behind it were read only through **search-returned excerpts**, so no list drawn from it is exhaustive. **Part of the evidence is a first-party artefact shipped inside a versioned editor extension**, with no public URL, no currency guarantee and diagnostic claims recorded as reported rather than verified. Every principal name in the area has changed at least once, one package has changed name twice, and **two package names for the same sandbox appear on live pages simultaneously.** And the note records the absence explicitly: **no inner-loop guidance exists for declarative agents at all**, and the exclusion is one sentence with nothing offered in its place.

### When the ceiling is a floor: the loop sets your appearance dial for you

The session has already conceded that a low-code tool caps **controllability** and caps nothing about **plausibility** — the artefact still looks decided to the person using it. **On this surface the position is worse than that, and it is worse in a way worth naming precisely.**

When the only available loop is provisioning into the live product, **the artefact arrives inside the customer's real tooling, under the customer's own branding, in fluent prose, adjacent to things that actually work.** Nothing about it says *sketch*. **You did not choose that appearance level. The loop chose it, and there is no lower setting available.**

So the four dials are no longer four choices. **Appearance is pinned high by the delivery mechanism**, behaviour is constrained by whatever the declarative surface can express, and the two dials genuinely left to you are **content** and **scope** — what is in it, and how much of the idea it claims to cover.

**The repair is not to fake roughness**, which is unavailable and would be dishonest anyway. It is three specific acts, and the stripping record should carry all three.

**Declare the dial that is not yours**, in the record, before the showing. A ceiling you did not set is still a ceiling somebody must know about — and a record that lists four dials as though all four were chosen has misdescribed the artefact.

**Compensate on the dials that are yours.** Narrow the scope hard, and raise content rather than appearance: real terminology, real edge cases from your [S034](/wiki/seminars/S034.md) record. **A wrong answer on a real case is a finding; a wrong answer on an invented one is a bug in your example.**

**And say it out loud, because verbal signalling is the only low-fidelity channel you have left.** When the artefact cannot look provisional, the only remaining way to invite rejection is to state, before anybody touches it, what it is not and what you want attacked. **That is weaker than a paper prototype and it is what is available**, and a session that pretended otherwise would be selling the countermeasure without its mechanism.

**One published fact makes the plan side of this concrete and belongs in the record rather than in the discussion.** The iteration cost includes a licensed tenant and a live round trip **per iteration**, which sets a floor under how many rounds a fixed budget buys — and the corpus's own rule is that a fixed budget should buy more iterations rather than fewer refined ones. **On this surface those two collide**, and the collision is a planning input rather than a preference. Exercise 6 already asks how many rounds you got; on a declarative surface, answer it in the currency the loop actually charges.

**Limits, again, and they matter for the plan more than for the argument.** **No cost figure, no iteration-time measure and no comparison against the code-based loop is published anywhere**, so everything above is direction and mechanism: **the loop is more expensive, and nobody has said by how much.** **This wiki asserts no magnitude here and none is available to assert.** The material is highly perishable and the exclusion is a product decision that could be reversed without notice — **if a local loop for declarative agents exists by the time you read this, that is the finding, and it is a better one than the section it replaces.**

### The instrument that made polish free is the same one that makes it dangerous

The reason item 5 has no settled answer is that the tool which produces your refined version in an hour is the same tool whose failures are not visible.

Capability in these systems is **jagged**: the boundary between what they handle well and what they do not is uneven and does not track how hard a task feels to a person ([research/04-professional-formation/dellacqua-jagged-technological-frontier.md](/research/04-professional-formation/dellacqua-jagged-technological-frontier.md)). Outside that boundary the failure mode is not a refusal but a confident, plausible, wrong answer. **That note rests on an abstract, its out-of-frontier result rests on a single task, and no magnitude from it is usable or asserted here.**

Combine the two facts and you get the specific hazard of this stage. A generated refinement adds plausible specifics you did not choose — a role, a step count, a screen, a field name — and those specifics are exactly the conversational attractors the mechanism warns about. **A detail you did not decide on will be read by the room as a decision you made**, and you will not be able to say who chose it, because nobody did.

### Why you are made to build both

This session could have told you to strip detail and moved on. It does not, and the reason is a design condition rather than a stylistic one.

The learning-design source behind this programme's ordering requires tasks that use **contrasting cases** — at least two, ideally contrasts that can be perceptually seen — and that **vary the cases**, holding some features constant and varying others, so that attention lands on the critical feature ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Two artefacts of the same idea at different fidelities, shown to different people with the same question, is that design. One artefact and a good explanation is not.

**That source's evidence is school, university and laboratory evidence**, and its author's claim that the design works in corporate and adult settings appears in his own voice with nothing in his reference list behind it. **This programme's use of it on working professionals is an extrapolation and is recorded as one.**

## The workflow

**Build the same idea twice, at two declared fidelities, and let a real person show you which one you learn from.**

**Step 1 — write the idea and the disagreement, in one sentence each.** What is the idea, and what exactly would somebody who disagreed with it be disagreeing with? If you cannot write the second sentence, you do not have an artefact to build; you have a topic.

**Step 2 — build the rough one first, and treat it as the artefact rather than as a draft.** Hand-drawn, cut from paper, three sentences in a text file — whatever form lets a person take a pen to it. **Keep the version you made in step 1 of the opening; you will need it in exercise 3.**

**Step 3 — declare your fidelity ceiling before you open the tool.** Write down, in the record, which of the four dimensions you are deliberately holding down: appearance, behaviour, content, physical form. A ceiling declared afterwards is a description of where you got tired.

**Step 4 — build the same idea in Copilot Studio and stop at the ceiling.** Use the low-code surface and prebuilt connectors and nothing else. Do not reach for a pro-code path to work around a formatting limit — **the limit is the instrument.** Note in the record every point at which you wanted to exceed the ceiling and what you would have been buying.

**Step 5 — write the stripping record as a file in the repository, not as a slide.** The corpus's own rule is to move engineering artefacts out of documents and into markdown in the repository, on the grounds that this is what makes them actionable, and the rule is right for a reason it does not state: a file gets reviewed and a slide gets presented.

```yaml
# docs/prototypes/shift-handover/stripping-record.yaml
idea: "A handover note is assembled from the shift log rather than written from memory."
disagreement: "That the log contains what the next shift needs. It may not."

fidelity_ceiling:              # DECLARED BEFORE THE TOOL WAS OPENED
  appearance: low              # default theme, no branding, no icon
  behaviour:  low              # one topic, no fallback handling, no memory
  content:    HIGH             # real terminology, real edge cases from S034
  physical:   n/a

removals:
  - detail: "A named shift-supervisor role on the approval step"
    would_have_provoked: "Whether that role exists at this site, and who covers nights"
    about_the_idea: false      # a staffing argument, not an idea argument
  - detail: "A rendered card with a severity badge"
    would_have_provoked: "Which severity scheme, and why not the one in the incident tool"
    about_the_idea: false
  - detail: "Two worked handovers using last month's real log entries"
    would_have_provoked: "Whether the log actually contains the causal detail"
    about_the_idea: true       # KEPT. This is the disagreement, so it stays.

ceiling_pressure:              # where the tool's published limits held the line
  - "Wanted custom response formatting; not available without a pro-code path."
  - "Wanted a richer card; card schema is pinned below what I had in mind."

position_on_costless_polish: >
  ONE SENTENCE, IN THE WORDS YOU WOULD SAY TO A COLLEAGUE. Not a principle.
```

**Step 6 — show both to two people who have seen neither, with the same question.** Record what each of them talked about **for the first two minutes** — not what they concluded. The comparison is the session's evidence and [S037](/wiki/seminars/S037.md) and [S038](/wiki/seminars/S038.md) both use it.

**Step 7 — attack your own artefact with the model, twice, and keep what beat you.** First: give it the refined version and ask it to name the three details most likely to capture the conversation, and which of them you did not consciously choose. Second, and this is the one that matters: **ask it to argue that deliberate low fidelity is obsolete now that polish is free**, at its strongest, and to name the observation that would settle the disagreement. Record what you kept and what you rejected. **An agent asked to improve your artefact will add to it; asked to destroy your position, it has to locate a weakness, and locating is the harder operation.**

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That a published product limit is doing work an intention cannot — you will feel the ceiling hold at step 4 in a way no resolution to be disciplined has ever held. And that the two artefacts produce two different conversations from the same question, which is the whole claim of the session and takes about ten minutes to verify or refute.

**The third build, on a surface where the cheap option is not available.**

Steps 1 to 7 assume a low-fidelity path exists to choose. **This part establishes whether it does, on the surface your design actually names**, and it is the more common case in delivery.

**Step G — name the agent kind, then find out whether a local loop exists for it.** Not whether one exists for the product family. **For your kind.** Search the documentation for the sandbox's supported scope and read the sentence that excludes something. Record the page and the date.

**Step H — if there is no local loop, price the one you are left with, per iteration.** Licences required, for whom. Provisioning time. Whether a round trip through the live product is needed to see a change. Whether anybody other than you can run it. **Then convert it into the only currency this session cares about: how many rounds a fixed budget buys.**

**Step I — record which dials the loop took away from you.** Extend the stripping record rather than starting a new artefact.

```yaml
# docs/prototypes/shift-handover/stripping-record.yaml   (continued)

agent_kind: ""                    # declarative | code-based | other
local_loop:
  exists: ""                      # yes | no | NOT STATED IN THE DOCS
  source: ""                      # page + date. One sentence usually decides this.
  substitute_proposed: ""         # RECORD 'none' IF NONE. That absence is the finding.

loop_cost_per_iteration:
  licences_required: ""           # for you, and for anyone who must see it
  round_trip: ""                  # what has to happen before a change is visible
  who_else_can_run_it: ""         # if the answer is 'nobody', say so
  rounds_a_fixed_budget_buys: ""  # the only number that matters here, and it is yours,
                                  # not the vendor's — no published figure exists

dials_not_mine:                   # a ceiling you did not set is still a ceiling
  appearance: ""                  # pinned HIGH if the only loop is the live product
  behaviour: ""                   # constrained by what the declarative surface expresses
  reason: ""                      # the loop, the tool, or the tenant — name which

dials_still_mine:
  content: ""                     # RAISE THIS. Real terminology, real edge cases from S034.
  scope: ""                       # NARROW THIS. It is the strongest lever you have left.

verbal_low_fidelity_signal: >
  THE SENTENCE YOU WILL SAY BEFORE ANYBODY TOUCHES IT. When the artefact cannot
  look provisional, speech is the only remaining channel for inviting rejection.
  Write it here, in the words you will use, and read it aloud once before the room.
```

**Step J — show it, and open with the sentence.** Say what the artefact is not and what you want attacked, before anyone uses it. **Then record the first two minutes exactly as in step 6**, so the three artefacts are comparable.

**Step K — attack the compensation, not the artefact.** Give the agent your `dials_not_mine` and `dials_still_mine` entries and ask it for **the dial you have recorded as forced that you could in fact have held down**, then for **the sentence in your verbal signal that is an apology rather than an invitation**. Then ask it to argue that naming a forced dial changes nothing about how the room behaves. **That last argument is strong and this programme has no answer to it** — record whether the instrument found it.

**What this third part makes observable.** That **a fidelity decision can be taken away from you by a delivery mechanism rather than by a tool's feature set**, and that the two look identical in a record which lists four dials as though all four were chosen. You will also watch the session's own repair fail on one surface: **the published-limit mechanism works by capping what you can add, and it does nothing at all when what has been removed is the cheap option itself.**

## What good looks like

- **The artefact tests one idea**, and you can say in a sentence what a person disagreeing with it would be disagreeing with.
- **The fidelity ceiling was declared before the tool was opened**, dimension by dimension, and the record shows where the tool's published limits held it and where your own intent had to.
- **Every removal is justified by a conversation it prevents**, named specifically. *It would have been distracting* is not a row.
- **The artefact can be changed while somebody is looking at it.** If altering it takes longer than the conversation, it is too finished.
- **It is answerable with a flat no**, and you can describe what that no would look like.
- **Nothing in it implies a commitment you have not made.** A rendered screen implies a screen; a named field implies a schema; a logo implies a decision about who owns this.
- **The record contains your position on item 5**, written as the sentence you would actually say to a colleague, not as a principle.

## How it goes wrong

- **The costless polish** — the refined version took no longer, so you showed it. *Warning sign:* you did not decide to polish it; you just did not decide not to.
- **The irrelevant argument** — the room spends its time on the equipment type, the workforce assumption, or whether the thing fits the physical constraints of the job, and never reaches the idea. *Warning sign:* the best question of the session was about something you invented to fill a gap.
- **The implied commitment** — a detail you added for plausibility is read as a decision. *Warning sign:* somebody asks who chose that, and you cannot remember choosing.
- **The undeclared dimension** — you called the artefact low fidelity because it looks rough, while its behaviour implies a whole workflow. *Warning sign:* your record names a fidelity level and not four.
- **The tool mistaken for the ceiling** — you chose a low-code path and stopped thinking, but the published limits cap controllability and not plausibility, and the thing still looks finished to the person using it. *Warning sign:* nobody who saw it asked a single question about whether the idea was right.
- **The suppressed no** — it looks like real work, so the person softens. *Warning sign:* the feedback is entirely additive.
- **The keepsake** — you cannot throw it away, so you defend it. *Warning sign:* the second version is the first version with amendments.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The artefacts and the stripping record are carried into [S037](/wiki/seminars/S037.md), where the low-fidelity one is broken on purpose, and into [S040](/wiki/seminars/S040.md), where the whole proof is priced.

**1 · Split one artefact into four dials.** Take the Copilot Studio version you built and rate it separately on appearance, behaviour, content and physical form. Then say which dial you would raise if you had one more hour, and what it would buy you.

> **Model answer.** Almost everyone raises **appearance** and almost nobody should. Appearance is the dimension that buys plausibility and no information: it makes the artefact look decided, which suppresses rejection, and it changes nothing about what the artefact can be wrong about. **Content is usually the dial worth raising** — real terminology, real edge cases from your [S034](/wiki/seminars/S034.md) record — because a wrong answer on a real case is a finding and a wrong answer on a made-up case is a bug in your example. If you rated the artefact with a single number rather than four, that is the exercise's actual catch.

**2 · Find the published limit and decide whether it helps you.** Using the platform comparison guidance, locate one stated limitation of Copilot Studio and write two sentences: what it prevents you from polishing, and what it does *not* prevent.

> **What you should find.** The documentation states a limited ability to fine-tune API calls, actions and response formatting compared with a pro-code environment; Adaptive Cards pinned to schema 1.6 and earlier; lifecycle tooling less integrated than the project-based pro-dev workflow; and slower feature availability. **Each of those is a ceiling on controllability and none of them is a ceiling on plausibility.** The agent will still look finished to the person using it. A learner who wrote only the first sentence has found the limit and missed the argument. **If the limitation you chose has already been closed since this page was written, record that as the finding** — the note marks this material highly perishable and says so.

**3 · Go back to your own item 5, then break it.** Find what you wrote for opening item 5 — the reason not to produce the refined version when it costs nothing. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then write your position properly.

> **Criterion.** A passing position names **which mechanism it is defending**, because there are two and they fail differently. The detail-attracts-conversation mechanism is defeated by care: you can produce a polished artefact with no invented specifics in it, and the argument against you is that this is now possible for free. The polish-signals-investment mechanism is **not** defeated by care, because it operates on the viewer rather than on the artefact's contents — finish is read as commitment whatever you intended. **A position that concedes the first and holds the second is stronger than one that holds both**, and a learner who concludes that low fidelity is obsolete without distinguishing them has argued against only half of it.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Count the first two minutes.** From your step 6 record, list what each viewer talked about first. Mark each item *about the idea* or *about something else*.

> **What to notice.** The refined artefact's list is usually longer and almost entirely in the second category, and it frequently contains at least one item you did not consciously put there. **That item is the finding.** If both lists came out the same, say so — a null result here is a real observation about your artefacts, and the honest reading is that your two versions differed in appearance and not in any dimension the viewer could act on.

**5 · Make the model argue the session is wrong.** Run the second half of step 7 and record the strongest argument it produced against deliberate low fidelity.

> **What to notice.** The strongest available argument is not that polish is free — it is that **a rough artefact now signals carelessness rather than provisionality**, because the audience knows a good version costs an hour. That is a real change in what fidelity communicates, and this programme has no answer to it. Record whether the model found it. If it produced only *low fidelity saves time*, it was arguing about cost while the session is about evidence, and you have watched an instrument miss the question.

**6 · State what your budget bought, in iterations.** The corpus's own rule is that given a fixed time budget you run more iterations rather than fewer and more refined ones. Say how many rounds you got, and whether you would make the same trade again.

> **Scoring criterion.** The answer passes if it names a specific thing an extra round would have caught. **If your two artefacts were the whole of your iteration, the rule was cited and not applied**, and that is worth noticing before [S037](/wiki/seminars/S037.md), where you get one break and no rehearsal.

**7 · Find the surface where the cheap option is not on offer.** Run workflow steps G and H for the agent kind your design actually names. Write two sentences: what a local loop would have cost you, and what the only available loop costs you instead.

> **What you should find.** That the sandbox everybody talks about **excludes declarative agents in a single sentence** — the documentation says it applies to code-based agents only, and that declarative agents and their plugins must be tested in the host product — and that **no substitute is proposed anywhere.** The observed loop is: author, provision, open the live product, ask it a question. **So provisioning into a licensed tenant is the inner loop**, and the documentation states every fact implying that without once naming it as a development cycle or discussing its cost ([research/06-microsoft-platform/agents-toolkit-and-sdk-family.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/agents-toolkit-and-sdk-family.md), **first-party and definitional, read 2026-07-31; roughly thirty pages read through search excerpts so no list is exhaustive; part of the evidence ships inside a versioned editor extension with no public URL; two package names for the same sandbox are live simultaneously**).
>
> **The criterion for the second sentence is where most answers fail.** A passing answer states the cost **in rounds**, not in adjectives. **"The loop is slower" fails.** **"Every change needs a provision and a round trip through the live product, both of us need a licence to see it, nobody outside the two of us can run it at all, and that turns the budget from several rounds into two" passes** — because it names what the constraint does to the only quantity this session cares about. **And note what you cannot write: no cost figure, no iteration-time measure and no comparison against the code-based loop is published anywhere.** The direction is documented and **the magnitude is yours to measure or to leave blank.** A learner who quoted one has invented it.
>
> **Then the harder half.** Say which dial the platform took from you. **Almost everyone answers *behaviour* and the more consequential answer is *appearance*:** when the only loop is the live product, the artefact arrives branded, fluent and adjacent to things that work, and **nothing about it can be made to say sketch.** If your record listed four dials as though you had chosen all four, that is the exercise's actual catch, and it is the same catch as exercise 1 one layer up.

## What this does not establish

Nothing here shows that low-fidelity artefacts produce better products.

**The mechanism is a practitioner argument from a corpus with no evidence base of any kind** — no citation, no study, no measurement anywhere in it, no recorded failure, and no cost side. It is a good argument, it is checkable against your own session, and it is not a finding. **Direction and mechanism only. No magnitude is asserted, and none is available.**

The claim that polish suppresses honest feedback because it signals investment is **cited from general knowledge and not verified here.** The interviewing and contextual-design sources support the practice — provocative concepts presented without visible authorship, rough prototypes altered during the conversation — but both are **practitioner method books reporting no controlled comparison of techniques**, one of them read as a first edition only.

**The platform layer establishes what the tool cannot do and nothing about whether choosing it that way works.** The Copilot Studio limits are first-party documentation with a selection-guidance purpose: definitional authority, **no adoption, cost, failure-rate or outcome data**, one vendor's options compared against each other, read through search excerpts with several tables truncated, and **highly perishable**. The argument that a published limit is a better enforcement mechanism than an intention is **this design's reasoning and has no external warrant whatever.**

**The costless-polish problem is genuinely open.** The corpus states it and abandons it. This session hands it to the learner because the alternative is to teach a countermeasure whose enforcement mechanism has been removed and not say so. **Whether any of this survives when the refined version is free is untested, and a cohort that concluded it does not would be reporting a real finding about the material.**

The jagged-frontier note **rests on an abstract**, and its out-of-frontier result rests on a single task. It licenses the shape of the warning — confident wrong output rather than visible breakdown — and no number.

**And the withdrawn-loop material establishes an absence, not a cost.** The documentation states that the sandbox does not support declarative agents, and the observed alternative is visible in its own tutorials. **What it does not state is anywhere near as important as what it does.** No page consulted names the provision-and-open-the-product cycle as the recommended inner loop, **none discusses its cost, and no substitute is proposed** — the exclusion is one sentence with nothing offered in its place. **So this session asserts a direction and refuses a magnitude: the loop is more expensive, and nobody has published by how much.** There is **no cost figure, no iteration-time measure and no comparison against the code-based loop anywhere**, and a learner who quotes one has invented it.

**The material is definitional, partial and shipping.** It is first-party documentation whose authority is definitional rather than evidential: the vendor may state what its sandbox supports and has measured nothing about the consequences. Roughly thirty pages behind it were read **through search-returned excerpts**, so no list is exhaustive. **Part of the evidence is an artefact shipped inside a versioned editor extension** — no public URL, no currency guarantee, diagnostic claims recorded as reported rather than verified. Every principal name in the area has changed at least once and **two package names for the same sandbox appear on live pages simultaneously.** And **the exclusion is a product decision that can be reversed without notice**: if a local loop for declarative agents exists by the time you read this, that is a finding and a better one than the material it replaces.

**The compensation this session proposes has no warrant at all.** That declaring a forced dial, narrowing scope, raising content and stating a verbal invitation recovers any part of what a rough artefact buys is **design reasoning with nothing behind it.** The one strong argument against it is in exercise 7's attack step — that naming a forced dial changes nothing about how a room behaves — and **this programme has no answer to it.** A cohort that concluded the compensation is theatre would be reporting a real result about the material.
