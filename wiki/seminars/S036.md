# S036 · Deliberate Low Fidelity

**Module:** [M08](/wiki/modules/M08.md) · **Quarter:** [Q4](/wiki/quarters/Q4.md) · **Complexity class:** 3
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** none
**Whitepaper:** [WP-036](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-036.md)
**Retrieves from:** S016, S031, S035
**Entry state:** either

## The situation

You have an idea and you want to know whether it is any good. You make something to show people.

Everything you do to make that thing better makes the answer you get worse, and the mechanism is specific enough to be checked rather than believed.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught.

1. Sketch your idea in one minute, by hand, on one sheet. Keep it.
2. List five details you left out. For each, write the conversation it would have started.
3. Which of those five conversations is about your idea, and which are about something else?
4. You show somebody a finished-looking artefact and they say it looks great. What did you learn?
5. Producing the refined version costs you nothing extra. Give the reason not to.

**Item 5 is the session's problem and it does not have a settled answer.** The argument for stripping detail was built when detail had a price. It no longer does.

## What you must produce

Two things, and the second is the assessed one.

- **A concept artefact at deliberately low fidelity** — one idea, made in a form that can be altered in front of the person looking at it, containing nothing that is not the idea.
- **A stripping record.** One row per detail you removed, naming the detail, the discussion it would have provoked, and whether that discussion is about the idea or about something else. **The record is the argument; the artefact is only its output.**

## What good looks like

- **The artefact tests one idea**, and you can say in a sentence what a person disagreeing with it would be disagreeing with.
- **Every removal is justified by a conversation it prevents**, named specifically. *It would have been distracting* is not a row.
- **The artefact can be changed while somebody is looking at it.** If altering it takes longer than the conversation, it is too finished.
- **It is answerable with a flat no**, and you can describe what that no would look like.
- **Nothing in it implies a commitment you have not made.** A rendered screen implies a screen; a named field implies a schema; a logo implies a decision about who owns this.
- **The record contains your position on item 5**, written as the sentence you would actually say to a colleague, not as a principle.

## How it goes wrong

- **The costless polish** — the refined version took no longer, so you showed it. *Warning sign:* you did not decide to polish it; you just did not decide not to.
- **The irrelevant argument** — the room spends its time on the equipment type, the workforce assumption, or whether the thing fits the physical constraints of the job, and never reaches the idea. *Warning sign:* the best question of the session was about something you invented to fill a gap.
- **The implied commitment** — a detail you added for plausibility is read as a decision. *Warning sign:* somebody asks who chose that, and you cannot remember choosing.
- **The suppressed no** — it looks like real work, so the person softens. *Warning sign:* the feedback is entirely additive.
- **The deck** — the artefact became a presentation, which is a thing people watch rather than change. *Warning sign:* it has a running order.
- **The keepsake** — you cannot throw it away, so you defend it. *Warning sign:* the second version is the first version with amendments.

## Do it

**Build the rough one first and keep it.** Not as a draft of the good one — as the artefact. Hand-drawn, in a text file, cut out of paper, described in three sentences: whatever form lets somebody take a pen to it.

**Then run the stripping record honestly, which means adding detail back in order to see what it costs.** Take the rough artefact and add one plausible specific — a device, a role, a number of steps, a screen. Show both to a peer and record what each one made them talk about. **The mechanism this session rests on is that the added detail drags the conversation onto things that are not the idea**, and it is cheap to observe: the concrete detail is more discussable than the abstraction, so people discuss it. **Polish suppresses honest feedback for a second and separate reason — it signals investment, and people do not attack investment.**

**Then face item 5 properly, because this is where the source stops.** The corpus that supplies the mechanism notices, and does not resolve, that its own tooling makes polished output nearly costless, and offers no advice on holding the line when the refined version costs the same as the rough one. **This programme has no advice either.**

So produce both versions — you can, cheaply, and that is the point — and write:

- what each version made a real person talk about;
- what you would say to a colleague who has already produced the refined one and does not see the problem;
- and, if you conclude the mechanism no longer holds when polish is free, the argument for that, in full.

**A learner who argues that low fidelity is obsolete and argues it well has done this session's work.** The position is not the assessed thing. The sentence is.

Finally, run the substitution test the corpus's own rule implies. Given a fixed budget it says to run more iterations rather than fewer and more refined ones. **State what your budget bought you, in iterations**, and whether you would make the same trade again.

## Show it

Give the rough artefact and the refined one to two different people who have not seen either, with the same question.

**Record what each of them talked about for the first two minutes.** Not what they concluded — what they talked about. The comparison is the session's evidence and you should keep it, because [S037](/wiki/seminars/S037.md) and [S038](/wiki/seminars/S038.md) both use it.

## What this does not establish

Nothing here shows that low-fidelity artefacts produce better products.

**The mechanism is a practitioner argument from a corpus with no evidence base of any kind** — no citation, no study, no measurement anywhere in it. It is a good argument, it is checkable against your own session, and it is not a finding. **Direction and mechanism only. No magnitude is asserted, and none is available.**

The claim that polish suppresses honest feedback because it signals investment is **cited from general knowledge and not verified here.**

**The costless-polish problem is genuinely open.** The source states it and abandons it. This session hands it to the learner because the alternative is to teach a countermeasure whose enforcement mechanism has been removed and not say so. **Whether any of this survives when the refined version is free is untested, and a cohort that concluded it does not would be reporting a real finding about the material.**
