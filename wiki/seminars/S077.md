## The situation

The capability you named at [S076](/wiki/seminars/S076.md) has to be built somewhere. There are two places it can go: inside the delivery, or after it.

After it is where everybody puts it, and after it is what gets cut. When the date holds and the work does not, the training week is the first item removed, because removing it costs nothing this month.

Inside the delivery is the alternative, and this session's honest position is stated before anything else. **It costs delivery speed.** Not a little, not only at the start, and not in a way you can engineer away by choosing the right tasks. A customer's engineer driving a change you could have made in twenty minutes will take considerably longer, will take a route you would not have taken, and will produce something you will want to rewrite. **That is the cost, it is real, and the argument of this session is that the cost is the point.**

**Answer these in writing before reading further.** Item 5 is the honest one.

1. Take three items from your current backlog. Which of them could the customer's engineer lead, today, with you beside them?
2. What would that cost you, in delivery terms? Be specific — name the item, the date it moves, and who notices.
3. Which item did you not choose, and was it because it was too risky or because it was too interesting?
4. Name one thing you will stop doing, so that somebody else has to start.
5. If the date came under pressure next week, what is the first thing you would take back? Write it down now.

Everybody has an answer to item 5 and almost nobody writes it down in advance, which is why the taking-back is invisible when it happens.

## What you must produce

**A teaching plan bound to real work**, and a pairing convention the tooling enforces.

1. **The named capabilities** from [S076](/wiki/seminars/S076.md), each mapped to **specific backlog items with dates** — not a category of work.
2. **Who holds the keyboard** for each item, by name.
3. **What you will do while they do it**, stated as an act: reviewing after, answering when asked, silent.
4. **What you will not do at all** — the withdrawal list. This column is the plan; everything else describes good intentions.
5. **The cost, in delivery terms**, and the name of the person who agreed to bear it.
6. **The reclaim rule** — the condition under which you take work back, who you tell, and what you record when it happens.
7. **A pairing convention in executable form**, constraining the tooling so that the customer's engineer remains the author of record rather than the audience.

## The material

### Say the cost out loud, because the alternative is that it gets paid silently

The strongest available evidence for this session's design also states its price, and states it in the same table.

Error-management training — training that encourages learners to make errors and then to learn from them — was compared against error-avoidant methods across two dozen studies ([research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md](/research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md)). The synthesis's first hypothesis distinguishes **performance measured during the training itself** from **performance on a separate task afterwards**, and predicts an effect on the second and not the first. The prediction held. Studies measuring within-training performance found no advantage; studies measuring post-training transfer did.

The authors' own statement of the mechanism carries the clause this session exists to make learners face: in error-management training, "immediate performance within the training phase **may not differ or may even be depressed**".

**Read that as a description of your delivery velocity for the fortnight in which you teach.** The design that produces transfer does not produce speed while it is running. Anybody who tells a sponsor that pairing costs nothing is describing a different intervention.

### What the method actually prescribes, including the part everybody drops

Two elements distinguish the method, and the second is the one that gets quietly discarded.

The first is **minimal guidance**: participants receive only basic information and then work independently on difficult tasks, "a procedure that almost inevitably leads to many errors". The second is **error-management instructions** — brief framing statements telling participants to expect errors and emphasising their informational function, in the source's own quoted wording: *"The more errors you make, the more you learn!"* and *"You have made an error? Great! Because now you can learn something new!"*

And then the operational detail that decides whether any of it is real: during the session the trainer repeats those statements and reminds participants to reflect on errors, **"but provides no further assistance when an error occurs"**.

**The framing is delivered; the help is not.** Almost every real pairing session delivers the framing and then supplies the help, because watching somebody struggle with something you could fix in a moment is genuinely unpleasant. A session in which you said *mistakes are how we learn* and then talked them through the fix has run the encouraging half of the intervention and none of the working half.

**Two structural limits govern every use of this source.** Every study in the pool compared error-management training against *another training method* rather than against no training, so all effects are relative — "better than a tutorial", never "better than nothing". And **no study in the pool measured on-the-job performance**; every outcome is a post-training task in a laboratory. The transfer established is transfer to a novel *task*, not to a job. A further caution belongs with it: the pooled variability was not statistically greater than sampling error would predict, so the moderators below were theory-driven partitions of a pool that did not require partitioning. The authors say so themselves.

### The condition that decides whether this works at all

The same synthesis contains the moderator that determines whether you should run this session's design on a given task, and it is not difficulty.

Because the method withholds guidance, **"the task itself needs to provide clearly interpretable and informative feedback"** for the design to work. Where feedback cannot be readily interpreted — the source's own examples are a statistics package producing output a novice cannot read, and a search returning records whose relevance the searcher cannot judge — the method may be *worse* than instruction that supplies external guidance. Studies with clear task feedback showed the effect; studies with unclear feedback did not reach significance.

**The authors are explicit that this moderator received only limited support** and that it cannot be concluded the method works only where feedback is clear. Take it as a design caution, not as a rule.

Applied here, the caution is sharp and it has a platform edge. **A pipeline that fails with a clear error is a good teaching surface. An agent that produces a plausible wrong answer in the same confident register as a correct one is the worst possible one**, because the learner receives no signal that anything went wrong. If you hand the customer's engineer a task where the tooling will fail silently, you have chosen exactly the condition under which this method has no support.

### Generation before instruction, under real stakes

The teaching design underneath the plan is generation before instruction, and one of its stated conditions is directly at odds with what you will want to do ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)).

The design is two-phase and order-dependent: learners generate solutions to a problem targeting something they have not been taught, fail, and are then taught. Its author's own framing is that this is proactive rather than reactive — "if failure is so powerful for learning, then we should not wait for it to happen. We should intentionally design for it."

**The binding condition is that the generation phase must not be scaffolded.** The author's own experiment manipulated guidance during the problem-solving phase and found that prompts and hints which reduced the failure rate *reduced learning*: "Encouraging or promoting failure facilitates learning. **Reducing failure hurts learning.**"

That is the sentence to hold when your hand moves toward the keyboard. **Taking the keyboard is scaffolding the generation phase.** It is not a small kindness that costs a little efficiency; it is the removal of the mechanism.

The same source reports a variant that bears directly on the pairing question. In a comparison of generating your own failed solutions against studying somebody else's failed solutions, generating placed first and studying second, with both ahead of direct instruction on deep understanding and transfer — and all three equal on procedural knowledge. **Watching you fail is better than watching you succeed, and doing it themselves is better than either.** The ordering is the argument for the withdrawal list.

**Its limits:** a trade book; school, university and laboratory evidence; no adult-professional study in its reference list; and the author's own concession that failure is sufficient but not necessary for the awareness it produces. Applying it to a customer's engineers during a live delivery is an extrapolation this programme states everywhere it uses the pattern.

### Practice is not sufficient, and this session must not overclaim

The corrective is held in this repository precisely so that a module about building capability does not slide into asserting that enough hours produce competence ([research/04-professional-formation/macnamara-deliberate-practice-limits.md](/research/04-professional-formation/macnamara-deliberate-practice-limits.md)).

That meta-analysis tested the strong claim — that individual differences in performance largely reflect individual differences in the amount of deliberate practice — and found it explains far less than the popular account holds, with the explanatory share varying enormously by domain. The pattern is the interesting part rather than any single figure. **Practice explains most in domains with stable rules, immediate unambiguous feedback and clearly defined performance criteria; it explains least in domains with unstable conditions, delayed or ambiguous feedback and contested criteria.**

Engineering delivery inside somebody else's organisation sits at the second end of that spectrum on every axis. **So the honest claim available to this session is narrow: pairing on real work creates the conditions under which capability could develop. It does not deliver capability, and nothing here entitles you to say it will.**

**Its limits:** this note rests on the official abstract rather than the full text, and a 2018 corrigendum by the same authors reanalysed the findings and reported no practical effect on the results. **No figure from it is reproduced here**; the domain ordering is the usable content.

### Feeling taught is not being taught

The measurement problem is the same one that runs through this programme, and here it lands on the person you are trying to help.

A randomised trial of experienced maintainers working on their own repositories found them **slower** with AI assistance while believing they had been made faster — and, the detail that matters most, **their post-hoc estimate after actually doing the tasks still went the wrong way** ([research/04-professional-formation/metr-ai-assisted-productivity-study.md](/research/04-professional-formation/metr-ai-assisted-productivity-study.md)). Direct personal experience did not correct the misperception.

**Transfer that to your pairing session.** A customer engineer who reports feeling more confident, who says they learned a lot, and who enjoyed the fortnight has told you about their experience of the fortnight. It is not evidence about what they can now do, and the study above is the strongest available demonstration that experience does not reliably produce that evidence even in the person who had it. **The only instrument for the capability question is watching them do it unaided**, which is [S079](/wiki/seminars/S079.md) and is why this session ends without a claim.

**Its limits are severe and must travel with it.** The population is narrow and adversarial to the hypothesis — expert maintainers of codebases they know intimately, close to the worst case for assistance. The developer sample is very small and time was self-reported. **The research group now labels the original result out of date and has redesigned the study**, because participants increasingly decline to work without AI at all, which compromises the control arm. Its later raw numbers point the other way and the group says they are not trustworthy. **Take the perception–measurement divergence and nothing else. No magnitude is asserted here.**

### Modelling cuts both ways, and the demonstration you enjoy is the one that costs most

The efficacy literature explains why the elegant demonstration is worse than useless ([research/04-professional-formation/bandura-self-efficacy.md](/research/04-professional-formation/bandura-self-efficacy.md)).

Of the four sources of efficacy belief, **only mastery experience is explicitly ranked**: "The most effective way of creating a strong sense of efficacy is through mastery experiences." Vicarious experience — watching somebody else — is the second named source, and it is conditional on perceived similarity, and it is symmetric: "Seeing people similar to themselves succeed by perseverant effort raises observers' beliefs... By the same token, observing others fail despite high effort lowers observers' judgments of their own efficacy." And: "If people see the models as very different from themselves their beliefs of personal efficacy are not much influenced by the models' behavior."

**An expert building something quickly and elegantly in front of a customer's engineer is a model who is not similar to them.** The performance transmits very little, and what it does transmit is a comparison the observer loses.

The passage that should reorganise your plan is about what a model actually transmits: **"Undaunted attitudes exhibited by perseverant models as they cope with obstacles repeatedly thrown in their path can be more enabling to others than the particular skills being modeled."** The valuable thing to demonstrate is not competence. It is what you do when the thing does not work — which means the useful demonstration is one where you are visibly stuck, and the polished one is the waste.

Two further passages bind the plan. **"If people experience only easy successes they come to expect quick results and are easily discouraged by failure. A resilient sense of efficacy requires experience in overcoming obstacles through perseverant effort."** That is the argument against the safe backlog item. And on what an effective teacher actually does: **"Successful efficacy builders do more than convey positive appraisals. In addition to raising people's beliefs in their capabilities, they structure situations for them in ways that bring success and avoid placing people in situations prematurely where they are likely to fail often."** The lever is task sequencing, not encouragement — which makes your teaching plan the intervention and your reassurance incidental to it.

**Its limits:** a conference collection edited by the field's founder, with no search strategy, no inclusion criteria and no disconfirming study in the editor's own chapter; empirical settings in schools, families, clinics, migration and addiction treatment; **no study of adult professionals learning a technical craft**; self-report measurement throughout; and the foundational 1977 paper unread in this repository. **Direction and mechanism only.**

### The source's own prohibition, and its own contradiction of it

The practice corpus behind this programme states a prohibition that this session extends: **design and enablement activity is interleaved into the engineering cadence and is never run as a separate workshop or a pause in delivery** ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md)). The prohibition is repeated across documents.

**And the corpus contradicts it**, with case material whose headline achievement is a compressed multi-day workshop. Neither document notices.

The corpus also carries the rule that governs how the tooling should be used in a teaching pair — **use it to make each step better, not to skip steps** — and contradicts that too, with a customer-facing anecdote in which a prototype is generated on day one with no discovery activity described at all. Read against the method, the flagship story is step-skipping presented as acceleration.

**So this session's structural claim rests on a rule its own source does not consistently follow, and extends that rule to capability transfer, which the source never does.** That is stated plainly rather than smoothed, and it is why the session's argument is about incentives rather than about evidence: work scheduled after delivery competes with nothing until the moment it is cut.

## The workflow

**Run one real backlog item in agent mode with the customer's engineer driving, under a convention that keeps them the author of record.**

**Step 1 — choose an item that can fail visibly.** Real, consequential, and instrumented well enough that a wrong answer announces itself: a failing test, a rejected deployment, a harness check that goes red. **Do not choose an item whose failure mode is a plausible wrong answer**, because that is precisely the unclear-feedback condition under which this method has no support.

**Step 2 — write the framing down and say it once.** That errors are expected, that they are the informational content of the session, and that you will not be helping when one occurs. Then stop saying it. Repeating encouragement while supplying assistance is the failure mode the method warns about.

**Step 3 — constrain the tooling so it cannot take the keyboard either.** Agent mode will happily produce the whole change, which converts your engineer from driver to reviewer of somebody else's work. Create `.github/instructions/pairing.instructions.md`:

```markdown
---
applyTo: "src/**,eval/**,infra/**"
---

# Pairing session: the customer engineer is the author of record

- Propose ONE step at a time. Stop and wait after each.
- Do NOT produce a complete implementation in a single response, even
  when asked to.
- Before writing code, state the assumption you are about to make and
  ask for it to be confirmed or corrected.
- When a check fails, report what failed and what it means. Do NOT
  offer a fix unless the fix is explicitly requested.
- You MUST NOT record, imply or summarise a human approval. Human review
  is a stop condition. Halt and say who must review.
- Every change MUST name the person who authored it in the commit trailer.
```

The last two rules are borrowed directly from an openly published agent configuration corpus, which prohibits an agent from checking human-review boxes and requires a workflow to stop when a required human review is absent ([research/06-microsoft-platform/hve-core-repository.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/hve-core-repository.md)). **The corpus's own transparency note is worth reading beside it:** the project ships no model and no safety classifier, host behaviour varies, and human oversight remains necessary. **Whether any of these rules holds depends on the host attaching them and the model complying, and neither is guaranteed by the file.** Its paths, counts and package versions are extremely perishable and moved during the reading.

**Step 4 — verify the convention arrived, on their machine.** Send one prompt and confirm the instruction text appears in the request payload. Then ask for something the convention forbids — a complete implementation in one response — and record what you get. **A convention you have not seen refused is a convention you have not seen.**

**Step 5 — sit on your hands and take notes.** They drive. You may answer a direct question. You may not type, you may not point at the screen, and you may not make the reassuring noise. **Count your interventions, including the small ones**, because the small ones are the ones you will not remember.

**Step 6 — debrief on the attribution rather than on the outcome.** Ask them to account for what went wrong, and record whether they name effort, an acquirable skill, the situation, or their own ability. That split is the efficacy signature, it is observable in a debrief, and it is more informative than any confidence rating they could give you.

**Step 7 — attack your own plan rather than improving it.** One prompt: *here is my teaching plan and my withdrawal list. Identify the item on the withdrawal list I will break first, and give the specific delivery circumstance in which I will break it. Then name one capability my plan builds that nobody will need, and one nobody has planned for.* Read it for the item you were already going to take back. Keep what is better than your own reasoning and record what you rejected. **Record separately that this instrument was the pairing tool and is therefore not independent of the session it is assessing.**

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** Two things, and they arrive in the same hour. The first is your own intervention count, which is always higher than the number you would have estimated. The second is the difference between an engineer who has watched you work and an engineer who has worked — visible in step 6, because the first group accounts for the failure by describing what you did and the second accounts for it by describing what they tried.

## What good looks like

- The plan names specific backlog items with dates, not a category of work. **A plan that says "pair on integration tasks" has scheduled nothing.**
- The customer's engineer leads at least one item whose failure would be visible, because nothing is learned from the trivial one — and whose failure would be *legible*, because nothing is learned from a silent wrong answer either.
- Your role during their work is stated as an act — reviewing after, answering when asked, silent — not as "supporting".
- **There is a withdrawal list**: things you are capable of doing quickly, and will not, because doing them removes the occasion. Naming these is the intervention.
- The cost is stated openly and in delivery terms, and the plan says who agreed to bear it. A transfer nobody has paid for will not survive its first bad week.
- The pairing convention exists in executable form and has been **observed refusing something**, not merely observed existing.
- Interventions are counted during the session, including the small ones, and the count appears in the plan's review rather than in your memory.

## How it goes wrong

- **The demonstration that is a performance** — you build it while they watch, elegantly, and both of you enjoy it. *Warning sign:* you were the one typing.
- **The safe item** — the customer's engineer is given work whose failure has no consequence, so nothing is discovered about their capability. *Warning sign:* you would not have bothered reviewing it.
- **The illegible item** — the work fails silently, the tooling produces a plausible wrong answer, and the learner receives no signal at all. *Warning sign:* you cannot say what red would look like on this task.
- **Transfer scheduled last** — the plan is real, and it all happens in the final fortnight. *Warning sign:* nothing in the first half of your plan has a date.
- **The quiet reclaim** — under pressure you take the item back, correctly, and record nothing. *Warning sign:* you have already decided which item, and it is not written anywhere.
- **The review that is not a review** — the customer's engineer is nominally the reviewer of record and you have already approved everything before they see it. *Warning sign:* they have never returned anything to you.
- **The agent as the third pair member who does all the work** — the engineer prompts, the tooling implements, and nobody has practised anything. *Warning sign:* the session produced a large diff and your engineer cannot say why any part of it is there.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The teaching plan and its intervention count are carried to [S079](/wiki/seminars/S079.md), where the acts it built are observed, and to [S080](/wiki/seminars/S080.md), where the whole stage is judged as an entrustment decision.

**1 · Price the fortnight.** Take the three backlog items from opening item 1 and, for the one you chose, write the delivery cost as three specific facts: which item moves, by how long, and who will notice.

> **Model answer shape.** *The connector work slips past the release window; the integration test suite stays amber for an extra sprint; the sponsor's fortnightly report will show one fewer completed item and she will ask about it on the fourteenth.* **A cost stated as "some slowdown" has not been priced**, and a plan whose cost has not been priced has not been agreed by anybody, because nobody was told what they were agreeing to.

**2 · Sort five tasks by feedback legibility.** For each, say whether a wrong answer announces itself, and whether you would use it for a pairing session:

- Adding a field to a schema with a migration and a test.
- Tuning a retrieval configuration and judging whether results improved.
- Fixing a failing deployment step whose error names the missing permission.
- Writing a runbook for an incident type that has not occurred.
- Adjusting an evaluator threshold and deciding whether the pass rate now means anything.

> **Model answers.** The first and third are legible: something goes red and the red says why. The second and fifth are the classic illegible cases — the output is a number that moved, and nothing in it tells the learner whether the move is real. The fourth has no feedback at all, because nothing will exercise it. **Two, four and five are unsuitable for an unassisted first session and are excellent for the third**, once the learner has enough judgement to interpret the output. That ordering is the teaching plan.

**3 · Go back to your own item 5, then break it.** Find what you wrote for opening item 5 — the first thing you would take back under pressure. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then write your reclaim rule properly.

> **Criterion.** A reclaim rule passes if it names **an observable condition, a person who is told, and an artefact that records it.** *"If we are running late"* fails, because every fortnight is running late. *"If the item is still open two working days before the release freeze, I take it back, I tell the customer's engineering lead in the stand-up, and it goes on the teaching plan as a reclaim with the reason"* passes. **A reclaim that follows a written rule is a decision. A reclaim that does not is the plan quietly ending**, and the difference is invisible from outside.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Run the pairing convention and try to break it.** Using the workflow, put the pairing instructions file in place, then ask the tooling for a complete implementation in one response and record what happens.

> **What you should find.** One of three things, and each is a different finding. The convention refuses, and you have evidence it is loaded. The convention is ignored, and you have learned that the rule is advisory on this host and model. Or nothing happens differently at all, and you cannot tell whether the file arrived — **which is the same state as not having written it**, and is the finding to record.

**5 · Count your interventions, then estimate them afterwards.** Have an observer count every intervention during a pairing session — every clarifying sentence, every hand raised toward the screen, every reassuring noise. Afterwards, and before seeing the count, write down what you think it was.

> **What to notice.** The gap, and its direction. **The relevant finding here is not the count but the divergence between the felt session and the recorded one**, which is the same divergence a randomised trial found between experienced developers' post-hoc estimates of their own speed and the measurement — a divergence that direct experience did not correct ([research/04-professional-formation/metr-ai-assisted-productivity-study.md](/research/04-professional-formation/metr-ai-assisted-productivity-study.md)). **That study's population is narrow, its sample small and its authors now label the result out of date.** Take the direction and no number.

**6 · Debrief for the attribution, not for the mood.** After a failed or partial pairing task, ask the engineer to account for what went wrong. Record which of four things they name: effort, an acquirable skill, the situation, or their own ability.

> **Scoring criterion.** A pass records the attribution verbatim and does not editorialise. Naming effort, acquirable skill or the situation is the efficacious pattern; naming ability is the inefficacious one, and it is the signal that a further unsupported task will not help. **A fail records an adjective about the person** — *keen*, *struggling*, *positive* — which is the same defect as recording willingness as ability, and it will reappear at [S079](/wiki/seminars/S079.md) if you do not catch it here.

## What this does not establish

**That capability built during delivery transfers better than capability delivered afterwards.** Nothing behind this programme measures either, and **no source it rests on reports a customer's engineers becoming more capable, by any route.** The practice corpus's case studies all succeed and it records no engagement in which capability transfer failed — an absence of looking rather than evidence of success.

**The training synthesis is relative and laboratory-bound.** Every study in its pool compared one training method against another rather than against no training, so nothing in it licenses "better than nothing". **No study in it measured on-the-job performance.** Its clear-feedback moderator, on which this session's task-selection advice rests, received only limited support by its authors' own statement, and its moderators were theory-driven partitions of a pool whose variability did not statistically require partitioning.

**The generation-before-instruction warrant comes from schools and laboratories**, with no adult-professional study in its author's reference list, and the author concedes failure is sufficient but not necessary for the mechanism.

**The practice corrective rests on an abstract** and carries a corrigendum by its own authors. Its usable content is that practice explains least in domains with unstable conditions and ambiguous feedback — which is this one. **No figure from it appears here.**

**The perception study is narrow, small and superseded by its own authors**, who have redesigned the experiment because participants will no longer reliably work without assistance. Its later raw numbers point the other way and are not trustworthy. Take the divergence, not a magnitude.

**The efficacy literature is about schoolchildren, families, clinics and treatment programmes**, and contains no study of adult professionals learning a technical craft.

**The session extends a prohibition its own source contradicts.** That is design reasoning with no external warrant, built on an inconsistent rule, and the argument for it is about incentives rather than evidence. **Direction and mechanism only. No magnitude, no rate, no proportion.**
