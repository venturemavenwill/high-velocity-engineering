## The situation

The capability you named at [S076](/wiki/seminars/S076.md) has to be built somewhere. There are two places it can go: inside the delivery, or after it.

After it is where everybody puts it, and after it is what gets cut. When the date holds and the work does not, the training week is the first item removed, because removing it costs nothing this month.

Answer these in writing before the session opens.

1. Take three items from your current backlog. Which of them could the customer's engineer lead, today, with you beside them?
2. What would that cost you, in delivery terms? Be specific.
3. Which item did you not choose, and was it because it was too risky or because it was too interesting?
4. Name one thing you will stop doing, so that somebody else has to start.
5. If the date came under pressure next week, what is the first thing you would take back? Write it down now.

**Item 5 is the honest one.** Everybody has an answer and almost nobody writes it down in advance, which is why the taking-back is invisible when it happens.

## What you must produce

A **teaching plan bound to real work.** For each named capability from S076: the actual backlog items through which it will be built, who holds the keyboard for each, what you will do while they do it, and **what you will not do at all.**

The last column is the plan. Everything else is a description of good intentions.

## What good looks like

- The plan names specific backlog items with dates, not a category of work. **A plan that says "pair on integration tasks" has scheduled nothing.**
- The customer's engineer leads at least one item whose failure would be visible, because nothing is learned from the trivial one.
- Your role during their work is stated as an act — reviewing after, answering when asked, silent — not as "supporting".
- **There is a withdrawal list**: things you are capable of doing quickly, and will not, because doing them removes the occasion. Naming these is the intervention.
- The cost is stated openly and in delivery terms, and the plan says who agreed to bear it. A transfer that has not been paid for by somebody will not survive its first bad week.

## How it goes wrong

Five failure modes, each with the warning sign that precedes it.

- **The demonstration that is a performance** — you build it while they watch, elegantly, and both of you enjoy it. *Warning sign:* you were the one typing.
- **The safe item** — the customer's engineer is given work whose failure has no consequence, so nothing is discovered about their capability. *Warning sign:* you would not have bothered reviewing it.
- **Transfer scheduled last** — the plan is real, and it all happens in the final fortnight. *Warning sign:* nothing in the first half of your plan has a date.
- **The quiet reclaim** — under pressure you take the item back, correctly, and record nothing. *Warning sign:* you have already decided which item, and it is not written anywhere.
- **The review that is not a review** — the customer's engineer is nominally the reviewer of record and you have already approved everything before they see it. *Warning sign:* they have never returned anything to you.

## Do it

Work from the supplied situation in [Q8](/wiki/quarters/Q8.md). The end date is fixed and the assessor's next sampling window does not move because of it, so the pressure in this exercise is real rather than notional.

Build the plan against the actual backlog. Then do the part that is genuinely hard: **write your reclaim rule.** State in advance the condition under which you take work back, who you tell, and what you record when it happens. A reclaim that follows a written rule is a decision; a reclaim that does not is the plan quietly ending.

Bring the plan to the technology function — the capable, unwilling group — and expect it to be refused. Their refusal is information about willingness, not about capability, and the response to it is not a better plan.

## Show it

State your withdrawal list aloud: the things you will not do.

Another learner's job is to find the item on it you will break first, and to say why. **The item you will break first is usually the one whose absence would be most visible to your own sponsor**, which is a fact about your incentives rather than about the recipient's capability.

## What this does not establish

That capability built during delivery transfers better than capability delivered afterwards. **Nothing behind this programme measures either, and no source it rests on reports a customer's engineers becoming more capable, by any route.**

The session borrows a prohibition from its practice corpus — that design and enablement activity is interleaved into the engineering cadence and is never run as a separate workshop or a pause in delivery — and extends it to capability transfer, which the corpus does not do. **The corpus also contradicts the prohibition itself**, with case material whose headline achievement is a compressed multi-day workshop, and the extension made here therefore rests on a rule that its own source does not consistently follow.

**This is design reasoning with no external warrant, built on an inconsistent prohibition.** The argument for it is that work scheduled after delivery is work that competes with nothing until the moment it is cut. That is an argument about incentives. It has not been tested.
