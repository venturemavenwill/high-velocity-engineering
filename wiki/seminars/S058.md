# S058 · Impact Assessment as a Living Document

**Module:** [M12](/wiki/modules/M12.md) · **Quarter:** [Q6](/wiki/quarters/Q6.md) · **Complexity class:** 4
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** Microsoft Purview DSPM for AI — an assessment that re-runs on a cadence rather than closing at sign-off
**Whitepaper:** [WP-058](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-058.md)
**Retrieves from:** S022, S024, S057
**Entry state:** this-programme-only

---

## The situation

[S057](/wiki/seminars/S057.md) established that the regime applies. Now you write the assessment, and the regime tells you — repeatedly, in several documents, in different words — that it is a **living document**.

Refresh at least annually. Refresh on every new intended use. Refresh before every release stage. Refresh at every milestone. Re-screen every sprint, in the team's own cadence.

**Now look at the form.** The signature block has one row shape and no way to sign again. The revision history has no version number and no change description. The submission is one-shot with no expiry. The completed assessment is filed in a document repository rather than in the system where the work happens.

**The best-designed control in the whole regime is the per-sprint re-screen, and it is the least observable thing in it — no artefact, no checkbox, nowhere at all to record that it happened.**

There is a working instrument, from a different part of the same vendor's estate, that does what the form only asserts. The data-posture tooling for AI **re-runs an assessment on a cadence, over a defined scope, with a stated latency, and expires its own results.** It is worth studying not because you will use it but because it has been forced to answer the three questions your assessment does not: **what did it look at, how often, and how late is the answer.**

Answer these before instruction.

1. Look at any assessment your organisation holds. When was it last true?
2. What in your process would cause somebody to reopen it?
3. Take one claim from an assessment. What measurement would show it false?
4. Your review fires. What does it cost, in whose time?
5. If nobody reopens it for a year, what breaks — and who finds out?

**Item 1 usually cannot be answered from the document itself**, which is the finding. **Item 4 is the one people skip**, and it decides whether anything you build here will ever run.

## What you must produce

**An impact assessment for your own system, with the instrumentation the regime omits, built and priced.**

1. **The assessment**, with at least one claim rewritten into falsifiable form: **a named measurement, a stated threshold, a verification method, an accountable owner.**
2. **A version field and a change description**, so that a reader can tell what moved.
3. **A re-sign-off row**, so that agreeing again is recordable.
4. **An expiry**, after which the document is stale rather than silent.
5. **A trigger bound to a state change**, not to a person remembering.
6. **A home in the system of record where the work lives**, not in a document repository.
7. **The resolution of your own instrument** — what the assessment looks at, how often, and how late its answer is — and what falls outside all three.
8. **The price of one review**, in whose time, and your judgement about whether it will be paid.

## The material

### A requirement asserted everywhere and instrumented nowhere

The regime this module is built on repeats the living-document requirement across several documents in different words, and then supplies a form that cannot express it ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md)). **The signature block has one row shape and no re-sign-off mechanism. The revision history carries no version number and no change description. The submission is one-shot with no expiry. The completed assessment is filed in a document repository rather than in the delivery system of record.**

**A living-document requirement with no version field, no re-sign-off row and no place to record the review is not a requirement.** It is an aspiration with an imperative mood, and the predictable outcome is an assessment written once to clear a commercial gate, thereafter describing a system that no longer exists.

The same corpus contains the fix, in a different document, and never connects the two. **Its environment withdrawal is triggered by a state change in an authoritative register rather than by a person remembering** — every environment is tagged to a project, the project register is declared the source of truth for status, and marking the project closed is what initiates decommissioning. **Reusing an environment after project close is named as a policy violation rather than an inefficiency.** That is a well-engineered trigger, and it sits three documents away from a governance requirement that has none.

**One part of the assessment template is directly reusable and this session says so plainly**, because most of the criticism above is structural and this is not. A handful of its fields force a claim to become falsifiable: **a named measurement, a stated threshold, a verification method, and an accountable owner.** Those four turn *the system will be monitored for bias* into something that can be true or false. The rest of the template is form-filling.

**The corpus's limits.** Access-controlled and paraphrased here, never quoted. **No citation of any kind across approximately ninety documents.** It contradicts itself in more than a dozen places. It records no failure, states no exclusion criteria and carries no cost side. **And it publishes no evidence that any assessment was ever revised, or that any revision altered anything** — which is the exact question this session is about.

### Every governance instrument has a scan scope, a cadence and a latency

The platform anchor exists because a real product has been forced to answer the questions a form can leave blank.

The data-posture tooling for AI runs a **data risk assessment** automatically on a recurring schedule over a defined subset of the estate — **the busiest sites rather than all of them** — with custom assessments available and a bounded number of sites eligible for item-level scanning ([research/06-microsoft-platform/purview-dspm-for-ai-and-defender-ai-spm-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/purview-dspm-for-ai-and-defender-ai-spm-docs.md)). It states an item ceiling per location above which counts may be inaccurate, and it excludes one storage service from item-level scanning entirely. Results **do not appear immediately**: policies need time before they produce data, custom assessment results take longer still, the first default assessment is delayed after configuration, **and custom results expire.**

**Read those as the resolution of the instrument rather than as small print.** A tool that samples part of an estate on a schedule with a delay gives you a posture estimate, not a real-time control. **An engineer who cannot state their instrument's scan scope, its cadence and its latency does not know what their controls cover**, and that sentence is the durable content of this session.

The assessment's detail view is organised as **Identify, Protect and Monitor**, which is a structure worth borrowing regardless of tooling, because it separates discovery from remediation from ongoing observation — three activities that a one-shot document collapses into one.

Two further details are worth carrying because they are governance judgements rather than features. Item-level remediation offers a small set of actions, and **the documentation flags removing a sharing link as "an action to be used sparingly because it could prevent people from accessing the item legitimately."** That is the whole governance dilemma in one sentence: **the safest action is also the most disruptive, and somebody has to decide.** And without an explicit policy, some workloads **audit that an interaction happened but not what was said.** **Knowing that an interaction occurred is not knowing its content**, and the default gives you only the former.

The strongest idea in that documentation is not a feature at all. **Generative AI "amplifies the problem of oversharing data"** by surfacing content that is obsolete, over-permissioned or ungoverned. Oversharing is not new; organisations have had over-permissioned sites for decades, and the reason little damage followed is that **nobody read them.** A capable retrieval system reads all of them, instantly, for anyone who asks. **Deploying capable retrieval converts every dormant permissions error in an organisation into a live one** — which means the assessment you write today is about a permissions estate that changes without you, and is therefore stale by construction rather than by neglect.

**The documentation's limits are severe and stated.** The page fetched for the data side is explicitly labelled **classic and superseded**, and its own notice says improvements will not be backported; the newer version was not read. The workload side was **read through search excerpts rather than a full page fetch**, so its structure is reconstructed. **No detection rates, no false-positive rates and no coverage measurements are published anywhere in it** — assert none. Agent-level capabilities on the workload side are moving to a separate licence. It is single-vendor: **the concepts transfer, the mechanisms and portal paths do not.**

### Why a one-shot assessment is structurally wrong rather than merely lazy

The usual explanation for stale assessments is discipline — people are busy, nobody reopens things. There is a better explanation and it is not about effort.

The evaluation literature names the phenomenon precisely: **criteria drift.** *Users need criteria to grade outputs, but grading outputs helps users define criteria* ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/shankar-evalgen.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/shankar-evalgen.md)). That is a genuine circularity rather than a workflow inefficiency. A person cannot fully specify what counts as good before seeing outputs, because **seeing outputs is how they discover which dimensions of quality actually vary and matter.**

The sharper finding is the one with methodological teeth. Some criteria appear to be **dependent on the specific outputs observed** rather than independently definable in advance, which the authors say **"raises serious questions for approaches that assume the independence of evaluation from observation of model outputs."** An impact assessment is exactly such an approach: it asks a team to enumerate harms before the system has produced anything, and then treats that enumeration as complete.

**So the assessment is not stale because somebody forgot. It is stale because the criteria it contains could not have been written correctly at the time it was written.** The design implication the same source draws is the one this session implements: **evaluation tooling should be built to expect the rubric to change** — supporting revision, re-grading and version history — rather than treating rubric revision as an exceptional event.

**Its limits.** The evidence is **qualitative**: the paper establishes that criteria drift is real and consequential and **does not quantify its prevalence or magnitude.** The user study is small by construction, as studies of novel interfaces usually are. It addresses criteria alignment rather than statistical validity. And it leaves a cost unresolved that this session inherits: **if criteria are revised after seeing outputs, the same outputs cannot cleanly serve as a held-out evaluation set.**

### Expiry is not pessimism, and the assessment literature already knew

The element learners resist most is the expiry, because it feels like an admission of failure. It is the opposite, and there is a precedent worth borrowing from a field that has thought about this for longer.

The entrustment framework used across this programme's assessment architecture allows an EPA description to carry an **expiry date**, on the explicit ground that if a learner has not practised the activity for a long time, **the entrustment decision may expire** ([research/08-assessment-epas/ten-cate-epa-framework.md](/research/08-assessment-epas/ten-cate-epa-framework.md)). **That is a discipline acknowledging that its own judgements decay, which most academic assessment schemes never admit.**

The same framework distinguishes **ad-hoc** from **summative** decisions, and the distinction transfers cleanly. An ad-hoc judgement is continuous, informal and contextual. A summative one has the nature of certification, is taken about future responsibilities, and requires **multiple observers in agreement and multiple observations.** **An impact assessment signed once by one person is being treated as summative on ad-hoc evidence**, and naming that is more useful than exhorting people to review more often.

**Its limits.** It is a clinical model: medicine has patients, legally responsible supervisors and a licensure endpoint, and **a course or an engagement has to construct the stakes artificially.** It is guidance from the concept's originator and an advocate — **it reports adoption, not effectiveness.** The empirical items in it are relayed from prior studies rather than generated there.

### A trigger is a state change somebody else makes

Pulling the three threads together gives the session's design rule. **A trigger bound to a person remembering is not a trigger. A trigger with a date and no actor is a calendar entry. A trigger bound to a state change in a register that somebody else updates for their own reasons will fire whether or not you are paying attention** — and that last property is the only one that matters, because the failure mode is inattention.

The one-setting-away column from [S057](/wiki/seminars/S057.md) is the natural input. Those are the artefacts whose governance class can change without anybody deciding to change it, and they are precisely what a state-change trigger should watch.

## The workflow

**Read a working instrument's own resolution, then give your assessment the properties it has and your form lacks.**

**Step 1 — record which version of the documentation you are reading.** The data-posture page for AI carries a notice saying it is the classic version and has been replaced. **Write down which one you read and the date.** This is not pedantry: a governance instrument documented in two generations is the same situation as a governance regime found in two generations, and [S057](/wiki/seminars/S057.md) has already shown you what comparing them is worth.

**Step 2 — extract the instrument's three properties.** From the documentation, write down what the assessment scans, how often it runs, and how late its results are. Then write down what it does not cover — the storage service excluded from item-level scanning, the cap on eligible sites, the item ceiling above which counts may be inaccurate. **The exclusions are the interesting half.**

**Step 3 — answer the same three questions about your own assessment.** What does it look at. How often does it get reconsidered. How long after a change does the reconsideration happen. **Most learners cannot answer any of the three, and discovering that is the point of doing it in this order.**

**Step 4 — make one claim falsifiable.** Take the vaguest commitment in your assessment and give it the four fields: a named measurement, a stated threshold, a verification method, an accountable owner. **These four are the reusable part of the whole regime.** Doing it once shows you how many of your other claims are decoration.

**Step 5 — build the missing instrumentation, in the artefact.**

```yaml
# assessment.md front matter, in the system of record   <- SHAPE, NOT SCHEMA.
version: 4                       # PRESENT IS NOT THE SAME AS USED. If this is still 1
changed: >                       # at submission, the field is decoration.
  Added proactive trigger to the summarisation agent. New intended use: unattended
  overnight run. Harm register reopened; two entries added.
changed_on: "<YYYY-MM-DD>"

resolution:                      # what THIS assessment covers, how often, how late
  scope: "<what it looked at, and what it did not>"
  cadence: "<what makes it re-run>"
  latency: "<how long after a change the answer updates>"
  outside_all_three: "<the things none of the above reaches>"

expiry:
  stale_after: "<condition, not a wish>"
  on_expiry: "reads as STALE, not as current"   # silence must not look like currency

trigger:                         # a state change somebody else makes for their own reasons
  watches: "delivery register: project.status"
  fires_on: ["release_stage_change", "intended_use_added", "scope_field_changed"]
  also_watches: "S057 one-setting-away list"   # governance class can change unattended
  actor: "<named role who receives the notification>"
  # NOT a calendar reminder. NOT 'the team will review quarterly'. NOT an intention.

re_sign_off:                     # agreeing again must be recordable
  - { by: "<name>", role: "<role>", on: "<YYYY-MM-DD>", agreed_to_version: 3 }
  - { by: "<name>", role: "<role>", on: "<YYYY-MM-DD>", agreed_to_version: 4 }

falsifiable_claims:
  - claim: "<the commitment, rewritten>"
    measurement: "<named>"
    threshold: "<stated>"
    verification_method: "<how, by whom>"
    owner: "<accountable person, not a team>"

review_cost:
  whose_time: "<roles, not hours>"
  displaces: "<what does not happen that week>"
  will_it_be_paid: "<yes / no / only when something has already gone wrong>"
  # 'no' is an acceptable and useful answer. An unaffordable review is not a review.
```

**Step 6 — move it to where the work lives.** A governance artefact in a document repository is filed. The same artefact in the system of record your team opens daily is held. **This is the cheapest change available and the one most likely to be refused**, because the document repository is where the compliance function looks.

**Step 7 — price one review honestly.** How long, whose time, what it displaces — then say whether it will be paid. **A review that costs more than anyone will pay is a review that does not exist.** The corpus behind this stage demonstrates the general case elsewhere: its wellness guidance is advisory inside a system of checklists, and **advisory guidance inside a system of checklists is guidance that loses.**

**Step 8 — attack the instrumentation you just built.** One prompt: *given this trigger definition, describe three changes to the system that would make this assessment wrong and would not fire it.* Then a second: *argue that this expiry condition will never be evaluated by anybody, and say what would have to exist for it to be.* Keep what beats your reasoning; record what you rejected. **An agent that told you the instrumentation looked thorough was not used as an instrument.**

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That a working governance tool has been forced to publish its scan scope, its cadence and its latency, and that your assessment has none of the three and nobody noticed. You will also observe the thing the vendor documentation makes unavoidable and a form cannot: **that the estate your assessment describes changes without you**, because a permissions error nobody had read for years becomes a live leak the day a retrieval system is switched on.

## What good looks like

- **The falsifiable claim has all four fields.** *The system will be monitored for bias* has none of them and is not a claim.
- **The trigger is a state change somebody else makes.** A release, a scope change, a new intended use entering a register. **Not a calendar reminder and not an intention.**
- **The version field is used**, not present. There is more than one entry by the time you submit.
- **The resolution is stated — scope, cadence, latency — and so is what falls outside all three.**
- **The document lives where the work lives.** If your team never opens the place it is stored, it is filed rather than held.
- **The review's price is stated in other people's time**, because that is what actually determines whether it happens.
- **You have said whether it will be paid**, honestly, including if the answer is no.

## How it goes wrong

- **The one-shot assessment** — written to clear a gate, never reopened, describing a system that stopped existing. *Warning sign:* it was completed in one sitting.
- **The calendar trigger** — refresh annually, with nobody owning the calendar. *Warning sign:* your trigger has a date in it and no actor.
- **The unversioned document** — it changed, and nothing records what. *Warning sign:* the revision history has one row.
- **The unfalsifiable claim** — a commitment nothing could disturb. *Warning sign:* you cannot say what observation would embarrass it.
- **The unstated resolution** — the assessment reads as though it covered everything. *Warning sign:* you cannot say what it did not look at.
- **The silent expiry** — the document goes stale and continues to read as current. *Warning sign:* nothing about the artefact changes when the expiry condition is met.
- **The unpriced ritual** — you designed a review nobody can afford, so it will not run. *Warning sign:* your review requires a meeting.
- **The wrong home** — correctly filed, permanently unread. *Warning sign:* you had to look up where it goes.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The assessment and its instrumentation are carried into [S059](/wiki/seminars/S059.md), where a determination is added to it, and into [S060](/wiki/seminars/S060.md), where the whole thing is defended on the work it produced.

**1 · Rewrite three commitments.** Give each the four fields — named measurement, stated threshold, verification method, accountable owner.

- *The system will be monitored for bias.* · *Users will be informed that they are interacting with an AI system.* · *Sensitive data will not be exposed.*

> **Model answer shape.** The third is the instructive one, because the honest rewrite exposes that nobody knows what it covers: *a named scan reports items shared beyond their intended audience, across a named scope, run on a named trigger, with a named owner who acts on the result — and the scan does not cover the storage service, the archived sites, or anything above the item ceiling.* **The exclusions are part of the claim.** A commitment that does not say what it excludes is claiming complete coverage by omission, which is the commonest false statement in a governance document.

**2 · Extract an instrument's resolution.** Using the workflow above, record from the documentation what the data risk assessment scans, how often it runs, how late its results are, and what it does not cover. Note which version of the page you read.

> **What you should find.** That it samples the busiest sites rather than the estate; that item-level scanning is capped at a small number of sites and excludes one storage service entirely; that there is an item ceiling per location above which counts may be inaccurate; that results are delayed and that custom results expire. **And that the page carries a notice saying it is the classic version and has been replaced.** Record all of it, including the version notice. **No detection rate and no false-positive rate is published anywhere in this documentation — assert none** ([research/06-microsoft-platform/purview-dspm-for-ai-and-defender-ai-spm-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/purview-dspm-for-ai-and-defender-ai-spm-docs.md)). The workload-side material was read through search excerpts rather than a full fetch, so re-verify before relying on its structure.

**3 · Go back to your own item 2, then break it.** Find what you wrote for opening item 2 — what in your process would cause somebody to reopen the assessment. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then write a real trigger and name the register it watches.

> **Criterion.** A trigger passes if it names **a state change that somebody else makes for their own reasons**, a register in which that state lives, and an actor who receives the result. **It fails if it depends on a person remembering** — *we will review quarterly*, *the team will revisit at each milestone*, *I will check when the scope changes* are all intentions, and an intention is not a control. The shape to copy is the one the same corpus already uses for environment withdrawal: **marking a project closed in an authoritative register is what starts the decommissioning**, and nobody has to remember anything.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Date your own criteria.** Take the harm register in your assessment and mark each entry with when it was written — before you had seen the system produce anything, or after.

> **What to notice.** How many entries you could not have written at the time the assessment was due. **That is criteria drift and it is structural rather than a discipline failure**: users need criteria to grade outputs, and grading outputs is how they discover the criteria ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/shankar-evalgen.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/shankar-evalgen.md)). Some criteria are dependent on the outputs observed and cannot be specified in advance at all, which the authors say raises serious questions for any approach assuming evaluation is independent of observing outputs. **An impact assessment is exactly such an approach.** Note the source's limits: the evidence is qualitative, it establishes that the phenomenon is real and consequential and **does not quantify how often or how much**, and it leaves unresolved the cost this exercise inherits — criteria revised after seeing outputs cannot be cleanly held out against the same outputs.

**5 · Ask somebody else the only question that matters.** Give your assessment to a person who has not read it and ask: **when was this last true?**

> **Scoring criterion.** A pass is when they answer **from the artefact** — not from you, not from a wiki page, not from a memory of a meeting. If they cannot, your instrumentation failed whatever you built. Then ask the second question: **what would have to happen for you to open this again?** If the answer is *somebody would ask me to*, your trigger is a person. **Note that this is an assessment-of-the-document exercise and not an assessment of your understanding**, and it is deliberately so: the artefact has to work when you are not there to explain it.

**6 · Set an expiry and defend it.** Write the condition under which your assessment stops being current, and say what visibly changes when it fires.

> **What to notice.** That your instinct is to resist, because an expiry looks like an admission that the work was provisional. **The assessment literature this programme's own architecture rests on already concedes the point**: an entrustment description may carry an expiry date, because an activity not practised for a long time makes the entrustment decision lapse ([research/08-assessment-epas/ten-cate-epa-framework.md](/research/08-assessment-epas/ten-cate-epa-framework.md)). **A discipline that admits its judgements decay is stronger than one that does not**, and most academic assessment schemes never admit it. That framework is a clinical model, is guidance from the concept's originator, and **reports adoption rather than effectiveness** — so borrow the expiry idea and not any claim that it works.

## What this does not establish

**Nothing here shows that reviewing an assessment changes a system.** The regime this session is derived from asserts the living-document requirement in several places and **publishes no evidence that any assessment was ever revised, or that any revision altered anything.**

**The instrumentation you build is a design argument, not a fix with a warrant.** A version field does not cause anybody to look. An expiry does not cause anybody to act. **They make staleness visible, which is a precondition for acting and not a substitute for it**, and the honest expectation is that most assessments will still be written once.

**The per-sprint re-screen deserves a specific note**, because it is the strongest idea in the regime and the weakest in practice. Screening in the working cadence is where a governance question can still change a build, which is exactly where a governance question should be. **It is also the one thing in the entire collection with no artefact, no checkbox and nowhere to record that it happened.** This session asks you to give it a place to be recorded. Whether anyone will use it is untested and this programme should not pretend otherwise.

**The platform anchor is a worked instrument, not a validated one.** The documentation describes capabilities and states operational limits, which is unusually useful, and it publishes **no detection rates, no false-positive rates, no measurement of how much oversharing exists in a typical estate, and no evaluation of the controls' effectiveness.** **Assert none of those.** The data-side page consulted is explicitly the classic version and has been superseded by one that was not read, so some described behaviour may already be historical; the workload-side material was read through search excerpts rather than a full fetch, so its structure is reconstructed. Agent-level capabilities there are mid-licence-migration. **It is single-vendor: the concepts transfer, the mechanisms and portal paths do not.**

**The oversharing amplification argument is the strongest idea in that documentation and it is an argument.** That capable retrieval converts dormant permissions errors into live ones is a mechanism, stated without measurement. **No source available to this programme reports how much of it happens, how often, or what it costs.**

**Criteria drift is established qualitatively.** The paper demonstrates that the phenomenon is real and consequential; it **does not quantify its prevalence or magnitude**, its user study is small by construction, and it leaves unresolved a cost this session inherits — criteria revised after seeing outputs cannot cleanly be held out against those same outputs.

**The expiry precedent comes from a clinical model.** Medicine has patients, legally responsible supervisors and a licensure endpoint; an engagement has none of those and has to construct the stakes. The framework is guidance from the concept's originator and an advocate, and **it reports adoption rather than effectiveness.**

**The one claim this session makes without hedging is definitional.** A requirement with no version field, no re-sign-off row and no place to record that the review happened is not a requirement. That is true of the regime's form, of your team's template, and of whatever replaces both.
