## The situation

Your design has one part in it that nobody has done before. You can feel which part. You cannot yet say what would settle it.

The team's instinct is to start on that part first, because it is the risky one. That instinct is correct about the ordering and wrong about the activity, and the difference between the two is a page of writing done beforehand.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught.

1. Name the one thing in your design that might not work. Now write the question whose answer would tell you.
2. What would you do differently on Monday if the answer were no?
3. You spend a week finding out and the answer is yes. What happens to the code you wrote?
4. Somebody asks what you recommend. You have run the investigation. Why might the honest answer be that recommending is not your job here?
5. A colleague generated a working version of the risky part in an afternoon. Is that a spike?

**Item 3 is the one the session cares about.** Almost everybody says the code is kept. It is the answer the tooling makes obvious, it is the answer that costs nothing to give, and it is the answer that converts an investigation into an unreviewed foundation.

## What you must produce

A **spike charter**, written before the investigation and short enough to be read aloud. It has six parts and the order is fixed.

- **The question**, stated so that it has an answer rather than a discussion.
- **The decision waiting on it**, and who takes that decision.
- **The bound** — a declared limit on the investigation, with the rule for stopping early in either direction.
- **What evidence would count**, named before you have any.
- **The disposal** — where the code will live and what happens to it when the charter closes.
- **What this spike will not decide.**

## The material

### A spike is an experiment, and an experiment has three parts

The word arrived from agile practice meaning a rough exploratory piece of work, and that meaning is too loose to be useful. **What this session teaches is narrower: a spike is an experiment, and an experiment has a question, a method and a stopping rule.**

Remove any one of the three and you get a recognisable failure. An investigation with no question is a piece of work — it will produce something, and what it produces will be judged on whether it looks impressive rather than on whether it settled anything. An investigation with no stopping rule ends when the person doing it feels finished, which is a fact about that person's stamina. An investigation whose method was chosen after the result is a description of what happened.

The measurement literature makes the same point in a setting where it has been worked out properly. An evaluation is an experiment, and the discipline that separates a real one from a token one is **doing the analysis before the run** — which is what converts *we tested and found nothing* into a statement about the thing rather than about the test ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md)). For a spike the analogue is exact and is the hardest line in the charter: **state, before you begin, what outcome would count as a no.** That source is analytical rather than empirical, its illustrative magnitudes rest on stylised assumptions, it addresses comparing scores rather than choosing what to score, and no number from it appears here.

### The published protocol, and the two rules that constrain what a spike may be used for

The openly published engineering playbook this programme draws on has a page on engineering feasibility spikes that is the most operationally prescriptive thing in its design section, and it is a complete protocol rather than a suggestion ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md), pages read 2026-07-30).

The spike **opens with a pre-mortem** framed on one question — **"Imagine the project has failed. What problems and challenges caused this failure?"** — and then runs **recurring share-out calls with the whole crew attending**, so that findings do not stay with the person who found them. The source attaches specific durations to both. **None of those numbers is reproduced here**, for the reason [M07](/wiki/modules/M07.md) gives: a duration lifted out of one organisation's cadence and printed in a curriculum stops being that organisation's choice and becomes a norm nobody validated. What transfers is the shape — the pre-mortem is fixed-length and short, the share-out recurs and is shorter. [S032](/wiki/seminars/S032.md) is the pre-mortem, taught in full.

The spike **closes in a fixed shape** — goal, method, evidence, conclusions, next steps — and the shape is load-bearing rather than decorative. Separating evidence from conclusions is what lets a reader reject your conclusion and still use your evidence. [S035](/wiki/seminars/S035.md) assesses exactly that separation.

And two standing rules constrain what the output may be used for. The first is a warning about the code: **"the code written for the spikes is not going to serve as the code for the final solution".** The second, from the separate technical-spike page, is sharper and less often honoured: **"The goal of a spike should be fact-finding, not decision-making or recommendation."**

**Those two rules are the whole of this session's substance, and both are stated as prescription with no comparative evidence behind them** — true of that publication throughout. Its pages carry footer dates spread across several years, roughly a fifth of the site is stubs or link lists, it contradicts itself in at least a dozen places without noticing, and it asserts four unsupported magnitude claims that this repository records as specimens and reproduces nowhere. Cite it by page and by the date that page was read, never as a whole.

### Why the code is discarded, and what the rule is actually protecting

Learners read the disposal rule as hygiene — throwaway code is scrappy, scrappy code should not ship, therefore delete it. That reading is comfortable and it is wrong, because it makes the rule negotiable by anybody whose spike code happens to be tidy.

**The real reason is authorisation.** A spike is authorised by a charter, and a charter authorises an investigation. A system is authorised by a design, and a design has been reviewed, costed, argued about and connected to a decision somebody took. When spike code survives, **the authorisation survives with it** — and a piece of the system now rests on a document that was never meant to license building anything. Nobody decided to build that. It arrived.

Everything else follows from that and none of it is the point on its own. The code was not reviewed as production code. No tests were written against a specification, because there was no specification. It was written to answer a question rather than to be operated, so it has no failure behaviour anybody chose. It embodies whichever shortcut made the question answerable fastest, and those shortcuts are invisible three months later.

The second half of the rule is the practical half. **It survives because it is enforced by where the code lives, not by anybody's resolve at the end of a hard week.** A spike kept on a branch named for its disposal, never merged, is discarded by default. A spike kept in the working tree with an intention to remove it is retained by default, and the default is what happens.

### The discarded spike is error-management training, and it has a boundary condition you must check

There is a research literature that describes precisely what a deliberately disposable investigation does to the person running it, and it is a workplace-training literature rather than a school one.

Error management training "involves active exploration as well as explicit encouragement for learners to make errors during training and to learn from them", and it is distinguished by two elements: **minimal guidance**, so that participants work independently on difficult tasks, and **explicit instructions that errors are informative** ([research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md](/research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md)). A spike is that arrangement with the code thrown away at the end, which removes the last incentive to protect the artefact rather than the finding.

Two results from that meta-analysis bear directly on how you should run yours, and they point in opposite directions.

The first is that **the advantage is absent during training and present afterwards**. Studies scoring performance during the training itself found no advantage at all; the advantage appeared on separate post-training transfer tasks, and was largest for tasks structurally unlike the ones practised — what the authors define as adaptive transfer, "using one's existing knowledge base to change a learned procedure, or to generate a solution to a completely new problem". **A spike that feels productive while it runs is not evidence that it was.** The evidence is what you can do afterwards on something you had not seen.

The second is the boundary condition, and it is the one that should change your charter. Because the method withholds guidance, **"the task itself needs to provide clearly interpretable and informative feedback if EMT is to be effective"** — and where feedback cannot be readily interpreted, the method may be worse than instruction that supplies external guidance. Applied here: **a spike is only worth running on a question whose result you will be able to read.** If nothing about the run will tell you whether it went wrong, you have not built an instrument, and the honest move is to find a different instrument rather than a longer bound.

**The limits on that source are heavy and travel with every use of it.** Every one of its studies compared error management training against *another* training method and none against no training, so every result is relative rather than absolute. **No study in the pool measured on-the-job performance**; all outcomes are laboratory post-training tasks. Almost all of them taught software skills. The feedback-clarity moderator — the one this session leans on hardest — **received only limited support by the authors' own statement**, and it was the least reliably coded of the four. **No magnitude from it is asserted here or anywhere in this wiki.**

The ordering underneath this session is the same one the whole programme uses: generate first, then be taught, then be *instructed to compare* the two ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). That evidence is from school, university and laboratory settings, with no adult-professional study in its reference list.

### A running artefact is not an answer, and the tooling has made the confusion cheap

The generative instrument you will use is well described in the planning literature as **a "universal approximate knowledge source"** — broad coverage across domains, no correctness guarantee ([research/02-technical-foundations/kambhampati-llms-cant-plan.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/kambhampati-llms-cant-plan.md)). The same position paper argues that auto-regressive models cannot, by themselves, plan or self-verify, with the parenthetical that closes the obvious escape route: self-verification "is after all a form of reasoning". The proposed architecture puts generation on one side and an **external verifier** on the other, with critique flowing both ways, and neither component trusted to do the other's job.

That is exactly the division a spike charter draws, and it is why the charter has to exist before the branch. **The agent generates the candidate. The charter's declared evidence is the verifier.** The code executing is not the finding unless the charter said, in advance, that it would be. It is a position paper rather than an experiment, its central claims are contested in the subsequent literature, and it targets auto-regressive models as of early 2024.

One published property of the tooling decides how the disposal rule must be implemented. Agent mode **determines its own steps and iterates rather than stopping after a single response**, and requests confirmation before running a terminal command or a tool that is not built in — **with that confirmation being a dismissible default rather than a boundary**, auto-confirmable per session, per solution or permanently ([research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md), read 2026-07-30; the page carried two product version ranges simultaneously at that reading). **A boundary that can be auto-confirmed away is not a boundary.** Your disposal therefore has to be structural — a branch that is deleted — rather than a dialog somebody declines under time pressure. That documentation is first-party and highly perishable, and publishes no adoption data, no failure-rate data and no measure of how often the agent reports completion on work that is wrong.

### A sandbox that publishes what it cannot emulate

The bound and the exclusion are the two parts of a charter learners write worst, and for the same reason: both are normally written from imagination, which makes them a guess about the author's own stamina and confidence. There is one case where the tool writes the exclusion for you, and it is worth knowing for that reason rather than for the tooling.

The **Agents Playground** is described as "an integrated sandbox to locally test and debug AI agents and traditional bots", and its stated advantage is subtractive: it eliminates "the need for a Microsoft 365 developer tenant, tunneling services like Ngrok, and app/bot registration" ([research/06-microsoft-platform/agents-toolkit-and-sdk-family.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/agents-toolkit-and-sdk-family.md)). It runs a local web application emulating the client and the connector service against mock data, mocks the surrounding organisational context, and lets you fire an event at a handler that has never met a real one. **A spike whose setup cost is near zero is a spike you will actually run**, and most of the investigations that never happen are killed by a tenant, a tunnel and a registration rather than by the question turning out to be uninteresting.

What makes it teaching material rather than tooling is the next section of its own documentation. **It publishes a table of what it cannot emulate.** Manifest-enabled features are unavailable "as Agents Playground doesn't process it". Only Adaptive Cards are supported among card types, with typeahead search, user mention, stage view and full width excluded by name. Mobile and meeting experiences are unsupported. Typing indicators, tabs, message extensions, dialogs, single sign-on and non-Adaptive cards are all listed as unavailable, set against basic messaging, mocked connector responses and mocked events, which are available.

**Read that table as the source of your charter's sixth part.** *What this spike will not decide* is normally written from imagination, which is why it is normally thin and unfalsifiable. Here it can be written from a published list, and a reader can check it against the same list.

Two further details in the same documentation matter more than the table, because they are the class of thing a sandbox usually hides.

**The convenient loop is the less faithful one, silently.** Run standalone, the sandbox's default channel is `msteams`. Launched through the toolkit, the default channel is `emulator` — and `emulator` "does not support some Teams-specific mock activities", specifically installation updates, channel and team conversation updates, and notification activities. **So the path with fewer steps emulates less, by default, and says nothing.** A default is a decision somebody else made, and this one changes what your evidence is evidence of.

**The mock configuration requires exactly five users** in its `users` section; configurations with fewer or more are stated to be unsupported, and **no rationale is published for the constant.** If your question concerns how behaviour changes with the number of participants, that is a question this instrument cannot be pointed at, and the sixth part of the charter should say so rather than working around it.

There is also a headless driver exposing a test client that runs conversations against the endpoint with each case isolated in its own conversation, plus an HTTP facade so a non-Node runner can post a scenario of turns and read back per-turn statuses. **It drives conversations and asserts on them. It scores nothing, and nothing else in this toolchain does either** — the seam between *my manifest is valid* and *my agent is any good* is unbridged and the documentation does not acknowledge it.

**The limits on that note travel with every sentence above.** It is first-party product documentation whose authority is definitional rather than evidential: **the vendor may define what the sandbox is, and has published nothing establishing that using it produces a better or faster outcome than not.** Roughly thirty of the pages behind it were read only through search-returned excerpts, so several of its lists are subsets rather than complete gates. The most operationally useful material about this toolchain ships inside a versioned editor extension with no public URL and no currency guarantee, and its diagnostic claims are reported rather than verified. Two package names for the same sandbox appear on live pages simultaneously. And the ergonomics vocabulary throughout — *streamlines*, *simplifies*, *rapid* — is marketing register with **no measurement anywhere behind it.**

**The silence that matters most is the one this session needs.** There is a *capability* table and there is **no fidelity claim.** Nothing published states how often an agent that passes locally behaves differently in a real tenant, and the `emulator` default makes that question sharper rather than softer. **The table therefore bounds what you tested and licenses nothing about what will happen in production**, and a charter treating a green sandbox run as a finding about the deployed system has quietly substituted a capability list for evidence it does not have.

### Is a grounded answer feasible at all, and can you tell from the citations

The other spike now available to this session is the cheapest useful one in the whole retrieval strand, and its artefact is genuinely disposable.

Attach an existing search index to a prompt agent, ask real questions, and **read the inline citations rather than the answers.** A prompt agent is declarative and consumes no agent compute, and the search tool connects an existing index directly so that the model answers with inline citations ([research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-agent-build-and-runtime.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-agent-build-and-runtime.md)). **What survives is a finding about the corpus** — whether the passage a real user needs is reachable at all — and the agent is discarded, which is precisely the disposal this session is about. The answers are the part you throw away; the citations are the part you keep.

**One operational constraint decides whether the spike can be run at all**, and it is the class of thing that eats a day when it is met at the keyboard rather than in the charter: **on a private virtual network the search connection must authenticate with a managed identity, because key-based authentication is not supported.** Establish that before the bound starts, not inside it.

**And one caution about what a citation proves.** Where the retrieval layer is a managed one, permission enforcement is opt-in per source and off by default: the vendor's own frequently-asked-questions page states that document-level access controls "aren't automatically honored" unless the knowledge source's documentation explicitly says they are supported **and** somebody configured the synchronisation ([research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-iq-intelligence-layer.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-iq-intelligence-layer.md)). **A spike that returns the right passage has not established that the right person may see it** — and the system looks like it works either way, which is what makes this belong in the charter's sixth part rather than in a later incident.

**The limits on both notes are heavy and are why no figure appears above.** Both are first-party documentation read on one day, definitional rather than empirical, with substantial portions read through search-returned excerpts rather than full page fetches. The agent-runtime note records that **four first-party surfaces disagreed about the agent definition file on the day it was read** — one of them formally deprecated, all of them rendering — which is the strongest available argument for pinning a citation to a host, a version and a date. The intelligence-layer note records that **no indexing latency or freshness bound is published for any layer**, that **nobody has published evidence that a managed knowledge layer outperforms a competently built plain retrieval implementation**, and that its own headline quality figure compares the product against itself at a lower setting rather than against the thing the documentation says it beat. **No figure from either note appears anywhere in this wiki.**

### The contradiction you are handed rather than given resolved

The internal practice corpus this programme draws on contributes one prohibition and then breaks it. It states, as a headline takeaway, that the tooling is to be used to make each step better rather than to skip steps. Its own customer-facing material's proudest anecdote is a prototype generated on the first day from a reusable starting point, with no discovery activity described at all. **Read against the method the same organisation publishes, that is step-skipping presented as acceleration, and neither document notices** ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md)).

It also supplies the best single instruction in the corpus for this stage: **a cheap prototype that fails immediately is to be treated as an instant win**, with stakeholders actively coached into reading it that way rather than as a setback. That is a coaching instruction and not a finding, and it is offered here as one.

**This session hands you the contradiction rather than resolving it**, because a spike and a day-one prototype are genuinely hard to tell apart from the outside. They can have the same author, the same tooling, the same elapsed time and the same running artefact. **The difference is entirely in the charter, and that is the argument for writing one.** The corpus is access-controlled and is paraphrased here and never quoted; it records no declined engagement, no failure narrative that is not synthetic, no cost side of any kind, and not one external citation anywhere in roughly ninety documents.

## The workflow

**Run one spike in GitHub Copilot agent mode on a branch whose name says it is going to be deleted, and end the session with the branch gone and the finding written.**

**Step 1 — write the charter before you open an editor, and commit it where the spike cannot reach it.** One page, six parts, on your main branch or in your design directory. **The charter is not on the spike branch.** If it is, it disappears with the disposal, and the charter is the only part of a spike that is supposed to survive.

**Step 2 — create the branch so that its name carries the disposal.** Something of the form `spike/<charter-id>-discard-<date>`. This is the cheapest structural control available and it does the work that resolve will not do at the end of a hard week. The date is the bound from your charter, written where a reviewer will see it.

**Step 3 — write an instructions file that makes the boundary a property of the repository rather than of your attention.** Create `.github/instructions/spike.instructions.md` with an `applyTo` glob covering the spike directory only:

```markdown
---
applyTo: "spikes/**"
---

# Spike conduct

- This is a fact-finding investigation. Do NOT produce a recommendation,
  a preferred option, or a sentence beginning "we should".
- Every claim about the spike's question MUST name the observation that
  supports it, in the same sentence as the claim.
- If the run does not settle the question, say "the question is not settled
  by this run" rather than reporting what the code does instead.
- Do NOT edit, create or move any file outside `spikes/`.
- Do NOT add this work to build files, CI configuration or dependency
  manifests. The code on this branch is going to be deleted.
- Record findings in `spikes/<charter-id>/FINDING.md` under the headings
  Goal, Method, Evidence, Conclusions, Next steps. Evidence and
  Conclusions are separate sections and must not be merged.
```

**Step 4 — verify the instructions actually arrived.** Send one prompt, then inspect the Copilot Chat output channel and confirm the instruction text appears in the request payload. **An instructions file you have not observed in a payload is a file, not a specification** — [S001](/wiki/seminars/S001.md)'s discipline, and it costs one prompt.

**Step 5 — run the investigation and watch where the confirmation goes.** Agent mode determines its own steps and iterates, and asks before running a terminal command or a tool that is not built in. Note what that dialog offers you: it can be auto-confirmed for the session, for the solution, or permanently. **Record which you chose.** You have just been shown, in the tool, why the disposal rule cannot rest on a prompt.

**Step 6 — write evidence into the finding as you go, not into code comments.** A comment in code that is about to be deleted is a finding you have arranged to lose. The separation the file enforces — Evidence, then Conclusions — is the one [S035](/wiki/seminars/S035.md) marks.

**Step 7 — attack your own output, and be specific about what you are attacking.** Do not ask the agent to review the spike. Give it your charter's question and your `FINDING.md`, and ask it for **the inputs your run did not cover, and the sentence in your evidence section that is actually a conclusion.** Then ask it to argue that the run establishes nothing about the charter's question. Keep whatever is better than your own reasoning; record what you rejected and why. **An agent that endorsed your charter was not used as an instrument**, which is [S001](/wiki/seminars/S001.md)'s claim arriving in a technical setting for the first time.

**Step 8 — dispose, and record the disposal as an act with a date.** Delete the branch. Write the line in the charter. If you kept anything, say what, say who reviewed it as production code, and say under whose authority — **retention reported honestly is a pass and retention omitted is the failure**, because the rule is enforced by disclosure.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That a running artefact and an answered question are two different objects, and that on this tooling the first is now much cheaper than the second. You will finish the session holding a written finding and no code, and the discomfort of deleting something that worked is the lesson. **You will also have watched a safety confirmation offer to remove itself permanently**, which is the clearest available demonstration that a boundary implemented as a dialog is a boundary implemented as a preference.

**The second spike, and why its setup cost is where you should have started.**

The run above needed a branch, an editor and an agent. The one below needs none of them, and that is the whole of its argument: **most spikes that never happen are killed by setup rather than by the question.** Run it after the first this term, and before it next term.

**Step A — pick the question the tenant was stopping you asking.** Something about behaviour on an event you cannot easily produce on demand — an installation, a member joining, a channel renamed, a team removed. Write it into a second charter with all six parts. **The sixth part is the one this run can fill honestly, and it is the only place in the programme where you can.**

**Step B — scaffold and start the sandbox with nothing attached, then set the channel deliberately.** No tenant, no tunnel, no registration. Do not accept whichever default your launch route hands you.

```yaml
# .m365agentsplayground.yml — the MOCK DATA file.
# Not to be confused with m365agents.playground.yml, which controls how the
# sandbox STARTS. Two files, near-identical names, different jobs. Read both.

# The channel is a decision, not a default. Launched through the toolkit the
# default is `emulator`, which omits installation updates, conversation updates
# and notification activities. Run standalone the default is `msteams`.
# Whichever you choose, write it in the charter beside the finding — a result
# obtained on one channel is a result about that channel.
channel: msteams

# The users section requires EXACTLY five entries. Fewer or more is documented
# as unsupported and no rationale is published for the constant. If your
# question is about how behaviour changes with the number of participants,
# this instrument cannot answer it. Say so in the charter's sixth part rather
# than inventing a workaround, because the workaround becomes the finding.
users:
  - { name: "", role: "" }
  - { name: "", role: "" }
  - { name: "", role: "" }
  - { name: "", role: "" }
  - { name: "", role: "" }
```

**Step C — write the charter's sixth part from the published limitations table, before you run anything.** Copy what the sandbox states it cannot emulate into *what this spike will not decide*, in the sandbox's own words, with the date you read the table. **You are borrowing somebody else's honesty**, and this is the only exclusion clause in the programme that a reader can check against a source rather than against your judgement.

**Step D — fire the activity your question needs, then fire one the menu does not offer.** The predefined triggers cover installations, member and channel and team additions, removals and renames; anything else you send as a raw payload yourself. **Record which of the two you had to use.** A question that needed a hand-written payload is a question about a surface the tool models loosely, and that belongs in the finding.

**Step E — attack the bound, not the output.** Give the agent your published-limitations section and your finding, and ask it for **the sentence in your finding that assumes fidelity the table does not license** — the place where you wrote *the agent handles X* when what you observed was *the sandbox's mock of X did not fail.* Then ask it to argue that the run is evidence about an emulator and not about a product. Keep whatever beats your own reasoning; record what you rejected and why. **An agent that endorsed your exclusion clause was not used as an instrument.**

**Step F — dispose, and notice what you are disposing of.** Delete whatever you scaffolded, on the same rule as before. **What you keep is a sentence about the question and a paragraph naming what the sandbox could not have shown you** — and the second half exists only because somebody else published their own limits. Nothing in the toolchain scored anything at any point, and your finding should say so.

## What good looks like

- **The question admits an answer you would not like.** If every outcome leaves the design unchanged, you have not written a spike, you have written a task.
- **The decision is somebody's**, by role, and they know it is waiting.
- **The bound has a stop rule and not only a target.** A limit you would blow through for a good reason is a target.
- **Evidence was specified before it existed.** A charter that names the evidence afterwards is a description of what you happened to find.
- **The disposal is stated as an act with a date**, not as an intention.
- **The disposal is structural.** You can name where it is enforced — a branch, a directory the instructions file forbids leaving, a review that would catch the merge — rather than only where it is intended.
- **The exclusion is real.** At least one thing a reader would expect this spike to settle is named as out of scope, with the reason.

## How it goes wrong

- **The spike that becomes the product** — the code works, the deadline arrives, and the investigation ships. *Warning sign:* your charter has no disposal line, or the disposal line has no date.
- **The recommendation in the goal** — the charter's question is *which should we choose*, so the investigation is a decision wearing an investigation's costume. *Warning sign:* your question contains the word *best*.
- **The unbounded enquiry** — the spike ends when the person doing it feels finished. *Warning sign:* your bound is expressed as a feeling about sufficiency.
- **The uncontested question** — the answer changes nothing, because the decision was already taken. *Warning sign:* nobody can tell you what happens on Monday if the answer is no.
- **The generated foundation** — an agent produced something that runs, and its running is treated as the finding. *Warning sign:* your evidence is that the code executed.
- **The unreadable question** — nothing in the run would tell you it had gone wrong, so the bound is spent producing an artefact rather than an answer. *Warning sign:* you cannot say what a failure would look like.
- **The day-one prototype** — a reusable starting point produced something impressive before anybody established what should be built. *Warning sign:* you have an artefact and no charter.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The charter and its finding are assessed at [S035](/wiki/seminars/S035.md), which is the module's assessment occasion.

**1 · Turn three goals into questions.** Each of these is a real spike goal as teams write them. Rewrite each so that it has an answer format and an outcome that would count as a no.

- *Investigate whether the retrieval layer is viable.*
- *Explore the best approach for document ingestion.*
- *Prototype the integration with the customer's case system.*

> **Model answers.** *Does a query answered from the customer's own document set return the passage a case handler needs, on twenty cases we did not choose — yes or no, per case?* · *Can a single ingestion run process one month of the real corpus without manual intervention, and what fraction of documents does it reject — a count, and the rejection reasons?* · *Can we read one case record end to end through the published interface within the access we already hold — yes, no, or blocked, with the blocker named?* **The test is whether somebody who was not there could tell, from the answer alone, whether the spike succeeded.** If the answer is a paragraph, the question is still a goal.

**2 · Find the boundary that removes itself.** Run workflow steps 4 and 5. Record what the confirmation dialog offered, which option you took, and where in your setup the disposal rule is actually enforced.

> **What you should find.** That the confirmation is a dismissible default rather than a boundary, and that it can be auto-confirmed per session, per solution or permanently. **The finding is not that this is wrong** — it is a reasonable ergonomic choice for a tool used continuously. The finding is that your disposal cannot be built on it. If you cannot name a structural enforcement in your own setup — a branch name, a directory the instructions file forbids leaving, a review that would catch the merge — then your disposal rule is an intention.

**3 · Go back to your own item 3, then break it.** Find what you wrote for opening item 3 — what happens to the code when the answer is yes. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then write your disposal line properly, as an act with a date, and name the person who would notice if it did not happen.

> **Criterion.** A disposal line passes if it names a location, an act and a date, and if a reader can say what would have to go wrong for the code to survive anyway. **"We will not use the spike code in production" fails** — it is a statement of intent with no mechanism and no observer. **"The branch `spike/S031-ingest-discard-2026-09-04` is deleted on the fourth; the finding is on main; anything retained goes through the same review as any other change to the ingestion component, named in the finding" passes.** Note what the passing version does: it makes retention *possible* and *visible*, rather than forbidden and invisible.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Sort five investigations by whether the task answers back.** For each, say whether the run itself would tell you it had gone wrong, or whether you would need somebody else to tell you.

- *Whether an ingestion pipeline can parse the customer's document formats.* · *Whether a generated summary is acceptable to a case handler.* · *Whether the integration authenticates within the access we hold.* · *Whether retrieval finds the right passage on cases we did not choose.* · *Whether the latency is tolerable in the room where the work happens.*

> **What you should find.** Parsing and authentication answer back — they fail loudly and locally, and the run is the instrument. Acceptability and tolerability do not; nothing in the run tells you, and a spike on those questions is an unguided investigation of something you cannot read, which is where the training literature's boundary condition says the method is least effective ([research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md](/research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md), **whose feedback-clarity result received only limited support by its authors' own statement**). Retrieval is the interesting one: it answers back **only if you fixed the correct passages before you looked.** That is not a property of the task; it is a property of your charter.

**5 · State what your run could not have detected.** For your own spike, write the difference the investigation would have shown and the difference it would have missed.

> **Scoring criterion.** The answer passes if it distinguishes *the run showed no problem* from *the run could not have shown this problem*. **An investigation that could not have produced a no has not investigated anything**, and nothing in the finished document reveals that ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md), **analytical rather than empirical; no number from it is used here**). If you cannot answer, put the sentence in the finding. **An admitted limit is a finding; an unadmitted one is a defect.**

**6 · Tell a spike from a day-one prototype.** Take the corpus's flagship anecdote as described in the material — a working prototype produced on the first day from a reusable starting point, with no discovery activity described — and write the charter that would have made it a spike.

> **What to notice.** You will find that most of the six parts can be written retrospectively and one cannot. The question, the bound, the evidence and the exclusion can all be reconstructed plausibly after the fact. **The decision waiting on it cannot**, because if no decision was waiting, none can be invented later without naming a person who was not in fact waiting. **That is why the charter is written first**, and it is the only part of this session that a tidy retrospective cannot fake.

**7 · Write the sixth part twice.** Take one spike you have already chartered. Write *what this spike will not decide* from your own judgement, seal it, then run workflow step C and write it again from the sandbox's published limitations table. Put the two side by side and write one sentence naming the difference.

> **What you should find.** That the judgement version names things you already suspected — usually scale, usually performance — and the published version names things you had not thought about, because you had no reason to: cards that render only in one family with several features excluded by name, mobile and meeting surfaces absent entirely, single sign-on unavailable, manifest-declared features not processed at all. **The finding is not that the second list is longer.** It is that the first list was generated by the same person who chose the question, and therefore inherits their blind spots, while the second was generated by somebody with no stake in your charter. **A limit published by the party who built the instrument is worth more than a limit inferred by the party using it**, which is the only reason this session is willing to build an exclusion clause on a vendor's table — and it is still a *capability* table, so write the last line yourself: **nothing published says how often something that passes here behaves differently in a tenant.**

**8 · Find the default that emulates less.** Run the sandbox twice on the same agent and the same question, once on each of the two default channels, without changing anything else. Record every behavioural difference, then reconcile your list against the published limitations table.

> **What you should find.** That the launch route you would naturally take — the one with fewer steps — sets the channel that omits installation updates, conversation updates and notification activities, and that nothing in the interface tells you this has happened. **Sort your differences into two piles: those the table predicted, and those it did not.** The first pile is why you read the table. **The second pile is the finding**, because it is a difference between two mocks of the same thing, neither of which is the thing, and it is the closest this session can get to showing you that a sandbox's fidelity is unmeasured rather than high. If the second pile is empty, say so — a null result here is a real observation about the two channels, and it is more useful than an invented one.

## What this does not establish

Nothing here shows that spikes improve outcomes.

The playbook that supplies this protocol is a working standard published openly, and it offers **no comparative evidence** that its practices outperform alternatives — a limit that applies to the whole document and is recorded in this repository's complete reading of it. Its pages carry footer dates spread across several years, roughly a fifth of the site is stubs or link lists, **it contradicts itself in at least a dozen places without noticing**, and it asserts four unsupported magnitude claims which this repository records as specimens and reproduces nowhere. The protocol is prescriptive practice, taught as practice, cited by page and by the date that page was read.

The internal practice corpus supplies no support either. It records **no failed engagement**, so it cannot supply a case in which a spike averted one, and its single failure narrative is synthetic and fictional by its own admission. It contains **no cost side anywhere**, so it cannot say what any investigation was worth. It carries **not one external citation in roughly ninety documents.** And it contains the contradiction this session hands over: a stated prohibition on using the tooling to skip steps, alongside a flagship anecdote in which a prototype is generated on day one from a template with no discovery described. **Nothing in the corpus reconciles them, and this session does not either.** It is access-controlled, paraphrased here and never quoted, and no figure from it appears anywhere in this repository.

**The disposal rule is a prohibition with no measurement behind it.** Nobody has shown that discarded spike code produces better systems than retained spike code. It is held here because retained spike code enters production carrying an authorisation that was written for an investigation, and that is an argument rather than a finding.

**The error-management warrant is about learning, not about delivery.** Every study in that meta-analysis compared one training method against another rather than against no training, so every result is relative; **no study in it measured on-the-job performance**; almost all taught software skills; and the feedback-clarity boundary condition this session leans on **received only limited support by the authors' own statement**, with the weakest coding reliability of the four moderators tested. **No magnitude from it is asserted here.** The generation-before-instruction ordering rests on school, university and laboratory evidence with no adult-professional study in its reference list.

**The platform observations are first-party and perishable.** The documentation describing agent mode's iteration and its confirmation behaviour is vendor documentation read on 2026-07-30, and the page carried two product version ranges simultaneously at that reading. It publishes no measure of how often the agent's self-correction loop converges, or how often it reports completion on work that is wrong — which is exactly the property step 7 of the workflow depends on.

The claim that a generative model is a source of candidates rather than of correctness comes from a **position paper**, not an experiment; its central claims have been contested in the subsequent literature and it targets auto-regressive models as of early 2024.

**And the sandbox establishes nothing about the system you will ship.** The published limitations are a **capability** table — what the emulator can and cannot imitate — and **no fidelity claim exists anywhere in the material.** Nothing states how often an agent that passes locally behaves differently in a real tenant, nothing states what proportion of production failure modes the sandbox can reach, and the default channel difference between the two launch routes makes that gap wider rather than narrower without announcing itself. **A charter built on this table is bounded honestly and is still bounded by somebody else's account of their own product.** The five-user mock constant is published with no rationale, no roadmap and no workaround. The most operationally useful material about this toolchain is not on the documentation site at all but inside a versioned editor extension, and its diagnostic claims are reported rather than verified. **And nothing in the toolchain evaluates anything** — the driver runs conversations and asserts on them, no page consulted acknowledges the absence of a scoring surface, and no vendor guidance exists on where to go next. The grounded-answer spike inherits its own silence: **no indexing latency or freshness bound is published for any managed knowledge layer, and no evidence has been published by anyone that such a layer beats a plain retrieval implementation.**
