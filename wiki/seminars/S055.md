## The situation

This is the module's assessment occasion and it marks something no previous occasion has.

**It marks what the plan refuses to claim.**

You have a model, a bound registry, an exclusion register, a mapping and a provenance trace. Assemble them into a security plan and defend it in front of an assessor who has read your [S051](/wiki/seminars/S051.md) model and nothing else.

**Understand this before you begin, because it changes what you should submit.** A plan in which every threat is unmitigated is a passing plan. If your closure criterion is evidence discoverable in the repository, and the evidence is not there yet, then the honest status is unmitigated — and **a false claim of implementation is strictly worse than a failure to notice a real one.** You are not marked on how much is done. You are marked on whether what you say is checkable.

Answer these in writing before you begin, and submit them.

1. Which claim in your plan is a reader most likely to take as established, and is it?
2. What in this plan would you have deleted before sending it to a customer?
3. Where in your process must a person agree before anything proceeds — and does that point exist?
4. Name one real fix your closure criterion would fail to detect.
5. Your plan will be read by somebody who does not want to read it. Which page do they read?

**Item 2 is the assessment's centre of gravity.** Every honest plan contains something its author would rather not send: an empty column, a long exclusion register, a threat nobody has an answer to. **The willingness to name your own is the professional disposition this module exists to build**, and item 4 is where you demonstrate that you understand your own instrument.

## What you must produce

A **security plan**, assembled from the module's work.

1. **Overview and system model**, with the diagram and the attribute table from [S051](/wiki/seminars/S051.md).
2. **A secrets inventory** — for each secret: purpose, location, how it is generated, how it is rotated and what that rotation costs in downtime, how it is distributed, and how long it lives. **The correct number of secrets in an architecture is zero, and every one still present is a defect awaiting a design change** — say so for each.
3. **The threat summary**, with recommendations interleaved against the entries rather than gathered at the back.
4. **The exclusion register** from [S052](/wiki/seminars/S052.md), grouped by category.
5. **The mapping and its three-way accounting** from [S053](/wiki/seminars/S053.md), with every control naming a surface, an owner and a verification method.
6. **The authorisation trace and the revocation-window statement** from [S054](/wiki/seminars/S054.md).
7. **The unmitigated list**, complete, and **one named enforcement point** where a control is enforced rather than intended.
8. **Closure criteria**, each expressed as evidence somebody could find in the repository — a file and a location — and each paired with **one real fix it would fail to detect.**
9. **Consolidated open questions.**
10. **An adversarial-input entry**, or a defended statement that your system reads no untrusted content.
11. **The approval point** — where a person must agree, what they are agreeing to, and what they are looking at when they do.
12. **A provenance mark on every unmitigated entry** — *sourced*, where the gap is a coverage boundary the vendor states in its own documentation, with the citation; or *inferred*, where it is your reasoning. **Both are legitimate. Not knowing which you wrote is not.**

## The material

### An honest threat model is unmitigated when it is first written

The openly published engineering handbook carries a worked threat model of an edge system — architecture diagram, asset table, dataflow narrative, an enumerated threat list, and a properties table mapping principle to threat to mitigation. **Every threat in it is marked "Not Mitigated"** ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md), pages read 2026-07-30).

That is not a defect in the example. **It is what an honest threat model looks like at the point it is first written**, and the note on that handbook draws the teaching consequence directly: **do not penalise a team for marking threats not mitigated — an honest unmitigated list is worth more than a fabricated mitigated one.** The example's other instructive habit is that its assumptions are stated separately from its threats, which is [S051](/wiki/seminars/S051.md)'s side-channel discipline arrived at independently.

**Its limits are severe and are used here as material rather than hidden.** The handbook cannot be dated as a whole; its page footers span years with nothing in the navigation to distinguish them. **It contradicts itself in at least a dozen places without noticing.** Its own threat-modelling page is a signpost rather than a treatment. The worked example's defects are clerical — duplicated row numbers, key artefacts held as images rather than text. And it **reaches for a magnitude in at least four places and warrants none of them; those four are recorded in this repository as specimens and reproduced nowhere.**

**Now the honest part about this occasion's own rule.** Requiring the unmitigated list to survive review is **a design choice with no external warrant.** No source read for this programme measures whether plans with intact unmitigated lists lead to more fixed vulnerabilities, better decisions or anything else. The argument is that a plan whose risk list is empty at sign-off has been edited to look finished, and that an artefact edited to look finished is no longer an instrument. **That is an argument. It is not a finding, and this occasion marks against it anyway** — which is a position this programme is obliged to state rather than assume.

### Why an unmitigated list survives only where naming an unfixed risk is safe

The rule above asks a learner to write down, in a document that goes to a customer, the things they have not fixed. **That is not primarily a technical act.** It is speaking up when silence is cheaper, which is exactly the behaviour the psychological-safety literature studies.

Edmondson's account opens on a neonatal nurse who notices a missing prophylactic order, steps forward, and stops — because she had recently seen the physician **"publicly berate another nurse for questioning one of his orders"** ([research/04-professional-formation/edmondson-psychological-safety.md](/research/04-professional-formation/edmondson-psychological-safety.md)). Her reading of the moment is the operative one: a quick, **"not entirely conscious, risk calculation"** in which the immediate interpersonal cost is overweighted against a delayed and more important consequence. She generalises it plainly: **"No one wakes up in the morning excited to go to work and look ignorant, incompetent, or disruptive."**

The consequence for this occasion is sharper than it first looks. **If naming an unfixed risk is conditional on safety, then requiring it of an individual is treating an environmental variable as a personal virtue.** A graduate who has learned to keep an unmitigated list intact, and who joins a team where an empty risk register is what gets approved, has been given a capability they cannot exercise. **This occasion does not solve that.** It names it, and it marks the derivation of the statuses rather than the courage of the author.

The quantitative synthesis is the reason this is treated as established rather than as folklore. It aggregates a large body of independent samples and does four things beyond pooling: it **ranks antecedents by relative strength**, so an intervention designer knows which lever matters; it tests **incremental validity** — whether the construct explains anything over adjacent ones such as good leader relations and work engagement, which is the sceptic's question and the right one; it tests **moderation by research design and national culture**; and it tests **homology**, whether the construct behaves equivalently at individual and group level ([research/04-professional-formation/frazier-psychological-safety-meta-analysis.md](/research/04-professional-formation/frazier-psychological-safety-meta-analysis.md)).

**Both sources carry limits that must travel with them.** The book is trade press: its central case studies are drawn almost entirely from secondary sources — published teaching cases, journalism, official investigation reports, a documentary — rather than from original fieldwork, and its prescriptive third part is a leadership framework rather than a finding. **The 1999 empirical article that defined the construct has not been read for this repository**; the book's summary of it is a summary written two decades later by the same author and is recorded as such. **The meta-analysis note is written from the official abstract; the full text is closed access and was not read.** So the *structure* of its contribution is verified and **no effect size from it is asserted here or anywhere in this wiki.**

### A control verified once is not a verified control

The closure criteria in your plan will each rest on an observation. Somebody ran a check, or read a configuration, or watched a request be rejected. **That is one sample, and the statistics of evaluation are unforgiving about what one sample licenses** ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md)).

That paper's diagnosis of prevailing practice is that results are reported with a **"highest number is best" mentality**, with a state-of-the-art figure highlighted in bold and no test of whether it is distinguishable from noise. Its corrective is to treat an evaluation as an experiment, and three of its moves transfer directly to a security plan.

**A measurement without a stated uncertainty is a number pretending to be a fact.** Your closure evidence has an uncertainty even when it is binary, because the conditions under which you observed it are a sample of the conditions under which it will operate.

**Non-independent observations make you more confident than you are entitled to be.** When items are drawn in related groups — the paper's examples are several questions about one passage, or one question translated into many languages — independence is violated and the naive calculation understates the true uncertainty. **Your controls are drawn in related groups too.** Five endpoints configured by one script, verified together, are close to one observation wearing five hats.

**And power analysis reframes the whole enterprise from reporting to planning.** Given what you want to detect, the arithmetic tells you in advance whether your instrument can detect it at all — which lets you decide before you start whether running the check can possibly answer the question you care about. **A verification procedure incapable in principle of detecting the failure it exists to detect is a ceremony**, and it is cheaper to discover that before you build it.

**Its limits:** an analytical paper rather than an empirical one, aimed at machine-learning evaluation rather than at security controls. Its recommendations are derived, its illustrative magnitudes are computed from stylised assumptions and labelled as such, and **the transfer to control verification made here is this programme's analogy rather than the author's claim.** **No figure from it appears anywhere in this wiki.**

### Closure re-derived from evidence, and its three blind spots

The best property of the pipeline this module rests on is the one your plan borrows ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-agentic-threat-modelling.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-agentic-threat-modelling.md)). **A threat's status is not something a person ticks. It is re-derived from the workspace.** A status stage searches source, pipeline definitions, container files, infrastructure definitions, configuration and documentation for evidence that each mitigation exists, and **a claim of "implemented" must cite a file and a location.** Re-run it after a fix and the status flips by itself.

**Evidence in the repository is the closure criterion.** That is the right shape and most organisations do not have it. The governing instruction is the one to carry into your own work: **when in doubt, classify as unclear, because a false claim of implementation is worse than a false negative.**

**Three blind spots belong with it and your plan must name one.** Nothing schedules the re-run, so a status is as current as the last time somebody remembered. **A fix that is real but invisible in the repository — a portal setting, a tenant policy, an agreement with a team — will never close and will regenerate as a work item indefinitely.** And the acceptance criteria a human might close a ticket against were written by the same model that wrote the mitigation.

The same source supplies this occasion's hardest finding, and it is an absence. **There is no approval gate anywhere in fourteen agent definitions.** Human decisions exist as *inputs* — which control frameworks apply, whether recommendations are in scope, where the backlog write goes. One machine gate exists and it checks a machine-written field. **There are placeholders shaped like sign-off** — a reviewer row with blanks for reviewer, date and disposition — and it is a form to fill in rather than a precondition for anything. **The only place a person is unavoidably present is authenticating the write to a live backlog, and that person is being asked for a credential, not for a judgement.**

**That source is internal and access-controlled: paraphrased here, quoted nowhere, attributable to nothing, supplying no external warrant, and reporting no outcome evidence of any kind. No figure from it is reproduced anywhere in this repository.**

### A named enforcement point, and what it can and cannot carry

Your plan needs at least one place where a control is *enforced* rather than *intended*, and the gateway is the clearest available example of the difference ([research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md)).

The documentation's framing is worth taking whole: the AI gateway **"extends API Management's existing API gateway"** and **"is not a separate offering."** The intellectual claim inside that sentence is that **AI traffic is API traffic with different resource semantics, not a new category of thing** — which is why every multi-tenant concern that was invisible in your prototype becomes visible here.

Five mechanisms matter for a plan.

**Token limits with a counter key.** A limit enforced over a period, keyed on **"any counter key, such as subscription key, originating IP address, or an arbitrary key defined through a policy expression."** The general lesson is the one to write down: **a shared finite resource with no per-consumer accounting will be consumed by whichever consumer is least careful.** The implementation detail worth teaching is **prompt-token precalculation at the gateway**, so that a request already over the limit never reaches the backend — a fail-fast design that rejects at the edge rather than paying to discover the rejection downstream.

**Managed identity.** Authentication to services **"so you don't need API keys for authentication"** — **the elimination of a long-lived secret rather than its better storage.** That distinction is the one your secrets inventory turns on.

**Content safety as a gateway policy.** Placing moderation here **guarantees uniform enforcement across every consumer and centralises a decision some applications might legitimately want to make differently.** Both halves are true and a plan that records only *moderation applied* has erased the trade-off.

**A circuit breaker with dynamic trip duration**, taking its recovery timing from the backend's own `Retry-After` header rather than from a guessed interval — a better design because it lets the failing service state its own recovery time.

**Token metrics with dimensions.** **Dimensions are what make a metric answerable**: total consumption tells you the bill, consumption per interface per user tells you who to talk to. And logging is described as supporting **"billing and auditing"**, which is the point at which observability stops being an operational nicety and becomes a governance requirement.

**Two cautions the documentation does not supply and your plan must.** Semantic caching reuses prior completions by **"comparing the vector proximity of the prompt to prior requests"**, and **the similarity threshold is a correctness knob**: set it loosely and the system returns an answer to a question that was not asked, silently and plausibly. And **logging prompts and completions creates a privacy surface the documentation does not discuss at all.** If your plan enables it, the plan owns that decision.

**Its limits:** first-party product documentation with a commercial frame. **It argues for a gateway and does not discuss the latency, cost or operational burden the gateway itself adds. No benchmarks, no latency figures, no cost measurements, no comparison against other products.** Capability availability **"varies by API Management service tier"**, so every claim about what is available is conditional on a tier you must confirm. Policy names, tier availability and preview status change frequently. **Its architectural reasoning is sound and asserted rather than demonstrated.**

### An unmitigated list you can source, rather than one you can only assert

The hardest part of this artefact is not deciding to keep the list. It is filling it with things a reader cannot dismiss.

An unmitigated list assembled from a practitioner's judgement invites one response, and it is the response you will get: *is that actually a gap, or are you being cautious?* **A list assembled from the vendor's own statements of what its controls do not cover invites a different conversation entirely** — not whether the gap is real, but whether you accept it. That is the conversation your plan exists to make possible, and it is why this occasion now asks you to mark each entry as sourced or inferred.

The agent control-plane documentation is unusually candid about its own boundaries, and reading it for those rather than for its capabilities is a skill worth more than the product ([research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-365-and-agent-identity.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-365-and-agent-identity.md)). What follows is not a list to copy. **It is a demonstration that such a list can be built from documentation, so that yours has to be.**

**Flows that are watched and not defended.** Audit covers agent-to-human, human-to-agent, agent-to-tool and agent-to-agent. Loss prevention covers the first two. **Agent-to-tool and agent-to-agent are visible and unenforced, and both facts are published.**

**A control whose subject is not told it fired.** The agent is not informed when a data movement is blocked; the documentation places the burden on the owner to monitor the policy and understand its effect on subsequent workflows. **The agent proceeds from a step that did not happen.**

**Output that carries none of its input's classification.** Newly created content does not inherit sensitivity labels from source items, and is therefore neither automatically labelled nor encrypted — the finding [S054](/wiki/seminars/S054.md) traced in full.

**Stages nobody scans.** An agent's guardrail fully overrides its model's, so any stage the agent's guardrail does not name is unscanned regardless of what the model beneath it is configured to do ([research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-agent-build-and-runtime.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-agent-build-and-runtime.md)). **For agents there is also no annotate-only mode**, so the plan cannot propose observing before enforcing.

**Access control that is honoured only where somebody configured it.** Document-level access controls are not automatically honoured unless a knowledge source's own documentation states out-of-the-box support, and synchronisation is additionally required ([research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-iq-intelligence-layer.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-iq-intelligence-layer.md)).

**Runtime protection with a named boundary.** Real-time protection evaluates tool invocations through a sanctioned tool layer, and **agents relying on unsupported tools, or not integrating with that layer, are not covered.** The documentation says so plainly.

**Discovery that is a list of names.** Unregistered agents are found by detecting a published set of named desktop and developer applications, of which a single one can be blocked, and only on centrally managed devices. **Anything not on the list is not found**, and nothing states what proportion of a real estate that leaves.

**An inventory with no completeness measure.** No coverage or completeness measure is published for the registry, so **nobody — including the vendor — can state what fraction of the agents actually present in a tenant it discovers.** The unmanaged-agent count is defined without any statement of how confidently unmanaged agents are found.

**And the trade that will fool a team that is doing everything right.** A default rule audits without stopping anything; a custom rule blocks. **When a blocking rule covers an agent, near-real-time alerts are no longer generated for that agent** — the events become queryable telemetry rather than alerts. Turn on enforcement and the alert queue for that agent goes quiet. **A team watching its queue will observe silence and may reasonably read it as success.** No page states the reasoning behind the trade. **This belongs in your plan as an unmitigated entry with a monitoring consequence attached, and it is the single best example in the module of a gap that is created by using the control correctly.**

One more, from the control plane rather than the agent plane, and it is a mechanism rather than a gap ([research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-control-plane-and-governance.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-control-plane-and-governance.md)). A compliance policy scoped over a subscription or resource group, with **named exceptions for the deployments that genuinely cannot comply**, publishes a **violations list** that refreshes without anybody remembering to look. **An exceptions list and a violations list are an unmitigated list generated mechanically**, which is the strongest available demonstration that this occasion's central discipline is implementable rather than merely admirable. Use it as the pattern; do not mistake it for coverage, because it prevents nothing and nothing measures how far behind reality its scan runs.

**Read all three sources for coverage boundaries and for nothing else.** First-party and definitional throughout. The control plane had been generally available for a short time only when read, with substantial parts preview or early-access, roughly thirty supporting pages read as search excerpts rather than fetched in full, and a dated migration falling inside this material's life; agent guardrails and much of the managed knowledge layer are preview. **None of them publishes a detection rate, a false-positive rate, a bypass rate or any coverage measurement for anything named here — assert none, because none exists.** There is **no incident-reduction evidence, no availability target and no service-level commitment anywhere in the material**, and the strongest empirical claim in it is a customer testimonial in a marketing announcement. **A gap you can cite is not the same as an efficacy you can claim, and this occasion assesses only the first.**

### What this occasion is actually assessing, and why it is a conversation

One last piece of the design, so that you know what you are walking into.

The assessment frame is entrustment. An entrustable professional activity is a unit of practice a supervisor could hand over whole, and the mechanism for probing whether a learner holds it is the **entrustment-based discussion** — a short focused conversation after the activity that **"should probe the student's deep understanding of what was done, but should include 'what-if' questions"** ([research/08-assessment-epas/ten-cate-epa-framework.md](/research/08-assessment-epas/ten-cate-epa-framework.md)). The what-if requirement is what separates it from a walkthrough. **Retrospective explanation shows you understood the case you met; counterfactual questioning is the only available evidence about the cases you did not meet** — and the frame is explicit that an entrustment decision implies accepting risk, because nobody can predict what a learner will encounter.

That is why the defence asks what your closure criterion would fail to detect rather than what it detects.

The second half of the frame explains why you get a written reason rather than a mark. Studies of what assessors actually do when reading a portfolio found that **they relied mainly on narrative feedback, and that "grades and qualifications were merely used to confirm impressions based on narratives"** ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/govaerts-narrative-assessment.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/govaerts-narrative-assessment.md)). The same work found something less comfortable: assessors reaching **similar final judgements by different reasoning**, and being **"inclined to stick to their initial judgments even when confronted with seemingly disconfirming evidence."** The design consequence they draw is that high-stakes decisions **should not be made individually but should result from group discussion**, because deliberation surfaces the divergent reasoning that identical scores conceal.

**Their limits:** medical-education research. The entrustment primer is a primer, its supervision scale and evidence standards are built for clinical training, and **its transfer to engineering is this programme's extrapolation rather than the author's claim.** The narrative-assessment note rests on two open-access studies substituted for several paywalled ones that were not consulted, and the substitution is recorded deliberately. **Both are used here for the shape of the instrument and for no magnitude.**

## The workflow

**Derive every status from evidence, then instrument one enforcement point, then try to have your own plan scored well.**

**Step 1 — derive the statuses before you write any prose.** Go through every mitigation and try to find the evidence: a file, a location, a definition, a configuration. **If you cannot find it, the status is unmitigated or unclear**, and that is the correct answer rather than an admission. Do this first, because prose written first will bend the statuses to fit it.

**Step 2 — write the deletions you would have made, and then do not make them.** One page: what you would have removed before sending this to a customer. **That page is the most valuable thing in the plan and it is the one that disappears before circulation.**

**Step 3 — name one enforcement point and make it real.** Pick one control from your [S053](/wiki/seminars/S053.md) inventory and place it at the gateway, where a request either passes or does not. Then write the policy fragment into the plan **beside its unmitigated neighbours**, so that a reader can see one enforced control and three unenforced ones on the same page:

```xml
<!-- security/plan/enforcement/gateway.policy.xml
     SHAPE, NOT SCHEMA. Policy names, attributes and tier availability change
     frequently; confirm every element against the docs on the day you use it,
     and confirm your API Management tier supports it.                        -->
<inbound>
  <!-- CTL-11  ENFORCED. Verification: send a request over the limit; observe
       rejection AT THE GATEWAY, before the backend is called.                -->
  <llm-token-limit counter-key="@(context.Subscription.Id)"
                   tokens-per-minute="..."
                   estimate-prompt-tokens="true"
                   remaining-tokens-variable-name="remainingTokens" />

  <!-- Dimensions are what make a metric answerable. Totals tell you the bill;
       per-consumer tells you who to talk to.                                 -->
  <llm-emit-token-metric namespace="llm-metrics">
    <dimension name="API ID"  value="@(context.Api.Id)" />
    <dimension name="User ID" value="@(context.Request.Headers
                                       .GetValueOrDefault("x-user-id","N/A"))" />
  </llm-emit-token-metric>
</inbound>

<!-- UNMITIGATED, and staying that way in this plan:
     THR-07 wallet attack from an unauthenticated path that does not traverse
            this gateway. No enforcement point exists on that path today.
     THR-12 semantic cache returns an answer to a question that was not asked.
            Threshold is a correctness knob; we have not chosen one and the
            failure is silent.
     THR-19 prompt and completion logging creates a privacy surface. The
            documentation does not discuss it. We own this decision and have
            not made it.                                                       -->
```

**Step 4 — verify the enforced one, once, and then say what one verification is worth.** Send a request that should be rejected and watch it be rejected at the edge. **Then write the sentence that matters: this was one observation, under one configuration, on one day.** A control verified once is not a verified control, and your plan should say which of its controls have been verified more than once and which have not.

**Step 5 — name each closure criterion's blind spot.** For each, the real fix it would miss: a setting applied in a console, a policy applied above your system, an agreement with a team. **Evidence discoverable in the repository is the right closure criterion and it is blind to every fix that leaves no trace there.** A criterion whose author cannot name its blind spot has not understood it.

**Step 6 — find the approval point, or record its absence.** Where in your process must a person agree before anything proceeds? **In the material this module derives from there is no such point anywhere.** If your process has the same shape, say so. **Naming the absence is worth more than inventing a gate.**

**Step 7 — write the adversarial-input entry.** Your process reads untrusted content and writes files. State the threat class and the control class in category terms only. **The source contains neither, in a pipeline whose entire subject is threat modelling**, and noticing that is the most useful thing in it.

**Step 8 — attack your own plan by trying to score it well.** Give the assembled plan to an agent and ask it to argue that this plan deserves a high score on a coverage rubric. **If it can, your plan is satisfiable by naming things** — and you have found out cheaply what a rubric would have told you expensively. Then a second prompt: *identify every claim in this plan that would fail if the reader asked for the file and the line.* Record what you kept and what you rejected.

**Step 9 — build the sourced half of the unmitigated list before you write the inferred half.** Go to the documentation for every control your plan credits and look for the sentences that say what it does not cover. **They are there, they are usually in a capability table rather than in a warning, and they are the most reliable content on the page.** One entry per boundary, each with the citation and the date you read it.

**Step 10 — mark every entry, and decide each one on the record.** Sourced or inferred; accepted or not; and if accepted, by whom and against what.

```yaml
# security/plan/unmitigated.yaml   <- SHAPE, NOT SCHEMA. Every boundary below is
# dated. Re-read the page before this plan is circulated, not before it is written.
- id: UNM-03
  gap: "Agent-to-agent and agent-to-tool movements are audited and not preventable
         by the loss-prevention control credited at CTL-06."
  provenance: sourced          # sourced | inferred
  citation: "vendor capability table, read <date>"
  accepted_by: "named person, not a team alias"
  monitoring_consequence: "Detection here is retrospective. Say so in the runbook."

- id: UNM-07
  gap: "Enabling the blocking rule stops near-real-time alerts being generated for
         the covered agent. Events remain queryable; the queue goes quiet."
  provenance: sourced
  citation: "vendor runtime-protection page, read <date>"
  accepted_by: "named person"
  monitoring_consequence: >
    A quiet queue after enforcement is a design consequence, NOT evidence of
    safety. Whoever watches the queue must be told this in writing.
  # This gap is created by using the control correctly. It cannot be closed by
  # configuring anything better.

- id: UNM-11
  gap: "No coverage or completeness measure is published for the agent inventory.
         The estate figure in this plan is a lower bound of unknown tightness."
  provenance: sourced
  accepted_by: "UNACCEPTED - nobody has agreed to this yet"
  # An unaccepted entry is a legitimate entry. Do not close it to look finished.
```

**Step 11 — attack the sourcing, not the plan.** One prompt, with the unmitigated list attached and the rest of the plan withheld: *for each entry marked sourced, state what the cited documentation actually says and whether it supports the gap as written; for each entry marked inferred, argue that it is speculation.* Keep what beats your reasoning; record what you rejected. **An agent given the whole plan will assess the plan. An agent given only the gaps has to check whether you can support them** — which is the question the assessor will ask, and the one a reader who is not on your side will ask louder.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** The distance between a control that is enforced and a control that is intended, seen on one page, in one document, in your own handwriting. You will also observe that the enforced one is harder to write, easier to verify, and shorter than the three unenforced entries beside it.

## What good looks like

These are the assessed elements. Each is checked as present and adequate, or not.

- **Every threat names a component and a flow number.**
- **The exclusion register is present and grouped into categories**, not listed as incidents.
- **No mitigation is generic.** An entry that would fit any system is a failed element, and it fails whether or not something is written in the cell.
- **Every control names a surface, a living owner and a verification method**, and the three-way accounting reconciles.
- **Observed and inferred values are distinguishable where the reader meets them**, not only in your working files.
- **The authorisation trace names every step**, including those where nothing is compared, and **the revocation window is stated as measured or unmeasured and never as zero.**
- **The unmitigated list is complete and has not been shortened.** Every item whose implementation cannot be found in the repository is marked unmitigated or unclear. **A plan in which nothing is mitigated satisfies this element.**
- **One control is enforced at a named point**, with the enforcement written into the plan and its single verification described as single.
- **Closure criteria are evidence, not assertion** — a file and a location — and each names one real fix it would fail to detect.
- **The plan names the point at which a person must agree**, what they are agreeing to, and what is in front of them. **If no such point exists in your process, the plan says so and says what would have to change**, which is a passing answer.
- **An adversarial-input threat and a control class for it appear**, or the plan defends the claim that no untrusted content enters the system.
- **Every entry on the unmitigated list is marked sourced or inferred**, and every sourced entry names what it is sourced to. **Neither mark scores better than the other**; an unmarked entry fails the element, and a sourced mark whose citation does not support the gap as written fails it too.

**Nothing else is assessed.** Whether the system is secure is not marked. Whether anything has been implemented is not marked. The number of threats is not marked, and a longer registry is not a better one. **The length of the unmitigated list is not marked in either direction.** No score is required, none is requested, and if you supply one it is not read. Presentation is not marked. Whether your exclusions were the right exclusions is not marked. Whether your smallest change from [S054](/wiki/seminars/S054.md) would have worked is not marked. Whether you measured a revocation window, as opposed to whether you stated honestly that you did not, is not marked.

## How it goes wrong

- **The tidy plan** — the exclusion register is short, the open questions resolved, the unmitigated list trimmed. *Warning sign:* the plan reads better than the fortnight went.
- **The optimistic status** — items marked mitigated on the strength of an intention. *Warning sign:* you cannot name the file.
- **The technology name as a control** — a product is named and the entry is treated as addressed. *Warning sign:* you have not deployed it.
- **The single verification read as assurance** — a control was checked once and the plan implies it holds generally. *Warning sign:* the word *verified* appears with no conditions beside it.
- **The scored plan** — you attach a number because it makes the document feel finished. *Warning sign:* you would not write *this measures the document, not the system* underneath it.
- **The invented gate** — you name an approval point your process does not have, because the element asks for one. *Warning sign:* nobody would notice if the approver did not turn up.
- **The absent adversary** — a plan about threat modelling that never considers content attacking the process that reads it. *Warning sign:* your system reads a repository and you did not mention it.
- **The buried refusal** — the honest parts are all in appendices. *Warning sign:* your answer to opening item 5 is not where the unmitigated list lives.

## Exercises

**These are the assessment.** Everything below is submitted, and exercise 7 is the oral defence.

**1 · Derive every status from evidence, before writing any prose.** Run workflow step 1.

> **Scoring criterion.** Each status is *mitigated* with a file and a location, or *unmitigated*, or *unclear*. **When in doubt, unclear** — the governing instruction from the source design is that a false claim of implementation is worse than a false negative, and this occasion applies it strictly. **A plan in which every status is unmitigated passes this element.** A plan with one status marked mitigated and no file behind it fails it, and fails it whatever else is in the document.

**2 · Name each closure criterion's blind spot.** Run workflow step 5.

> **Model answer.** *This criterion searches the repository, so it will never detect a control applied as a tenant policy above our subscription, a setting somebody made in a console, or an agreement with the platform team that exists only in a meeting.* **Any of those is a pass.** A fail says the criterion is *comprehensive* or lists only theoretical gaps. **The point of this exercise is that evidence-in-the-repository is the right closure criterion and is blind to a whole class of real fixes, and a criterion whose author cannot name that class has not understood their own instrument.**

**3 · Enforce one control, verify it once, then say what one verification is worth.** Run workflow steps 3 and 4.

> **Scoring criterion.** A pass shows the enforcement in the plan, describes the single verification, and states its conditions — one configuration, one day, one path. **A pass also notices that several of your controls were configured together and verified together, which makes them close to one observation rather than several**; the statistics of evaluation are explicit that observations drawn in related groups violate independence and that a naive reading then makes you more confident than you are entitled to be ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md)). **That paper is analytical rather than empirical, is about model evaluation rather than security controls, and the transfer is this programme's analogy; no figure from it is used.**

**4 · Go back to your own item 2, then break it.** Find what you wrote before you began — what in this plan you would have deleted before sending it to a customer. **Do not delete it, and do not rewrite the answer yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then place the item properly in the plan, in the section where a reader will actually meet it.

> **Criterion.** A passing answer names a *specific artefact or entry* and says what makes it uncomfortable — a long exclusion register, an unmitigated threat with no owner, a column that is empty because nobody knows, an approval point that does not exist. It fails if it names a presentational worry (*it is too long*, *the formatting is rough*) or if it names nothing, because **a plan whose author would delete nothing has either been written for a reader who will not check or has already been edited.**
>
> **The deeper reason this item is asked before instruction and not after.** Writing down an unfixed risk in a document that goes to a customer is speaking up when silence is cheaper. Edmondson's account of the nurse who notices a missing order and stops describes a quick and **"not entirely conscious, risk calculation"** in which an immediate interpersonal cost is overweighted against a delayed and more important one ([research/04-professional-formation/edmondson-psychological-safety.md](/research/04-professional-formation/edmondson-psychological-safety.md)). **Asked after instruction, you would answer as somebody who has just been told the right answer. Asked first, you answered as yourself.** That book is trade press whose case studies come largely from secondary sources, and the empirical article that founded the construct has not been read for this repository.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this occasion's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**5 · Try to have your own plan scored well.** Run workflow step 8.

> **What you should find.** That it can be, and easily — because most rubrics of this kind reduce to whether some mitigation somewhere mentions a thing, and **the operative definition of a *specific* mitigation is usually that it names a concrete technology, so naming a technology you have not deployed scores full marks.** **A team that learns the rubric can raise the score without touching the system.** Record the argument the agent made for you. It is the argument somebody will one day make about your plan in a room you are not in.

**6 · Source your unmitigated list, then find the gap you cannot close by trying harder.** Run workflow steps 9 and 10. Then identify the entry on your list that exists *because* a control is being used correctly.

> **Scoring criterion.** A pass marks every entry sourced or inferred, cites each sourced entry to a stated coverage boundary with the date it was read, and identifies at least one gap that configuration cannot close. **The canonical instance is the enforcement trade: enabling a blocking rule stops near-real-time alerts being generated for the covered agent, so the alert queue for a properly protected agent goes quiet.** A candidate who records that and writes the monitoring consequence beside it has produced the most useful sentence in the plan. A fail marks nothing, which is the ordinary outcome, because an unmitigated list reads as uniform and its entries have wildly different evidential weight.
>
> **Two failing moves in opposite directions, and both are common.** One is to mark an entry *sourced* on the strength of a general impression that the vendor admits something — the citation must support the gap **as written**, not the neighbourhood of it. The other is to omit a real gap because no documentation states it, which converts the sourcing requirement into a filter on truth rather than a mark on provenance. **Inferred is a passing mark. Unmarked is not.**

**7 · The defence.** The assessor has your [S051](/wiki/seminars/S051.md) model and nothing else.

> **What you will be asked.** Three questions and one manoeuvre. **What does this plan not claim. What would you have deleted. Where does a person decide.** Then the manoeuvre: **the assessor will offer you a way to make the plan look better** — a plausible mitigation for an unmitigated threat, a reasonable-sounding basis for marking a status as done. **Accepting it is the failure.** Declining it, and saying precisely what evidence would be needed before you accepted it, is the occasion's strongest available answer.
>
> **Expect counterfactuals rather than a walkthrough.** The assessment frame requires **"what-if" questions** rather than retrospective description, because retrospective explanation shows only that you understood the case you met, while counterfactual questioning is the only available evidence about the cases you did not ([research/08-assessment-epas/ten-cate-epa-framework.md](/research/08-assessment-epas/ten-cate-epa-framework.md)). **A medical-education primer whose supervision scale and evidence standards are built for clinical training; its transfer to engineering is this programme's extrapolation.**
>
> **You will receive a written reason and not a mark.** Studies of assessors reading portfolios found they relied mainly on narrative and used grades only to confirm impressions already formed from prose ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/govaerts-narrative-assessment.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/govaerts-narrative-assessment.md)). The same work found assessors reaching similar conclusions by different reasoning and **sticking to initial judgements even against disconfirming evidence**, which is why the reason is written down and why you may argue with it. **That note rests on two open-access studies substituted for several paywalled ones that were not read, and the substitution is recorded deliberately.**

## What this does not establish

**This occasion shows that you can produce an assurance artefact whose claims are checkable. It does not show that the system is safe, and it cannot.**

**No source behind this module reports any outcome for any plan produced this way** — no comparison against a human-authored threat model, no defect-escape record, no coverage against a known vulnerability set, no evidence that a plan of this shape has ever prevented anything. The specification therefore marks form and honesty, and marks neither completeness nor correctness, because nothing available supplies a standard for either.

**The rule that the unmitigated list must survive is a design choice with no external warrant.** Nothing measures whether plans with intact risk lists lead to better outcomes. The argument is that a plan edited to look finished has stopped being an instrument. **That is an argument, this occasion marks against it, and the honest statement of that is part of the module rather than a footnote to it.**

**The unmitigated list is not evidence of good practice.** It is evidence that your statuses were derived rather than asserted. A plan full of unmitigated items may describe a system in serious trouble or a system at the start of its work, and this occasion cannot distinguish them. **It marks the derivation and refuses to mark the state**, which is a real limitation and is the price of not marking something it has no basis to mark.

**The psychological-safety material is read at two different depths and neither is deep.** The book is trade press: its case studies come almost entirely from secondary sources, its prescriptive part is a leadership framework rather than a finding, and **the empirical article that defined the construct has not been read for this repository** — the book's summary of it is a summary written two decades later by the same author. **The meta-analysis note is written from the official abstract only; the full text is closed access.** The structure of what it contributes is verified and **no effect size from it is asserted anywhere in this wiki.**

**The statistical argument is an analogy.** The evaluation-statistics paper is analytical rather than empirical and is about model evaluation, not security controls. Its recommendations are derived, its illustrative magnitudes are computed from stylised assumptions, and **the transfer to control verification is this programme's inference. No figure from it appears here.**

**The gateway documentation argues for a gateway.** First-party with a commercial frame; **no benchmarks, no latency figures, no cost measurements, no comparison against alternatives**, and no discussion of the burden the gateway itself adds. Capability varies by service tier. **It does not discuss the privacy implications of the prompt and completion logging it recommends**, which is a control whose own risk it leaves to you.

**The published worked threat model is a genre specimen, not a standard.** Its handbook cannot be dated as a whole, contradicts itself in at least a dozen places, and asserts four magnitudes it never warrants. **None is reproduced here.**

**A sourced gap is a documented boundary and not a measured risk.** The coverage boundaries this occasion has you cite are first-party statements of what a control does not cover. **They establish the boundary and nothing beyond it.** Nothing published states how often the uncovered flows are used, how much material passes through them, how often an unscanned stage matters, or what any of it has ever cost anybody. **No detection rate, no false-positive rate, no bypass rate and no coverage measurement is published for any control named in this occasion, and none may be asserted here or anywhere in this wiki** — a prohibition these sources confirm from an independent direction rather than weaken. **There is no incident-reduction evidence, no availability target and no service-level commitment anywhere in the material**, and the strongest empirical claim in it is a customer testimonial in a marketing announcement.

**The most consequential silence is about the population.** **No registry coverage or completeness measure exists**, so no party can state what fraction of the agents actually present in a tenant the inventory finds — which means every estate figure in your plan is a lower bound of unknown tightness, and the honest entry says so. **Nothing anywhere characterises what falls outside all the routes by which an agent built elsewhere can be governed at all**, so the uncovered remainder is neither measured nor described. **A plan built on this material can say precisely what is not covered and cannot say how much is out there.** Both halves belong in the deliverable, and the second one is the sentence candidates leave out.

**The sources are perishable in a way that bears on the sourcing mark itself.** The control plane had been generally available only briefly when read, substantial parts of it are preview or early-access, a dated migration falls inside this material's life, and roughly thirty of its supporting pages were read as search excerpts rather than fetched in full. **A citation to a coverage boundary is a citation to a page on a date**, and an entry marked *sourced* against a page that has since changed is an entry whose mark has quietly become false. **That is the same defect [S054](/wiki/seminars/S054.md) taught you to name, arriving in the artefact this occasion assesses.**

**And the assessment frame is borrowed from a different profession.** The entrustment primer and the narrative-assessment studies come from medical education, where supervision, observation and the consequences of error are all differently shaped. **The transfer is this programme's and is stated as such at [WP-001](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-001.md) §8 and again here.**
