# S061 · Observability That Answers a Question

**Module:** [M13](/wiki/modules/M13.md) · **Quarter:** [Q7](/wiki/quarters/Q7.md) · **Complexity class:** 4
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** Foundry tracing under the Microsoft Foundry Control Plane, and Microsoft Agent 365's own inventory and activity tables, beside APIM AI gateway diagnostics — telemetry designed backwards from a question somebody will actually ask, with content recording off until somebody owns the decision to switch it on
**Whitepaper:** [WP-061](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-061.md)
**Retrieves from:** S023, S043, S058
**Entry state:** this-programme-only

## The situation

The system is running. There is a dashboard. Somebody stood it up in an afternoon, mostly from whatever the platform offered by default, and it has been there ever since.

Nobody has asked what question any panel on it answers.

That is not a slur on the person who built it. **A default dashboard is a reasonable first act and a terrible steady state**, and the reason it persists is that nothing ever forces the question. Telemetry is cheap to add, invisible to keep, and nobody is ever blamed for collecting too much.

Then something happens. A customer says the assistant "got worse last Tuesday". The finance lead asks which team is responsible for the spend. Somebody in security asks whether a particular document was ever surfaced to a particular person. And you discover that the estate is dense with signal and cannot answer any of the three.

**Answer these in writing before reading further.** They are not rhetorical, and item 2 is the one this session is actually about.

1. What question does each panel on your dashboard answer, and who asks it?
2. What are you collecting that nobody aggregates and nobody alerts on?
3. Where does a correlation identifier get assigned in your system, and what happens to a request that arrives before that point?
4. Of logging, metrics and tracing, which would you drop first if the bill doubled — and on what basis?
5. If your entire observability configuration were deleted tonight, how long would it take to rebuild, and from what?

Most people answer 1, 3 and 4 with something. **Item 2 is where estates fail immediately**, and the failure is invisible because unaggregated telemetry costs money quietly and produces nothing loudly. Item 5 is the second-hardest, and if your answer is a person's name rather than a repository path, you have already found something.

## What you must produce

**An observability specification, held in the repository as code.**

1. **A signal register.** Every signal the system emits, with four columns: the question it answers, the person who asks that question, the decision that changes when the answer changes, and the retention.
2. **A deletion list.** What you stopped collecting, and why — with what will now be missed stated for each entry.
3. **A review list.** What survived without an asker, with a date and a named owner, because "cheap and might be needed" is a decision rather than an exemption.
4. **The configuration itself** — dashboards, alerts and queries — in source control and deployed the way the rest of the system is deployed, so that a change to them arrives through review.
5. **A correlation statement.** Where the identifier is assigned, what propagates it, and which segment of the request path is blind.
6. **A privacy line.** What is captured from prompts and completions, who can read it, under what authority, and for how long.

## The material

### Observability is a control property, not a data volume

The production machine-learning literature imports its framing from control theory and states the governing asymmetry directly: **you can only control a system to the extent that you can observe it** ([research/02-technical-foundations/crowe-machine-learning-production-systems.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/crowe-machine-learning-production-systems.md)). That sentence is quoted often and operationalised rarely. The same source does the operationalising, and it is worth more than the slogan. **Alertability** means metrics and thresholds that make a failure unambiguous. **Actionability** means enough signal to support finding the cause.

Hold those two side by side and the definition of waste falls out. **A stream you can neither alert on nor act from is cost, not observability.** It is not neutral, it is not "might be useful later", and it is not free — it is a line on a bill, a row in a retention policy and a surface in a privacy review, all of it producing nothing.

The same source separates **functional monitoring**, which watches predictive behaviour and the distribution of what arrives, from **non-functional monitoring**, which watches failures, latency and resource use — and it notes that the second matters regardless of the first, because if the system is down it does not matter how good the model is. Two of its named sensitivities explain why observability in these systems is harder than in ordinary software. **Entanglement**: changing one thing changes everything, so a small upstream difference reappears somewhere unrelated. **Configuration**: versions, parameters and feature lists live in configuration, where a small error produces radically different behaviour that ordinary software tests will not catch.

**That source's limits are heavy and must travel with it.** It is practitioner guidance from a commercial publisher, not research: no study design, no comparison group, no measurement that its practices outperform anything. Three of its four authors worked at one large vendor, so its default toolchain is that vendor's. Some of its foundational claims rest on **anonymous practitioner testimony** that cannot be checked. And **every quantity in it is a property of a particular system after a particular training run.** No figure from it appears here.

### The three instruments are separated by what they cost to keep

The openly published engineering handbook supplies one distinction that is genuinely under-taught and that this session treats as load-bearing. **Logs, metrics and traces are separated by data volume and processing cost rather than by semantics** ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md), pages read 2026-07-30). That is not how the distinction is usually taught, and it is the more operationally honest framing: the question it answers is what each instrument costs to keep, not what each one means. The same page frames tracing as an optimisation instrument rather than an incident-response one — **"Its purpose isn't reactive, but is instead focused on optimization."**

Read opening item 4 again in that light. A question about which instrument to drop is a question about unit cost weighed against answerable questions, and almost everybody answers it on semantics instead.

The handbook also supplies four rules this session adopts outright, each quoted from a named page:

- **"any data that is collected but not aggregated or alerted on must be reviewed if it is still required."** This is the answer to opening item 2, and almost no estate passes it first time.
- **"The Correlation ID for a transaction must be assigned as early as you can."** Assigned late, every hop before it is invisible, and the invisibility does not announce itself.
- **"Personally Identifiable Information or any other customer sensitive information should never be logged"**, and **"Keep sensitive data out of URLs, since request URLs are typically logged by proxies and web servers."** The second is the one people miss, because the logging is being done by infrastructure they do not own.
- **"configuration and management of observability assets… must be managed as code."** The stated argument is reproducibility. **The better argument is review** — a dashboard in source control is a dashboard somebody else looks at before it takes effect.

Two further rules govern volume: **"Log a raised exception only once"**, and **"do not use unique strings or those with high cardinality as the value of a label."** The first stops error volume becoming a function of call depth. The second stops metrics cost becoming a function of user count.

**Its limits are severe and specific, and this session uses the source as a specimen as well as a standard.** Its observability section is the most rotted part of that site: its tracing page is short and defines neither a span nor propagation, several tools it recommends are archived or deprecated, and a standards page still quotes a roadmap years out of date. **A page that renders is not a page that is current.** The handbook also contradicts itself in at least a dozen places without noticing and asserts four unsupported magnitude claims, which this repository records as specimens and **reproduces nowhere.** Cite it by page and by that page's own footer date; the site carries no single date and one cannot be quoted.

### A trace is a grouping, and the tooling requirement is smaller than you think

**A trace is a logical grouping of log records**; for a conversational system, the trace is the conversation ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/husain-your-ai-product-needs-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/husain-your-ai-product-needs-evals.md)). The practical requirement attached to it is unglamorous and correct: **searching, filtering and reading traces are the essential features** of whatever tooling you pick, and a generic viewer is usually not enough — the argument is for a small domain-specific viewer that shows which feature and which scenario is in play and links out to the underlying record. **Its limits:** one practitioner's case study, no control condition, no measured improvement, no released dataset, and dated tooling references. Take the pattern, not the product.

The engineering-practice literature makes tracing a design requirement rather than an add-on, and states the test: **"Ideally, you should be able to trace each query's transformation step-by-step through the system. If a query fails, you should be able to pinpoint the exact step where it went wrong"** ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/huyen-ai-engineering.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/huyen-ai-engineering.md)). The same source supplies the correction most teams need on latency: **"Because latency is a distribution, the average can be misleading"**, so report percentiles, and it names *goodput* — the requests per second that satisfy the service objective — as the quantity that actually matters. It is scathing about the reflex of quoting accelerator utilisation, ending with a sentence worth pinning above the dashboard: **"A higher utilization rate means nothing if the cost and latency both increase."** **Its limits:** a commercial practitioner book, first edition; where it reports figures it is usually reporting somebody else's work, and none is reproduced here.

### Dimensions are what make a metric answerable

The gateway documentation is where the abstract becomes configuration. The AI gateway's observability capabilities are logging prompts and completions, tracking token metrics per consumer, a built-in dashboard, custom policy expressions, and cross-application quota management ([research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md)). The mechanism that matters is a policy which attaches **custom dimensions** to emitted token metrics — client address, API identifier, a user identifier lifted from a header.

**Total token consumption tells you the bill. Token consumption per API per user tells you who to talk to.** That is the session in one line, and it is a lesson about design rather than about a policy: the question *which team is responsible for the spend* is answerable only if somebody decided, before the traffic existed, that consumer identity was a dimension worth carrying. Nothing recovers it afterwards.

Two further facts from the same source belong in the specification. The documentation states that logging supports **"billing and auditing"** — which is the moment observability stops being an operational nicety and becomes a governance obligation with a retention consequence. And the documentation **does not discuss the privacy implications of logging prompts and completions at all**, despite recommending the logging. That silence is not an argument that no implications exist; it is an argument that nobody will raise them for you.

**Its limits:** first-party product documentation with a commercial frame, fetched and read in full but **perishable** in its policy names, attributes and tier boundaries; capability availability varies by service tier; the Foundry integration and the unified model API are **preview**; and it offers **no performance or cost-saving data of any kind** — the benefits it describes are plausible mechanisms, not measured outcomes.

### The gap this session used to declare is closed, and what stood beside it is not

The anchor names tracing and observability in the vendor's agent platform beside gateway diagnostics. **Until this term no note in this repository covered that tracing surface, and the session said so rather than inventing a warrant.** A note now exists ([research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-agent-build-and-runtime.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-agent-build-and-runtime.md)), and the three subsections below are what it supplies.

**Read the closure as a correction, and read what it did not close as the more important half.** What was missing was an account of what the platform emits and how to reach it. That is now available and is taught below. What is still missing — and is now confirmed by a dedicated search rather than merely not encountered — is any measure of how *complete* that emission is, and any retention period the platform owns.

**No measure of tracing completeness exists.** The documentation states what tracing captures. It states nothing about what proportion of an agent's decisions appears in a trace, whether a step can go unrecorded, what sampling does to completeness, or how a reader would detect a missing span. **A trace is presented as a record and never as a sample, and no page states the difference.** That is a stronger finding than the absence it replaced, because it is a property of the product rather than of this repository's reading list — and it lands directly on your register. **A signal whose coverage is unstated cannot carry a coverage claim, however complete it looks.**

**There is no platform-owned retention period.** There is a portal display window for out-of-the-box traces, and there is an explicit statement that retention and sampling follow your own Application Insights and Log Analytics configuration. **Those are different objects, and a display window is not a retention policy.** A team that reads *managed* as *managed for me* will go looking for a retention setting the platform does not have. The obligation is delegated to a resource somebody on your side owns, pays for and configures — which is to say the retention column in your register is not a lookup. It is a decision, and if nobody made it the answer is whatever a workspace default happens to be.

**The note's limits are the heaviest attached to any source in this programme and they travel with every sentence below.** It is **the most perishable material in this repository, and it perished visibly while being read**: four pages read in full, roughly thirty more reached only through search excerpts with tables frequently truncated, several consulted pages explicitly superseded and still rendering, and preview status pervasive and load-bearing. **It is definitional and not evidential** — an accurate account of what the platform is configured to do, and no evidence that doing so produces any outcome. **Every number in it is a dated platform parameter and none is reproduced here.**

### Writing one query across the inventory, and knowing what it cannot see

The estate-governance surface publishes named tables holding agent inventory and agent activity, queryable in the same hunting language the rest of the security estate already uses ([research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-365-and-agent-identity.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-365-and-agent-identity.md)). That matters here for one reason and it is not the syntax. **It means a question in your register can be answered by a query you wrote rather than by a panel somebody shipped** — and a query is a thing you can put in the repository, review, diff, deploy and hand over. A panel is not.

The discipline is this session's own, applied one layer up. Pick one question — *which agents invoked a tool nobody approved, in the window I care about* — and write the single query that answers it across the inventory table and the activity table. Then, in the same file, write the sentences saying what the query cannot see. The documentation supplies them: agents that emit no observability data are absent; agents that do not travel the sanctioned tool path fall outside the coverage the protection layer claims; and drafts are visible from one named build surface and from nowhere else.

**Those sentences are worth more than the query.** A join across two tables looks authoritative in a way a single panel does not, and that authority is exactly what makes an unstated coverage boundary dangerous. **The population your query returns is not the population of agents. It is the population of agents that emit.** Nothing in the result distinguishes the two, and nothing in the interface will tell you.

**The limits on that source are severe and unusually recent.** It is first-party documentation for a surface that reached general availability within months of being read, **much of it preview and some of it behind a further preview programme again**; three announced capabilities carry a stated future date and were not re-verified; the announcement page part of it rests on arrived heavily fragmented, with two capability tables reduced to headers; several central pages — including the inventory table's own schema — were **not read at all**; and **no coverage measure of any kind is published**, so nothing states what proportion of the agents actually present in a tenant the inventory finds. **No detection rate and no false-positive rate is published for anything in it, and none is asserted here or anywhere in this wiki.**

### Content recording is off, and switching it on is a decision somebody owns

This is the cleanest illustration of *collect what you need* available in the whole platform, and it is worth taking slowly.

**Server-side tracing requires no code change.** Connect an Application Insights resource to the project and the platform captures inputs, outputs, tool calls and results, retries, token consumption and time signals ([research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-agent-build-and-runtime.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-agent-build-and-runtime.md)). **Message content is not among them.** An environment variable switches content recording on, and the documentation warns in the same sentence that the content may contain personal data.

So the default gives you the shape of every run and none of the words. **That default is correct, and the interesting part is what happens when you override it.** With content recording on, the trace carries the agent's planning steps, its memory management, and its arguments to and results from every tool. **A trace is where the reasoning becomes inspectable and where the sensitive data goes, and both are true at once** — which is why redaction is a design step taken before the switch is thrown rather than a cleanup performed after.

Three further facts turn that into an entry on your privacy line rather than a note in somebody's runbook. The governance surface records that **tracing is off by default and that connecting the resource which turns it on requires a senior role on the account**; that **anyone holding a common monitoring reader role on the connected workspace can then read prompts and completions**; and that some agent shapes appear in the estate inventory and emit nothing at all ([research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-control-plane-and-governance.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-control-plane-and-governance.md)). The tracing documentation adds the access requirement from the other side: querying the telemetry needs a reader role on the connected resource, and a further role again where the underlying tables are protected.

**Put those together and the privacy line writes itself, which is the point.** Who may switch collection on is a small set. Who may read the result afterwards is a much larger set, defined by a role somebody granted for an unrelated reason on a workspace that predates your engagement. **The gap between those two sets is what the privacy line exists to name**, and no documentation will prompt you for it — the published guidance says to redact before telemetry and to treat traces as production data under the same controls and retention as logs, and stops there. **Enabling telemetry is a privacy act before it is an engineering act.**

**The governance note's limits travel with all of this.** Vendor documentation about vendor governance, **definitional and not evidential**, renaming its own roles mid-documentation, with preview features load-bearing throughout and classic and current surfaces both live and both reachable from a search. **Two interpretive thresholds are asserted in it on no stated basis at all**, and are reproduced nowhere in this wiki.

## The workflow

**Write the question column first, before you open a tool.**

**Step 1 — enumerate what is emitted, with three empty columns.** Every log stream, every metric, every trace, every dashboard panel, every alert. Columns: the question, the asker, the decision that changes. Leave them empty for now.

**Step 2 — fill the columns, and do not adjust the question to fit the signal.** That move is almost irresistible, because the signal already exists and the question does not. **A question invented to justify a panel is not a question; it is a caption.** The test is whether you can name a person and a week in which they asked it.

**Step 3 — delete.** Everything with an empty asker is a candidate. Some survives because it is cheap and might be needed; that survival is a decision, and it goes on the review list with a date and an owner. Everything else goes, and each deletion is recorded with what will now be missed.

**Step 4 — put the survivors in the repository.** Dashboards, alerts and queries as code, deployed like everything else, raised as a change and reviewed. Let somebody argue with a panel.

**Step 5 — emit the dimension that makes attribution possible.** At the gateway, attach dimensions to the token metric so that spend is attributable rather than merely visible. Verify the policy names and attributes against current documentation before you use them — the fragment below is shape, not schema.

```xml
<!-- APIM inbound policy fragment. SHAPE, NOT SCHEMA.
     Policy names, attributes and tier availability are version-bound.
     Verify against current documentation and record the date you checked. -->
<inbound>
  <base />
  <llm-emit-token-metric namespace="llm-metrics">
    <dimension name="API ID"    value="@(context.Api.Id)" />
    <dimension name="Consumer"  value="@(context.Subscription.Id)" />
    <dimension name="Client IP" value="@(context.Request.IpAddress)" />
  </llm-emit-token-metric>
</inbound>
```

**Step 6 — write the register beside it, in the repository, as the artefact of record.**

```yaml
# observability/signals.yaml   <- the specification. The dashboard is downstream of this file.
privacy:
  prompt_capture: "completions only, hashed user id, no free-text prompt bodies"
  readable_by: "on-call engineers and the named data owner"
  authority: "<agreement clause or policy reference>"
  retention: "<stated period, agreed with the customer>"
correlation:
  assigned_at: "gateway inbound, before backend selection"
  propagated_by: "x-correlation-id header, echoed by every downstream component"
  blind_segment: "the customer's own front end, before it reaches the gateway"   # KNOWN GAP

signals:
  - name: tokens_by_consumer
    question: "Which team's traffic is driving this month's spend?"
    asker: "the customer's finance lead"          # A PERSON, NOT A TEAM
    decision_changed: "whether a per-consumer quota is introduced next quarter"
    instrument: metric        # chosen on cost-to-keep, not on semantics
    review: null              # has an asker, so no review date is needed

  - name: retrieval_latency_percentiles
    question: "Are we inside the response-time floor agreed at S023?"
    asker: "the service owner"
    decision_changed: "whether the deployment is scaled or the floor renegotiated"
    instrument: metric
    review: null

  - name: verbose_component_debug
    question: null            # NO QUESTION
    asker: null               # NO ASKER
    decision_changed: null
    instrument: log
    review: { date: "2026-10-01", owner: "<named person>", note: "cheap; keep one quarter, then justify or delete" }

deleted:
  - name: per_request_embedding_dump
    reason: "no asker; high volume; duplicated by the trace"
    now_missed: "reconstructing an embedding for a single historical request"
```

**Step 7 — check the request path against the correlation statement.** Trace the identifier back to the earliest point the system controls. If requests exist before it, you have a blind segment, and it belongs in the register as a named gap rather than being quietly tolerated. It is also the first thing [S063](/wiki/seminars/S063.md) will ask you about.

**Step 8 — write one query that answers one question, and write what it cannot see in the same file.** Not a dashboard. A query, in source control, beside the register entry it serves, carrying its own coverage boundary as a comment. The boundary is the deliverable; the query is how you earn the right to state it.

```kusto
// observability/queries/unapproved-tool-use.kql
// One question, one query. SHAPE, NOT SCHEMA.
// Table names, column names and join keys are version-bound, and the
// inventory table's own schema page was not read for this repository.
// Verify against current documentation and record the date you checked.
//
// QUESTION: which agents invoked a tool nobody approved, in my window?
// ASKER:    <a named person, not a team>
// DECIDES:  whether the tool approval path is enforced or merely advisory

let lookback = <the window you chose, and stated in the register>;
AgentActivity
| where TimeGenerated > ago(lookback)     // your window. NOT a portal default.
| where ActionType == "ToolInvocation"
| join kind=leftouter AgentInventory on AgentId
| where isnull(ApprovedTools) or ToolName !in (ApprovedTools)
| project TimeGenerated, AgentId, AgentDisplayName, ToolName, Sponsor

// WHAT THIS CANNOT SEE — carried in the file, not in somebody's head:
//   1. agents that emit no observability data at all
//   2. agents that do not travel the sanctioned tool path
//   3. drafts, visible from one build surface only
//
// THEREFORE: the result is the population of agents THAT EMIT.
// It is not the estate, and no published figure says how far apart
// those two are. Report the query with these three lines or not at all.
```

**Step 9 — decide content recording deliberately, and name who may read what it produces.** Confirm the default is off. Decide whether the question in your register actually needs the words, or only the shape. If it needs the words, write the redaction rule *before* you switch it on, then enumerate every principal holding a monitoring reader role on the connected workspace — including the ones granted for an unrelated system years ago — and put that list on the privacy line. **The list is almost always longer than the person switching it on expects, and discovering that afterwards is the failure.**

```yaml
# observability/signals.yaml  <- the privacy line, extended. Same file as step 6.
privacy:
  content_recording: off            # DEFAULT. Overriding it is a decision, dated below.
  override:
    decided_by: "<name and role of the person who owns this>"
    decided_on: "<date>"
    question_it_serves: "<the register entry that cannot be answered without words>"
    redaction_rule: "<written BEFORE the switch, not after>"
  readable_by:
    - "<every principal holding a monitoring reader role on the workspace>"
    - "<enumerate them. do not write 'the on-call rota'.>"
  retention:
    owner: "<the platform does not own this. somebody on your side does.>"
    period: "<set on the workspace, not read off a portal display window>"
  coverage_caveat: "a trace is a record, not a stated sample; completeness is unpublished"
```

**Step 10 — use an agent to attack the register, not to fill it.** It is good at enumerating what is emitted and bad at deciding what matters, so give it the harder half. One prompt: *for each signal I kept, argue that its stated question is a caption invented to justify an existing panel, and name the observation that would show it.* A second, for the material added above: *for each coverage sentence I wrote beside my query, argue that it is incomplete, and name a population it still fails to exclude.* Read what came back, keep whatever is better than your own reasoning, and **record which of its objections you rejected and why.** The rejections are the interesting part, and an agent that approved your register was not used as an instrument.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That the number of signals with a real asker is far smaller than the number of signals, and that you cannot tell which is which until the column exists. **You will also observe the asymmetry that gives the session its name: adding telemetry is a minute's work, and answering a question from it is a design decision taken months earlier** — so an estate is always richer in data than in answers.

## What good looks like

- **Every retained signal names a question and an asker.** Not a category, not a team — a question somebody actually asked and a person who asked it.
- **Anything collected but not aggregated or alerted on is on a dated review list**, with a named owner and a recorded decision. This is the openly published handbook's own standing rule, and almost nothing passes it first time.
- **The deletion list is not empty, and every entry names what will now be missed.** A specification that retained everything has specified nothing.
- **Dashboards, alerts and queries are in source control and arrived through review.** A configuration that exists only in a console fails this regardless of quality.
- **The correlation identifier is assigned as early as the system can assign one**, and any segment before it is named as a blind segment rather than omitted.
- **No personal or customer-sensitive data is logged, and no sensitive value appears in a URL** — request URLs are routinely logged by proxies and servers you do not control.
- **The choice between a log, a metric and a trace is defended on what each costs to keep**, not on what each means.
- **The privacy line names what is captured, who can read it, under what authority and for how long** — and it exists because you wrote it, not because the documentation asked for it.

## How it goes wrong

- **The inherited dashboard** — panels nobody chose, kept because removing them feels like losing something. *Warning sign:* you cannot name a person who looks at it.
- **The caption** — a question written after the signal existed, in order to justify it. *Warning sign:* the question is phrased in the vocabulary of the metric rather than of the asker.
- **Collect because you can** — telemetry retained because it was easy to emit. *Warning sign:* your answer on retention is "the default".
- **The click-built alert** — configuration made in a console, invisible to review, undiffable and unattributable. *Warning sign:* nobody can tell you who changed it or when.
- **The exception logged at every layer** — the same raised exception logged on each frame of its way up. *Warning sign:* your error volume is a function of your call depth.
- **The high-cardinality label** — a unique or near-unique string used as the value of a metric label. *Warning sign:* your metrics cost tracks your user count.
- **The late correlation identifier** — assigned after the first hop, so the first hop is invisible. *Warning sign:* your traces start at the second service and nobody has noticed.
- **Tracing as an incident tool** — expecting a trace to tell you why production broke, when the source that defines it states that its purpose is not reactive. *Warning sign:* your incident procedure opens with "check the traces".
- **The undeclared privacy surface** — prompts and completions logged for billing and auditing, with nobody named as able to read them. *Warning sign:* your privacy line is empty and the documentation never prompted you for it.
- **The specification nobody can rebuild from** — a beautiful document, and the running configuration does not match it. *Warning sign:* your answer to opening item 5 is a person's name.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The specification itself is assessed at [S065](/wiki/seminars/S065.md), on whether every retained signal names a question and an asker and whether the configuration arrived through review.

**1 · Sort four signals by what each costs to keep.** For each, say which instrument it should be and why, on cost-to-keep rather than on meaning: *every request's full prompt and completion* · *count of requests per consumer per minute* · *the step-by-step path of one failing request* · *the deployment's current model version*.

> **Model answers.** The first is the most expensive thing on the list and is a privacy surface as well as a bill; if it is retained at all it is a sampled trace with an authority statement, not a log stream. The second is a metric — small, aggregable, alertable, and the one that makes spend attributable. The third is a trace, and it is worth noticing that you want it for the *failing* request, which means sampling on outcome rather than uniformly. The fourth is not telemetry at all; it is configuration, and it belongs in the record of what was deployed. **The general rule: the instrument is chosen by data volume and processing cost, and the source that states this most plainly also frames tracing as an optimisation instrument rather than an incident one.**

**2 · Find one signal with no asker and delete it.** Run workflow steps 1 to 3 on a real system. Record what you deleted and what will now be missed.

> **What you should find.** That the deletion is emotionally harder than it is technically, and that the resistance is always the same sentence — *we might want it*. **That sentence is the review list, not the specification**, and the difference between those two places is the whole session. If you genuinely cannot delete anything, that is the finding: record what prevented it, because "the retention is set at the platform level and nobody here can change it" is a real constraint and a different problem from indecision.

**3 · Go back to your own item 2, then break it.** Find what you wrote for opening item 2 — what you are collecting that nobody aggregates and nobody alerts on. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then produce the real list, from the running configuration rather than from memory.

> **Criterion.** A pass enumerates signals from the running configuration and, for each, states the aggregation or alert that does not exist. It fails if it is a category — "probably some debug logging" — because a category cannot be deleted and a signal can. The rule the criterion comes from is stated plainly in the openly published handbook: **any data collected but not aggregated or alerted on must be reviewed to see whether it is still required.** Note that the rule says *reviewed*, not *deleted*; this session's addition is the date and the named owner, and that addition is the design's, not the source's.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Emit one dimension and answer a question you could not answer yesterday.** Using the gateway policy in workflow step 5, attach a consumer dimension to an emitted token metric. Then answer: which consumer drove the largest share of tokens in the last window?

> **What you should find.** That the question was unanswerable before the dimension existed and is trivial afterwards, and that nothing about the traffic changed. **Attribution is a design property of the telemetry, not a property of the data**, and no amount of later analysis recovers a dimension that was never emitted. Record the policy names and attributes you used and the date you verified them, because they are version-bound and this session's citation of them is pinned to a single reading.

**5 · Find the blind segment.** Trace your correlation identifier to its assignment point and write down what happens to a request that arrives before it.

> **Scoring criterion.** A pass names the assignment point, names the propagation mechanism, and names the segment that is invisible — including segments outside your control, such as the customer's own front end. A fail says the identifier is assigned "at the gateway" without establishing whether anything precedes the gateway. **The stated rule is that the identifier must be assigned as early as you can**; the interesting engineering is in the word *can*, and the honest answer often names an organisational boundary rather than a technical one.

**6 · Read one page of a published standard and date it.** Open the observability section of the openly published handbook, pick one tool page, and check whether what it recommends is still maintained.

> **What to notice.** That the page renders perfectly, and that several of the tools recommended across that section are archived or deprecated. **The rot is not visible as a broken link** — almost every page on that site resolves. This is the concrete reason every citation in this programme is pinned to a page and a reading date rather than to a site, and it is why your own specification carries a review date rather than a completion date.

**7 · Hand the specification to a stranger.** Give it to somebody who did not build the system. Ask them to pick one panel and tell you the decision it changes. Then ask the harder question: show them one signal you kept and ask what would be lost if it stopped tomorrow.

> **Scoring criterion.** If the answer to the second question is "we might want it", the signal belongs on the review list rather than in the specification, and the difference between those two places is the whole session. **Note who says it** — if the sentence comes from you rather than from the stranger, the specification has not yet been separated from its author, and separating it is the point of handing it over.

**8 · Write one query, then write the sentences it cannot answer.** Take one question from your register that a single panel cannot answer, and write the query that does — across the agent inventory and the agent activity tables if you have them, across whatever two sources you do have if you do not. Then write, in the same file, the sentences naming the population the query silently excludes. Submit the comment block, not the query.

> **Scoring criterion.** A pass names at least one exclusion that is a property of the *instrument* rather than of the filter — something the query could not see whatever its where-clause said. The documented ones are the model answer: agents emitting no observability data are simply absent; agents off the sanctioned tool path are outside the coverage the protection layer claims; and drafts are visible from one build surface only. A fail lists filter conditions, which is the query describing itself. **The general form: your result is the population that emits, and no published figure says how far that is from the population that exists.** A student who writes "this covers the estate" has asserted a coverage rate nobody has measured — including the vendor.

**9 · Run the same agent twice, once without the words and once with.** Trace a run with content recording at its default, then again with it switched on. Put the two traces side by side and answer one question from each: *where did this response come from*, and *which step introduced the error*. Then write the redaction rule that would make the second trace shippable.

> **What you should find.** That the first trace answers the shape questions perfectly well — which step, how long, which tool, how many retries — and that a surprising number of the register's real questions never needed the words at all. And that the second trace contains the agent's planning steps, its memory handling, and the arguments it sent to every tool, which is to say **it contains both the reasoning you wanted and the personal data you did not ask for.** Those are the same bytes. **Now do the part that is actually assessed elsewhere:** list every principal who can read that second trace, by role, on the workspace it lands in. The list is defined by a role somebody granted for an unrelated reason, it is longer than the person who threw the switch expected, and **nothing in the platform prompted anybody to look at it.** That is the privacy line, and it is why the design asks for one.

## What this does not establish

**Nothing here shows that better observability produces fewer incidents, shorter incidents or better decisions.** No source behind this session measures that. The openly published handbook, from which two of the session's central rules are taken verbatim, offers no comparative evidence of any kind and states none. It is a working standard published in public, which is a different and lesser thing than a tested one.

**The source is also uneven in a way that matters here.** Its observability section is the most rotted material on its own site: several tool pages recommend software that has since been archived, removed from the product it monitored, or deprecated in favour of a successor — and every one of those pages still renders. **A page that renders is not a page that is current.** Its tracing material is short and defines neither a span, nor a trace context, nor propagation; those definitions live on pages that are not filed as pillars at all.

**None of that undermines the rules this session takes.** It does mean the session takes rules and not a section, and that every citation from that document is pinned to a page and to that page's own footer date, because **the site has no single date and one cannot be quoted.**

**The production-monitoring literature is practitioner guidance, not research.** No study design, no comparison group, no measurement that its practices outperform alternatives; three of its four authors worked at one vendor whose toolchain is its default; some of its foundational claims rest on anonymous practitioner testimony; and every quantity in it is a property of a particular system after a particular run. **No figure from it appears here.**

**The gateway documentation makes no performance or cost claim that could be checked.** Its benefits are plausible mechanisms rather than measured outcomes, capability availability varies by service tier, parts of it are preview, and it is silent on the privacy consequences of the logging it recommends. That silence is recorded here as a silence rather than filled by inference.

**The hole where the anchor's own tracing surface should be is filled. What stood beside it is not.** Earlier revisions of this session recorded that this repository held no research note covering tracing and observability in the vendor's agent platform, and taught the derivation while refusing the syntax. **A note now exists**, the surface is taught above, and the derivation still comes first — question, then signal — with every observation of the product surface pinned and non-generalising. **What closing that gap did not close is larger than the gap was.** No measure of tracing completeness is published anywhere: nothing states what proportion of an agent's decisions appears in a trace, whether a step can go unrecorded, or how a reader would detect a missing span, so **a trace is offered as a record and never as a sample.** And **no retention period is owned by the platform at all** — there is a portal display window, and there is a delegation to a workspace configuration you own, and those are different objects. **Both absences were confirmed by a dedicated search rather than by non-encounter**, which makes them findings about the product rather than gaps in this repository's reading.

**Nothing in the estate-inventory material establishes coverage either.** No published figure states what proportion of the agents actually present in a tenant the inventory discovers, and no detection rate or false-positive rate is published for any signal in it. **A query across the inventory returns the agents that emit; whether that is the estate is unmeasured, and this session asserts no rate of any kind.** The same note is first-party documentation for a surface that reached general availability within months of being read, is substantially preview, and rests in part on a page that arrived fragmented with its capability tables reduced to headers.

**And the correction itself is worth reading as a specimen.** This session declared an absence honestly, the absence was closed by ordinary work rather than by anything clever, and closing it produced two sharper absences that were invisible while the first one was in the way. **A stated gap is a cheap thing to carry and an expensive thing to leave unstated**, which is the same argument the register makes about a signal with no asker.

**The register, the asker column, the deletion list and the review date are this design's inventions.** The underlying rule about unaggregated data is quoted from a published standard; requiring a named person and a dated owner is not in that standard, and nobody has tested whether it changes what engineers keep.

**The one claim this session makes without hedging is definitional:** a signal that answers no question is a cost with no return. That is true by the meaning of the words, which is exactly why it is not evidence about anything.
