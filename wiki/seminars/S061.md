# S061 · Observability That Answers a Question

**Module:** [M13](/wiki/modules/M13.md) · **Quarter:** [Q7](/wiki/quarters/Q7.md) · **Complexity class:** 4
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** none
**Whitepaper:** [WP-061](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-061.md)
**Retrieves from:** S023, S043, S058
**Entry state:** this-programme-only

---

## The situation

The system is running. There is a dashboard. Somebody stood it up in an afternoon, mostly from whatever the platform offered by default, and it has been there ever since.

Nobody has asked what question any panel on it answers.

That is not a slur on the person who built it. **A default dashboard is a reasonable first act and a terrible steady state**, and the reason it persists is that nothing ever forces the question. Telemetry is cheap to add, invisible to keep, and nobody is ever blamed for collecting too much.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught.

1. What question does each panel on your dashboard answer, and who asks it?
2. What are you collecting that nobody aggregates and nobody alerts on?
3. Where does a correlation identifier get assigned in your system, and what happens to a request that arrives before that point?
4. Of logging, metrics and tracing, which would you drop first if the bill doubled — and on what basis?
5. If your entire observability configuration were deleted tonight, how long would it take to rebuild, and from what?

**Item 2 is the one that hurts.** Most estates fail it immediately, and the failure is invisible because unaggregated telemetry costs money quietly and produces nothing loudly.

## What you must produce

**An observability specification, held in the repository as code.**

For every signal the system emits: the question it answers, the person who asks that question, the retention, and a review date. Plus the dashboards, alerts and queries themselves, in source control and deployed the way the rest of the system is deployed, **so that a change to them arrives through review.**

And a deletion list: what you stopped collecting, and why.

## What good looks like

- **Every retained signal names a question and an asker.** Not a category, not a team — a question somebody actually asks and a person who asks it.
- **Anything collected but not aggregated or alerted on is on a dated review list**, with a decision recorded against it. This is the openly published playbook's own standing rule and almost nothing passes it first time.
- **Dashboards, alerts and queries are in source control** and arrive through peer review. The argument is not reproducibility; it is that somebody else looks at them before they take effect.
- **The correlation identifier is assigned as early as the system can assign one**, and propagated through every downstream component and log.
- **No personal or customer-sensitive data is logged, and no sensitive value appears in a URL** — request URLs are routinely logged by proxies and servers you do not control.
- **The choice between a log, a metric and a trace is defended on what each costs to keep**, not on what each means. That is the more operationally honest framing and it is the one your bill will use.
- **The deletion list is not empty.** A specification that retained everything has specified nothing.

## How it goes wrong

- **The inherited dashboard** — panels nobody chose, kept because removing them feels like losing something. *Warning sign:* you cannot name a person who looks at it.
- **Collect because you can** — telemetry retained because it was easy to emit. *Warning sign:* your answer on retention is "the default".
- **The click-built alert** — configuration made in a console, invisible to review, undiffable and unattributable. *Warning sign:* nobody can tell you who changed it or when.
- **The exception logged at every layer** — the same raised exception logged four times on its way up. *Warning sign:* your error volume is a function of your call depth.
- **The high-cardinality label** — a unique or near-unique string used as the value of a metric label. *Warning sign:* your metrics cost tracks your user count.
- **The late correlation identifier** — assigned after the first hop, so the first hop is invisible. *Warning sign:* your traces start at the second service and nobody has noticed.
- **Tracing as an incident tool** — expecting a trace to tell you why production broke. Its purpose is not reactive; it is an optimisation instrument. *Warning sign:* your incident procedure opens with "check the traces".
- **The specification nobody can rebuild from** — a beautiful document, and the running configuration does not match it. *Warning sign:* your answer to opening item 5 is a person's name.

## Do it

**Write the question column first, before you open a tool.**

Take every signal the system currently emits and put it in a table with three empty columns: the question, the asker, the decision that changes when the answer changes. Fill them in. **Do not adjust the question to fit the signal** — that is the move this session exists to catch, and it is almost irresistible because the signal already exists and the question does not.

**Then delete.** Everything with an empty asker column is a candidate. Some of it will survive because it is cheap and might be needed; that survival is a decision and it goes on the review list with a date. Everything else goes.

**Then put the survivors in the repository.** Dashboards, alerts and queries as code, deployed like everything else. Raise the change as a review and let somebody argue with a panel.

**Then check what you are logging.** Personal and customer-sensitive data out. Sensitive values out of URLs. The same exception logged once, at the layer that can say something useful about it.

**Then find your correlation identifier.** Trace it back to the earliest point the system controls. If requests exist before it, you have a blind segment and it belongs in [S063](/wiki/seminars/S063.md)'s register rather than being quietly tolerated.

Use an agent on the deletion pass — it is good at enumerating what is emitted and bad at deciding what matters — and **record which of its deletion candidates you rejected and why.** The rejections are the interesting part.

## Show it

Give the specification to somebody who did not build the system.

Ask them to pick one panel and tell you the decision it changes. Then ask the harder question: **show them one signal you kept and ask what would be lost if it stopped tomorrow.**

If the answer to the second is "we might want it", the signal is on the review list rather than in the specification, and the difference between those two places is the whole session.

## What this does not establish

**Nothing here shows that better observability produces fewer incidents, shorter incidents or better decisions.** The openly published playbook this session's two central rules come from offers no comparative evidence of any kind, states none, and should not be read as though it did. It is a working standard published verbatim, which is a different and lesser thing than a tested one.

**The source is also uneven in a way that matters here.** Its observability section is the most rotted material on its own site: several tool pages recommend software that has since been archived, removed from the product it monitored, or deprecated in favour of a successor — and every one of those pages still renders. **A page that renders is not a page that is current.** Its tracing material is a few lines and does not define a span, a trace context or propagation; those definitions live on pages that are not filed as pillars at all. Its own dashboards page is filed in one place in the navigation and lives in another.

**None of that undermines the two rules this session takes.** It does mean the session takes two rules and not a section, and that every citation from that document is pinned to a page and to that page's own footer date, because **the site has no single date and one cannot be quoted.**
