# S067 · Attribution, and Who Is Responsible for It

**Module:** [M14](/wiki/modules/M14.md) · **Quarter:** [Q7](/wiki/quarters/Q7.md) · **Complexity class:** 4
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** APIM AI gateway token attribution by subscription and product — making attribution a recorded technical fact rather than a negotiated claim
**Whitepaper:** [WP-067](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-067.md)
**Retrieves from:** S024, S060, S066
**Entry state:** this-programme-only

---

## The situation

The number moved. Somebody is going to say your system moved it.

They may be right. Nothing you currently hold establishes that they are, and in most delivery organisations **nobody is assigned the job of establishing it.** Cost is forecast by named engineers against named telemetry. Return is chosen by the customer from a menu, measured with instrumentation the customer may not possess, over a period nobody specifies. The causal step between the two is performed by nobody and reviewed by nobody.

This is where measurement stops being a technical activity. **A number with no owner is a number nobody will act on, and a number with a contested owner is worse than none**, because the argument about whose number it is will outlive whatever it was measuring. [S066](/wiki/seminars/S066.md) was about a record. This session is about who is accountable for what the record is taken to mean.

The framework this module teaches from does something sharper and worse. **Several of its metric definitions define themselves as the change *attributable to* the system.** A measure defined that way cannot be evidence that the system caused a change, because the attribution is inside the definition. The framework also poses the confounding question correctly — how would you separate the value of the system from the value of the learning, if a previous solution already exists — **and answers it nowhere.**

Answer these in writing before anything is taught.

1. Who, by name, is responsible for establishing that your system caused the change?
2. What else could have caused it?
3. A previous solution already exists. How do you separate its effect from yours, and both from the learning that happened alongside them?
4. Which of your metric definitions contains the word *attributable*, and what work is that word doing?
5. What would you accept as evidence that your system did **not** cause the change?

**Item 1 is the session.** Most answers are a role nobody holds, a team, or a silence — and the silence is the most honest of the three.

## What you must produce

**An attribution statement**, and a **gateway attribution record** that it rests on.

1. **A named person** responsible for establishing causation. A human being with a name, not a role and not a team.
2. **The competing explanations**, listed, with at least one that is genuinely plausible.
3. **What was done to separate them** — and where nothing was done, what the claim has been downgraded to.
4. **The rewritten definitions**: any metric defined as the change attributable to the system, restated so that the attribution is a conclusion rather than a premise, with the original retained beside it.
5. **The two attribution layers, kept apart on the page.** What the request path records as a technical fact, and what a person is asserting as a causal claim.
6. **The counter key and the dimensions** you configured, with what each one makes answerable and what it does not.
7. **The disconfirming evidence**: what you would accept as showing the system was not the cause.

## The material

### Two things are called attribution and only one of them is a fact

The word does two jobs in the same document and almost nobody separates them.

**Technical attribution** answers *whose consumption was this?* It is a property of the request path. If the request carried a subscription key, an application identifier and a user header, and the gateway recorded them alongside the tokens consumed, then the answer is a fact recorded at the moment the event happened. It is not a judgement and there is nothing to negotiate.

**Causal attribution** answers *did this system cause that change?* It is a claim about the world, made by a person, on evidence that is always partial. It is the thing this session says must have a name attached.

**The trap is that a document containing excellent technical attribution feels as though it has settled the causal question, and it has not.** Knowing exactly which team spent which tokens tells you nothing about whether the spend produced anything. **A ratio whose denominator is well instrumented and whose numerator is not is not a well-instrumented ratio, but it will feel like one** — and the better your gateway telemetry, the stronger that feeling gets.

The session's structure follows from that. The workflow makes the first kind of attribution genuinely solid, in order to demonstrate on the page how little that settles about the second.

### What the gateway makes into a recorded fact

The gateway is a reverse proxy in front of model endpoints, and the argument for putting governance there is that AI traffic is API traffic with different resource semantics rather than a new category of thing — the documentation states that the AI gateway "extends API Management's existing API gateway" and "is not a separate offering" ([research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md)).

The reasoning that matters here is the scarcity argument, and it is the cleanest on the page. Tokens are the scarce resource; providers assign quota as tokens per minute; and that quota must be distributed "across your model consumers, such as different applications, developer teams, or departments within the company." With one application a limit set directly on the deployment suffices. As the portfolio grows, "you need to make sure that one app doesn't use the whole TPM quota and block other apps from accessing the backends they need."

**That is a noisy-neighbour problem, and it generalises into the sentence this session needs: a shared finite resource with no per-consumer accounting will be consumed by whichever consumer is least careful, and nobody will be able to say who.**

The mechanism is a token limit policy enforcing a rate or a longer-period quota, keyed on **"any counter key, such as subscription key, originating IP address, or an arbitrary key defined through a policy expression."** **The counter key is the attribution decision.** Key on subscription and you can answer questions about subscriptions; key on IP and you can answer questions about network origins and nothing about teams. A quota you cannot attribute is a quota you cannot enforce fairly, and the choice is made once, in a policy file, usually by somebody who was not thinking about reporting.

Two further details are worth having exactly. The policy "enables precalculation of prompt tokens on the Azure API Management side, minimizing unnecessary requests to the AI service backend if the prompt already exceeds the limit" — a fail-fast design that rejects at the edge rather than paying to discover the rejection downstream. And the remaining budget can be exposed to downstream policy through a named variable, so the gateway can do more than accept or reject.

The observability side is where attribution becomes reportable. A metric-emitting policy attaches custom dimensions — client address, API identifier, a user header — to the token metrics it emits. **Dimensions are what make a metric answerable. Total token consumption tells you the bill; token consumption per API per user tells you who to talk to.** The documentation also notes that logging supports "billing and auditing", which is the point at which observability stops being an operational nicety and becomes a governance requirement.

**The source's limits are recorded and two of them are this session's business.** It is first-party product documentation with a commercial frame: definitional rather than empirical, with **no benchmarks, no latency figures, no cost-reduction measurements and no comparison against any other gateway.** The architectural reasoning is sound and asserted rather than demonstrated. Capability **varies by service tier**, several named capabilities are **preview**, and policy names and attributes drift between revisions. **And it does not discuss the privacy surface created by logging prompts and completions, despite recommending that logging for billing and auditing.** That gap is not a footnote: an attribution record fine-grained enough to name a user is a personal-data record, and the documentation will not tell you that.

### The circular metric, and the word that does the work

The internal value framework recorded at [research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md) contains the module's sharpest single defect. **Several of its metric definitions define themselves as the change *attributable to* the system.**

Read one slowly. If a measure is defined as *the reduction in handling time attributable to the assistant*, then computing it requires you to have already decided which part of the reduction the assistant caused. **The attribution is inside the definition, so the measure cannot be evidence for it.** Offering the resulting number as proof that the system worked is circular, and it is very hard to see because the sentence is grammatical, the number is real and the arithmetic is correct.

The framework poses the confounding question correctly — how would you separate the value of the system from the value of the learning, where a previous solution already exists — **and answers it nowhere.** There is no worked separation in the corpus, no case in which a previous solution's contribution was distinguished from a new one's, and no reported instance of any metric being measured at all.

**Its limits travel with every use:** access-controlled and unverifiable by any public reader, paraphrased and never quoted, no outcome data of any kind, and no figure in it to reproduce — which is the module's subject rather than an incidental fact about it.

### The cause is frequently not where the effect appeared

The production machine-learning literature names two properties that make attribution structurally hard in systems of this kind, and both are reasons a competing-explanation list has to be longer than feels reasonable ([research/02-technical-foundations/crowe-machine-learning-production-systems.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/crowe-machine-learning-production-systems.md)).

**Entanglement**: changing one thing changes everything, so a small upstream difference reappears somewhere apparently unrelated. **Configuration**: versions, parameters and feature lists live in configuration, where a small error produces radically different behaviour that ordinary software tests will not catch. Together they mean **the region where an effect is observed is weak evidence about where its cause lives**, which is exactly the assumption a short confounder list rests on.

The same source supplies the framing that makes gateway telemetry worth the cost: observability is imported from control theory, with **alertability** — thresholds that make a failure unambiguous — and **actionability** — enough signal to support finding a cause. **A stream that supports neither is cost rather than observability**, and an undimensioned token metric is exactly that stream.

**Its limits are heavy.** It is practitioner guidance from a commercial publisher, not research: no study design, no comparison group, no measurement that its practices outperform alternatives. Three of its four authors worked at one large vendor, so its default toolchain is that vendor's. Some foundational claims rest on **anonymous practitioner testimony** that cannot be checked. **Every quantity in it is a property of a particular system after a particular run, and no figure from it appears here.**

### Published comparisons are shaped by the conditions of their production

Attribution has a public form too, and its failure mode is instructive because the mechanism is statistical rather than moral.

An audit of the leaderboard that had become the de facto public ranking of frontier models found that its scores were distorted not by a technical flaw but by undisclosed platform policy ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/singh-leaderboard-illusion.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/singh-leaderboard-illusion.md)). Some providers could test many variants privately and publish only the ones they liked, with the ability to retract unfavourable results — which the authors describe as producing **"biased Arena scores due to selective disclosure of performance results."** **A best-of-many reported as a single draw is a bias with a computable shape, not merely bad manners.** Separately, battle data was allocated unequally across providers, and models were removed without disclosure, which reshapes the historical record. The conclusion the authors reach is **"overfitting to Arena-specific dynamics rather than general model quality"** — a rising position is evidence about the leaderboard before it is evidence about the model.

For this session the transfer is direct and uncomfortable. **A number's meaning depends on the rules governing who could produce it, how many attempts they had, and what happened to the attempts they did not publish.** When your customer quotes an external figure at you, those are the questions, and they are almost never answerable. When *you* produce a figure, they are the questions somebody should ask you.

**The audit's limits:** all allocation figures are external estimates rather than platform-disclosed values, and the paper says so; the findings are specific to one platform at one moment and policies can change in response to exactly this kind of audit; **and nothing in it establishes that models are not improving or that preference-based evaluation is invalid** — reading it as leaderboard nihilism misses the reform argument entirely. **No figure from it is reproduced here.**

### If you reach for a judge, you have not found a neutral party

A tempting shortcut in an attribution dispute is to have a model adjudicate. The judging literature closes that route with two named biases that apply precisely here ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/zheng-mt-bench-chatbot-arena.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/zheng-mt-bench-chatbot-arena.md)).

**Self-enhancement bias** means a judge drawn from the same family as the system under test is not independent of it. **Limited reasoning** means a judge cannot reliably grade a problem it cannot itself solve — and separating a system's effect from a co-timed reorganisation is not a problem it can solve. **Position bias** and **verbosity bias** further mean that presentation order and length move verdicts independently of content, which matters the moment two competing explanations are put to a model side by side.

The useful half is that the paper proposes mitigations rather than merely cataloguing faults: swap the presentation order and require consistency, control for length, and **use a judge from a different family than the system under test.** The last is the one an engineer can act on this week.

**Its limits:** the work predates current models; its bias taxonomy has held up better than its figures, **none of which is reproduced here**; and agreement with human preference is not correctness in any case. **The published benchmark for a judge is agreement with humans at the level humans agree with each other — which is a ceiling rather than a guarantee, and says nothing about whether the preference being reproduced is right.**

### The honest thing a cost side can do for a return side

The evaluation book in this repository puts cost and latency in the same bucket as domain capability, generation capability and instruction-following — one of four criteria an application is supposed to define before it is built ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/huyen-ai-engineering.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/huyen-ai-engineering.md)). That placement is the argument for treating gateway attribution as first-class rather than as finance's problem.

It also supplies the discipline that keeps an attribution statement honest: **tie the evaluation metric to the business metric, and state the usefulness threshold** — what score must the application achieve for it to be useful at all. The mapping between the two is where every unearned causal claim hides, and writing it down as a mapping rather than as a sentence makes the assumption visible.

**Its limits:** a practitioner book rather than a study, heavily citational, with a substantial part of its most useful material resting on unattributed interviews. **Its illustrative mappings are explicitly illustrations of shape rather than measurements, and no magnitude from it appears here.**

## The workflow

**Make one attribution a recorded fact of the request path, then observe how little it settles.**

**Step 1 — choose the counter key deliberately and write down the question it answers.** Subscription, product, application identifier or a policy expression. **This single choice determines the set of attribution questions your telemetry can answer for the life of the engagement**, and it is usually made by whoever wrote the first policy file.

**Step 2 — apply the token limit policy with prompt-token precalculation.** Reject over-limit requests at the edge rather than paying the backend to reject them. Expose the remaining budget to downstream policy so that a near-exhausted consumer can be handled rather than merely refused.

**Step 3 — emit token metrics with the dimensions you will actually need.** An undimensioned metric tells you the bill. A dimensioned one tells you who to talk to.

```xml
<!-- SHAPE, NOT SCHEMA. Policy names, attributes and tier availability drift between
     revisions - verify every element against the current reference before use. -->
<inbound>
  <!-- The counter key IS the attribution decision. Key on subscription and you can
       answer questions about subscriptions; key on IP and you cannot. -->
  <llm-token-limit counter-key="@(context.Subscription.Id)"
                   tokens-per-minute="500"
                   estimate-prompt-tokens="true"
                   remaining-tokens-variable-name="remainingTokens" />

  <!-- Dimensions are what make a metric answerable rather than merely large. -->
  <llm-emit-token-metric namespace="attribution">
    <dimension name="Subscription" value="@(context.Subscription.Id)" />
    <dimension name="Product"      value="@(context.Product?.Id ?? "unattributed")" />
    <dimension name="API"          value="@(context.Api.Id)" />
    <!-- A user-level dimension makes this a personal-data record. The documentation
         recommends logging for billing and auditing and does NOT discuss that. -->
    <dimension name="CallerTeam"   value="@(context.Request.Headers.GetValueOrDefault("x-team","N/A"))" />
  </llm-emit-token-metric>
</inbound>
```

**Step 4 — generate traffic from two consumers and prove the separation.** Send load under two subscriptions, then produce a per-consumer breakdown from the emitted metrics. **Do not accept the dashboard as evidence that attribution works. Deliberately send one request with no team header and find it in the `unattributed` bucket**, because the size of that bucket is the honest measure of how good your attribution is.

**Step 5 — write the two layers side by side, in the same document.** On the left, what the request path recorded: consumption by subscription and product, as a fact, with the counter key named. On the right, what a person is claiming: that this consumption produced a change in the world. **Put them in adjacent columns so that the asymmetry is visible rather than argued.**

**Step 6 — find the word.** Search every metric definition you were handed for language that assumes causation — *attributable to*, *driven by*, *resulting from*, *enabled by*. Rewrite each so the measure describes an observable quantity and the attribution becomes a separate claim, keeping the original beside it. This is the discipline [S054](/wiki/seminars/S054.md) installed for inferred security attributes, arriving in a financial document: **uncertainty relocated is uncertainty preserved; uncertainty stripped is a guess wearing a fact's clothes.**

**Step 7 — find a name.** Go and ask a person to own the causal claim. **This is uncomfortable and it is the point:** you are asking somebody to be accountable for a judgement most organisations leave unowned precisely because it cannot be made safely. **If nobody will take it, that refusal is your finding** and it goes in the statement with what they said.

**Step 8 — attack your own statement.** One prompt: *argue that the change would have occurred without this system, using only the competing explanations I listed — then name the one I failed to list.* Read it for the explanation you omitted. Record what you kept and what you rejected, **and record that a model asked to adjudicate between explanations is subject to position and verbosity bias and shares a family with your executor**, so it is a generator of candidate objections and never a verdict.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** The size of the unattributed bucket, which is the only honest measure of an attribution scheme and is invisible in any summary that reports totals. **You will also observe the session's central asymmetry directly: within an hour you can make consumption attribution exact, and no amount of the same work will make causal attribution exact.** One is a property of the request path. The other is a claim about the world, and it needs a person.

## What good looks like

- **Attribution has a name attached.** **A claim whose attribution is assigned to nobody is not a claim** — it is a sentence somebody will quote.
- **The two layers are separated on the page.** What the request path recorded, and what a person is asserting, are not in the same column.
- **The counter key is named, with the questions it can and cannot answer.** A quota you cannot attribute is a quota you cannot enforce fairly.
- **The unattributed bucket is reported.** Its size is the honest measure of the attribution scheme, and a report that omits it is a report of the part that worked.
- **The competing explanations include at least one that would be awkward for you.** A list containing only implausible alternatives is a rhetorical device.
- **Every definition that presupposed attribution has been rewritten**, and the original is retained beside it so a reader can see what changed. **A measure defined as the change caused by the system assumes the conclusion and cannot then supply it.**
- **Where the previous solution's effect could not be separated, the claim is stated as an association** and says so in the sentence, not in a footnote.
- **The learning confound is addressed explicitly.** The customer got better at the problem while using your system. Some of the change is theirs.
- **The asymmetry is disclosed**: where one side of a ratio comes from telemetry and the other from a workshop, the document says which is which.

## How it goes wrong

- **The circular metric** — a measure defined as the change caused by the system, then offered as evidence the system caused a change. *Warning sign:* the word *attributable* appears in the definition and nobody has noticed it is doing the work.
- **Technical attribution mistaken for causal attribution** — the spend breakdown is exact, so the value claim feels established. *Warning sign:* your value section cites a cost dashboard.
- **The unassigned responsibility** — everybody agrees causation matters and nobody owns it. *Warning sign:* your governance record has an owner for every risk and none for the attribution.
- **The undimensioned metric** — you know the total and can answer no question about it. *Warning sign:* your first attribution question requires a new deployment to answer.
- **The invisible unattributed bucket** — requests arriving without the key are dropped from the breakdown rather than shown in it. *Warning sign:* your per-team figures sum to the total exactly.
- **The co-timed change** — a reorganisation, a policy change, a price change, a seasonal shift. *Warning sign:* you cannot say what else happened in the period.
- **The learning confound claimed as yours** — the customer improved at the task while using the system, and the whole change is claimed. *Warning sign:* you delivered training inside the measurement window and it is not in the list.
- **The engineered denominator** — the cost side is instrumented, the return side is elicited, and the ratio is presented as though both were measured. *Warning sign:* one side of your ratio comes from telemetry and the other from a menu.
- **The role that nobody holds** — attribution assigned to "the programme" or "the steering group". *Warning sign:* you cannot email the owner.
- **The unexaminable separation** — a method for separating causes is asserted and not described. *Warning sign:* your document says "controlled for".

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The attribution statement and the gateway record are carried to [S070](/wiki/seminars/S070.md), where the statement is defended and the gateway telemetry is used as the independent record against which your own evaluation results are checked.

**1 · Classify eight sentences as technical or causal attribution.** For each, say which kind it is and what would establish it.

- *The claims team consumed the majority of last month's tokens.* · *The assistant reduced handling time.* · *Product B exceeded its quota on the fourteenth.* · *Adoption drove the improvement in first-contact resolution.* · *Requests without a team header account for a share of spend we cannot allocate.* · *The savings attributable to the system exceeded its cost.* · *The circuit breaker tripped twice on the primary backend.* · *Automation freed up analyst capacity.*

> **Model answers.** The first, third, fifth and seventh are technical: each is a property of the request path or the gateway's own behaviour and is settled by a record. The second, fourth, sixth and eighth are causal, and none is settled by any record the gateway can produce. **Note what the sixth does: it embeds the attribution in the noun.** *Savings attributable to the system* is not a measurement whose value could be zero; it is a conclusion with a number attached. **The general rule: if the sentence would still be checkable after deleting every causal verb, it is technical. If deleting them empties it, it is a claim and needs a name.**

**2 · Rewrite three circular definitions.** Restate each so that the measurable quantity and the causal claim are separate sentences.

- *Hours saved attributable to the assistant.* · *Revenue enabled by the new workflow.* · *Error reduction driven by automated checking.*

> **Model answers.** *Total handling hours for queue X in the stated period, compared against the recorded baseline period; the claim that the assistant caused the difference is made separately, by a named person, against the listed competing explanations.* The pattern is identical in all three: **name the observable, name the comparison, then make the attribution a separate sentence somebody signs.** **The test of a good rewrite is that the measure can come out at zero without the sentence becoming incoherent.** A definition that cannot express *no effect* was never a measurement.

**3 · Go back to your own item 1, then break it.** Find what you wrote for opening item 1 — who is responsible for establishing causation. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then go and get an actual name, or record the refusal.

> **Criterion.** An attribution owner passes if you can email them, if they know they hold it, and if they could describe what evidence would change their mind. *"The programme"* fails on all three. *"The steering group"* fails on the first two. *"The delivery lead, who has accepted it in writing and has named the competing explanation she wants ruled out"* passes. **A recorded refusal also passes**, and is a stronger artefact than an unowned claim.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Measure your own unattributed bucket.** Using the workflow, produce the per-consumer breakdown and report the share of traffic that could not be attributed. Then send one deliberately unlabelled request and confirm it lands there.

> **What you should find.** A non-empty bucket, usually larger than expected, populated by health checks, retries, internal tooling and anything routed before the header was added. **The finding is not that attribution is broken. It is that a breakdown summing exactly to the total has silently dropped what it could not classify**, and every dashboard you are ever shown by a supplier has the same property unless it says otherwise.

**5 · Interrogate a quoted external comparison.** Take one published comparison your customer has cited. Write down the three governance questions and record which are answerable from what is published: how many attempts did the producer have, what happened to the attempts they did not publish, and who decided what was included.

> **What to notice.** Almost none of the three is answerable, and their unanswerability is the finding rather than an accusation. **A best-of-many reported as a single draw is biased upward by a computable mechanism**, and selective disclosure plus unequal data access plus undisclosed removals produce a ranking that is evidence about the platform before it is evidence about the systems on it. **Do not conclude that the comparison is worthless.** Conclude that its authority is bounded by rules nobody published, and say that in the sentence where you cite it.

**6 · Try to buy your way out with a judge, and fail on purpose.** Put two competing explanations to a model and ask it to adjudicate. Then swap their order and ask again.

> **What you should find.** Frequently a different verdict, which is position bias operating on a question you wanted settled. **The mitigations are real and worth using — swap the order and require consistency, control for length, and use a judge from a different family than the system under test — but none of them makes a judge competent at a causal question it could not itself answer.** Record the result as evidence that adjudication was attempted and did not settle it, which is a better artefact than the verdict you were hoping for.

## What this does not establish

**Nothing here establishes that any particular claim is causal.** This session teaches who is responsible for the question, how to make one layer of attribution a recorded fact, and how to downgrade when the other layer has not been answered. **It does not teach a method for establishing causation, and it does not have one.**

**The framework it corrects poses the confounding question and answers it nowhere.** There is no worked separation anywhere in the corpus, no case in which a previous solution's contribution was distinguished from the new one's, and no reported instance of any metric being measured at all. **The correction taught here is therefore a discipline imposed on an absence.**

**The gateway documentation is definitional and commercially framed.** It argues for a gateway and does not discuss the latency, cost or operational burden the gateway itself adds. It offers **no performance data, no cost-saving data and no comparison against any alternative.** Capability varies by service tier, several named capabilities are preview, and policy names and attributes drift between revisions. **And it recommends logging prompts and completions for billing and auditing without discussing the privacy surface that creates** — which is a silence you inherit the moment you add a user-level dimension.

**Excellent technical attribution licenses nothing about value.** Knowing exactly whose tokens were spent is a fact about consumption. **The session's whole design exists to make that distinction felt rather than asserted**, and a learner who leaves with a beautiful cost breakdown and an unowned value claim has taken the wrong half.

**The leaderboard audit's allocation figures are external estimates**, its findings are specific to one platform at one moment, and it establishes nothing about whether models are improving. It licenses the claim that a number's meaning depends on the rules under which it was produced. It licenses no conclusion about any particular published figure.

**The judging literature predates current models.** Its bias taxonomy has held up better than its figures, none of which is reproduced here, and agreement with human preference is not correctness in any case.

**The named-person requirement is written past every source.** Nothing in the corpus assigns responsibility for causation to anybody, and the recommendation that it should be assigned is this repository's own reading of the material. **It is design reasoning, it has never been tested on an organisation, and the person who accepts it takes on an accountability that outlasts the engagement with no evidence that anybody benefits from holding it.** A learner should expect refusal to be the common answer and should treat a recorded refusal as a successful outcome rather than a failed one.
