## The situation

You have spoken to six people and watched three of them work. Two of them contradict each other. One said something memorable that everybody on your team has now repeated four times.

Somebody asks what the customer needs.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught.

1. One person said the release process is the bottleneck. What are you now entitled to claim?
2. Two informants disagree about how approvals work. Which one is wrong?
3. A quotation from your notes has been in three of your team's slides this week. What do you know about the person who said it?
4. You have six informants and one of them talked far more than the others. What does your finding actually describe?
5. Name somebody whose work this system will change who you did not speak to.

**Item 2 has no answer as asked.** A contradiction between informants is usually a missing distinction — a role, a policy, an incentive, a context — and resolving it by choosing is how a finding becomes wrong quietly.

## What you must produce

A **consolidated finding** covering your engagement's informants. It carries five things:

- **The claim**, stated about a named population rather than about "the customer".
- **The cases it rests on** — which informants, in what roles, observed or reported.
- **At least one preserved contradiction**, with the distinction you think explains it and the evidence you do not yet have.
- **Who was not sampled**, named by the position they hold rather than by their absence.
- **What would change the claim** — the observation that would make you withdraw it.

## The material

### One informant licenses a claim about one informant

This is the whole session in a sentence and it is harder to hold than it looks.

An individual visit cannot license a claim about a population, which is why the method that most closely matches this module's subject consolidates before it designs ([research/05-fde-craft/beyer-holtzblatt-contextual-design.md](/research/05-fde-craft/beyer-holtzblatt-contextual-design.md)). Everything beyond *this person said this* — that the organisation has this problem, that engineers find it frustrating, that release is the bottleneck — requires an argument running from several cases to a population, and that argument is the work.

**Its limits.** The source is a method text resting on practitioner experience and accumulated project examples; **it offers no controlled comparison showing that the full method outperforms alternatives**, and it says so. Its sampling guidance is explicitly heuristic. This session uses its mechanism-level argument — each technique answers a named failure mode — and nothing beyond it.

### Build the hierarchy upward, then go looking for the hole

The consolidation move is mechanical and slow, and its order is the whole point.

Place observations together by similarity **before** naming anything. Label the clusters that emerge. Group the clusters. The resulting hierarchy is an inductive account of what matters across the people you actually met, and it preserves detail at the leaves while making large patterns available above.

The order matters because **an imported taxonomy cannot be surprised.** If you sort observations into categories you brought, the categories will be confirmed, and the top-level headings would have been the same for a different customer.

Then the check that the method itself insists on: **a polished hierarchy can still encode a narrow or biased field study.** From outside, a broad study and a narrow one produce artefacts that look identical. So the deliberate move is to ask which role has no observations under any heading, and to put that role in the not-sampled list.

A companion discipline comes from the field-interviewing literature: analyse cases before extracting themes, and **a vivid quotation without its case does not enter** ([research/05-fde-craft/portigal-interviewing-users.md](/research/05-fde-craft/portigal-interviewing-users.md)). A theme traced to decontextualised quotations cannot be evaluated, because a reader cannot tell whether the quotations came from people in comparable situations. **First edition; the later edition has not been read here, and the source reports no controlled comparison of anything.**

### A contradiction is a missing distinction before it is an error

Two informants disagreeing about how approvals work is almost never one of them being wrong.

Both sources say the same thing in different vocabularies. Contradictory observations can both be useful, and no single representation is expected to carry the whole truth. Assume apparent inconsistency makes sense within a framework you have not yet understood — a role, a policy, an incentive, a context that differs between the two people. **Explore the missing distinction rather than averaging the conflict away.**

Resolving it by choosing is how a finding becomes wrong quietly, and the account that gets chosen is reliably the one that supports the work the practitioner came to do.

### The merged segment manufactures ambivalence

The subtler failure, and the one that produces the most confident bad decisions.

Broad labels hide people with incompatible goals, workflows, buying processes and constraints ([research/05-fde-craft/fitzpatrick-the-mom-test.md](/research/05-fde-craft/fitzpatrick-the-mom-test.md)). When evidence from users, approvers and budget owners is aggregated as one customer voice, the mixed signal looks like an ambivalent market. **In fact three populations answered clearly and differently, and the hedge was manufactured by the aggregation.**

The repair has a checkable form: a useful segment is a **findable** one — a kind of person paired with the place or behaviour through which they can be reached. If you cannot say where the population is, you have not described a population.

**Its limits.** A founder-oriented practitioner handbook; the local copy carries **no publisher, year or edition metadata**; it reports no controlled comparison, no measured effect and no validated threshold, and its numerical guidance is the author's own experience.

### The exact analogue: a blend that improves on aggregate and is worse for every query

Here is where the retrieval literature earns its place in a discovery session, because it has already made this mistake carefully and written it down.

BM25 is not a folk formula. It is a derivation from stated probabilistic assumptions, and two of its components are decisions about how to combine evidence ([research/02-technical-foundations/robertson-zaragoza-bm25-and-beyond.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/robertson-zaragoza-bm25-and-beyond.md)). **Term-frequency saturation** encodes the claim that a document using a term twenty times is more likely to be about it than one using it twice, but not ten times more likely. **Length normalisation** encodes the ambiguity that a long document may be long because it is verbose or because it genuinely covers more, and exposes a parameter to interpolate between the two theories rather than committing to one.

The part that transfers directly is BM25F, the extension to documents with several fields. The correct approach is to **combine term frequencies across fields with per-field weights before applying saturation, rather than scoring fields separately and combining the scores.** Read that as a claim about consolidation and it is this session's thesis: you combine the evidence, keeping which field it came from, and you do not average finished verdicts. **Averaging verdicts throws away the thing that made them different.**

**Its limits.** The monograph is paywalled and **its full text was not retrieved for this repository** — the parameter roles and the treatment of eliteness, BM25F and relevance feedback are reconstructed from the framework as established in the wider literature rather than read from the source. Its conventional parameter defaults are conventions from past collections, not universal optima. It predates dense retrieval entirely.

The empirical companion is BEIR, and its finding is the one to hold ([research/02-technical-foundations/thakur-beir.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/thakur-beir.md)). Evaluating retrieval systems across a deliberately heterogeneous set of tasks and domains shows that a lexical baseline does not win and **does not collapse on any domain** — and that stability is itself the result, because a method whose worst case is acceptable is operationally different from a method with a higher average and an unknown worst case. The most expressive families achieved the best average and cost the most; the efficient ones often underperformed out of domain.

Then the sentence a consolidator should tattoo somewhere: the benchmark's own note records that **the constituent datasets differ in the density and construction of their relevance judgements, so per-dataset scores are not strictly comparable to one another.** An average over incomparable measurements is a number with no referent. That is the merged segment, in a different discipline, with the same consequence.

**Its limits.** A 2021 snapshot; the specific model rankings have since changed; eighteen datasets are a sample of retrieval tasks rather than a map of them; and the benchmark has since become a target of optimisation, which weakens its zero-shot premise over time.

### Aggregate scores drift away from what they purport to measure

The governance version of the same failure is worth one paragraph because your finding will be read like a leaderboard.

An audit of the leaderboard that had become the public ranking of frontier models found that its ordering was distorted by undisclosed policy rather than by any technical flaw: unequal opportunity to test privately and publish selectively, unequal allocation of the data that both determines and improves a rating, and undisclosed removals ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/singh-leaderboard-illusion.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/singh-leaderboard-illusion.md)). The consequence the authors name is **overfitting to the leaderboard's own dynamics rather than to general quality** — the score becomes about the instrument.

The transfer is direct. A consolidated finding is an aggregate produced by a process with its own access asymmetries: you spoke most to the people who were easiest to reach, and their views are now weighted accordingly. **Your evidence is thickest where your access was easiest, and nothing in the artefact records that unless you record it.**

**Its limits.** All allocation figures in that work are external estimates rather than platform-disclosed values; the findings are specific to one platform at one moment; and this repository's note is written from the abstract and listing metadata rather than the full paper. **No figure from it is quoted here.**

### Six informants from one team are not six observations

The last technical idea, and it is the one that most changes what you may claim.

When the units of an evaluation are drawn in related groups rather than independently, treating them as independent overstates the precision of the result, and the correction is to compute the standard error with the clustering accounted for ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md)). The paper's warning is that failure to adjust may lead an analyst to suppose a measurement is much more precise than it actually is.

Now read your informant list. Six people from one team, sharing a manager, a toolchain, a release process and a lunch table, are **one cluster with six voices**, not six independent draws. The direction of the error is always the same: you will believe you know more than you do.

The same source supplies the discipline that closes this session's artefact: an evaluation is an experiment, and a reported result needs its number of units and an honest account of how they were drawn. **Do the analysis before you run**, so that finding nothing is a statement about the world rather than about your sample.

**Its limits.** The paper is analytical rather than empirical; most of its illustrative magnitudes rest on stylised assumptions; and **no number from it appears anywhere in this session.** It addresses comparing scores rather than choosing what to score, so a tight interval around a meaningless finding is still meaningless.

### Why the delegated cluster is the wrong economy

You will be able to paste every note into an agent and receive a clean set of themes in seconds, and they will look like the themes you would have built.

**Do not**, and be able to say why. The clustering is not the deliverable. The deliverable is a team that has argued about which observations belong together, because that argument is where the missing distinction behind a contradiction gets found. The organisation whose practice this stage draws on **marks its own discovery work as manual** in its own item-by-item automation tagging ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-fde-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-fde-delivery-practice.md)) — a judgement made by people with a commercial interest in the opposite answer, which is the strongest support this position has and is not strong. **That note is a paraphrase of internal material, much of it gated even to a reader inside the organisation, and it contains one recorded internal inconsistency that remains unresolved.**

**Use the agent to attack the finding instead**, which is the move [S001](/wiki/seminars/S001.md) established and this session applies to its own artefact.

## The workflow

**Index your cases, then watch a hybrid ranking bury the informant who disagreed.**

You will build a small Azure AI Search index over your own case write-ups and run the same question three ways. **A warning about warrant before you start:** this repository's Azure AI Search note was fetched and read in full for document-level access control and **does not cover the ranking surface at all**. Every ranking-specific name, parameter and default below must be verified against current documentation before you rely on it, and much of the relevant surface is preview. What is durable here is the shape of the comparison, not the syntax.

**Step 1 — one document per case, never one per quotation.** Each case carries the informant's role, the team, whether the material was observed or reported, and the text. **The role and team fields are what make the clustering visible later**, and a corpus of decontextualised quotations cannot be repaired by any ranking.

**Step 2 — record the access basis on the document, because an index is a copy.** Copies do not inherit their originals' access rules, and a consolidation corpus contains people's opinions about their colleagues.

```json
{
  "name": "cases-s018",
  "fields": [
    { "name": "id",       "type": "Edm.String", "key": true },
    { "name": "informant","type": "Edm.String", "filterable": true },
    { "name": "role",     "type": "Edm.String", "filterable": true, "facetable": true },
    { "name": "team",     "type": "Edm.String", "filterable": true, "facetable": true },
    { "name": "basis",    "type": "Edm.String", "filterable": true },
    { "name": "case",     "type": "Edm.String", "searchable": true },
    { "name": "native_terms", "type": "Collection(Edm.String)", "searchable": true },
    { "name": "allowed_groups", "type": "Collection(Edm.String)", "filterable": true }
  ],
  "scoringProfiles": [
    {
      "name": "evidence_weighted",
      "text": {
        "weights": {
          "case": 1.0,
          "native_terms": 3.0
        }
      }
    }
  ]
}
```

**Step 3 — run the same question three ways and keep all three result lists.** Lexical only. Vector only. Then the hybrid. **Do not look at the hybrid first.** Write down, before you run it, which of your cases you expect each to surface.

**Step 4 — find the case that the hybrid loses.** Compare the three lists per query rather than in aggregate. You are looking for one specific pattern: **a case that appears in exactly one of the single-signal lists and in neither the other nor the blend.** That is usually the informant with the unusual vocabulary — the exception handler, the person on the older toolchain, the one using a native term nobody else uses.

This is the session's demonstration and it costs ten minutes. A blend can score better on the aggregate you are looking at and be worse for the individual query that mattered, and **the blend gives you no signal that this has happened.** Exact-term matching remains essential for rare identifiers, product codes and domain jargon that embeddings smooth over, and smoothing over domain jargon is precisely how a dissenting informant disappears.

**Step 5 — weight the evidence rather than the verdicts.** Raise the weight on the field carrying native terms and re-run. Notice what you did: you changed how evidence contributes **before** the score is formed, which is the BM25F move, rather than re-ranking two finished lists against each other. Then say in one sentence which population that weighting privileges. Every weighting privileges one.

**Step 6 — facet by team and count.** Run the facet over `team` and `role`. **If most of your top results come from one team, you have one cluster and you have been treating it as several observations.** Put the count in the finding.

**Step 7 — attack the finding, not the notes.** Give the agent your consolidated claim and your case list and ask two things: *which of these cases does the claim not fit*, and *what population have I actually described*. Then ask it to write the finding a competitor would produce from the same cases. Record what you kept and what you struck. **An agent that agreed with you was not used as an instrument.**

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That a combination which looks better in aggregate can lose the specific case you needed, and that you would not have noticed, because the aggregate went the right way. It also makes the clustering problem countable: **a facet over team turns "we spoke to six people" into "we spoke to one team six times"**, and no amount of careful writing does that.

## What good looks like

- The claim's subject is a population you can describe, and **not everybody in the room is in it.**
- Each case is legible as a case. A reader can see the person behind the point rather than a decontextualised quotation.
- The contradiction is preserved rather than resolved, and your proposed distinction is stated as a hypothesis with a test attached.
- **The not-sampled list contains somebody inconvenient** — an operator, an approver, an exception handler, somebody downstream who does not attend meetings. [S007](/wiki/seminars/S007.md) asked who is not in the room; this is where their absence gets a cost.
- The clustering was built upward from the observations rather than downward from a category you brought.
- **A single informant's remark has not become a requirement anywhere in the artefact.**

## How it goes wrong

- **The utterance promoted** — one person said one thing and it is now a backlog item. *Warning sign:* you can trace a requirement to exactly one sentence from exactly one person.
- **The merged segment** — evidence from users, approvers and budget owners has been aggregated as one customer voice, so the mixed signal looks like an ambivalent market rather than three markets. *Warning sign:* your finding uses the word "users" for people with incompatible goals.
- **The disappeared dissent** — the contradiction was resolved during a team discussion and the losing account is no longer in any artefact. *Warning sign:* your finding has no negative case.
- **The loudest informant** — the person who spoke most is now the population. *Warning sign:* your evidence is thickest where your access was easiest.
- **The imported taxonomy** — you sorted the observations into categories you had before you started, so the categories cannot be surprised. *Warning sign:* the top-level headings would have been the same for a different customer.
- **The cluster you did not build** — you handed the notes to an agent, took the themes, and now cannot say what any theme rests on. *Warning sign:* you can present the finding and cannot answer *which cases*.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed here.** Two products of this session are assessed at [S020](/wiki/seminars/S020.md): that the consolidated finding is present with its preserved contradiction intact, and that the not-sampled list names positions rather than recording absence in general terms.

**1 · Cases, then clusters, then the hole.** Write each informant up as a case. Cluster the observations by similarity before naming anything. Name the groups that emerge, group the groups, then find the role with no observations under any heading.

> **Scoring criterion.** A passing hierarchy has top-level headings that would **not** have been the same for a different customer. If they would have been, you imported a taxonomy and it cannot be surprised. And a passing not-sampled list contains somebody inconvenient — an operator, an approver, an exception handler, somebody downstream who does not attend meetings. [S007](/wiki/seminars/S007.md) asked who is not in the room; this is where their absence acquires a cost.

**2 · Preserve one contradiction properly.** Take two informants who disagree. Write the distinction you think explains it, as a hypothesis, with the observation that would test it.

> **Model answer shape.** *A says approvals take a day, B says a week. Hypothesis: A is describing the standard path and B the path taken when a change touches the shared schema. Test: pull the last ten approvals and split them by whether the schema was touched.* **A passing answer names a test somebody could run this week.** "We need more data" is a suspension, not a preservation, and the difference is whether the artefact says what would settle it.

**3 · Three rankings, one lost case.** Run the workflow through step 4 and identify a case that the hybrid ranking loses.

> **What you should find.** The lost case usually belongs to the informant with the unusual vocabulary, because that is precisely the signal a dense representation smooths over and a blend then dilutes. **Write down what your finding would have said if you had only ever seen the blended list.** That sentence is the session, and you will have produced it yourself rather than been told it.

**4 · Facet by team and restate the claim.** Run the facet from step 6 and rewrite your claim's subject to match what the counts actually support.

> **Criterion.** A passing rewrite names a population that **does not include everybody in the room.** "Engineers at this customer" almost never passes; "engineers on the two teams that own services with a shared schema dependency" usually does. If most of your cases came from one team, the honest claim is about that team and the finding says so.

**5 · Go back to your own item 2, then break it.** Find what you wrote to opening item 2 — *which one is wrong?* **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference.

> **Criterion.** The question has no answer as asked, and a passing response says so and then names a candidate distinction. If you picked one informant, note which one — it is almost always the account more compatible with the work you were hoping to do, and noticing that about yourself is worth more than the exercise.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). **School, university and laboratory evidence; no adult-professional study in its reference list.**

**6 · Three questions, out loud.** Present the finding in one minute. The room asks: **which cases? who is not in it? what would you withdraw it for?**

> **What to notice.** The first two are answerable from the artefact and the third is not. **A learner who did not build the hierarchy can usually answer the first two and cannot answer the third**, which is the only detection this session has for a delegated consolidation — and it is a weak one, because a careful learner can reconstruct an answer afterwards. Say so when you use it.

## What this does not establish

**Nothing here shows that consolidated findings produce better systems than anecdote.**

The consolidation method used here comes from a contextual design text **read in full for this repository**, which states that its effectiveness rests on practitioner experience and not on comparative evidence, and that its sampling guidance is heuristic. The case-before-theme discipline comes from a first-edition field manual whose **later edition has not been read here**, and which reports no controlled comparison. The segmentation argument comes from a customer-conversation handbook whose local copy carries **no publisher, year or edition metadata**, and which explicitly disclaims measured effects and validated thresholds.

**A set of conversations can expose mechanisms and meanings. It cannot establish prevalence.** Do not turn repeated anecdotes into frequency claims — that failure is the one this session most easily produces, and no source here licenses it.

**The retrieval material is an analogy, and analogies do not transfer warrant.** BM25F's field-combination rule is a fact about a ranking function, not evidence about consolidating interviews; the monograph behind it is paywalled and **its full text was not retrieved for this repository**, so several of its specifics are reconstructed. The benchmark result is a 2021 snapshot whose model rankings have since changed and which has itself become a target of optimisation. **No effect size from any of it is asserted, and none of it establishes anything about discovery.**

**The clustering statistics are analytical, not empirical.** The clustered-standard-error argument is a derivation; its worked magnitudes rest on stylised assumptions and **no number from it appears here**. It tells you your precision is overstated when units are grouped; it does not tell you by how much in your case, and this session offers no method for computing that.

**The leaderboard audit is a governance critique of one platform at one moment.** Its allocation figures are external estimates, this repository's note is written from the abstract, and nothing in it establishes that your consolidation is distorted — only that aggregates produced under unequal access have been shown to drift in a describable way.

**The claim that a team argument is where the missing distinction gets found is design reasoning with no external warrant**, and the prohibition on delegated clustering rests on one organisation's marking of its own work, which is evidence that the belief is held rather than that it is correct.
