# S019 · The Commitment Ask

**Module:** [M04](/wiki/modules/M04.md) · **Quarter:** [Q2](/wiki/quarters/Q2.md) · **Complexity class:** 2
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** none
**Whitepaper:** [WP-019](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-019.md)

## The situation

The discovery went well. Everybody was helpful, the sponsor is enthusiastic, and three people have said they would love to be involved.

You have nothing.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught.

1. A stakeholder says: *this is exactly what we need — send me a proposal and I'll take it forward.* What have you got?
2. Rank these by what they cost the person giving them: a follow-up meeting, a named engineer for two days, an introduction to their security reviewer, a signed statement of intent, access to representative data.
3. Somebody agrees to everything you ask. What should you check before you write it down?
4. You ask for a commitment and they say no. **Is that a worse outcome than item 1?**
5. A user in the affected team has no budget, no authority and no say. What may you ask of them?

**Item 4 is the session.** A clear refusal ends an unresolved position and tells you something. A warm sentence about the future tells you nothing and feels better.

## What you must produce

A **commitment ledger** for your engagement. One row per stakeholder:

- **Who they are**, by the authority they hold.
- **The smallest ethical commitment** that would make your next step credible.
- **What it costs them** — meaningful time, reputational exposure, or money.
- **Whether they can actually give it**, given their authority.
- **What was asked, and what happened** — given, refused, deferred with a named condition, or not asked.
- **What a refusal would teach you.**

## What good looks like

- Every commitment is something the person **gives up**, not something they say. Time that is already theirs to waste is not time. A free trial that costs nothing to open is not a trial.
- The ask is **the smallest one that would work**, not the largest one you could get away with. An oversized ask that fails teaches you about the ask rather than about the customer.
- Deferrals are converted into **a person, a condition, a date or a next action**, or recorded as not converted.
- **At least one row records a refusal or a non-advancement**, and the ledger treats it as evidence rather than as a gap.
- Nobody is asked for a commitment they lack the authority to give, and nobody without a legitimate currency is asked to surrender one.
- **The ledger contains at least one ask that was never made**, honestly recorded, with the reason.

## How it goes wrong

- **The meeting as progress** — another conversation is scheduled and the pipeline is reported as advancing. *Warning sign:* your most recent evidence of seriousness is a calendar entry.
- **The costless trial** — an account was opened, nobody changed a workflow, nobody supplied data, and you have written down that they are piloting. *Warning sign:* you cannot say what the trial cost anybody.
- **The unauthorised commitment** — an enthusiastic person committed something that belongs to somebody else. *Warning sign:* the commitment is real and the person cannot name who else must agree.
- **The extracted commitment** — you applied pressure, they conceded, and the concession will be quietly withdrawn. *Warning sign:* the commitment was given in the room and has not been repeated since.
- **The ask never made** — you finished the engagement without asking, so you have neither an advancement nor a refusal. *Warning sign:* everyone is still friendly and nothing has a date.
- **The affected non-buyer treated as a lead** — you asked somebody with no authority and no stake in the purchase to demonstrate seriousness. *Warning sign:* your ask would embarrass you if their manager heard it.

## Do it

Build the ledger, then rehearse the three hardest asks in pairs, then rebuild it.

The currencies are **meaningful time, reputational risk, and money**, and in an enterprise engagement they usually appear as: named engineer time with a date attached, access to representative rather than sample data, a named security or compliance reviewer, a pilot cohort, an agreed workflow change, procurement engagement, or explicit budget sponsorship. Which one is appropriate depends on the person's authority, and asking for the wrong currency reads as not having understood the organisation.

**Then check the ask against the person.** A user in an affected team may have no legitimate currency at all, and the correct ask may be nothing more than continued honest participation. Discovery is not a sales funnel, and people who bear the consequences of a system without controlling its purchase are owed truthful framing rather than a request for evidence of seriousness. This constraint is stated in the interviewing literature and it is not stated in the corpus.

Now the one place where the corpus and the research agree, and it is worth saying so out loud: **the practice material behind this stage insists that a session ending in thanks and good feelings with no committed next step has failed, and names awareness and enthusiasm as unacceptable outcomes.** That is the same claim the customer-conversation literature makes about advancement, arrived at independently, from a delivery organisation rather than from a research tradition.

**Two independent arrivals is not evidence, but it is worth more than one assertion**, and this session says so rather than pretending the agreement is a finding.

Finally, connect to [S009](/wiki/seminars/S009.md). A refusal is only informative if you know what it cost the person to refuse. If declining is free for them, their no is as weak as their yes.

## Show it

Make one ask, live, to a peer playing the stakeholder — who has been instructed to be warm, agreeable, and to concede nothing.

**You are marked on whether you left with a refusal.** Not a commitment. A refusal is a pass; an unconverted warm sentence is not.

## What this does not establish

Nothing here shows that engagements with commitments succeed more often than engagements without them.

The commitment and advancement framework comes from a customer-conversation handbook whose local copy carries **no publisher, year or edition statement**, and which is explicit that it reports no controlled comparison, no measured effect and no validated threshold. Its examples are the author's own experience. **It is a diagnostic vocabulary, not a predictor.**

**A commitment is evidence of seriousness, not proof of anything.** A stakeholder may commit for political reasons, for experimental reasons, or to maintain a relationship. A person with a genuine need may be unable to commit at all. Both cases are common and neither is a signal about the problem.

The corpus's insistence that thanks without commitment is failure carries **no outcome data of any kind** behind it, and no engagement it declined on those grounds.

## Perishable content in this session

This stage has no platform anchor, so the second column records organisational and tooling instances rather than a version-bound service.

| Durable claim | Perishable instance |
|---|---|
| A commitment is identified by what was given up | Present procurement conventions and what a stakeholder can commit within them |
| A clear refusal is more informative than a warm deferral | Current expectations about how refusal is expressed in a given customer culture |
| The ask must match the authority, and some people have no currency to give | The customer's current organisational structure and who holds which decision |
| A trial that cost nobody anything is not evidence | Present trial and sandbox provisioning practice |
