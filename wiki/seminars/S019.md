# S019 · The Commitment Ask

**Module:** [M04](/wiki/modules/M04.md) · **Quarter:** [Q2](/wiki/quarters/Q2.md) · **Complexity class:** 2
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** Provisioned throughput for Foundry models — a commitment is a capacity decision with a price and a term
**Whitepaper:** [WP-019](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-019.md)
**Retrieves from:** S009, S016, S018
**Entry state:** this-programme-only

---

## The situation

The discovery went well. Everybody was helpful, the sponsor is enthusiastic, and three people have said they would love to be involved.

You have nothing.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught.

1. A stakeholder says: *this is exactly what we need — send me a proposal and I'll take it forward.* What have you got?
2. Rank these by what they cost the person giving them: a follow-up meeting, a named engineer for two days, an introduction to their security reviewer, a signed statement of intent, access to representative data.
3. Somebody agrees to everything you ask. What should you check before you write it down?
4. You ask for a commitment and they say no. **Is that a worse outcome than item 1?**
5. A user in the affected team has no budget, no authority and no say. What may you ask of them?

**Item 4 is the session.** A clear refusal ends an unresolved position and tells you something. A warm sentence about the future tells you nothing and feels better.

## What you must produce

A **commitment ledger** for your engagement. One row per stakeholder:

- **Who they are**, by the authority they hold.
- **The smallest ethical commitment** that would make your next step credible.
- **What it costs them** — meaningful time, reputational exposure, or money.
- **Whether they can actually give it**, given their authority.
- **What was asked, and what happened** — given, refused, deferred with a named condition, or not asked.
- **What a refusal would teach you.**

## The material

### A commitment is something the stakeholder gives up

The distinction the whole session rests on is between **commitment** — a stakeholder gives up something they value — and **advancement** — the relationship moves to a defined next state ([research/05-fde-craft/fitzpatrick-the-mom-test.md](/research/05-fde-craft/fitzpatrick-the-mom-test.md)). Neither is a warm sentence about the future.

The reason the distinction is load-bearing is that expressed enthusiasm costs nothing to produce, is socially rewarded, and is what a professional supplies when a guest has spent two days being helpful. It therefore carries no information about whether anything will happen. **If you cannot say what the commitment cost them, you do not have one.**

The source is blunt about the near-misses. **A trial is not automatically commitment**: opening a free account may cost almost nothing, while onboarding a team, supplying data, changing a workflow or accepting a public case study may cost a great deal. The unit is what *this person* is actually sacrificing, not the category of the act. And a vague future offer — an introduction later, a note at launch — remains weak until it acquires **a person, a condition, a date or a next action.**

**Its limits.** A founder-oriented practitioner handbook; the local copy read here carries **no publisher, year or edition statement**; it reports no controlled comparison, no measured effect and no validated threshold, and its conversation counts and timings are the author's own heuristics. **It is a diagnostic vocabulary, not a predictor.**

### Three currencies, and what they look like in an enterprise

The currencies are **meaningful time, reputational risk, and money**, and their evidential strength rises as the cost to that particular stakeholder becomes more real.

In an enterprise engagement they usually appear as: named engineer time with a date attached; access to representative rather than sample data; a named security or compliance reviewer; a pilot cohort; an agreed workflow change; procurement engagement; explicit budget sponsorship. **That mapping is this repository's course-design inference and is recorded as such in the source note** — it is not a claim the book makes about forward-deployed work, and you should treat it as a starting list rather than a taxonomy.

Which currency is appropriate depends on the person's authority, and **asking for the wrong currency reads as not having understood the organisation.** An enthusiastic person committing something that belongs to somebody else has told you a fact about the organisation rather than about their seriousness, and the ledger's authority field exists to catch exactly that.

### A refusal is the second-best outcome, and the deferral is the worst

This is the session's counter-intuitive claim and it is the one to hold.

A clear rejection ends an unresolved position and supplies negative evidence, which is otherwise almost unobtainable in discovery. **A warm deferral supplies nothing and feels better**, which is exactly why it accumulates: the deferral is invisible to your own organisation while the refusal is not. The incentive runs precisely backwards, and the practitioner's characteristic failure is therefore not a poor ask but an absent one.

The qualifier matters. **A refusal is only informative if it was cheap enough for the person to give.** If declining is free for them, their no is as weak as their yes; and if declining is dangerous for them, you have learned about their situation rather than about the work. That is why [S009](/wiki/seminars/S009.md) is retrieved here, and it is why a stakeholder who cannot safely disagree in front of a particular colleague should not be asked in front of that colleague ([research/04-professional-formation/edmondson-psychological-safety.md](/research/04-professional-formation/edmondson-psychological-safety.md)). **That source is a trade book by the construct's originator, a narrative review with no protocol and no reported disconfirming study; the 1999 paper was not read here.**

### The alternative to agreement is the standard against which an ask is judged

The commitment ask is a negotiation, and the negotiation literature supplies the frame ([research/05-fde-craft/fisher-ury-getting-to-yes.md](/research/05-fde-craft/fisher-ury-getting-to-yes.md)).

The operative concept is **the alternative to agreement, on both sides.** Yours is what you do if this ends without agreement; theirs is what they do. An offer — including the offer you are making, which is *give me this and I will continue* — is acceptable when it beats the alternative and unacceptable when it does not. **Work out both alternatives before you ask**, because an ask made without knowing what the customer's next-best option is will be sized wrong in one direction or the other.

Two further moves from the same source apply directly. **Negotiation jujitsu**: when a stakeholder responds to your ask with a position — *we never give external parties data access* — treat it as one option and ask about the interest and the principle behind it rather than mirroring it. And **after asking, let the silence stand.** The ask is a question, and rescuing somebody from answering a question is how an ask becomes a conversation about scheduling.

**Its limits.** A practitioner framework developed through analytic distinction and worked cases; **it reports no controlled comparison of anything**, and its historical examples illustrate mechanism without isolating causes. No effectiveness claim for teaching it is warranted by it.

### Some people have no currency, and may not be asked for one

Discovery is not a sales funnel. A user in an affected team may have no legitimate currency at all — no budget, no authority, no say — and the correct ask of them may be nothing more than continued honest participation.

People who bear the consequences of a system without controlling its purchase are owed truthful framing rather than a request for evidence of seriousness. The interviewing literature states this constraint plainly, including that an incentive must not become coercion and that the researcher's need for a result does not override a participant's withdrawal ([research/05-fde-craft/portigal-interviewing-users.md](/research/05-fde-craft/portigal-interviewing-users.md)). **The corpus behind this stage does not state it**, and the difference is worth noticing rather than smoothing over. **First edition; its consent and incentive material is a practitioner account from one jurisdiction at one time and is not legal advice.**

The standard's test is deliberately about your own discomfort: **would this ask embarrass you if their manager heard it?**

### The platform makes the same distinction in money, and it is not a metaphor

Here is why this session has a platform anchor at all.

Buying dedicated model capacity forces every distinction above into a form that has a price and a term ([research/06-microsoft-platform/provisioned-throughput-for-foundry-models.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/provisioned-throughput-for-foundry-models.md)). A provisioned deployment **holds a fixed amount of processing capacity exclusively for your deployment's use, whether or not requests are being made** — and everything else follows from that clause. Capacity held whether or not it is used has to be paid for whether or not it is used, which is why the billing unit changes from tokens to time, and why the deployment stops being a configuration setting and becomes a capacity decision.

The distinction to carry into the ledger is **quota against capacity.** Quota is a policy limit enforced by the platform and **it has no associated cost**. Capacity is the actual amount available to be deployed. The documentation's call-out is the sentence to memorise: **having quota does not guarantee that capacity is available**, and a deployment requesting more than the region holds simply fails. Permission is not availability.

Read that against a stakeholder. **A person's authority is quota. Their willingness this quarter is capacity.** A sponsor may be entirely entitled to release two engineers and entirely unable to, and the two facts feel identical in a meeting. The ledger's authority field asks the first question; the ask itself is the only way to answer the second.

Two more properties transfer without strain. Capacity **changes throughout the day based on demand**, so the answer to *can I have this* is time-dependent rather than a fact about the organisation. And releasing capacity is a one-way door in practice even though it looks reversible: deleting or scaling down a deployment returns capacity to the pool with **no guarantee the same capacity is available later.** A commitment you release politely is not a commitment you can retrieve politely.

### Deploy, verify, then commit — and never scale a commitment with traffic

The documentation's procurement guidance is the transferable part, and it is stated in the correct order.

**Reservations are a discount applied to the billing meter, not to individual deployments, and they do not guarantee capacity.** The instruction is explicit: create deployments to confirm that capacity is available, *then* purchase the reservation to lock in the rate. **The intuitive sequence — commit, then deploy — is exactly wrong**, and it is the sequence a discovery engineer follows every time they write a statement of work before establishing that the named engineer will actually be released.

The second warning is against treating dedicated capacity as an autoscaling mechanism, and the two reasons given are worth transposing directly: capacity might not be available when you need to scale back up, and continuous hourly billing at high utilisation typically exceeds the committed rate. **A commitment repeatedly given and withdrawn costs more than one commitment held, and may not be available the third time you ask.** Every experienced practitioner knows this about goodwill and very few write it down.

**Spillover** is the graceful-degradation pattern: when the reserved capacity is saturated and the platform returns an error, overflow requests are redirected to shared capacity. Note the trigger — **an error response, not a prediction.** The commitment ledger's equivalent is naming, before you ask, what you will do when the answer is no; and the discipline is the same, because a fallback designed after the refusal is an improvisation.

**Its limits.** First-party product documentation, definitional rather than empirical. **Perishable**: product names, quota defaults, model ratios, minimum deployment sizes and portal paths change frequently, and the concrete figures live on companion pages that were not fetched for this repository and are not reproduced anywhere. It compares one vendor's capacity model against nothing, and its choice architecture is the vendor's.

### Size the ask before you make it, or a yes will tell you nothing

The last idea comes from the measurement literature and it is the one that makes the ledger's *smallest ethical ask* field more than a slogan.

An evaluation has a minimum effect it is capable of detecting, and doing that analysis **before** the run is what turns "we tested and found nothing" into a statement about the system rather than about the test ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md)).

Transpose it. **If the thing you are about to ask for would be granted just as readily by an unserious customer as by a serious one, the answer cannot discriminate and the ask is decorative.** A follow-up meeting fails this test almost always. Named engineer time with a date usually passes it. The question to ask yourself before you open your mouth is what a yes and a no would each rule out — and if the answer is the same in both cases, ask for something else.

**Its limits.** Analytical rather than empirical; its illustrative magnitudes rest on stylised assumptions and **no number from it appears here**; and it addresses comparing scores rather than choosing what to score, so a well-sized ask about an irrelevant commitment is still irrelevant.

### Where the corpus and the literature agree

One place in this stage, and it is worth saying out loud rather than burying.

The practice material behind this module insists that a session ending in thanks and good feelings with no committed next step **has failed**, and names awareness and enthusiasm as explicitly unacceptable outcomes ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md)). That is the same claim the customer-conversation literature makes about advancement, arrived at independently, from a delivery organisation rather than from a research tradition.

**Two independent arrivals is not evidence, but it is worth more than one assertion**, and this session says so rather than pretending the agreement settles anything. The corpus is access-controlled, held here only as an original paraphrase, and it publishes **no outcome data of any kind** — including none for this claim.

## The workflow

**Turn one commitment into a capacity plan with a price and a term, then attack the plan.**

You will size a provisioned deployment from the traffic your discovery implies. **Every step is executable on paper**; the platform steps confirm what the arithmetic already told you. **The concrete per-model figures live on companion pages this repository did not fetch, so none appears below and none should appear in your plan without a citation and a date.**

**Step 1 — write the traffic model your discovery supports, and mark each input's provenance.** The sizing inputs are the request shape — expected requests per minute, average prompt size in input tokens, average response size in output tokens — plus the output-to-input ratio and the cache rate. **Beside each, write whether it came from an observation, a report or an inference.** This is [S017](/wiki/seminars/S017.md)'s separation applied to a number, and it is the point of the exercise: capacity planning is an inference from a traffic model, so **every simplification in the traffic description propagates into the answer.**

```yaml
# capacity-plan.yaml — the numbers are yours; the provenance field is the lesson.
traffic_model:
  requests_per_minute:
    value: '<from your discovery>'
    basis: observed | reported | inferred
  avg_input_tokens:
    value: '<...>'
    basis: observed | reported | inferred
  avg_output_tokens:
    value: '<...>'
    basis: observed | reported | inferred
  output_to_input_ratio:
    value: '<per-model; verify against current documentation>'
    basis: vendor_doc
    checked_on: '<date>'
  cache_rate:
    value: '<...>'
    basis: inferred

# Cached input tokens do not consume dedicated capacity. Output tokens cost more
# capacity than input tokens. Both facts move the answer more than the traffic does.
sizing:
  normalized_tpm: '<derived from the three inputs above>'
  input_tpm_per_unit: '<per-model divisor; verify, and record the date you verified it>'
  required_units: '<normalized_tpm divided by input_tpm_per_unit>'

routing:
  variant: global | data_zone | regional   # availability falls as residency tightens
  why: '<the compliance constraint that decided it, named>'

fallback:
  spillover_to: '<a standard deployment in the same resource>'
  triggered_by: 'an error response, not a prediction'
  user_visible_change: '<state it, or you have not designed the fallback>'

commitment:
  sequence: [deploy, confirm capacity, then reserve]
  note: 'A reservation discounts a meter. It holds nothing.'
```

**Step 2 — vary the cache rate and the output ratio, and watch the answer move.** Recompute with a higher cache rate and then with a heavier output ratio. **The requirement is more sensitive to these two than to the request count**, which is the opposite of what most teams assume when they estimate. Write one sentence naming which input your plan is most sensitive to and how confident you are in it.

**Step 3 — separate quota from capacity explicitly, in writing.** Record what your subscription is *permitted* to deploy in your region and deployment type, and record separately whether that capacity is *available now*. They are different facts, held in different places, and they change on different schedules. **A plan that records only the first has recorded a permission and called it a resource.**

**Step 4 — choose the routing variant and pay for it in availability.** Global, data-zone and single-region routing form a residency-versus-availability gradient. Name the compliance requirement that decides yours, then state the availability you gave up to satisfy it. **A compliance requirement with no stated cost has not been costed.**

**Step 5 — design the fallback before you need it.** Configure spillover, and then answer the question the configuration does not: **what changes for the user when overflow requests are served by shared capacity?** If you cannot say, you have a redirect rather than a degradation strategy.

**Step 6 — map the plan onto your commitment ledger, row by row.** For each row: what is this stakeholder's quota, what is their capacity, what does the commitment cost them per unit of time, what is the term, and what is your spillover if they refuse? **A ledger row with no fallback is an ask you have not sized.**

**Step 7 — attack your own plan and your own ask.** Give the agent your capacity plan and your three hardest ledger rows and ask it to argue that **the commitment is not worth what it costs the customer.** Then ask it to name the single observation that would settle the disagreement. Record what you kept and what changed. **Do not ask it to strengthen your ask** — an instrument that improved your position was not used as an instrument.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That a commitment has a term, a unit and a price, and that the arithmetic is sensitive to inputs you guessed rather than to the ones you argued about. It also makes the quota-and-capacity distinction physical: **you will hold a permission you cannot spend**, which is the exact experience of a sponsor who is entitled to release an engineer and cannot.

## What good looks like

- Every commitment is something the person **gives up**, not something they say. Time that is already theirs to waste is not time. A free trial that costs nothing to open is not a trial.
- The ask is **the smallest one that would work**, not the largest one you could get away with. An oversized ask that fails teaches you about the ask rather than about the customer.
- Deferrals are converted into **a person, a condition, a date or a next action**, or recorded as not converted.
- **At least one row records a refusal or a non-advancement**, and the ledger treats it as evidence rather than as a gap.
- Nobody is asked for a commitment they lack the authority to give, and nobody without a legitimate currency is asked to surrender one.
- **The ledger contains at least one ask that was never made**, honestly recorded, with the reason.

## How it goes wrong

- **The meeting as progress** — another conversation is scheduled and the pipeline is reported as advancing. *Warning sign:* your most recent evidence of seriousness is a calendar entry.
- **The costless trial** — an account was opened, nobody changed a workflow, nobody supplied data, and you have written down that they are piloting. *Warning sign:* you cannot say what the trial cost anybody.
- **The unauthorised commitment** — an enthusiastic person committed something that belongs to somebody else. *Warning sign:* the commitment is real and the person cannot name who else must agree.
- **The extracted commitment** — you applied pressure, they conceded, and the concession will be quietly withdrawn. *Warning sign:* the commitment was given in the room and has not been repeated since.
- **The ask never made** — you finished the engagement without asking, so you have neither an advancement nor a refusal. *Warning sign:* everyone is still friendly and nothing has a date.
- **The affected non-buyer treated as a lead** — you asked somebody with no authority and no stake in the purchase to demonstrate seriousness. *Warning sign:* your ask would embarrass you if their manager heard it.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed here.** One product of this session is assessed at [S020](/wiki/seminars/S020.md): that the commitment ledger contains at least one refusal or recorded non-advancement.

**1 · Rank five asks by what they cost.** A follow-up meeting; a named engineer for two days; an introduction to their security reviewer; a signed statement of intent; access to representative data. Rank them by cost **to the person giving them**, then rank them again for a different stakeholder.

> **What you should find.** The ranking changes between stakeholders, which is the point. For a sponsor, a signed statement is cheap and an engineer's time is expensive; for a team lead the reverse. **A commitment is measured in what this person sacrifices, not in the category of the act.** If your two rankings are identical you have ranked the acts rather than the people.

**2 · Convert three deferrals.** *Send me a proposal and I'll take it forward.* · *Let us know when it launches.* · *I'd love to be involved.* Convert each into a person, a condition, a date or a next action — or record that it could not be converted, with what was said.

> **Model answers.** *Who else needs to see the proposal before it moves, and when do they next meet?* · *What would have to be true for you to trial this before launch?* · *What could you commit two hours a week to, starting when?* **A passing conversion produces an answer that could be false.** "They said they'd look at it" is a report of a deferral wearing a date.

**3 · Size the ask before you make it.** For your three hardest ledger rows, write what a yes would rule out and what a no would rule out.

> **Criterion.** If the two answers are the same, the ask cannot discriminate and is decorative. **A follow-up meeting fails this almost always**, because a serious and an unserious customer both grant one. Named engineer time with a date usually passes. This is the discovery version of asking, before you run an evaluation, what effect it is capable of detecting.

**4 · Build the capacity plan and find its sensitivity.** Run the workflow through step 2 and name the input your requirement is most sensitive to.

> **What you should find.** The cache rate and the output-to-input ratio move the answer more than the request count does, and both are usually the inputs you guessed rather than the ones you argued about. **State how confident you are in each, and then notice that your plan's precision is entirely bounded by your least confident input.** A capacity plan is an inference from a traffic model, and the traffic model was built out of interviews.

**5 · The live ask.** Make one ask to a peer playing the stakeholder — warm, agreeable, and instructed to concede nothing.

> **Scoring criterion.** **You pass by leaving with a refusal.** Not a commitment. An unconverted warm sentence is a fail, and a commitment is a pass only if you can say what it cost them. When they respond with a position rather than an answer — *we never give external parties data access* — the correct move is to ask about the interest and the standard behind it rather than to restate your request. **Then let the silence stand.** Most learners fill it, and filling it is how an ask becomes a conversation about scheduling.

**6 · Go back to your own item 4, then break it.** Find what you wrote to opening item 4 — whether a refusal is a worse outcome than an enthusiastic request for a proposal. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference.

> **Criterion.** A passing answer says the refusal is better and names what it resolves: an unresolved position ends, a population question is answered, and the cost of the refusal to the person tells you how much their yes would have been worth. A failing answer treats the refusal as a setback to be recovered from. **Then say honestly whether your original answer was about the evidence or about how the two outcomes would look to your own organisation.**
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). **School, university and laboratory evidence; no adult-professional study in its reference list.**

**7 · Name the sequence.** Closed book: in what order do you deploy, confirm capacity and buy the reservation, and why is the intuitive order wrong?

> **Answer.** Deploy, confirm that capacity is available, then reserve. A reservation is a discount applied to a billing meter and **holds nothing**, so committing first can leave you paying a discounted rate for capacity you cannot obtain. **Then transpose it:** a statement of work signed before the named engineer has actually been released is the same error, and it fails in the same direction.

## What this does not establish

**Nothing here shows that engagements with commitments succeed more often than engagements without them.**

The commitment and advancement framework comes from a customer-conversation handbook whose local copy carries **no publisher, year or edition statement**, and which is explicit that it reports no controlled comparison, no measured effect and no validated threshold. Its examples are the author's own experience. **It is a diagnostic vocabulary, not a predictor.** The enterprise translation of its three currencies is this repository's course-design inference and is recorded as such.

The negotiation material is a **practitioner framework that reports no controlled comparison of anything**; its historical cases illustrate mechanism without isolating causes, and no effectiveness claim for teaching it is warranted by it.

**A commitment is evidence of seriousness, not proof of anything.** A stakeholder may commit for political reasons, for experimental reasons, or to maintain a relationship. A person with a genuine need may be unable to commit at all. Both cases are common and neither is a signal about the problem.

The corpus's insistence that thanks without commitment is failure carries **no outcome data of any kind** behind it, and no engagement it declined on those grounds. Its agreement with the published literature is an agreement, not a finding, and it cannot be checked by any reader outside the organisation that holds it.

**The platform material is definitional and perishable.** It states how one vendor's capacity model behaves; it makes no performance claim that could be independently checked, offers no comparison against other capacity models, and its concrete figures live on companion pages this repository did not fetch. **No per-model ratio, minimum size, price or quota default is asserted anywhere in this session.** The analogy between organisational authority and platform quota is this programme's, not the documentation's, and it is an analogy rather than an argument.

**The ask-sizing analogy is exactly that.** Power analysis is a statistical procedure for a measurement with a defined variance; a commitment ask has neither. What transfers is the discipline of deciding what an answer could rule out before you obtain it. **No number, threshold or method from the statistical source is used here.**

**The psychological-safety material is direction-only and its level is wrong for this use.** The construct is theorised at group level and this session applies it to an individual stakeholder's ability to refuse. That is a stretch, it is recorded as one, and the trade book behind it is a narrative review with no protocol and no reported disconfirming study.
