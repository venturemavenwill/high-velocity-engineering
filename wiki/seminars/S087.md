# S087 · Engineering Feedback That Goes Somewhere

**Whitepaper:** [WP-087](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-087.md)

## The situation

Three pieces of friction with the tooling were found during this engagement. Two were written up and filed. Nobody can say what happened to either. The third was never written, because the engineer who hit it left that week.

There is a published standard for how to write one of these. It specifies a title, a summary, a categorisation block naming the product area, the kind of problem, the stage of work it was hit at, its impact and its time frame, an ordinal priority, then steps to reproduce it and any notes. It is a good standard and the fields are the right fields.

**It describes no routing, no triage, no ownership, no service-level expectation and no closure loop, anywhere.** It does not claim to. It teaches an engineer how to write a report a product team could act on, and stops.

**Answer these in writing before reading further.** Item 5 is the honest one and it is what this session is about.

1. Write the third report — the one that was never written. Whose knowledge is now unavailable, and what did it cost?
2. For the two that were filed: what would tell you the report was never read? Name the observable.
3. Who receives a report of this kind in your organisation, and what is their obligation on receiving it?
4. What is the difference between a report a product team *can* act on and one a product team *will* act on?
5. If nobody ever acts on any of them, what should an engineer do differently — and is *stop writing them* on your list of answers?

Most people produce a serviceable answer to 1 and 3, hedge on 2, and leave 5 blank or answer it with an exhortation. **A writing standard with nothing behind it is a request for unpaid work from the person who hit the problem, and the person who hits the problem knows that.** Any loop you design has to survive that reading, and a design that assumes goodwill has assumed away the only interesting constraint.

## What you must produce

Two things, and the second is the session's work.

1. **One feedback item**, written to the published standard, about something this engagement actually hit. Reproducible by somebody who was not there, specific enough to be implemented or refuted, and about a defect or a friction rather than about a preference.
2. **The routing the standard omits** — six answers in order: who receives it; what they are obliged to do and by when; who owns it afterwards; what states it can be in; what closes it; and what tells the original reporter that any of this happened.
3. **One published silence**, found by you in first-party documentation for a tool this engagement used, written up in the same form. Not a bug — an absence that a person relying on the documentation would need and cannot get.

## The material

### The standard is good, and it is the shorter half of the problem

The openly published engineering handbook this programme draws on carries the field set in full ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md)). A title. A summary. A categorisation block naming the service, the type of problem, the stage of work it was hit at, the impact and the time frame. An ordinal priority. Then reproduction steps and notes.

Those are the right fields, and they are right for a reason worth stating: **they make the report assessable by somebody who was not present.** A report that names the stage of work is a report whose reader can judge whether it matters. A report with reproduction steps is a report that can be refuted. **A report that can be refuted is a report that can be believed**, and that equivalence is most of what separates engineering feedback from complaint.

**And that is where the source stops.** No routing, no triage, no ownership, no service-level expectation and no closure loop appears anywhere in the section. It does not claim to describe one. **A reader who takes it for an institutional mechanism has supplied that reading themselves.**

Two limits on the source travel with every use of it. **The playbook must be cited by page and by that page's own footer date, never as a whole** — its pages carry individual dates spanning years, and **the engineering-feedback guidance page carries the oldest date on the whole site**. That is not a criticism of the standard; it is the single most relevant fact about it, and a reader who cites the site rather than the page will not have noticed. The second limit is that the same handbook asserts, on a different page, that the cost of doing code reviews is negative, with no evidence, no measurement, no comparison and no source. **This repository records that as a specimen and reproduces neither it nor the three other unsupported figures alongside it.** Naming the pattern is the teaching; repeating the figures would install the hazard.

### What a vendor's own documentation looks like when it is trying to receive feedback

The session's anchor is a first-party documentation family that is unusually candid, and its candour is exactly what makes it a good specimen for this session ([research/06-microsoft-platform/learn-mcp-server-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/learn-mcp-server-docs.md)).

Start with what it admits about its own failure mode. The Get Started page carries a section headed **"Set instructions (recommended)"** whose opening line is **"When the AI agent doesn't use the MCP tool when expected, it may be useful to configure specific instructions for the tool."** The project's own repository is blunter: **"Even tool-friendly models like Claude Sonnet 4 sometimes fail to call MCP tools by default; use system prompts to encourage usage."**

Read that as a feedback artefact rather than as a caveat. **A vendor is documenting, in its own getting-started material, that its product's primary failure mode is silent non-invocation — and then shipping a remedy whose effectiveness it does not measure.** That is a defect report the vendor has written about itself. It has a reproduction condition, an impact and a proposed mitigation, and no result.

Now the closure loop. The release notes record **seven dated changes between 2025-06-12 and 2026-03-23** — the initial release, **two tool additions after it**, the general-availability declaration, and three changes to the access surface. **That register is the only publicly observable evidence that anything a user reported was ever acted on**, and it does not attribute a single change to a report. The best-practices page contains an invitation to experiment and give feedback that begins to enumerate failure modes and **was truncated in the fetch**, so this note cannot say what it asks for. **A change log that never says why is a closure notice addressed to nobody.**

And here is the fact this session and the next are both built on. The engineering blog describing the knowledge service behind the server records that the team built a **"golden dataset"** of questions, approved answers and best source articles, and used it for before-and-after tests on indexing changes. **It publishes no result from any of it.** No recall figure, no ranking metric, no groundedness score, no before-and-after difference of any kind. **The apparatus of measurement is described in detail and its outputs are withheld.**

That is what feedback looks like when it goes somewhere internal and nowhere external. Somebody's report changed an index. A test was run. **Nothing that reached the outside distinguishes that from nothing having happened.**

**The source's limits are severe.** First-party throughout, definitional in authority and evidential nowhere. Extremely perishable — the developer reference reserves the right to change the tool list and its request and response formats without notice. Collapsed sections on three host pages did not expand and were not read. The Terms of Use, a stated condition of use, was not read. **And the project's repository carries outcome claims that are marketing and must not enter teaching material in any form**, including as paraphrase.

### A published silence is the highest-value report you can write

Most feedback is about something present that is wrong. **The more useful kind is about something absent that a reader will assume.**

The same documentation family supplies a clean set of specimens. Freshness is stated as cadence — incremental refresh after content updates and a full refresh once a day — which **bounds nothing about the interval between a page being corrected and that correction becoming retrievable**. Rate limits are confirmed to exist, and **no value, window, error code or retry guidance is published**, so a team planning an agentic loop cannot size it from the documentation. Corpus scope is defined negatively — publicly available documentation, not training or user profile information — with locale coverage, versioned-document handling and non-documentation content all unaddressed. Deprecated content **"redirect[s] to the next best appropriate content"**, so a fetch of a known-good address may silently return something else.

Every one of those is reportable in the standard's own fields. The stage of work is *planning*; the impact is *cannot size a loop*; the reproduction step is *read the page and look for the number*. **A silence written up in the fields of a defect report is a defect report**, and it is the class of report a documentation team can act on most cheaply, because the fix is a sentence.

### Why most reports go nowhere, and why that is a governance fact rather than a moral one

The honest answer to opening item 5 needs a mechanism, and the evaluation-governance literature supplies the cleanest available one.

An audit of the leaderboard that had become the de facto public ranking of frontier models found its scores distorted **by undisclosed platform policy rather than by any technical flaw** ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/singh-leaderboard-illusion.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/singh-leaderboard-illusion.md)). Private testing with the ability to retract unfavourable results produced **"biased Arena scores due to selective disclosure of performance results"**, and the paper's conclusion was **"overfitting to Arena-specific dynamics rather than general model quality"**. Its recommendations are policy recommendations — disclosure of private testing, equitable sampling, transparent deprecation.

**That is the shape of the answer to item 5.** The thing that determines whether a report lands is not the report's quality. It is whether the receiving institution has a published policy about receipt, and whether that policy is observable from outside. The audit's authors could see the distortion **only because battle records were externally visible**; where a process is not externally visible, no amount of good reporting produces evidence that it works.

**The audit's limits:** its allocation figures are external estimates by its own account, its findings are specific to one platform at one time, and **nothing in it establishes that models are not improving** — reading it as leaderboard nihilism misses its reform argument. **No figure from it is reproduced here.**

### Reporting friction is a voice behaviour, and voice behaviour is conditional

One further mechanism, and it is the reason a loop with no legitimate refusal state collapses.

Writing up a defect in somebody else's product is speaking up when silence is cheaper. That is the construct Edmondson's psychological-safety work studies ([research/04-professional-formation/edmondson-psychological-safety.md](/research/04-professional-formation/edmondson-psychological-safety.md)) and Frazier's meta-analysis synthesises ([research/04-professional-formation/frazier-psychological-safety-meta-analysis.md](/research/04-professional-formation/frazier-psychological-safety-meta-analysis.md)). Edmondson's own formulation is that psychological safety is **"the belief that the work environment is safe for interpersonal risk taking"**, and that the construct **"seems to 'live' at the level of the group"** rather than the organisation.

Two consequences follow directly. **First, the report that was never written is the expensive one**, and it was not written for a reason that has nothing to do with the standard. **Second, a loop with no legitimate way to say no will be quietly ignored**, because a receiving team obliged to action everything stops reading, and a reporter who learns that stops reporting. **A considered no, with a reason, is what makes the loop survivable at both ends.**

**The limits on both notes.** The Edmondson note covers a 2019 trade book read in full and a 1999 paper that was **not read**; its case studies are drawn almost entirely from secondary sources. The Frazier note is written from the official abstract of a closed-access meta-analysis. **Direction and mechanism only, and no magnitude from either appears here.**

## The workflow

**Write one report to the standard, then find one silence in the vendor's own documentation and write that to the same standard.**

You will use the Microsoft Learn MCP Server, because it is remote, unauthenticated, free of charge and requires no tenant — which means every learner can run this regardless of licensing.

**Step 1 — attach the server and confirm it is actually being used.** Add the endpoint to your editor's MCP configuration. Then check the References section of a chat response and confirm the tool was called. **Attaching is not invoking**, and when it goes wrong it goes wrong silently: the host documentation states that if you decline trust, "chat requests will continue without using the tools provided by the server." The request succeeds. The answer arrives. It is ungrounded and nothing in the transcript says so.

**Step 2 — pick a question the corpus should answer and a question it should not.** Run both. Record the tool used, the query, the page returned and the date. **The answer is not the artefact; the citation is.**

**Step 3 — go looking for a silence, deliberately.** Choose a quantity a team would need in order to plan: an indexing lag, a rate limit, a default value, a coverage boundary. Search for it, fetch the page most likely to carry it, and read for the absence. **Record where you looked**, because an absence is only evidence if the search was competent.

**Step 4 — write both items to the standard, in a file, in the standard's own fields.**

```yaml
# .feedback/items.yaml   <- the published FIELD SET, plus the loop the standard omits.
# Fields above the divider are specified by the source. Fields below it are not
# specified by anybody, and inventing them is this session's actual work.

- title: "Indexing lag between a corrected page and its retrievability is unbounded"
  summary: >
    Freshness is documented as cadence (incremental after updates; full refresh daily).
    No statement bounds the interval between a page being corrected and that correction
    being returned by search. A team told to "check the docs and record the date" cannot
    know whether the date they recorded is the date the content was true.
  categorisation:
    product_area: "<service or documentation set>"
    problem_type: silence          # defect | friction | silence | regression
    stage_hit: planning            # discovery | planning | build | test | operate
    impact: "cannot size a polling or agentic loop; cannot date a citation"
    time_frame: "blocking for design decisions taken this sprint"
  priority: P2                     # ordinal, per the published scale
  reproduction:
    - "fetch the overview page and the FAQ"
    - "search both for a stated interval or upper bound"
    - "observe: cadence is stated, latency is not"
  notes: "Read on <YYYY-MM-DD>. Cite the page and the date, never the address alone."

  # ---- below this line the published standard specifies NOTHING ----
  loop:
    receives: "<named person or role. NOT a queue.>"
    obligation_on_receipt: "acknowledge and classify within <n> working days"
    owner_after_triage: "<named role>"
    states: [received, triaged, accepted, declined-with-reason, fixed, superseded]
    closes_it: "a published change note that names the report, or a declined-with-reason"
    reporter_sees: "<the observable that distinguishes acted-on from never-opened>"
    if_author_leaves: "<who inherits it, and how they find out>"
    unknown: []                    # write 'unknown' here rather than inventing a process
```

**Step 5 — answer the six loop questions, and write *unknown* where you cannot.** An honest blank is the finding. **Inventing a process you have never seen is the failure mode this session is about**, and it is more tempting than it sounds because the fields look like they want filling.

**Step 6 — find the closure evidence, or record its absence.** Open the product's public release notes and try to attribute one change to one report. Record what the register does and does not say. **A change log that never says why is not a closure loop**, and noticing that is worth more than the report you wrote.

**Step 7 — attack your own report.** One prompt to the agent: *read my reproduction steps as somebody with a different environment and no context, list every step that assumes something I have not stated, then argue that my silence item is a preference rather than a defect.* Read it for the assumption you could not see. Keep what is better than your own text and record what you rejected. **Record separately that the agent shares a model family with the tooling being reported on and is not independent of it**, and that an agent which endorsed your report was not used as an instrument.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** Two things, both physical. **The first is that a silence is findable in about ten minutes and is invisible if you are not looking for it** — you will read the sentence that should have contained the number, and it will read as complete. **The second is that the six loop fields are harder to fill in than the whole report above them**, and you will discover that by trying rather than by being told.

## What good looks like

- The report is reproducible by somebody who was not there. **A report that cannot be reproduced is a complaint with fields.**
- It is specific enough to be assessed, implemented or refuted. A generic report is worse than an absent one, for exactly the reason a generic mitigation is: nothing can be done with it and nobody can say it was wrong.
- **The silence item names where you looked, not just what you did not find.** An absence is evidence only if the search was competent, and a reader must be able to judge that.
- **The routing names a person or a role, not a queue.** A queue is where a report goes to be counted.
- There is a state meaning *seen and declined*, with a reason. **A loop with no legitimate way to say no is a loop that will be quietly ignored**, in exactly the way a compliance regime with no approvable exception is.
- The reporter learns the outcome. **If the only observable difference between a report that was acted on and one that was never opened is on the product team's side, the loop does not exist.**
- The design says what happens to the report when its author leaves, because in this engagement that already happened once.

## How it goes wrong

- **The report as the loop** — a well-written item is filed and the mechanism is considered to exist. *Warning sign:* your account of the process ends with the word *submitted*.
- **Volume as evidence** — the number of reports filed becomes the measure, and their fate is not measured at all. *Warning sign:* somebody can tell you how many were raised this quarter.
- **The unreproducible report** — real friction, badly captured, closed as not reproducible, and the reporter concludes the loop is theatre. *Warning sign:* your steps assume the reader has your environment.
- **The report nobody may decline** — every item must be actioned, so the receiving team stops reading them. *Warning sign:* your design has no state for a considered no.
- **The knowledge that left with the person** — the friction was real, the engineer moved on, and nothing captured it. *Warning sign:* the best story about your tooling this engagement is being told verbally.
- **The invented loop** — the six fields are filled in with a process nobody has ever operated, because a blank looked like a failure. *Warning sign:* you cannot name the person who agreed to the service-level expectation you wrote.
- **The silence reported as a preference** — an absence is written up as a wish, so it is triaged as a feature request and closed. *Warning sign:* your item says *should document* rather than naming who cannot do what without it.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The feedback item and its loop are carried to [S089](/wiki/seminars/S089.md), where the same act is performed on a practice rather than on a product, and to [S090](/wiki/seminars/S090.md) as evidence.

**1 · Sort five statements into defect, silence, preference and closure evidence.**

- *The endpoint returns an error when opened in a browser.* · *No rate limit value is published.* · *The configuration key should be consistent across hosts.* · *A release note records that a tool was added on a stated date.* · *The agent did not call the attached tool and nothing said so.*

> **Model answers.** The first is a **defect** with a documented reproduction — and note that the vendor documents it itself, which is what a mature product does with a known behaviour. The second is a **silence**, and it is the most valuable of the five, because the fix is a sentence and the cost of its absence falls on every team planning a loop. The third is a **preference**: real, reasonable and unactionable as written, because it names no impact. The fourth is **closure evidence**, and it is weak closure evidence, because it does not say why. The fifth is a **defect the vendor has already reported about itself**, in its own getting-started material, with a remedy it does not measure. **The general rule: a statement is a defect if it has a reproduction, a silence if it has an absence with a named consequence, and a preference if it has neither.**

**2 · Find a silence in ten minutes, using the platform.** Attach the Learn MCP Server. Pick a quantity a team would need in order to plan. Search for it, fetch the most likely page, and read for the absence. Record the tool, the query, the page and the date.

> **What you should find.** At least one of: no bound on indexing lag; no rate limit value, window or retry guidance although limits are confirmed to exist; no default token budget; no locale or versioned-document coverage statement; no measure of how often a model declines to call an attached tool. **If you find one of these now published, that is a real finding and this session's material needs revising** — record the page and the date, and file it as a report against this page.

**3 · Go back to your own item 5, then break it.** Find what you wrote for opening item 5 — what an engineer should do differently if nobody ever acts on any of it. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then write the honest version.

> **Criterion.** A pass names a change in behaviour that is rational for the individual engineer under the assumption that most reports go nowhere, and states what the organisation would have to change for the old behaviour to become rational again. *"Keep writing them, because it is the right thing to do"* fails — it is an exhortation, and it will not survive a second unanswered report. *"Write only silences, because they are cheap to write and cheap to fix, and stop writing friction reports until a receipt state exists"* passes: it is a decision, it is defensible, and it names the condition that would reverse it. **A design that assumes goodwill has assumed away the constraint.**
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Attempt attribution and report where it stops.** Open a product's public release notes and try to attribute one change to one external report. Record how far you got.

> **What to expect.** It stops immediately. The register for the anchor product records dated changes across nine months, including two that added tools which did not previously exist, and attributes none of them to anything. **Report that as the result. The failed check is the work**, and it is the same failed traceability check you performed at [S068](/wiki/seminars/S068.md) on a framework that asserted nothing.

**5 · Have your reproduction steps executed by a stranger.** Hand your report to a learner from another team, with no explanation. Their only job is to follow the steps and stop at the first one they cannot.

> **Scoring criterion.** A pass is that they reach the end, or that they stop at a step you can immediately name as an unstated assumption. **A fail is that they stop and you have to explain**, because that is exactly what happens to a real report and nobody is there to explain. Count the assumptions, not the failures; the useful output is the list of things you did not know you knew.

**6 · Design the refusal state and then defend it.** Write the state that means *seen and declined, with a reason*. Then argue against it: why would a receiving team not want one?

> **What to notice.** That the arguments against it are all about appearances and all of them are real — a visible refusal is a record of a decision somebody may be asked about later. **That is the same asymmetry the psychological-safety literature describes on the reporter's side**, arriving on the receiver's, and a loop that ignores it will produce a queue where nothing is ever declined and nothing is ever done.

## What this does not establish

**That any of this reaches a product.** The field set is verified and public. The loop is not described anywhere, by anybody, in any of the material behind this programme — and the source that specifies the fields does not claim to describe one. **This session is written into that absence**, and inferring a practice from an omission is a weak move that this programme has now made in three separate stages. **That makes the loop design class 3.**

**The source's own currency is uneven and visible.** Its pages are individually dated and the dates run across several years with no signal in its navigation distinguishing a maintained page from an abandoned one. **The feedback guidance page carries the oldest date on the whole site.** That is not a criticism of the standard; it is the single most relevant fact about it.

**No outcome is reported for any of it.** Nothing establishes that structured feedback of this kind changes a product, that a routed report is acted on more often than an unrouted one, or that an engineer who receives an outcome writes more reports afterwards. Every one of those is plausible and none is evidenced.

**And the anchor's most important fact is an absence rather than a finding.** A first-party engineering blog describes a golden dataset of questions, approved answers and best source articles, and before-and-after tests on indexing changes, in detail — **and publishes no result from any of it.** No recall figure, no ranking metric, no groundedness score, no difference. **The apparatus is described and its outputs are withheld**, which is the same object you audited at [S068](/wiki/seminars/S068.md) wearing a documentation team's clothes. Nothing here establishes that grounding through that server improves an answer; the strongest claims to that effect are marketing and are prohibited in this wiki.
