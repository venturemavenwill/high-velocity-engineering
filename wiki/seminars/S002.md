## The situation

You wrote a position yesterday. Today somebody asks who you think you are to hold one.

It is a fair question. In most delivery organisations the decision to take work is made by people who are not engineers, and you were assigned afterwards. Before you are taught anything about the role, answer these in writing.

1. Name the last thing you built. Who else could have used it, and would it have worked for them unchanged?
2. If you are judged on the customer's outcome rather than on what you shipped, what changes about how you pick your next task?
3. Who in your organisation is allowed to decide that something you built for one customer becomes part of the product?
4. What would you have to stop doing in order to be accountable for an outcome you do not fully control?
5. The organisation that named this role says in public that it cannot specify the skills the role requires. What does that mean for a programme claiming to teach them?

**Item 3 is the one that matters, and most answers to it are wrong in the same direction.** People assume the decision is theirs because they wrote the code.

## What you must produce

A one-page **role boundary statement**: what you are accountable for, what you may only propose, and — for each thing you may only propose — the named person or function that decides it.

## What good looks like

- Accountability is stated as something the customer experiences, **not as an artefact you deliver.**
- At least one thing you would instinctively claim is explicitly given away, with the holder named.
- The statement distinguishes what you decide from what you recommend, and the two lists are both non-empty.
- Where you accept accountability for an outcome, you name who would notice if it moved.

## How it goes wrong

Four failure modes, each with the warning sign that precedes it.

- **The consultant's shrug** — the role is defined entirely by what it is not. *Warning sign:* your page has more sentences beginning "unlike" than sentences beginning "I am accountable for".
- **Borrowed authority** — you claim decision rights that sit with a product owner, an architect or a security function elsewhere. *Warning sign:* nothing in your statement requires anybody else's agreement.
- **The undivided self** — everything is owned, because giving anything away feels like a demotion. *Warning sign:* your "may only propose" list is empty or contains nothing you care about.
- **Outcome without instrument** — you accept accountability for a result nobody measures. *Warning sign:* you cannot name the person who would produce the number a second time.

## Do it

Work from [Brief A](/wiki/quarters/Q1.md). Write the statement, then hand it to a peer playing the product owner, whose only instruction is to decline your most useful generalisation and give a roadmap reason for declining it.

Rewrite the statement so that it survives the refusal. **A boundary that only holds when the other side agrees is not a boundary.**

## Show it

Read out the "may only propose" list, and nothing else. Your reader has one job: to say whether any item on it is something you would in practice do anyway.

If the answer is yes, the item is on the wrong list, and finding that out here costs nothing.

## What this does not establish

The definitional account of this role comes from the organisation that named it, in material written to recruit people into it. It is primary evidence of that organisation's stated model and evidence of nothing else. It does not show that splitting product engineering from deployment engineering produces better outcomes than not splitting it; no source behind this programme measures that, and the organisation itself offers a statement of values where a causal account would go.

**The same organisation declines, in public, to specify the competencies the role requires.** This programme specifies them anyway, because it must. That is a design decision of this programme's own, and it is stated here rather than presented as something read off the industry.
