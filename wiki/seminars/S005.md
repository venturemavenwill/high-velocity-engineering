# S005 · The Qualification Decision ⊘

**Module:** [M01](/wiki/modules/M01.md) · **Quarter:** [Q1](/wiki/quarters/Q1.md) · **Complexity class:** 1
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** Microsoft Foundry versus Copilot Studio build-path selection, recorded as an architecture decision record against the capability, timeline and control triple
**Whitepaper:** [WP-005](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-005.md)
**Retrieves from:** S001, S002, S003, S004
**Entry state:** this-programme-only

## The situation

Four sessions ago you wrote a position on an engagement you knew almost nothing about. Since then you have located your own boundary, stripped the technology out of the request, and read the room you were invited into.

Today you decide, and somebody argues with you.

Answer these in writing before the session opens. They are the last set you will answer cold in this stage.

1. What do you now believe the problem is, and what changed that belief since [S001](/wiki/seminars/S001.md)?
2. Which of your beliefs about this engagement rests on something you observed, and which on something you were told?
3. Is the disqualifying condition you wrote at S001 still the right one? If you amend it, what fired the amendment?
4. What is the decision, and what would reverse it?
5. Who has to agree with you for the decision to have any effect, and have they?

**Item 2 is where most positions come apart.** Almost everything a learner believes at this point was told to them by somebody with an interest in the answer.

## What you must produce

A **qualification decision** — proceed, proceed on a named condition, or decline — in six parts:

1. **The problem**, stated as a difficulty a person experiences, with no technology in the sentence.
2. **Known and assumed**, in two columns, with each assumed belief naming who told you.
3. **The condition that would reverse the decision**, naming who would observe it and when.
4. **The authority it depends on** — whose agreement the decision requires, and whether you have it.
5. **The build-path decision record** — an architecture decision record for Foundry against Copilot Studio, argued on capability, timeline and control, in `Proposed` status.
6. **The defence** — delivered live, against a challenger.

## The material

### The decision has a shape, and the shape is older than this programme

You have four artefacts and no more information than you had at [S001](/wiki/seminars/S001.md) about whether the engagement is a good idea. That is the normal condition. **A qualification decision is not a decision taken once the facts are in; it is a decision about how much more you are willing to spend finding out.**

What can be made rigorous is the *form*. Microsoft's published engineering playbook specifies the fields of an architecture decision record precisely, and they map onto a qualification decision almost without alteration ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md)): an ascending number and a title; a date; a status drawn from "[Proposed/Accepted/Deprecated/Superseded]"; a context section that must be "a value-neutral description of the forces at play"; a decision section whose sentence begins "We will…" or "We have agreed to…"; and a consequences section.

Three of those do more work than they look like they do.

- **"Value-neutral description of the forces at play"** forbids the commonest defect in a decision document, which is a context section written to justify the decision that follows. If a reader can predict your decision from your context, your context is an argument.
- **The status vocabulary is closed**, and it includes `Superseded`. A decision that can only be right or wrong has no way to be *replaced*, which is what actually happens to almost every decision worth recording.
- **Records are "added as pull request in the proposed status"**, so the record enters review as a proposal rather than arriving as a settled fact. That is the same move as your defence today.

The playbook also gives a collision rule for parallel authorship — give the record the identifier of the work item it relates to — and states the maintenance rule for design artefacts flatly: artefacts that are not up to date are bugs in the design.

**That source has limits.** It has no single date: its pages carry footer dates spanning several years and the unevenness is invisible in the navigation, so a citation must be pinned to a page and not to the site. It contradicts itself in at least a dozen places, and on trade studies specifically its two pages disagree with each other about how many options to compare, while its own worked example does not follow its own template.

### The platform decision is three axes, and only three

The published selection criteria for agent technology are stated as "your organization's technical capabilities, timeline, and control requirements" ([research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md)). **Capability, speed, control.** That triple generalises well beyond agent-building, and it is the axis set your record must argue on.

The two paths you are choosing between differ on all three, and the documentation is unusually candid about the costs.

**Copilot Studio** is software-as-a-service for low-code development. Its published advantages: a drag-and-drop interface "without coding"; "hundreds of prebuilt connectors"; suitability for "smaller, targeted agents that don't require large-scale enterprise governance"; and prebuilt governance reducing IT complexity. Its published disadvantages, stated equally plainly: "limited ability to fine-tune API calls, actions, and response formatting compared with a pro-code environment"; pro-dev tooling "less integrated than the project-based workflow in Agents Toolkit"; Adaptive Cards "limited to schema 1.6 and earlier"; and slower feature availability. And the line to read hardest: it is "designed for small-scale deployments, such as department-level solutions."

**Microsoft Foundry** is platform-as-a-service, supporting declarative and pro-code development with your own choice of models, consolidating runtime, orchestration and integration into a managed environment. Its own internal split is a hosting distinction: prompt agents use declarative config with no agent compute; hosted agents run your containerised code on managed container compute. And its setup options carry a consequence a brief will silently assume away — the **basic setup** is "for rapid prototyping and individual development when speed and ease of access are priorities" and **"lacks network isolation"**, while the standard setup provides "fine-grained control over data, security, and networking".

Two further published facts belong in any honest record. Distribution is constrained by build choice: "Only agents built with the Teams SDK, Microsoft 365 Agents SDK, or Foundry can be published to the Microsoft Commercial Store with the Agents Toolkit." **A decision made at the start binds at the end.** And the choice is not exclusive: the documentation states that the options "aren't mutually exclusive", giving an example in which one runtime exposes tools through MCP that agents on another consume. **The unit of decision is a component, not a system**, and a record that assumes otherwise has answered a question nobody asked.

**This is first-party product documentation with a selection-guidance purpose.** Its authority is definitional — it tells you what the products are, not that choosing them this way works. It offers no adoption data, no failure-rate data, no cost comparison and no evidence that its recommended mappings produce better outcomes. It was read here through search-returned excerpts rather than full page fetches, several of its comparison tables were truncated in the excerpts read, and it is the most perishable material in this repository. **Treat it as a well-constructed decision framework, not as validated findings.**

### Known and assumed are different objects, and the difference is where positions fail

Opening item 2 asks which of your beliefs rest on something you observed and which on something you were told. Almost everything a learner holds at this point was told to them by somebody with an interest in the answer.

That is not a criticism of the learner. It is the structure of the situation, and it is the reason the two-column form is the artefact rather than a prose position. A column forces a decision on each line; prose lets a belief drift between classes depending on the sentence it appears in.

The negotiation literature supplies the discipline for what to do with the assumed column. It insists on independent standards — market value, precedent, professional judgement, efficiency, cost — and on separating trust from verification without turning verification into an accusation ([research/05-fde-craft/fisher-ury-getting-to-yes.md](/research/05-fde-craft/fisher-ury-getting-to-yes.md)). It also names the protection that makes a decline survivable: a **BATNA**, the course of action actually available if no agreement is reached, and the observation that **an unknown one is dangerous, because a negotiator may accept less than the alternative or reject more.** A learner who declines without knowing what happens next has not made a decision; they have made a gesture.

**That book is a practitioner framework, not a controlled evaluation**, developed through analytic distinctions and worked examples across an extremely wide case range.

### Why you are not marked on the decision

This is the design choice that carries the session, and it deserves a defence rather than an announcement.

An assessor who marks the decision is asserting they know which decision was right. Nobody does. The brief is invented, the customer is invented, and no outcome exists to check anybody against. Marking the decision would therefore install a hidden preference for the answer the assessor favours, and would assess agreement rather than reasoning.

The assessment frame this programme uses says the same thing in its own terms. An entrustable professional activity is a bounded unit of practice that can be handed over whole; the judgement is whether a supervisor would let this person do this unsupervised ([research/08-assessment-epas/ten-cate-epa-framework.md](/research/08-assessment-epas/ten-cate-epa-framework.md)). **Qualifying an engagement is a plausible activity. Declining one is not**, because a decline is an outcome rather than an activity and its correctness depends on facts the assessment cannot hold constant.

The same source supplies the shape of today's defence, and it is more specific than "a viva". An **entrustment-based discussion** is a short, focused conversation after the activity that "should probe the student's deep understanding of what was done, but should include 'what-if' questions". The what-if requirement is what separates it from a walkthrough: retrospective explanation shows you understood the case you met, and **counterfactual questioning is the only available evidence about the cases you did not meet.** That matters because an entrustment decision implies accepting risk about what the person will encounter next.

That framework also names three professionalism characteristics in behavioural terms — **integrity**, truthfulness in reports to a supervisor; **reliability**, stable and conscientious working; and **humility**, the ability to observe one's own limitations and the willingness to ask for help. The third is the one today's defence exposes, because a learner who cannot name what would reverse them is not displaying confidence.

**The primer is guidance from medical education by the concept's originator, explicitly guidance rather than an evaluation**, and its transfer to engineering is this programme's inference.

### Why the assessor writes a reason rather than a mark

There is a second literature behind the assessment design, and it is the reason your defence produces prose rather than a score.

Studies of assessors judging complex evidentiary records found that they "mainly relied on narrative feedback to inform their judgment", and that grades "were merely used to confirm impressions based on narratives" ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/govaerts-narrative-assessment.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/govaerts-narrative-assessment.md)). The numbers were not the evidence; they were a check on a conclusion already formed from the prose.

The finding with the sharpest consequence for you is a different one. Assessors reached similar final pass/fail judgements while differing in their information-processing approaches and in the reasoning behind them. **Convergence on an outcome conceals divergence in reasoning**, which is why high-stakes judgements in that literature are made in group discussion rather than individually, and why assessors are required to substantiate what they conclude. Substantiation is what makes divergent reasoning visible and therefore discussable.

So your specification produces a written reason from the assessor, not a mark. And the same finding is a warning about you: assessors began forming an impression the moment they began observing, and "were inclined to stick to their initial judgments even when confronted with seemingly disconfirming evidence". **Your first thirty seconds of defence will do more work than the next ten minutes**, which is an argument for opening with what you do not know.

**Those studies are from medical education, and the transfer is an inference.**

### What the practice you are entering does not do

One last piece of material, and it is the reason this stage exists at all.

The corpus of professional practice this programme is built on contains no worked example of declining an engagement. Its case studies all succeed. Its single failure narrative is a synthetic composite, fictional in its particulars by its own admission. It states no exclusion criteria — no domain limit, no scale limit, no regulatory limit, and no description of a customer who would be unsuitable ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md)).

There is exactly one recorded refusal in the whole corpus: a proposal to replace human reviewers with AI in a legal-review process was judged irresponsible, because those reviewers catch issues with legal consequences. **That refusal is real, it is correct, and it is a single instance.** It establishes that the practice *can* refuse and simply does not document doing so, which is a thinner basis than a stated exclusion criterion and is not offered as more.

**This stage is therefore written past its source**, and that is a class 3 claim — design reasoning with no external warrant. The corpus is internal and access-controlled; it is paraphrased here, never quoted, its organisation is not named, and nothing in it may be linked.

## The workflow

**Record the build-path decision as an architecture decision record, in `Proposed` status, argued on the three published axes.**

**Step 1 — write the record before you rehearse the defence.** The record is evidence about your reasoning. Rehearsing first produces a record shaped by what you expect to be asked.

**Step 2 — create it at the specified path.** Add a file under `doc/adr/`, numbered ascending, and give it the identifier of the work item it relates to so that two people writing records in parallel do not collide.

```markdown
# 0007. Build path for the reconciliation assistant

Date: <the date you wrote it>
Status: Proposed

## Context

<A value-neutral description of the forces at play. No preference may
appear here. A reader must NOT be able to predict the decision below
from this section alone.>

- Capability: <what the team can build and operate today>
- Timeline: <what date exists, and who set it>
- Control:  <data, network and governance requirements, and who owns them>

## Options considered

| Option | Capability | Timeline | Control | Documented limit + date checked |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Copilot Studio |  |  |  |  |
| Foundry, basic setup |  |  |  |  |
| Foundry, standard setup |  |  |  |  |
| Composed: one component each |  |  |  |  |

## Decision

We will <...>.

## Consequences

- What this forecloses at the end of the project: <distribution, scale,
  schema currency, feature latency>
- What would make us supersede this record: <the observation, the
  observer, and when>
- Not addressed by the documentation: <the questions no fetched page
  answered>
```

**Step 3 — fill the documented-limit column from first-party pages only.** Each cell needs a page and a date, or the word `NOT ADDRESSED`. **Do not infer a limit about one product from a limit stated about another**, which is the commonest way this table is falsified.

**Step 4 — include the composed option even if you reject it.** The documentation states that the build paths "aren't mutually exclusive" and compose through MCP. A record with three single-platform options has assumed away a published possibility, and the assumption belongs in `Context` where a reader can see it.

**Step 5 — test your context section for neutrality.** Give the `Context` section alone to somebody who has not read the rest. If they can predict your decision, rewrite it. **A context a reader can predict from is an argument wearing a description's clothes.**

**Step 6 — attack your own record.** Ask an agent to write the strongest possible `Decision` and `Consequences` sections for the option you rejected, using only your own `Context` and `Options considered` table. Do not ask it to review your record. If its version is better than yours, your context was neutral and your decision was weak — which is a good problem, and the one you want to find before the defence rather than during it. Record what you asked, what you kept and what you rejected.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That a decision and its justification are separable artefacts, and that most decision documents fuse them so completely that the decision cannot be argued with. Filling a table with dated page references also makes visible how much of a platform choice rests on documented fact and how much on preference — and the proportion is usually not what the author expected.

## What good looks like

- The problem is stated as a difficulty a person experiences, with no technology in the sentence.
- Known and assumed are separated on the page, **and the assumed column is not empty.**
- Each condition names who would observe it and when, so somebody other than you could tell whether it had fired.
- The decision is consistent with the boundary you wrote at [S002](/wiki/seminars/S002.md): you do not claim a decision you already told us you may only propose.
- The decision record's `Context` section does not predict its `Decision` section.
- Every cell of the options table carries a page and a date, or the words `NOT ADDRESSED`.
- Under challenge you can name evidence that would reverse you, and you do not reach for it defensively.

## How it goes wrong

- **The confident inventory** — everything is stated as known, because the assumption column looks like weakness. *Warning sign:* your evidence column contains things nobody in the room has seen.
- **The condition that cannot fire** — the disqualifying condition survives contact with the brief because it was never capable of contacting it. *Warning sign:* you cannot describe a Tuesday on which it would occur.
- **Authority drift** — the decision is stated as though it were yours, four sessions after you wrote down that it was not. *Warning sign:* your decision and your boundary statement do not mention the same people.
- **Defence by repetition** — challenged, you restate the decision more slowly. *Warning sign:* your answer to the objection contains no new information.
- **The advocating context** — the value-neutral section argues for the answer. *Warning sign:* a stranger reading only your `Context` guesses your decision correctly.
- **The decision with no alternative** — you declined and cannot say what happens instead. *Warning sign:* your record has no consequences section for the path not taken.
- **The single-platform assumption** — you chose one product for the whole system without noticing that you could have chosen per component. *Warning sign:* the word "platform" appears where the word "component" belongs.

## Assessment

**This session is the assessment occasion for Stage 1.** It assesses the engagement position written at [S001](/wiki/seminars/S001.md) and developed through [S002](/wiki/seminars/S002.md), [S003](/wiki/seminars/S003.md) and [S004](/wiki/seminars/S004.md).

Checked elements:

1. The problem is stated without naming a technology.
2. Beliefs are separated into observed and reported, and each reported belief names its source.
3. At least one condition is stated in a form that could be observed to occur, with a named observer and a time at which they would observe it.
4. The decision is consistent with the role boundary written at [S002](/wiki/seminars/S002.md).
5. The decision names the person or function whose agreement it requires.
6. The build-path record carries a value-neutral context, a closed-vocabulary status, and at least one documented limit with the date it was checked.
7. Under challenge, the learner names at least one piece of evidence that would reverse the decision.

The assessor records **a written reason and not a mark**, and the reason must say which element carried the judgement.

**Nothing else is assessed.** The decision itself is not marked, the challenger's satisfaction is not marked, the quality of the underlying engagement idea is not marked, and **whether the learner would in fact have declined is not marked and never will be.**

## Exercises

**Exercises 1 to 3 are preparation and are not assessed. The defence is.** Everything below exists to make the specification survivable; none of it is collected.

**1 · Sort your own beliefs.** Take every claim in your current position and place each in one of three columns: *observed by me*, *reported to me by —*, *assumed*. Count the columns.

> **Criterion.** A passing sort has a non-empty middle column with a name against every line, and a non-empty right column. **A position with an empty assumption column has not been sorted, it has been defended.** If your observed column is the largest, check it: at this stage of an engagement almost nothing has been observed, and "the sponsor showed me a dashboard" is a reported belief about the dashboard's contents.

**2 · Go back to your own item 2, then name the difference.** Find what you wrote to opening item 2 — which beliefs rest on observation and which on report. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside your completed three-column sort and write one sentence naming the difference. Then say which single belief moved the furthest, and what moved it.

> **Criterion.** The commonest movement is from *observed* to *reported*, and the commonest cause is realising that you saw somebody's description of a thing rather than the thing. A passing answer names the belief and the moment it moved.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: presenting a correct answer beside a learner's wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). **That evidence is school, university and laboratory work, and its application to working professionals is an extrapolation this programme concedes.**

**3 · Write the record for the option you rejected.** Using the workflow's step 6, produce the strongest `Decision` and `Consequences` for the path you did not take.

> **What you should find.** If the rejected option's record is better than yours, one of two things is true and both are worth knowing before the defence: your context was genuinely neutral and your decision is weak, or your context was neutral about the wrong forces. **A decision you cannot argue against is a decision you have not made.**

**4 · Locate a published limit and a published silence.** From first-party documentation, find one stated limit that constrains your build path and one question the documentation does not answer.

> **What you should find.** Limits are published for some paths plainly — the department-level scale statement, the Adaptive Cards schema ceiling, the basic setup lacking network isolation, the commercial-store publishing constraint ([research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md)). **Silences are more common than limits.** No adoption data, failure-rate data or cost comparison appears anywhere in that family, and no scale envelope is stated for most of the tools it compares. Record both with dates.

**5 · Rehearse one what-if.** Have a peer ask you a single counterfactual: *what would you do if the operations lead who gave you the workaround left next month?* Answer in under a minute.

> **Criterion.** The entrustment literature requires what-if questioning specifically because retrospective explanation shows only that you understood the case you met, and "an entrustment decision implies the acceptance of risk, as it can never be fully predicted what the student will encounter" ([research/08-assessment-epas/ten-cate-epa-framework.md](/research/08-assessment-epas/ten-cate-epa-framework.md)). A passing answer changes something in the decision. "I would find somebody else" changes nothing and is the answer most people give.

**6 · Name your alternative to agreement.** If you decline, what happens instead — to the customer, to your organisation, to you? Write three sentences.

> **Criterion.** "They find another supplier" is a start; "they proceed with the internal team who built the current workaround, on the same date" is an answer. **An unknown alternative is dangerous in both directions**, because it lets a negotiator accept less than the alternative or reject more ([research/05-fde-craft/fisher-ury-getting-to-yes.md](/research/05-fde-craft/fisher-ury-getting-to-yes.md)). **Then compare:** look back at your answer to opening item 4 — what the decision is and what would reverse it. Did your reversal condition assume that declining had no cost?

## What this does not establish

**That this decision is right, or that decisions made this way are better than decisions made some other way.** Nothing behind this programme measures qualification against any outcome, and the practice corpus it rests on records no engagement anybody declined and states no exclusion criteria of any kind.

**What is being assessed is a form of reasoning, not a result.** A learner who passes here has shown they can hold a position under pressure and say what would change it. Whether that capacity improves an engagement is untested, and the argument for it is made in [WP-005](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-005.md) rather than demonstrated anywhere.

**A learner can pass this assessment while holding a position that is comprehensively wrong.** The design accepts that, because the alternative is an assessor pretending to a knowledge they do not have.

**The assessment frame is borrowed from medical education** and is guidance rather than an evaluation. The narrative-assessment findings come from portfolio assessors, not engineering reviewers, and their transfer is this programme's inference.

**The platform material is definitional and perishable.** It states what the products are, not that choosing them this way works; it offers no adoption, cost, failure-rate or outcome data; and it was read through search excerpts with several comparison tables truncated.

**The decision-record template is drawn from an undated, internally inconsistent source.** Its trade-study pages disagree with each other and its own worked example does not follow its own template. The ADR fields are used here because they are precise, not because they are validated.
