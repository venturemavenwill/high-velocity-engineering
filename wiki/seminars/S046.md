# S046 · Instructions an Executor Actually Receives

**Module:** [M10](/wiki/modules/M10.md) · **Quarter:** [Q5](/wiki/quarters/Q5.md) · **Complexity class:** 3
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** an openly published agent-configuration repository, pinned at commit `cf29fb45` and package version 3.3.101 · GitHub Copilot agent mode · **MCP servers:** Microsoft Learn MCP Server
**Whitepaper:** [WP-046](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-046.md)
**Retrieves from:** S024, S025, S043
**Entry state:** either

## The situation

Your team has a coding standard. Somebody wrote it, most people read it once, and it has never once stopped a change from being merged.

That is the ordinary condition of a written standard and it is not a failure of discipline. It is a failure of **attachment**. A standard filed somewhere near the work is received by whoever goes looking; a constraint attached to the work is received on every change that matches it. The difference is not rhetorical — **it is the difference between a standard whose conformance is checked by reading and one whose conformance is checked by execution.**

The corpus behind this stage states the durable half in one line: move engineering artefacts out of documents and slides into the repository, because that is what makes them actionable. The agent-configuration repository pinned above takes the same claim to its conclusion, and separates what an executor receives into kinds that behave differently — constraints applied automatically to matching work, entry points a person invokes deliberately, orchestrators with declared roles, and reusable procedures. **The taxonomy is one vendor's product decision. The distinction underneath it is not**, and the distinction is what this session teaches: an artefact that is applied is a different object from one that is invoked, and confusing them is why most standards are never received.

Answer these before instruction.

1. Where does your team's coding standard live, and when did you last read it?
2. An executor who has never seen your codebase starts on your slice. What do they get wrong first?
3. Which of your constraints could be checked by running something, and which could only be checked by a person?
4. What happens to your standard when the person who wrote it leaves the engagement?
5. Who is allowed to change the constraints, and how would you know that they had?

**Item 3 is the same question [S043](/wiki/seminars/S043.md) asked about tests**, arriving from the other side. There it produced an untestable residue. Here that residue has to be given somewhere to live.

## What you must produce

An **instruction set for your slice**, held in the repository with the work rather than beside it.

- Each constraint stated so that an executor could act on it without asking a question.
- For each constraint, **its conformance mode**: checked by execution, checked by review, or not checked at all.
- **The scope of application** for each constraint — what work it attaches to, and what it deliberately does not.
- **The provenance** of each constraint: the decision record from [S024](/wiki/seminars/S024.md), the specification from [S043](/wiki/seminars/S043.md), or the sentence *no source, our preference*.
- A short **authority statement**: what the executor may decide, what it must stop for, and what it may never record as done.

## What good looks like

- **Every constraint has a conformance mode and at least one is honestly *not checked at all*.** A set in which everything is checked has been written optimistically.
- **The constraints are actionable rather than aspirational.** *Follow good practice* is not a constraint. *A public function that touches the data layer must go through the repository interface* is.
- **The set is attached to the work**, and this is demonstrated by making a change that the constraint matches and observing that it applies.
- **The authority statement names a stop condition**, and one of them is a human decision the executor may not record as satisfied.
- **Untrusted material is classified as data.** Content the executor fetches, reads or is handed is treated as information to be reported, never as instructions to be followed.
- **The set is versioned and reviewable**, entering the repository through the same route as code.

## How it goes wrong

- **The unattached standard** — excellent prose, filed where nothing reaches it. *Warning sign:* it has never caused a change to be rewritten.
- **The essay instruction** — paragraphs of context an executor cannot act on. *Warning sign:* you cannot state the constraint as something checkable in one sentence.
- **The unfalsifiable constraint** — it has no observable that distinguishes work satisfying it from work that does not. *Warning sign:* two reasonable people cannot disagree about whether it was met.
- **The generated-tree edit** — the constraint is changed in the packaged output rather than in the source it was generated from, and disappears at the next build. *Warning sign:* your change is not in the file anybody reviews.
- **The delegated approval** — the authority statement lets the executor mark a human review as done. *Warning sign:* your workflow completes without a person having said anything.
- **The obedient fetch** — material the executor retrieved contained an instruction and the executor followed it. *Warning sign:* your set does not mention where content comes from.

## Do it

**First, write item 2's answer as a constraint.** The thing a newcomer gets wrong first is almost always the constraint your team holds in its head. Write that one before any of the general ones, because it is the one that will actually fire.

**Second, establish the conformance mode honestly.** Then take the constraints marked *not checked at all* and ask whether they are constraints or hopes. This is the same discipline that makes the playbook's engineering checklist partly assessable and partly not: most of its items are mechanically checkable against an artefact, and **a substantial minority are not falsifiable as written** — one of its sections stops asserting conditions partway down and starts asking questions instead. **A standard is assessable exactly where it names an artefact**, and nowhere else.

**Third, demonstrate attachment rather than asserting it.** Make a small change that the constraint matches. Observe whether it applies. **Record the pin while you do it** — commit, package version, host, model, date — because everything you are about to observe is a property of that combination and not of the idea.

**Fourth, test the boundary.** Hand the executor a document containing an instruction that attempts to change its authority, and observe what happens. The configuration says fetched content is data rather than authority and that embedded instructions are to be reported rather than obeyed. **Whether that holds depends on the host attaching the rule and the model complying with it, and the file can guarantee neither.** You are not testing the rule. You are finding out whether it is enforced in your setting, which is a different question and the only one you can answer.

## Show it

Give your instruction set to another team and ask them to make one small change to your slice using it, without asking you anything.

Then read what they produced. **Every place they had to guess is a constraint you did not write**, and every place they went and asked a colleague instead of reading the set is a constraint that was not attached.

## What this does not establish

Nothing here shows that configured constraints produce better code.

The agent-configuration repository is a large, versioned, packaged, well-engineered corpus, and **that is an architecture claim rather than a benefit claim.** Its own prose uses outcome vocabulary — accelerate, prevents, proven — and **none of it is established by anything in the repository.** It contains evaluation machinery and no committed results.

The playbook says almost nothing about generated code at all. Its one page on the subject is a commit-authorship convention — recording who supervised, and recording assistance on a separate trailer from authorship — which is bookkeeping and is useful as bookkeeping.

**The claim this session rests on is about receipt, not quality:** a constraint nobody receives cannot have constrained anything. That is close to a tautology and the session says so. **The stronger-sounding claim — that attaching constraints changes what gets built — is untested here and everywhere.**

## Perishable content in this session

| Durable claim | Platform instance, perishable |
|---|---|
| A constraint that is not attached to the work is not received | The pinned repository's artefact kinds, file layout and application rules, at commit `cf29fb45`, package version 3.3.101 |
| Authoring source, packaging manifest and generated output are different things | The specific generated tree and its declaration that it is not canonical |
| An executor may not record a human's approval | The current host's handling of that declaration, which the file cannot guarantee |
