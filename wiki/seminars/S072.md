# S072 · Runbooks, and the Operator Who Is Not You

**Module:** [M15](/wiki/modules/M15.md) · **Quarter:** [Q8](/wiki/quarters/Q8.md) · **Complexity class:** 5
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** APIM AI gateway policies and Foundry deployment configuration as the runbook's actual subject, written for someone who was not in the room
**Whitepaper:** [WP-072](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-072.md)
**Retrieves from:** S061, S063, S071
**Entry state:** this-programme-only

---

## The situation

Something you built will fail at an hour when you are not available, in front of somebody who has never seen it before, who has been woken up, and who cannot ask you anything.

**Everything you know about the system is the reason you cannot write for that person.**

You will write a runbook. It will be a good one. It will be correct in every particular, and it will be written — without your noticing, without any way for you to notice — for a reader who shares your assumptions, your vocabulary, your mental model of which components exist, and your sense of what *normal* looks like on a dashboard. That reader is your own future self. Your own future self is not the operator.

Answer these in writing before the session opens.

1. Take the last real failure this system had. Write down how you knew it had started.
2. Would your answer to item 1 be available to somebody who is not you? Say how they would get it.
3. At the first branch in your diagnosis, what evidence distinguishes the two paths?
4. What is the point at which the operator should stop and escalate, and to whom — by role, not by name?
5. What in your runbook is true only because of something you happen to remember?

**Item 5 has no honest answer from the inside.** That is the point of the session: you cannot audit your own context, so somebody else has to execute the document while you watch and say nothing.

## What you must produce

**One runbook, for one failure that has actually occurred**, executed cold by a person who did not write it, while you are present and silent.

1. **The entry condition**, stated as something observable on a named surface.
2. **The diagnosis**, as branches, each naming the evidence that selects it and where that evidence is read.
3. **The actions**, each with its expected observable effect and its reversal.
4. **The stop**, with a named role, a route and a stated condition for using it.
5. **The end state**, and how the operator knows the incident is over rather than paused.
6. **The execution record** — where the executor stopped, what they asked that the document did not answer, and what they did that you did not intend.
7. **The revision**, with each change traced to a specific stop.

Not a set of runbooks. **One, executed, is worth more than a library that has never been read by anyone but its author.**

## The material

### The document is knowledge; the execution is evidence

This is the same distinction [S071](/wiki/seminars/S071.md) installed, applied to the one manifest line most likely to be satisfied by writing something.

Hiatt separates **knowledge** — "the information, training and education necessary to know how to change" — from **ability**, "turning knowledge into action", achieved "when a person or group has the demonstrated capability to implement the change at the required performance levels" ([research/05-fde-craft/hiatt-adkar.md](/research/05-fde-craft/hiatt-adkar.md)). His summary of the gap: "The presence of knowledge is often insufficient by itself."

**The sentence in that source that this session actually turns on is a different one, and it is the best line in the book:** "If the feedback channel is not open, then the employee will appear to be failing when the system is actually at fault."

Read that as an operations claim rather than a change-management one and it describes exactly what happens when a runbook meets its first real operator. The operator stalls. The organisation concludes the operator is weak. **The document was at fault and nothing in the situation was constructed to reveal that**, because the only person who could have seen the defect was the author, who was not there, and whose absence was the entire premise. This session's structure — a cold execution with the author present and silent — is a feedback channel built deliberately, and it is the only reliable one available.

**Its limits travel with it.** ADKAR is a trademarked practitioner model published by the firm that sells its training; its element ordering is argued from the meanings of the words with no study behind it, and no independent evaluation of the model appears anywhere in the book. It also carries a retention-by-modality claim from a commercial trainer's handbook which this repository does not reproduce. **Take the knowledge-versus-ability distinction and the feedback-channel observation. Take no number, because there is none worth having.**

### Your runbook's real subject is a configuration you did not write down

Now the specific. Most AI-system runbooks are written about the application and the failure is in the gateway, because the gateway is where the multi-tenant concerns live and the multi-tenant concerns are invisible until the second application appears.

Microsoft's documentation for the AI gateway in API Management is unusually clear about this, and the framing is the most useful thing in it: the AI gateway "extends API Management's existing API gateway" and is "not a separate offering" ([research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md)). AI traffic is API traffic with different resource semantics.

The five challenges it names are a runbook index for a system nobody has written a runbook for: authenticate and authorise access; load balance across endpoints; monitor and log interactions; manage token usage and quotas across applications; enable developer self-service. **Every one is a multi-tenant concern**, and a single application against a single model needs none of them.

Four mechanisms in that page produce failures your operator will meet and your runbook will not name.

**The token limit policy** enforces a limit or quota keyed on "any counter key, such as subscription key, originating IP address, or an arbitrary key defined through a policy expression". The scalability argument behind it is stated cleanly enough to teach verbatim: with a growing portfolio "you need to make sure that one app doesn't use the whole TPM quota and block other apps from accessing the backends they need." **A noisy neighbour is the failure, and from the affected application's side it looks like the model is down.** Your runbook's entry condition — errors from the model endpoint — is satisfied by a fault whose cause is a different team's traffic and whose diagnosis is a counter key your operator has never heard of.

**The circuit breaker** "features dynamic trip duration, applying values from the `Retry-After` header provided by the backend." That is a better design than a fixed timeout because it lets the failing service state its own recovery time. It is also a runbook problem: an operator watching a tripped breaker cannot infer when it will close, because the answer came from a header they cannot see.

**Priority-based load balancing** is recommended to "ensure optimal utilization of specific Microsoft Foundry endpoints, particularly those purchased as PTU instances" — you have paid for dedicated capacity whether or not you use it, so route to it first and overflow elsewhere. **The failure mode is silent and expensive:** overflow is working correctly and the bill has changed. Nothing alerts, because nothing is broken.

**Semantic caching** compares "the vector proximity of the prompt to prior requests" and returns a stored completion. The documentation states the benefits and does not state the caution, which this session must: **a similarity threshold is a correctness knob.** Set it loosely and the system returns an answer to a question that was not asked. The failure is silent and the output is plausible.

And one caveat on that page prevents the commonest wrong repair: "While API Management can scale gateway capacity, you also need to scale and distribute traffic to your AI backends to accommodate increased load… deploy backend AI services in the same regions as your API Management gateways." **Scaling the front door does not widen the corridor behind it.** An operator who has been told to scale out will do it, and it will not help.

**The documentation's limits.** It is first-party product documentation, definitional rather than empirical: policy names, tier availability and preview status change frequently, several capabilities are marked preview, and the Foundry integration is preview. It publishes no failure rates, no latency data and no evidence that any recommended configuration outperforms another. It does not discuss the privacy consequences of logging prompts and completions, which it recommends "for billing and auditing" — **and a runbook that instructs an operator to read a prompt log is instructing them to read customer content.**

### Silent failure is the characteristic failure of data systems, and runbooks are written for loud ones

The runbooks people write are for failures that announce themselves. The failures that matter do not.

The production-ML literature names the canonical case precisely. **Training–serving skew** arises from performing different preprocessing at training and at serving, and it is described as a common production failure that is hard to detect because the model continues to return plausible answers ([research/02-technical-foundations/crowe-machine-learning-production-systems.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/crowe-machine-learning-production-systems.md)). No error. No exception. No obviously wrong output.

The same source separates **drift** from **skew** — drift compares successive iterations of one source over time, skew compares two different sources — and separates **data drift**, a change in the distribution of the inputs, from **concept drift**, a change in the relationship between inputs and labels. That vocabulary is what converts a complaint into a locatable defect, and it belongs in your runbook's branch labels, because "the model seems worse" is not a branch.

It also supplies the two properties a monitoring surface needs before a runbook can be written against it, both imported from control theory: **alertability**, meaning metrics and thresholds that make failure unambiguous, and **actionability**, meaning enough signal to support finding a cause. **An entry condition can only be written against an alertable surface**, and most dashboards are neither.

And it warns that **aggregate metrics conceal subgroup failure**, with the choice of slices being a domain question rather than a statistical one. A runbook whose entry condition is an aggregate will not fire for the failure that is destroying one customer segment.

**Its limits:** a practitioner survey from a commercial publisher, not research. No comparison group, no study design, no measurement that its practices outperform alternatives. Three of four authors work at the vendor whose toolchain supplies both worked pipelines. **Every number in it is a property of a particular system after a particular training run and none is reproduced here.**

### Write for the reader who does not share your dialect

The last piece is linguistic and it is the one authors cannot see.

Evans's ubiquitous language is negotiated rather than adopted: the model "will typically derive from the domain experts' own jargon but will have been 'cleaned up,' to have sharper, narrower definitions", and the experts hold a veto — "the domain experts should object if these definitions diverge from the meanings accepted in the field" ([research/05-fde-craft/evans-domain-driven-design.md](/research/05-fde-craft/evans-domain-driven-design.md)). He expects multiple dialects and permits them: "Multiplicity of languages is often necessary, but the linguistic division should never be between the domain experts and the developers."

Your operator is a third party to that negotiation and was not present for it. **Their dialect is the operations dialect and yours is the delivery dialect**, and the two overlap enough that neither of you will notice the gap until a step fails to parse.

Evans supplies the live test, and it is a runbook test wearing a documentation test's clothes: "If the terms explained in a design document don't start showing up in conversations and code, the document is not fulfilling its purpose." The cold execution is that test, run in an hour instead of a quarter. He also gives the modelling technique that predicts most of what you will learn from it: "One of the best ways to refine a model is to explore with speech… Rough edges are easy to hear." **Read your runbook aloud to somebody before you hand it to them and you will catch the third of the defects that are audible.** The rest need the execution.

**Its limits:** a practitioner book making no quantitative claim of any kind; its evidence is the author's own named projects, several of which he reports failing; its methods assume iterative development and continuing access to domain experts, stated as prerequisites.

### Why most published runbooks are not runbooks

One structural warning before you write, from the openly published engineering handbook this programme uses elsewhere.

Across its pages that handbook resolves into several distinct genres — normative practice pages, artefact templates, worked examples, tool-pinned recipes, link lists and stubs — **and they are interleaved with no signal in the navigation that distinguishes them** ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md)). Reading it as a uniform standard overstates the depth of close to half of it and understates the parts that are genuinely excellent, because the good pages are not marked either.

**Your runbook will have the same problem at a smaller scale.** Some of its steps are commands. Some are judgements. Some are context. Some are stubs you meant to finish. An operator at three in the morning cannot tell which is which, and will attempt the judgements as though they were commands.

**Cite that handbook by page and by the date that page was read, never as a whole**; its footers span years, several tools it recommends are archived or deprecated, it contradicts itself in places without noticing, and it asserts unsupported magnitude claims which this repository records as specimens and **reproduces nowhere**.

## The workflow

**Write a runbook for a gateway failure, then hand it to somebody who has never seen your system.**

**Step 1 — pick a failure that happened.** From the incident history inherited from Stage 7. **Not one you can imagine**, because an imagined failure produces an imagined entry condition, and the entry condition is the part that fails first.

**Step 2 — find the configuration your failure actually lives in.** Read the gateway policy, not the application. Identify the counter key, the limit, the load-balancing mode, the breaker settings and the cache threshold, and record which of them you did not know before you looked.

**Step 3 — write the entry condition against a named surface.** Not *the system seems slow*. Name the metric, the dimension, the surface it is read on and the value that means *you are in this runbook*. The gateway's metric policy is what makes a dimension available at all:

```xml
<!-- Fragment. Names, tiers and preview status change; re-derive from the current docs. -->
<llm-token-limit counter-key="@(context.Subscription.Id)"
                 tokens-per-minute="<limit>"
                 estimate-prompt-tokens="false"
                 remaining-tokens-variable-name="remainingTokens" />

<llm-emit-token-metric namespace="llm-metrics">
  <dimension name="API ID"    value="@(context.Api.Id)" />
  <dimension name="Client IP" value="@(context.Request.IpAddress)" />
</llm-emit-token-metric>
```

**A metric with no dimensions tells you the bill. A metric with dimensions tells you who to talk to** — and an entry condition can only be written against the second. If your runbook's first step is *find out which application is responsible*, and no dimension carries that, your runbook has a step nobody can perform.

**Step 4 — write the branches with their evidence.** Each branch names what distinguishes it and where the operator reads it. Use the drift-versus-skew vocabulary where it applies; *the answers got worse* is not a branch, and *the serving distribution has moved away from the training distribution, seen at this dashboard* is.

**Step 5 — write the stop.** A role, a route, and the condition for using it. Not a name. Not your name.

**Step 6 — read it aloud to yourself.** Rough edges are audible. This is free and catches the errors you would otherwise waste your executor on.

**Step 7 — hand it over and say nothing.** Pair with a learner from a different engagement. Your only permitted act is to record: where they stopped, what they asked, what they did that you did not intend. **If a stated safety condition fires — the executor is about to do something with consequences outside the exercise — you stop the run rather than coach it, and you record that you did.**

**Step 8 — attack your own revision with the agent, and do not let it improve the prose.** One prompt: *here is my runbook and here is a description of an operator who has never seen this system. For each step, state the specific piece of knowledge the step assumes the reader already holds, and name the step that will fail first. Do not suggest wording.*

Read it for the assumed knowledge, not the suggestions. **Record what you rejected and why.** An agent asked to improve a runbook adds context, which is the wrong repair — see below. An agent asked to enumerate assumed knowledge has been given a task with an answer. Note that the instrument was trained on documents like yours and shares your dialect, so it will miss the assumptions you and it hold in common.

**Step 9 — revise, and trace each change to a specific stop.** The revision is the deliverable and the first draft is the instrument.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** The moment your executor stops. You will feel it as an urge to speak, and the urge is data: it is the exact size of the gap between what the document says and what the system requires, measured in the only unit available. **You cannot get that number by rereading your own document, because rereading it reinstalls the context that the document is missing.**

## What good looks like

- **The entry condition is observable.** A person can tell whether they are in this runbook without judgement, from something on a named surface.
- Each branch names the evidence that selects it, and the evidence is available to the operator at that moment.
- **Steps are marked by kind** — command, judgement, context — so an operator can tell which is which at three in the morning.
- Each action states its expected observable effect and its reversal.
- There is an explicit stop, with a named role and a route somebody will actually answer.
- **It terminates.** There is a stated end state and a way to know the incident is over rather than paused.
- The runbook's subject includes the gateway, not only the application, where the failure lives there.
- The execution record contains at least one place where the executor stopped. **A runbook executed cold with no stops was executed by somebody who already knew the system.**
- Each revision traces to a specific stop, and no revision is a paragraph of general context.

## How it goes wrong

- **The runbook that assumes its author** — every step is correct and several are unperformable by anybody else. *Warning sign:* a step begins with "check that the pipeline looks normal".
- **The happy-path runbook** — it documents the procedure and not the failure, so it is a description of the system rather than a response to it. *Warning sign:* it contains no branch.
- **The wrong-layer runbook** — it is written about the application and the failure is in the gateway. *Warning sign:* you cannot say what your counter key is.
- **The escalation to a person** — the route ends at a name, a personal number or a private channel. *Warning sign:* the name is yours.
- **The unstartable runbook** — its trigger is a feeling. Something seems slow; the answers look worse than usual. *Warning sign:* your entry condition contains a comparative with no reference value.
- **The unmarked judgement** — a step requiring a decision is written in the grammar of a command, and the operator executes it. *Warning sign:* a step contains the word *appropriate*, *reasonable* or *as needed*.
- **The rescue** — the executor stalls, you explain, everybody feels the session went well, and no defect was recorded. *Warning sign:* you spoke.
- **The context patch** — the repair is a paragraph explaining the background, which would have stopped this executor asking this question and generalises to nobody. *Warning sign:* your revision is longer than the gap it closed.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The runbook and its execution record are carried to [S075](/wiki/seminars/S075.md), where the executed runbook is one of the assessed artefacts.

**1 · Classify seven entry conditions as enterable or not.** For each, say what an operator would read and where.

- *Error rate on the completions endpoint exceeds the stated threshold on the service dashboard.* · *The system is behaving oddly.* · *A tenant's token quota counter has reached its limit for the current window.* · *Responses are less relevant than usual.* · *The circuit breaker for the primary backend is open.* · *Cost for the day exceeds the modelled figure by more than the stated tolerance.* · *Users are complaining.*

> **Model answers.** Enterable: the error-rate condition, the quota counter, the open breaker and the cost condition — each names a surface and a value. Not enterable: *behaving oddly* and *less relevant than usual*, both comparatives with no reference value; and *users are complaining*, which is a real signal and not an entry condition, because it does not tell the operator which runbook they are in. **The general rule: an entry condition is enterable if two operators reading the same screen would agree they are in it.** Note that *less relevant than usual* is the hardest one and the most common in AI systems — making it enterable requires an evaluation harness, which is [S073](/wiki/seminars/S073.md)'s subject and is why the two sessions sit together.

**2 · Find the failure your runbook cannot see.** Using the gateway documentation, name one failure mode of the AI gateway that your current runbook would not detect, and state what surface would have to exist for it to be detectable.

> **What you should find.** Most learners find either the noisy-neighbour case — one application consuming shared quota, which presents to the victim as the model being unavailable — or the overflow case, where priority-based routing spills off the provisioned endpoint and nothing alerts because nothing is broken. **If you found neither, you were reading the application and not the gateway.** Record the surface that would be needed, and note that in both cases it is a dimension on a metric rather than a new component.

**3 · Go back to your own item 5, then break it.** Find what you wrote for opening item 5 — what in your runbook is true only because of something you happen to remember. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside your execution record and write one sentence naming the difference between what you predicted and what your executor actually stopped on.

> **Criterion.** A pass names at least one assumption the executor exposed that you did not predict, and says why you could not have predicted it. *"I knew the naming convention was unclear"* fails, because a predicted gap is one you could have fixed by rereading. **The finding is the unpredicted stop**, and its existence is the evidence that self-audit does not work here.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Rereading your runbook is not the exercise. Naming the difference between your prediction and the observed stop is.

**4 · Mark every step by kind.** Go through your runbook and label each step command, judgement or context. Count the judgements.

> **What to notice.** That judgements are more numerous than you expected and that several are written in the grammar of commands. **An operator executes grammar, not intent.** The repair is not to remove the judgements — some are irreducible — but to mark them, state what the judgement is between, and give the evidence that decides it. A judgement with no stated alternatives is a step your operator will resolve by guessing and will not know they guessed.

**5 · Rewrite one escalation.** Take the escalation in your runbook and rewrite it so that it survives the departure of every individual currently on the engagement.

> **Criterion.** A pass names a role, a route that exists independently of any person, and the condition under which it is used. **A pass also states what the operator should do if the route does not answer**, because an escalation with no fallback is a stop with no exit. Note that this is the same defect as an unaccepted manifest line from [S071](/wiki/seminars/S071.md), appearing one layer down.

**6 · Read the difference between two repairs.** Take one stop from your execution record and write two repairs: one that adds context, one that changes a step. Then say which generalises.

> **What to notice.** The context repair is longer, feels more helpful, and is addressed to the person in front of you. The step repair is shorter and is addressed to every future operator. **The commonest revision after a cold execution is the first**, and it is the one this session is built to catch, because it converts a document defect into a document about a document defect.

## What this does not establish

**That an executed runbook produces a competent operator, or that a system with runbooks survives longer than one without.** Nothing behind this programme measures either.

**The knowledge-versus-ability distinction is borrowed from a practitioner change model published by the firm that owns its trademark and sells training in it.** Its evidence is that firm's own survey work, reporting what practitioners say caused outcomes rather than measured outcomes; no independent evaluation of the model is cited anywhere in it. **The distinction licenses direction and mechanism only, and no magnitude of any kind.**

**One executor, once, on a prepared morning, is a demonstration and not a measurement.** Nothing establishes that your executor's difficulty predicts your recipient's, and the pairing is with a learner who shares your training, your vocabulary and this programme's assumptions — which makes them a *more* forgiving reader than the real operator, not a representative one. The exercise is therefore a lower bound on the gap.

**The platform documentation is definitional and perishable.** Policy names, tier availability and preview status change frequently. It publishes no failure rates, no latency figures and no evidence that any configuration outperforms another, and several capabilities named here are marked preview.

**The silent-failure material is a practitioner survey, not research.** It offers no comparative evidence for any recommended practice, its toolchain is not neutral, and every quantity in it is a property of a particular system. None is reproduced here.

**The step-kind taxonomy — command, judgement, context — is this design's invention.** It is derived from a verified observation about a published handbook's interleaved genres, and no source establishes that marking steps this way improves execution.
