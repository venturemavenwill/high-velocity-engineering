# S012 · Readiness, and the Workshop You Should Not Run

**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** none

## The situation

You have a charter. The session is in eleven days. The customer's sponsor is enthusiastic, the room is booked, and two of the four people your charter names as necessary have not replied.

Somebody asks whether you are ready.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught.

1. List every condition that would have to be true for this session to be worth running. Now mark each one **customer** or **us**.
2. Look at your marks. How many are **us**?
3. The session runs badly. Write the sentence your organisation will use to explain why. Whose fault does it assign?
4. Your sponsor says a condition cannot be met but asks you to proceed anyway. Under what circumstances is that the right answer, and what gets recorded?
5. What is the difference between a condition and a preference?

**Item 2 is the session.** Most readiness lists point outward. A list that points only outward cannot fail in a way that implicates the person holding it.

## What you must produce

A **readiness gate** for your charter, with three parts:

- **Conditions**, each stated so that a person who was not there could check it. At least one must be about your own organisation — your preparation, your understanding of the customer, your access, your staffing.
- **A waiver rule**: who may proceed without a condition, and what is written down when they do.
- **A stop position**: the one condition you will not waive, and the sentence you would say to the sponsor.

## What good looks like

- Every condition is **checkable by somebody else**, on a date, without a conversation. "The customer is committed" is not checkable. "The named engineers have accepted the invitation" is.
- At least one condition points at you and could plausibly be unmet. If none can be, the gate is decoration.
- The waiver rule **produces a record**, and the record names who waived rather than saying that a waiver occurred.
- The stop position is genuinely disqualifying — it would stop the session, not merely complicate it. [S010](/wiki/seminars/S010.md) established the difference and this is where it is applied one level down.
- The gate distinguishes **a condition for the session to be useful** from a condition for it to be comfortable.

## How it goes wrong

- **The disposition gate** — every condition describes the customer's attitude: they are engaged, they are open to change, they are willing to invest. Attitudes are unfalsifiable in advance and infinitely available as explanations afterwards. *Warning sign:* your gate would let you explain any bad outcome without changing anything you do.
- **The waived prerequisite** — conditions are stated and then followed by an offer to proceed regardless. **They are therefore not conditions.** *Warning sign:* the document that lists the conditions also contains the words *even if*.
- **The unrecorded waiver** — the condition was waived, everybody remembers agreeing, nobody wrote down who decided. *Warning sign:* you can say a waiver happened but not who owns it.
- **Readiness theatre** — the gate is completed because completion is required, by the person whose session it would block. *Warning sign:* the gate has never once come back unmet.
- **The gate that only points outward** — the customer is assessed and you are not. *Warning sign:* nothing in your gate could be fixed by doing more work yourself.

## Do it

Take the corpus's own readiness posture as your worked example.

The practice material behind this stage arms its engineers against an anticipated objection: **the tooling improved and delivery did not speed up.** The prepared answer reattributes the shortfall to the customer's own organisational constraints. That answer sits alongside a readiness rubric composed almost entirely of customer dispositions.

Put those two together and write down what you get. **A model whose readiness criteria are the customer's attitudes, and whose prepared explanation for disappointment is the customer's constraints, has arranged its evidence so that a disappointing result confirms the thesis rather than challenging it.** There is no outcome that counts against it.

Now do the same to your own gate. Write the two sentences — *this went badly because…* for a customer-caused failure and for an us-caused failure — and check that your gate contains a condition that would have caught the second one. If it does not, add it.

Then write the stop position, and read it against [S010](/wiki/seminars/S010.md)'s refusal. **They should be consistent.** A person who can decline an engagement but cannot decline a session has not generalised the skill.

## Show it

Hand your gate to a peer with one question: **"What would have to happen for you to tell me not to run this?"**

If they cannot answer, the gate is not a gate. If their answer is something that has never happened to anyone, it is still not a gate.

## What this does not establish

Nothing here shows that gated sessions go better than ungated ones.

The corpus supplies **no session it declined to run**, no exclusion criteria of any kind, and no domain, scale or regulatory limit on the model. Its prerequisites are stated and immediately waived on its own pages. The argument that a disposition-only rubric is unfalsifiable is **an argument about the structure of the rubric, not a finding about outcomes**, and it should be held as one.

The claim that a recorded waiver behaves differently from an unrecorded one is **design reasoning with no external warrant.** It is included because an unrecorded decision cannot be reviewed, which is a property of records rather than evidence about behaviour.
