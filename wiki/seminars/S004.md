# S004 · Proof as an Act of Communication

**Module:** [M01](wiki/modules/M01.md) · **Quarter:** [Q1](wiki/quarters/Q1.md) · **Complexity class:** 1
**Strands:** MS · PE · CF
**Platform anchor:** Copilot as a proof-checking adversary; Learn MCP for definition grounding · **MCP servers:** Microsoft Learn MCP Server
**Whitepaper:** [WP-004](wiki/whitepapers/WP-004.md)
**Retrieves from:** S002, S003

---

## What this day is for

Students meet proof as a formalism to be satisfied — a ritual with a required opening, a required ending, and a marker who knows whether it worked. That framing produces students who can complete a proof template and cannot construct an argument, and it does not survive contact with professional life, where nobody grades your reasoning and everybody depends on it.

This day reframes proof as **a claim addressed to a specific reader who is entitled to disbelieve you**. Under that framing the standard of proof is not absolute; it is whatever suffices to move *that* reader. That is the same standard that governs a capacity argument at S020, an evaluation defence at S050 and a safety case at S087.

The day is therefore a mathematics day and a professional-formation day at once, and it is deliberately not possible to tell which course it belongs to.

**It is also the day the agent is put in the reviewer's chair, and fails there differently from how it fails as an author.** An agent asked to produce a proof produces a competent one. An agent asked whether a proof is adequate produces agreement, or a concern it manufactured to have something to say. Neither is a review. **A reviewer who never rejects and a reviewer who always objects are the same reviewer**, and the day's mandatory-rejection protocol — designed years before this anchor existed, to solve exactly this problem in students — turns out to be the instrument that exposes it in a tool.

---

## Phase 1 · Pretest and prediction (25 min)

Students receive three short arguments for the same claim, all reaching a true conclusion.

- **Argument A** is valid and complete.
- **Argument B** is valid but omits a step that a sceptical reader would demand.
- **Argument C** contains a genuine gap and reaches the right answer anyway.

Items:

1. Which of these would you accept from a colleague?
2. Which contains an actual error, and where?
3. Predict: will the room agree with you?

Item 3 is the calibration item. The room does not agree, and the disagreement is the day's opening fact.

**The Copilot prediction, sealed.** A fourth item:

4. The agent will be shown the same three arguments and asked, for each, whether it would accept it from a colleague. **How many will it reject — and will it identify the gap in argument C?**

Most students predict that the tool will find C's gap, on the ground that finding gaps is a mechanical task and mechanical tasks are what tools are for. Argument C reaches a true conclusion, and that is the property the prediction fails to account for.

**Warrant.** Pretesting on untaught material, with a generation demand. The disagreement establishes, empirically and in the first half hour, that sufficiency is reader-relative — which is the day's thesis, arrived at rather than announced. Item 4 is sealed at phase 1 per instrumentation rule 8.

## Phase 2 · Cumulative retrieval set (20 min)

Free recall, no notes, interleaved across the three prior days.

- State the S003 common-structure sentence.
- What is the complete state of the S002 machine?
- Why does changing the order of a sum change the result?
- One error class from S002 phase 6, and one from S003 phase 6, and what distinguishes them.

The final item requires comparison across days, which is the interleaving component.

**Warrant.** Retrieval at expanding intervals, with a discrimination item that cannot be answered from either day alone.

## Phase 3 · Contrasting cases and generation (45 min)

*First, 25 minutes, unchanged.* Groups receive **the same theorem proved three times**, each proof written for a different reader:

- for a fellow mathematician,
- for a sceptical engineer who wants to know when it fails,
- for a future maintainer who wants to know what they may rely on.

The three differ substantially in what they include, what they assume, and what they emphasise. All three are correct.

Task: **state what each proof's author assumed about their reader**, and then — the hard part — construct a fourth reader for whom none of the three would be adequate, and say what that reader would need.

Groups commit in writing.

*Then, 20 minutes: four prompts, run.* The same theorem is given to the agent four times, each time with one of four named readers — the mathematician, the sceptical engineer, the maintainer, and the group's own constructed fourth reader. Each group runs one.

**The payload is that the four outputs differ in register and agree in content.** The mathematician's version is terse and the maintainer's has headings; the same steps are established in all four, the same things are assumed in all four, and the reader-specific *question* — what will this reader not grant — is answered in none of them. The room predicts either four near-identical documents or four genuinely different arguments. It gets neither: **it gets one argument in four costumes.**

That is the exact failure phase 4 point 2 is about, produced by a tool rather than by a student, and it is more legible in the tool because the four outputs can be laid side by side and diffed. A group that has just written *the maintainer needs to know which assumptions the code now depends on* can point at the place where that sentence is absent from all four.

For the fourth-reader run there is a second finding, and it is the useful one for groups who underspecified. A reader described only as *someone who needs more detail* produces a longer document. A reader described by **what they will act on** produces a different one. The specification's quality determines the output's kind, which is [S003](wiki/seminars/S003.md) point 6 arriving in a register with no code in it.

**Warrant.** Contrasting cases with invariant deep structure: one theorem, three surfaces. The fourth-reader construction is the generation demand and is genuinely difficult; groups typically produce a reader who needs more detail, and rarely a reader who needs *different* detail. The four executions hold the theorem constant and vary only the named reader, which is the day's dimension, and the payload is available only because the room committed to its own account of reader-difference first.

## Phase 4 · Consolidating instruction (50 min)

1. **What a proof is for.** It transfers warranted belief from one person to another. It is not a certificate and not a ritual.
2. **The reader determines sufficiency.** What may be assumed, what must be shown, what level of detail is respectful rather than tedious.
3. **Proof techniques as rhetorical moves**, not templates: direct argument, contraposition, contradiction, exhaustion of cases, counterexample. Each introduced by the situation that calls for it.
4. **The counterexample as a first-class object.** Disproof is cheaper than proof and is frequently the more valuable professional contribution. A student who can construct a counterexample can refute a colleague's plan.
5. **Where arguments actually fail**: unstated assumption, unjustified case split, quantifier confusion, and the assumption of the thing to be shown.
6. **Grounding a definition is not the same as grounding a claim.** The Learn MCP Server will supply the documented definition of a term, from a corpus of publicly available Microsoft Learn documentation, refreshed incrementally and fully once a day. It will not tell you whether the definition supports the claim you are making with it. **The tool closes the citation gap and not the inference gap**, and the failure classes at point 5 all live in the inference gap. This is the day's version of *a source is not an argument*.

   *Perishable, marked as such:* the endpoint, the three tool names, the absence of authentication, the daily full refresh, and the fact that deprecated content redirects to the next best appropriate page — which means **a citation you recorded may later resolve to something you did not read**, and that is a professional problem, not a website problem. *Durable:* that a citation locates a claim and does not warrant an inference.

7. **A default the platform chose for you: what the reviewer was told to be.** Nobody in the room instructed the agent about how hard to press. Where an instruction is absent the tool supplies its own disposition, and the disposition it supplies is agreeable. The programme's own [specification rule 1](wiki/program/03-Assessment-Architecture.md) predicts this exactly: a reviewer given no statement of what is actually checked will produce a quality judgement, and the quality judgement it produces is the one that costs least. **Defaults are opinions, and an unstated review standard is an opinion about how much disagreement you want.** *Perishable: which disposition this version supplies. Durable: that an unspecified standard is not an absent standard.*

8. **The professional transposition**, stated explicitly. A capacity argument, an evaluation defence and a safety case are all this, addressed to readers with authority and no obligation to be charitable — and increasingly, the first reader of all three will be a tool that has no obligations at all.

**Warrant.** Direct consolidation indexed to the fourth-reader constructions on the board. Point 8 is the forward link and is stated once, plainly. Points 6 and 7 are placed between point 5 and the transposition because both are claims about what a reviewer supplies when unasked, and point 5's failure classes are the vocabulary they need. Scope rule, strong form: point 6 is safe as a statement about what a documentation-grounding tool can settle and unsafe as a statement about any corpus other than the one named, or any version of it other than today's.

## Phase 5 · Whole-task learning task (90 min)

Students prove a small claim — a property of the S002 machine, chosen so that the proof is genuinely short — **for a named reader**, and then have it rejected.

Structure:

1. **Round 1 (25 min).** Write the proof for a named reader supplied to you.
2. **Rejection (10 min).** Exchange. Your partner, playing the named reader, must reject your proof and state *specifically* what they were not given. Rejection is mandatory; "it's fine" is not an available response.
3. **Round 2 (25 min).** Repair the proof against the rejection.
4. **Second rejection (10 min).** A *different* reader, with a different standard, rejects the repaired proof.
5. **Round 3 (20 min).** Produce a version adequate for both readers, and state what it cost to serve both. **Then submit that version to the agent for review, twice: once asked simply to review it, and once under an instructions file stating what a rejection must contain — the form the human rejectors were held to.** Adjudicate both reviews. For every concern raised, record one of three verdicts: *genuine and I have addressed it*, *genuine and I decline to address it, for this reason*, or **manufactured**.

Step 5's cost statement is the day's key artefact: serving two readers is not free, and naming the cost is the same move as naming what a fix discards at S003.

**The adjudication is the day's build**, and it is a build in the sense that matters: the thing constructed is a *review standard*, written as an instructions file, whose effect on a reviewer's output the student can demonstrate. A student who writes a rejection form that produces manufactured concerns has built a bad standard, and has evidence of it. That is more than most professional review processes possess.

**The two rejections at steps 2 and 4 remain human and remain mandatory.** The agent is a third reviewer and not a substitute for either. This is deliberate: the day's affective work depends on students delivering and receiving critique from a person, and a tool that will reject on demand without discomfort would remove exactly the difficulty the protocol exists to create.

The three additional deliverables:

- **The prompt record**, including the rejection-form wordings tried and abandoned.
- **The intervention record.** An empty record fails unless the step 5 adjudication is present with at least one *manufactured* verdict reasoned out — that adjudication is the demonstration that the design could have caught an intervention-worthy error.
- **The frontier statement.** The usual honest answer on this day is that the agent drafted a competent proof faster than the student could, and reviewed it worse than a fourteen-minute partner did.

**Warrant.** Whole-task at class 1 with the method given. The mandatory-rejection protocol is the day's central design decision — it makes critique a role rather than a judgement, which is what allows it to be delivered and received in week four. The agent review is placed at step 5 rather than earlier so that the student has already experienced two substantive rejections and therefore has a standard against which the tool's review is legible as thin.

## Phase 6 · Error clinic (40 min)

Opened by the instructor with an argument of their own that was accepted and was wrong — ideally one that appears in this programme's own materials.

Then students: an argument you made that you now believe was inadequate, and what the reader should have asked you.

Collected by **failure class** from phase 4 point 5, not by student.

**The agent case.** A step 5 review from the room in which the agent **accepted a proof that a human rejector had already rejected on substantive grounds** — the same document, one reader finding a gap the other did not. The room is asked which of the two reviewers was wrong, and the answer is neither: they were applying different standards, which is the day's thesis. Then the harder question, and it is the one the clinic is for: **the student had the human rejection in hand and still recorded the agent's acceptance as reassurance.** That is not a failure of the tool. It is the ordinary human preference for the reviewer who agrees, and every student in the room recognises it.

The instructor's own disclosure at the top of the phase is chosen to rhyme with this: an argument that was *accepted* and was wrong, because acceptance is not validation, and it is not validation regardless of who or what is doing the accepting.

**Warrant.** Error management. The instructor disclosing a wrong argument that was *accepted* is the more useful disclosure than a wrong argument that was caught, because it demonstrates that acceptance is not validation. The agent case is selected so that the error under examination is the student's use of the review rather than the review itself, which is the only form in which this failure is correctable.

---

## Phase 7 · Peer articulation (30 min)

Pairs, adversarial by design. One student states a claim they believe about their own recent work — a program is correct, a method terminates, a value is always in range. The partner's task is to **demand a counterexample search**, and to keep demanding until the claim is either qualified or withdrawn.

Nearly every claim gets qualified. That is the phase's result.

**Warrant.** Interactive-tier articulation, applied adversarially. This is the first rehearsal of the entrustment-based discussion protocol that assessment uses in year three, and it is introduced here in a stakes-free form so that its later appearance is familiar.

---

## Phase 8 · Calibration and commitment (20 min)

- Item 3 resolved: the room did not agree about argument B, and the distribution is shown.
- **Item 4 resolved.** The cohort's predictions about the agent's rejections are compared against the run. It rejects fewer arguments than the room did, and it does not identify argument C's gap — a gap in an argument that reaches a true conclusion. The room predicted the opposite. Recorded on the frontier map, with the observation that this is the second day in a row on which the cohort's error about the tool ran in the direction of expecting mechanical tasks to be done mechanically.
- Each student records which failure class from point 5 appeared in their own phase 5 proof.
- Each student names one claim they currently believe about their own work that they cannot yet defend, to be defended or withdrawn by S008.
- Retrieval schedule: next morning, day 3, week 2, week 6.

## Assessment

One specification item in MS101. Binary. Resubmittable. No entrustment evidence.

Checked:

1. The three-round proof artefact, with both human rejections recorded verbatim, both repairs, and the cost statement from step 5.
2. The rejection-form instructions file, and the two agent reviews it governed — one unguided, one under the form.
3. The adjudication: a verdict for every concern raised, from *genuine and addressed*, *genuine and declined with a reason*, or *manufactured*.
4. **The prompt record**, including abandoned rejection-form wordings.
5. **The intervention record.** An empty record fails unless item 3 is present with at least one reasoned *manufactured* verdict.
6. **The frontier statement**, both halves, specific to this build.

**Nothing else is assessed.** Whether the agent's review was any good is not assessed. Whether the student's adjudication agrees with the assessor's is not assessed — only whether every concern received a verdict with a reason.

A rejection that does not state *specifically* what was not given fails the specification for the **rejecting** student, not the proving one. This is stated in advance and applies only to the two human rejections; the agent is not a student and its reviews are adjudicated rather than graded.

*Annotation at the point of change.* Item 1 is the original specification, unchanged, and the [Assessment Architecture](wiki/program/03-Assessment-Architecture.md)'s S001–S040 non-retroactivity boundary holds over it. Items 2 to 6 are new element classes and are stated in the closed form the rule requires.

## Perishability register

**Durable — assessed.** That a proof transfers warranted belief to a reader entitled to disbelieve. That validity is not reader-relative and what may be left implicit is. That the four failure classes name where arguments fail. That a citation locates a claim and does not warrant an inference. That an unspecified review standard is not an absent one.

**Platform instance — not assessed.** The Learn MCP Server's endpoint, its three tool names, its lack of authentication, its refresh cadence and its redirect behaviour on deprecated content. The agent's default reviewing disposition, and the model that supplied it. The instructions-file mechanism by which a review standard is delivered. **None of this grounds the day's claims**; the day's claims are about readers and arguments, and the tool is the instance on which they were exhibited this year.
