# S004 · Reading a Room You Were Invited Into

**Module:** [M01](/wiki/modules/M01.md) · **Quarter:** [Q1](/wiki/quarters/Q1.md) · **Complexity class:** 1
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** Copilot Studio declarative agents versus custom engine agents — using the initiation model and collaboration scope as a map of who a system is actually for
**Whitepaper:** [WP-004](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-004.md)
**Retrieves from:** S002, S003
**Entry state:** either

---

## The situation

You have been given an attendee list and an agenda. Both were written by somebody who has already decided what this session is.

The list is evidence. It tells you what the customer thinks you are, what they believe the problem is, and who they were unwilling or unable to release for a morning. Read it before you read the agenda.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught.

1. List everyone who will be present. Next to each name, write what that person can decide alone.
2. Which of them is worse off if this succeeds?
3. What did the invitation ask you to bring, and what does that tell you about what they think you are?
4. How would you find out what this room already knows, without asking them?
5. What would you see in the first minutes that would tell you to abandon the agenda?

**Most decision-rights columns end up repeating job titles.** Seniority is what an attendee list makes visible and authority is what you need, and they are not the same column.

## What you must produce

A **room reading**, in five parts:

1. **Attendance against decision rights** — every name, what that person can settle alone, and how you know.
2. **The absent** — who is not coming, who would lose if this succeeded, and which of those two lists overlap.
3. **One indirect probe** — a task or artefact request the room performs, never a question about how capable they feel.
4. **A dated pre-commitment** — a signal you might see and the change you will make if you see it, written before the room.
5. **The system's own room** — for the agent named in the brief, who it can act for, whether it can act unasked, and where the documentation says so.

## The material

### The attendee list is the most honest artefact of the engagement

The customer chooses who comes. That choice is made before you arrive, by somebody balancing what they think the session is against who they can spare, and it is the least performed thing they will do all week.

Read it as evidence. A room of managers with no hands-on engineers cannot commit to anything requiring hands. A room of engineers with no manager cannot commit to anything requiring a budget. The practice corpus behind this programme reaches the same conclusion from field experience and states it as a rule: sessions with only leaders produce plans nobody executes, and sessions with only practitioners lack the authority to change anything ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md)).

The most useful thing in that corpus is a set of workshop antipatterns whose warning signs are **observable in advance** rather than diagnosable afterwards: a customer asking for a tool overview; a room of managers with few hands-on engineers; nobody requesting an environment to work in; an agenda finalised before anyone understood the customer; labs prepared against sample data rather than the customer's own scenarios. **Every one of those is legible on the invitation.** None of them requires the session to start.

That corpus also contains a venue rule that reverses the usual ordering and is worth stealing: the pain point determines which engineers must be present, which determines where the session is held. Location is an output of the problem statement, not an input to it.

**It is internal, access-controlled and never quotable.** It is paraphrased here, its organisation is not named, and nothing in it may be linked. It records no declined engagement, no failure narrative that is not synthetic, and no measurement of anything.

### Seniority is what the list shows you; decision rights are what you need

The two columns are not the same column, and conflating them is the commonest failure of this exercise.

Seniority is a claim about position in a hierarchy. A decision right is a claim about what a person can settle without asking anybody — and in most organisations the two are distributed differently, because senior people delegate the decisions they find boring and retain the ones they find political. The person who can stop your engagement is frequently not the sponsor. It is whoever owns the system you need access to, or the review you have to pass, or the operational rota you are about to disturb.

So the question for each name is not *how senior are they* but *what does this person say yes to without a meeting*. If you cannot answer that, you have a title and not a right, and you should write "unknown" rather than infer.

### Positions, interests, and the person who has not spoken

The negotiation literature supplies the vocabulary this session needs, and it is more precise than the everyday use of the words suggests. **A position is a proposed answer; interests are the needs, fears, hopes, constraints and incentives that make an answer attractive** ([research/05-fde-craft/fisher-ury-getting-to-yes.md](/research/05-fde-craft/fisher-ury-getting-to-yes.md)). Opposed positions can conceal both conflicting and compatible interests, and each party normally has several — including basic concerns like security, recognition and control, which routinely outweigh the visible term under dispute.

The operational move is a pair of questions, and the second is the one people skip. Ask why a party wants its position. Then ask **why they have not already accepted the obvious alternative** — which requires reconstructing the choice as they see it, including the consequences for constituents who are not in the room. That second question is what turns an attendee list into a map, because the answer is usually about somebody absent.

The same source supplies the warrant for the artefact's fourth part. Fisher and Ury propose a **trip wire**: a deliberately formulated early-warning threshold, less favourable than the hoped-for result but better than the walk-away alternative, that triggers a pause before drifting into an unattractive commitment. It is explicitly not a rigid bottom line — its function is to create time to reassess. **That is exactly what a dated pre-commitment is**, transposed from a negotiation to a room. The later edition adds a *micro-BATNA* for a particular meeting and recommends preparing an exit line for the case where no commitment is reached, which is the same discipline at the scale of one morning.

**The book is a practitioner framework, not a controlled evaluation.** It develops its method through analytic distinctions, worked examples and scripts across family disputes, commercial deals and diplomacy; that range makes the mechanics memorable and establishes no treatment effect. The edition read here is the Penguin third edition of 2011.

### Who can speak in this room is not a property of the people in it

Here is the part that changes how you read silence.

Edmondson's account of why people withhold what they know is not about courage. It is about a rapid, barely conscious risk calculation, and she derives from interviews a set of **implicit theories of voice** — taken-for-granted rules about when it is appropriate to speak to those above you ([research/04-professional-formation/edmondson-psychological-safety.md](/research/04-professional-formation/edmondson-psychological-safety.md)). The rules are specific: do not criticise something the boss may have helped create; do not speak unless you have solid data; do not speak up if the boss's boss is present; do not say anything negative about the work in a group.

Underneath them sits an asymmetry that no amount of encouragement dissolves. **Voice benefits the organisation, after a delay, with low certainty. Silence benefits oneself, immediately, with high certainty.** Her summary of the consequence is blunt: no one was ever fired for silence.

Two further findings bear directly on your reading. The first is that silence is invisible — "Silence — when voice was possible — rarely announces itself! The moment passes, and no one is the wiser except the person who held back." **You will not see the thing you most need to see.** The second is that the construct lives at the level of the group rather than the organisation: even inside a company with a strong culture you will find pockets of high and low safety. So a room is not inheriting a company-wide condition. **It has its own**, and the person who has not spoken may be reading rules that do not apply three desks away.

The quantitative synthesis of this literature is worth knowing about for what it tested rather than for what it found. Frazier and colleagues aggregated a large body of samples and asked four structural questions: which antecedents matter most relative to each other; whether psychological safety explains anything over and above adjacent constructs like good leader relations and work engagement; whether the findings survive differences in study design and national culture; and whether the construct behaves the same way at individual and group levels ([research/04-professional-formation/frazier-psychological-safety-meta-analysis.md](/research/04-professional-formation/frazier-psychological-safety-meta-analysis.md)). **Those are the sceptic's questions, and asking them is what distinguishes a synthesis from a promotion.**

**Both notes carry access limits that matter.** The 1999 paper that introduced the construct as a research object has not been read for this repository; everything above comes from Edmondson's 2019 trade book, whose second part draws its case studies almost entirely from published teaching cases and journalism. The meta-analysis note is written from the paper's official abstract, because the publisher version is closed. **Direction and mechanism only.**

### Capability is assessed by what a room does

The practice corpus states the rule and gives the reason: assess a stakeholder's literacy with the tooling indirectly, because a direct question will not get an honest answer. That is correct and under-argued in its own material, and the voice literature supplies the missing half — admitting unfamiliarity in front of one's manager is precisely the interpersonal risk people are trained from childhood to avoid.

So the probe must be a task, not a question. Ask the room to open the last rejected submission. Ask somebody to walk you through the handheld application while you watch. Ask for a record that came back wrong. Each of those produces evidence about capability, about access, about who actually holds the credentials, and about whether the artefacts the brief assumes exist actually exist — and none of them requires anybody to appraise themselves out loud.

The corpus's difficult-situations catalogue is the other genuinely hard-won thing in it, and its entry on the sceptic is the strongest: validate the history, ask what was different last time, ask what would need to be true now, and recruit them explicitly as the person who can explain the obstacles. **A sceptic who feels heard is the best-informed person in the room about why the last attempt failed.**

One more flag from the same source, cheap to check and rarely checked: if you ask who the users are and the answer is "everyone" or "I don't know", treat it as a signal and escalate rather than proceeding.

### A system has a room too, and its documentation names it

Now the move that makes this session's platform work something other than an analogy.

An agent's build path is a published statement about who the system is for, and it is more explicit than anything you will get from a person. The vendor comparison material distinguishes declarative agents — "designed to be used by individuals", "limited to Copilot's orchestrator and models", which do not support proactive interactions and "rely on user-initiated interactions", integrated into Microsoft 365 apps — from custom engine agents, which "support both individual use and group collaboration", are "fully customizable, including choice of AI models and orchestration", and "enable agents to trigger actions automatically, even without direct user input" ([research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md)).

Five axes are doing work in that comparison: orchestration control, model choice, **initiation model**, **collaboration scope**, and hosting location. The middle two are the ones this session uses, because they answer the same question as an attendee list.

- **Initiation model.** An agent that cannot act without being asked is a fundamentally different system from one that can — in its architecture, its failure modes and its governance requirements. A reactive agent is for a person who has already decided to ask. A proactive one is for a workflow whose owner may not be watching.
- **Collaboration scope.** The documented trigger for group use is explicit: multiple users collaborating with or benefiting from the same agent in a channel or a meeting. An agent scoped to individuals is for individuals, and no amount of enthusiasm in the room changes that.

**So the brief's chosen build path already tells you who the customer thinks this is for, and it may not match the room they invited.** A room full of team leads asking for something a single analyst will invoke on demand is a mismatch you can find before the session starts, on a documentation page, without asking anybody an awkward question.

**That documentation is first-party product material with a selection purpose.** Its authority is definitional — it tells you what the products are, not that choosing them this way works. It offers no adoption data, no failure-rate data and no outcome evidence, it was read here through search-returned excerpts rather than full page fetches, and it is the most perishable material in this repository.

### The same conclusion is not the same reasoning

One last warning, from an unexpected place, and it is the reason your reading must be written before the room rather than after it.

When assessors judge a complex record, they converge on outcomes while diverging on reasoning: studies of portfolio assessors found they "reached similar final (pass–fail) judgments" while differing in their information-processing approaches and the reasoning behind them ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/govaerts-narrative-assessment.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/govaerts-narrative-assessment.md)). Worse for our purposes, most raters began forming an impression of the person the moment they began observing, and having reached a view they "were inclined to stick to their initial judgments even when confronted with seemingly disconfirming evidence" — their later reading produced more elaborate versions of the same conclusion rather than revised ones.

**Transpose that into a room and it is devastating.** You form your reading in the first minutes. Everything afterwards elaborates it. And when your team agrees with you at the debrief, the agreement may conceal four incompatible reasons, which will surface as a disagreement about something else three weeks later. **A pre-commitment written and dated in advance is the only artefact that survives this**, because it fixes the reasoning before the impression exists.

**Those findings come from medical education, with assessors reading portfolios rather than engineers reading rooms**, and the transfer is this programme's inference rather than a finding of that literature.

## The workflow

**Read a system's documentation the way you read an attendee list: to find out who it is actually for.**

You will use the published comparison guidance for Copilot Studio's two agent kinds, and then write a scope declaration for the agent your brief names.

**Step 1 — write your answer first and timestamp it.** In one sentence: who is the system in Brief A for? Save it before you open anything.

**Step 2 — find the initiation model.** Locate, in first-party documentation, whether the build path the brief names can act without being asked. Record the page and the date. This is a two-value answer and there is no third option.

**Step 3 — find the collaboration scope.** Locate whether it is documented for individual use or for group use, and what the published trigger for group use is. Record the page and the date. **If the documentation does not say, record the silence — do not infer it from a statement about a different product.**

**Step 4 — write the scope declaration.** Express what you found as a manifest fragment, so that the claim is in a file rather than in your head:

```json
{
  "name": "Reconciliation assistant",
  "description": "Answers questions about returned records for one analyst at a time.",
  "initiation": "user-initiated only; cannot act unprompted",
  "collaboration_scope": "individual",
  "acts_on_behalf_of": ["the invoking user, using that user's permissions"],
  "must_not": [
    "act when nobody has asked",
    "answer for a second person present in the same conversation",
    "record an approval on behalf of a named reviewer"
  ],
  "documented_at": {
    "page": "<the page you fetched>",
    "checked": "<the date you checked it>",
    "not_addressed": ["deployment scale for this build path"]
  }
}
```

**The `not_addressed` array is the part that matters.** A scope declaration listing only what the documentation confirmed is a marketing summary. One that lists what the documentation was silent about is a reading.

**Step 5 — map the system's room onto the human one.** Put your scope declaration beside your attendee list. Ask three questions and write the answers down. Does the system's collaboration scope match the number of people in the room who expect to use it? Does its initiation model match who the customer believes is accountable when it acts? And is there anybody on the attendee list whose work the system would act on, who has no say in whether it exists?

**Step 6 — attack your own reading.** Ask an agent to argue that the system is for somebody other than the person you named in step 1 — and to make the case using only your own scope declaration and attendee list. Do not ask it to check whether you are right. Record what you asked, what you kept and what you rejected.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That "who is this for" is a question with a documented answer, published by the vendor, available before the meeting — and that the answer is frequently not the one the room assumes. It also makes the durable claim of the session physically checkable in the one place where the room cannot be polite to you: **a system's scope is a fact about the system, and a room's scope is a fact about the room, and neither is changed by anybody's enthusiasm.**

## What good looks like

- At least one attendee is identified as able to stop the work, and it is not the sponsor.
- Every decision-rights entry says what the person settles without a meeting, or says "unknown" — no entry restates a job title.
- The absent list names somebody who would lose if the work succeeded.
- The probe is a task or an artefact request — something the room does — rather than a question about how confident they feel.
- **The pre-commitment is written before the room and names both the signal and the change**, specifically enough that somebody else could tell whether it fired.
- The scope declaration cites a page and a date, and its `not_addressed` list is non-empty.
- The reading distinguishes what the invitation asked for from what the engagement needs, and says what you will do about the gap.

## How it goes wrong

- **The seniority proxy** — attendance is read as authority, so the most senior person present is treated as the decision. *Warning sign:* your decision-rights column is a second copy of your job-title column.
- **The direct literacy question** — you ask the room how comfortable they are with the tooling. *Warning sign:* everybody reports being comfortable, and the answers arrive in order of seniority.
- **Demonstration drift** — the invitation asked for an overview of a product and you agreed to give one, so the session becomes a tour. *Warning sign:* the agenda was finalised before anybody understood the customer.
- **Reading after the fact** — the signals are all identified in the debrief, where they are obvious. *Warning sign:* your pre-commitment has the same timestamp as your notes.
- **Silence read as consent** — nobody objected, so you record agreement. *Warning sign:* the quietest person in the room is the one whose work changes most.
- **Agreement that conceals four reasons** — the team converges on a reading and nobody states why. *Warning sign:* your debrief produced a conclusion and no disagreement about anything.
- **The assumed audience** — you accept the room's belief about who the system is for without checking what the build path documents. *Warning sign:* your answer to step 1 and your answer after step 3 are identical.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The reading feeds the decision assessed at [S005](/wiki/seminars/S005.md), where it is used as evidence and never marked for accuracy — nobody in the room knows what the invented customer's real decision rights are.

**1 · Convert titles into rights.** For each, write what the person plausibly settles alone and what they must escalate:

- *Director of Operations* · *Lead Data Engineer* · *Information Security Manager* · *Team Leader, Field Services*

> **Model answers.** *Director of Operations:* settles who on their staff spends time with you; escalates anything changing headcount or a service commitment. *Lead Data Engineer:* settles schema and pipeline design inside their estate; escalates anything crossing a network boundary or changing who can read the data. *Information Security Manager:* settles whether an exception is granted against existing policy; escalates a change to the policy itself. *Team Leader, Field Services:* settles the daily rota, and **is very often the person who can stop the work by declining to release anybody** — which is why they are the answer to "who can stop this, and it is not the sponsor".

**2 · Design an indirect probe, then justify it twice.** Write one probe for Brief A. Then write two sentences: why the direct question fails, and what your probe reveals that a truthful answer to the direct question still would not.

> **Criterion.** A passing probe is performable in the room, by somebody present, in a few minutes, on the customer's own material. "Show me the last submission that came back rejected" passes. "How familiar is the team with agent tooling?" fails on both counts — it asks for a self-appraisal, and it asks for it in front of a manager, which is one of the recorded conditions under which people do not speak ([research/04-professional-formation/edmondson-psychological-safety.md](/research/04-professional-formation/edmondson-psychological-safety.md)). **The second sentence is the harder one:** a truthful self-report tells you what somebody knows, and the probe additionally tells you what they can reach, what exists, and who has to be asked for a password.

**3 · Go back to your own item 1, then name the difference.** Find the decision-rights column you wrote against the attendee list in opening item 1. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then rewrite it.

> **Criterion.** A passing entry answers *what does this person say yes to without a meeting*. It never restates a title, and it says "unknown" where you are inferring. **Most first attempts are a second copy of the job-title column** — count how many of your entries would change if you deleted the titles.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: presenting a correct answer beside a learner's wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference is. **That evidence is school and laboratory work and its application to working professionals is an extrapolation.**

**4 · Find the system's audience.** Using the workflow, determine the initiation model and collaboration scope for the build path named in Brief A, and write the scope declaration.

> **What you should find.** The comparison guidance states plainly that one kind of agent relies on user-initiated interactions while the other can trigger actions without direct user input, and that the group-use trigger is multiple users collaborating with or benefiting from the same agent in a channel or meeting ([research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md)). **You should also find at least one question it does not answer**, and that belongs in `not_addressed`. **Highly perishable: record the date, and expect the boundary to have moved by the time anybody reads your file.**

**5 · Interrogate a position.** The operations director insists the tool must run inside the existing shift-handover meeting. Write the two questions the negotiation literature prescribes, and one interest each might reveal.

> **Model answers.** *Why that meeting?* might reveal that it is the only time the whole team is reliably present — an access interest, not a tooling one. *Why have you not already put it in the morning stand-up instead?* might reveal that the morning stand-up belongs to somebody else, which is a control interest concerning a person who is not in your room. The second question is the load-bearing one: reconstructing why the obvious alternative was rejected "requires reconstructing the choice as the other side sees it, including consequences for absent constituents" ([research/05-fde-craft/fisher-ury-getting-to-yes.md](/research/05-fde-craft/fisher-ury-getting-to-yes.md)). **The framework is a practitioner method with no controlled evaluation behind it.**

**6 · Test your pre-commitment on a stranger.** Read it to somebody who has not seen the brief. Their only job is to say whether they could tell, from outside, that the signal had fired.

> **Criterion.** If they could not, the signal is a feeling and not a signal. **A trigger nobody else could recognise is a trigger you will decline to notice when it is inconvenient.** The negotiation literature's trip wire is the same device — a threshold set in advance whose function is to create a pause rather than to force a decision — and it only works if crossing it is observable.
>
> **Then compare:** look back at your answer to opening item 5, on what you would see in the first minutes that would make you abandon the agenda. Is it the same signal? If your pre-commitment is more specific than your item 5, name what made it so.

## What this does not establish

**Nothing here shows that a room read well produces a better engagement.** The material behind this session is a catalogue of observable warning signs assembled by experienced facilitators, offered without a single measurement, a single declined engagement or a single recorded failure to compare against. **It is hard-won operational knowledge and it is not evidence.** The two facts sit in the same documents in the same register, which is the reading skill this stage is actually training.

**The voice literature is cited at the level of mechanism only, and its access is limited.** The paper that introduced psychological safety as a research construct is unread here; the account used is the author's own trade restatement two decades later, whose case studies come almost entirely from secondary sources. The meta-analysis note rests on an official abstract because the publisher version is closed. **No magnitude from either is asserted.**

**The assessor-cognition findings come from a different setting.** Portfolio assessors in medical education are not engineers in a workshop, and the transfer of "you form a view immediately and then elaborate it" from one to the other is this programme's inference.

**The negotiation framework is a practitioner method.** It develops through analytic distinctions and worked examples across a very wide range of cases, which makes it memorable and establishes no treatment effect for any of its moves.

**The platform facts are definitional and perishable.** The comparison material tells you what the products are, not that any mapping between a product and an audience works. It was read through search excerpts rather than full page fetches, it states no scale envelope for most of what it compares, and its capability boundaries move.
