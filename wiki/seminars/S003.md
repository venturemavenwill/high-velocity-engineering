# S003 · The Problem Behind the Request

**Module:** [M01](/wiki/modules/M01.md) · **Quarter:** [Q1](/wiki/quarters/Q1.md) · **Complexity class:** 1
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** Microsoft Learn MCP Server with Copilot agent mode — generating alternative solutions to the customer's stated problem and testing which ones the documentation actually supports
**Whitepaper:** [WP-003](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-003.md)
**Retrieves from:** S001, S002
**Entry state:** either

## The situation

The request in [Brief A](/wiki/quarters/Q1.md) is specific. It names a technology, a document, a deadline and an outcome. Everybody in the room can picture it.

Nothing in it names a difficulty that anybody has.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught.

1. Write the request out verbatim. Underline every word that names a technology or a product.
2. What difficulty would remain if none of the underlined things existed?
3. When did that difficulty last actually occur? Who was present?
4. What is the current workaround, and who performs it?
5. What answer to item 3 would make you stop investigating this problem category altogether?

**Item 5 is the one people leave blank.** Once a problem category has been accepted, almost anybody will help you rank details inside it, whether or not the category matters to them.

## What you must produce

A **problem restatement with its evidence**, in five parts:

1. **The difficulty**, in one sentence containing no product, vendor or feature name.
2. **A datable last occurrence** — when it happened, and who was present.
3. **The current workaround**, and the named person who performs it.
4. **Three alternative solutions** to the difficulty as restated, of which the customer's original request is at most one.
5. **The documentation verdict on each alternative** — supported, outside the stated envelope, or not addressed — with the page and the date you checked.

## The material

### A stated solution is the normal form in which this work arrives

You will rarely be handed a problem. You will be handed an answer, and the answer will be confident, detailed and internally consistent, because somebody has already done the thinking that produced it in a room you were not in.

The practice corpus behind this programme states the pattern directly from field experience: customers routinely arrive having chosen a solution without ever having articulated a problem, and the real difficulty is often mentioned once, in passing, in the middle of a long conversation about solutions ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md)). Some customers have genuinely lost track of what they were solving, because it moved across handoffs or because the person who had it left. **Noticing the buried problem is the job**, and it is a listening task rather than an analytical one.

The same corpus names the failure mode the new tooling introduces, and it is the one this stage exists to prevent: solving the wrong problem quickly is the characteristic failure of accelerated delivery rather than a residual one. Speed does not create the error. **Speed removes the interval in which the error used to become obvious.**

**That corpus is internal, access-controlled and never quotable.** It is paraphrased here, its organisation is not named, and nothing in it may be linked. It records no declined engagement, no non-synthetic failure and no outcome data of any kind.

### The customer's account is weak evidence, and it is not about honesty

The interviewing literature is unambiguous that asking people about their problems produces unreliable data, for a reason that has nothing to do with integrity. Fitzpatrick's argument in *The Mom Test* is that the defect is in the question rather than in the respondent: asking whether an idea is good exposes the interviewer's hopes and invites protection, encouragement or casual opinion ([research/05-fde-craft/fitzpatrick-the-mom-test.md](/research/05-fde-craft/fitzpatrick-the-mom-test.md)).

His three rules are the whole method compressed: discuss their life rather than your idea; ask about specific past events rather than generic or future behaviour; and listen more than you speak. The evidence he treats as usable is correspondingly concrete — current costs, time spent, allocated budget, existing workarounds, previous searches for a solution, switching costs, and the most recent occurrence of the problem. **A hypothetical price gains no reliability by containing a number**, and neither does a hypothetical adoption rate.

The contextual-design literature reaches the same place from a different premise, and the premise is the more useful one. People "know their own activities intimately but do not ordinarily articulate their structure" — familiar action becomes tacit, retrospective accounts omit detail, general questions invite general answers, and "requests for features elicit solutions shaped by the tools people already know" ([research/05-fde-craft/beyer-holtzblatt-contextual-design.md](/research/05-fde-craft/beyer-holtzblatt-contextual-design.md)). On that account the customer is not withholding the problem. They cannot see it, because it is the water they swim in.

Portigal adds the observation that most often rescues a restatement from being useless. A reported pain point may be tolerated because changing it costs more than living with it — he uses *satisficing* for that acceptance of a good-enough arrangement — and he rejects outright the "scooping model" in which researchers collect ready-made needs and engineers implement them ([research/05-fde-craft/portigal-interviewing-users.md](/research/05-fde-craft/portigal-interviewing-users.md)). **Insight requires inference, not transcription.** If your restatement is the request with the verbs changed, you have scooped.

**All three sources are practitioner texts and none of them measures anything.** Fitzpatrick's book reports no controlled comparison, sampling method or validated threshold, and the copy this repository read carries no publisher, year or edition, so it cannot be cited by edition. Portigal's is the 2013 first edition and its footnotes point at examples and related methods rather than at any empirical evaluation of the method. The contextual-design volume is a method, and its Cool Concepts are described by its own authors as design lenses rather than a scored instrument. **Direction and mechanism only.**

### The three contaminants, and their repairs

Fitzpatrick classifies the three ways conversational data goes bad, and gives one recovery action for each. You will meet all three in your first hour.

- **Compliments** are socially pleasant and evidentially empty. *Deflect* — acknowledge and return to a fact or a commitment.
- **Fluff** is generic self-description, future promise and hypothetical possibility. *Anchor* — tie it to one past event and ask for the sequence, the attempted remedies and the outcome.
- **Ideas and feature requests** should be recorded and not obeyed. *Dig* — find the job, the constraint or the purchasing condition beneath the request.

The digging move has a companion in the practice corpus that is better than anything in the books, because it is taught as a dialogue rather than as a principle: asking *why is that a problem for you* immediately after a complaint is what converts a cosmetic preference into an underlying constraint. The worked example turns a request to dim the lights into a redesign of what happens when alerts fire. **A request names an object; the question after it names a mechanism.**

He also diagnoses the interviewer. The *Pathos Problem* is what happens when you expose your idea together with your investment in it: respondents protect you, even when invited to be candid. Pitching compounds the damage by correcting the respondent's understanding until agreement is obtained. The repair is unheroic — notice the pitch, apologise, return to their last thread, postpone the explanation.

### Premature zoom is the failure that feels most like progress

This is the one to fear, and it is why item 5 exists in the opening set.

Once an interviewer has signalled that a problem category matters, respondents will usually help rank details inside it **even when the category is irrelevant to their priorities**. The ranking they produce will be internally coherent, will feel like collaboration and will be about nothing. Fitzpatrick's instruction is to start broad enough to establish that the goal or problem is important, active and connected to existing effort, before examining features or sub-problems.

The practice corpus contains an independent rediscovery of the same rule, and enforces it structurally rather than advising it — a mandatory breadth-first block before any deep dive. **That is the best thing in its discovery material.** The rest of that material is a warning. Every section of its question bank asks the customer where the interviewer's own solution category would help, which is the central prohibition of the question-quality literature installed as the template rather than committed as an occasional slip. Nearly every question ships with specimen answers, which is defensible as preparation and lethal in the room, because appending examples narrows the response set. And there is no observation of any kind anywhere in it — nobody is asked to demonstrate a deployment, share a screen during a pipeline run, or set up an environment while being watched.

**This session teaches against its primary source on this point**, and does so on the strength of an argument rather than a finding.

Three absences in that question bank are worth carrying as a checklist of your own: it has no question about where the data comes from, who may see it or whether anyone consented; no question asking what result would make the customer conclude it had *not* worked; and no question asking who loses if it succeeds. You met the third at [S001](/wiki/seminars/S001.md). You will meet it again at [S004](/wiki/seminars/S004.md), where it is the whole session.

### Generating alternatives is the instrument, not the deliverable

Here is the move that makes the rest of this checkable, and it is the reason today's workflow generates rather than evaluates.

If you restate a problem and produce one solution, you cannot tell whether you found the problem or rediscovered the request. If you restate it and produce three, the restatement becomes falsifiable: a difficulty that admits only the customer's original answer was never restated at all. The contextual-design method builds this in deliberately — the team makes several visions before evaluating any of them, so that early convergence does not suppress possibility, and it appoints a designated challenger whose job is to introduce alternatives when the group settles too quickly. Its crossing from evidence to invention is deliberately controlled: ideas are posted beside the observations that prompted them, and "Posted ideas are prompts for later design, not accepted requirements."

The same source supplies the discipline that stops this becoming brainstorming. A participatory-design proposal from a user is evidence, not a specification: a requested handle may reveal a need to move the object rather than mandate a handle. **Every alternative you generate today is a probe into whether you understood the difficulty, and none of them is a plan.**

This is also, and not coincidentally, the shape of the learning design the whole programme uses. Generating several candidate solutions to a problem you have not been taught to solve is the generation phase of Productive Failure, and the failure is the point ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). **That evidence base is school, university and laboratory work; its application to working professionals is an extrapolation this programme concedes rather than hides.**

### What the agent can do here, and what you must not delegate to it

You are about to use an agent to help generate alternatives and to check them against documentation. Be clear about which half is safe.

Retrieving a documented constraint and quoting it with its source is a task these systems suit well. Deciding which difficulty matters is not, and the reason is structural rather than a matter of current capability: the argument that large language models do not plan in the sense the word usually implies bears directly on what may be delegated in discovery ([research/02-technical-foundations/kambhampati-llms-cant-plan.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/kambhampati-llms-cant-plan.md)). A model will produce a fluent restatement of a brief on request. **A fluent restatement is a translation, not an investigation**, and the two are indistinguishable on the finished page.

So the division for today is fixed: the agent proposes candidates and retrieves constraints; you decide which difficulty is real, and you supply the occurrence, the workaround and the name. Nothing in the model's output can supply those, because none of them is in any corpus it has read.

## The workflow

**Generate alternatives to the restated problem, then find out which ones the first-party documentation will actually support.**

You will use GitHub Copilot in agent mode with the Microsoft Learn MCP Server attached. The server is a remote streamable-HTTP endpoint, requires no authentication and carries no charge. Its tools cover documentation search, documentation fetch and code-sample search.

**Step 1 — commit your restatement before opening the agent.** Write parts 1 to 3 of the artefact and save them with a timestamp. **An artefact written after the tool has spoken cannot be compared with one written before**, and the comparison is the exercise.

**Step 2 — attach the server and confirm the agent will use it.** Add the Learn MCP Server to your editor's MCP configuration. Attaching is not invoking, and the gap fails silently: declining the trust prompt means requests continue **without** the tools and without an error, and the server's own guidance opens its recommended-instructions section with the case *when the AI agent doesn't use the MCP tool when expected*. A connected server is an available tool, not a used one.

**Step 3 — write an instructions file that forces the absence into view.** Create `.github/instructions/discovery.instructions.md`:

```markdown
---
applyTo: "**/*.md"
---

# Alternative generation and documentation checking

- When asked for alternatives, produce at least THREE that differ in
  MECHANISM, not in configuration. Two options that differ only by a
  setting count as one.
- At least one alternative MUST require no new software.
- Every capability claim MUST carry a citation to a fetched documentation
  page, in the same sentence as the claim, with the date fetched.
- Classify every alternative as exactly one of:
    SUPPORTED         - the documentation states the capability exists
    OUTSIDE ENVELOPE  - the documentation states a limit that excludes it
    NOT ADDRESSED     - no fetched page speaks to it
- "NOT ADDRESSED" is a finding. Do NOT answer from general knowledge and
  do NOT infer a limit from a limit stated about a different product.
- Never restate the customer's request as if it were the problem.
```

**Step 4 — ask for alternatives to the problem, not to the request.** Give the agent your one-sentence difficulty from part 1 — the version with no technology in it — and ask for three mechanisms that would reduce it. Do not give it the original brief yet. If you give it the brief, you will get the brief back.

**Step 5 — test each alternative against the documentation.** For each, ask what the first-party documentation states about the capability it depends on. Useful shapes of question: does the tool support acting without being asked? Is a stated deployment scale published for it? What does the documentation say about network isolation in its fast-start configuration?

**What you should expect to find.** Some capability boundaries are published plainly — the comparison guidance records that declarative agents "rely on user-initiated interactions" while custom engine agents "enable agents to trigger actions automatically, even without direct user input", and that Copilot Studio is "designed for small-scale deployments, such as department-level solutions" ([research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md)). Others are not published at all. **The `NOT ADDRESSED` rows are the ones to read carefully**, because they are where an ungrounded agent would otherwise have produced a fluent paragraph.

**Step 6 — now show it the original request** and ask which of your three alternatives it most resembles. If the answer is "none", either your restatement has drifted from the customer's world or the request was never a response to this difficulty. Both are findings and they require different next steps.

**Step 7 — attack your own restatement.** Ask the agent to argue that the customer's original request was correct all along and that your restatement removed something load-bearing. Do not ask it to improve your restatement. Record what you asked, what you kept and what you rejected, with a reason for each rejection.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** Two things. That a grounded answer is accurate within its corpus and silent outside it, with nothing in an ungrounded answer marking which side of the line you are on — the in-sentence citation rule is the apparatus that renders the silence visible. And that the number of mechanisms a restatement admits is a property of the restatement: a problem statement that yields three genuinely different mechanisms was written about a difficulty, and one that yields three configurations of the same product was written about a request.

## What good looks like

- The restatement contains no product, vendor or feature name, and would be legible to somebody who had never heard of any of them.
- There is a specific occurrence with a date attached, **not a pattern described in the present tense.**
- The workaround is named and so is its performer, because a difficulty nobody has worked around is usually a difficulty nobody has.
- The three alternatives differ in mechanism rather than in configuration, and at least one requires no new software.
- Every capability claim carries a page and a date, or is recorded honestly as not addressed.
- Your stated stopping condition is something an informant could actually say out loud.
- The prompt record shows at least one agent-proposed alternative you rejected, with the reason.

## How it goes wrong

- **Premature zoom** — you begin ranking options inside a category before establishing that the category is consequential to anyone. *Warning sign:* your first three questions all accept the request's framing without testing it.
- **The obliging witness** — you ask whether your approach would help, and are told that it would. *Warning sign:* nothing anybody said surprised you.
- **Requirement by transcription** — the request is copied into a backlog item with the verbs changed. *Warning sign:* your restatement and the original request share their nouns.
- **The unanchored anecdote** — a difficulty described entirely in generalities. *Warning sign:* the account uses "usually" and "typically" and never a date.
- **Alternatives that are one alternative** — three options that differ only by a setting. *Warning sign:* the same product name appears in all three.
- **The specimen answer** — you offer examples with your question and the informant picks one. *Warning sign:* the answer you received is on the list you supplied.
- **The inferred limit** — the documentation is silent, and you read a limit stated about a different product as if it applied. *Warning sign:* your `OUTSIDE ENVELOPE` row cites a page about something else.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The restatement is assessed at [S005](/wiki/seminars/S005.md), on whether the evidence and the assumption are separable — never on whether the problem you identified was the right one.

**1 · Repair four questions.** Rewrite each to be answerable only by recounting something that happened:

- *Would an agent that summarised these reports be useful?*
- *Is the current handover process painful?*
- *How often does this go wrong?*
- *Would your team adopt something like this?*

> **Model answers.** *When did you last need one of these reports summarised — walk me through what you actually did.* · *Tell me about the last handover that went badly.* · *When did it last go wrong, and when before that?* · *What was the last tool this team was asked to adopt, and what happened to it?* The test is whether the answer is a memory or a prediction. **Note what the third repair costs you:** you traded a rate for two dates, and you should prefer the two dates, because a remembered rate is fluff and a remembered event is not.

**2 · Dig beneath a request.** A customer asks for an export button on a dashboard. Write the three questions you ask before agreeing, and say what each is trying to distinguish.

> **Model answers.** *When did you last export something from here, and what did you do with the file next?* separates a real downstream job from a habit. *Who receives what you produce from it, and what do they do with it?* finds the actual consumer, who may be the person with the problem. *What do you do today when you need this and there is no button?* finds the workaround, and a request with no workaround behind it is usually a preference. Fitzpatrick's rule is that a requested integration may be optional convenience, a repeated reason alternatives failed, or shorthand for a costly reporting job — and **those three cases imply completely different engineering decisions** ([research/05-fde-craft/fitzpatrick-the-mom-test.md](/research/05-fde-craft/fitzpatrick-the-mom-test.md)).

**3 · Go back to your own item 5, then name the difference.** Find what you wrote to opening item 5 — the answer that would make you stop investigating this problem category altogether. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then rewrite it.

> **Criterion.** A passing answer is a sentence an informant could plausibly say, in their own words, about something that already happened. *"If they aren't really committed"* fails — nobody says that about themselves. *"The last three times this came up we just did it by hand and nobody minded"* passes. It is falsifiable, it is a memory, and it would stop you.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: presenting a correct answer beside a learner's wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference is.

**4 · Run the documentation test on a capability nobody has published.** Using the workflow, pick one alternative whose feasibility depends on something you suspect the documentation does not address. Record the `NOT ADDRESSED` result properly — the pages you fetched, the date, and the question that went unanswered.

> **What you should find.** The vendor comparison material publishes some limits plainly and states no scale envelope at all for most of the tools it compares; the department-scale statement about one product is **the exception rather than the pattern** ([research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md)). Record the absence as an open question. **Do not infer a limit from a limit stated about a different product**, which is the commonest way this exercise is failed.

**5 · Strike out the nouns.** Hand your restatement to a reader who strikes out every technology noun. Whatever survives is your actual problem statement.

> **Criterion.** If nothing survives, you have written the request again in a different register. This is the commonest outcome and is worth discovering here rather than in week six. **Then compare:** look back at your answer to opening item 2 — what difficulty would remain if none of the underlined things existed. Is the surviving sentence the same difficulty you named then, or a different one?

**6 · Find the buried problem in a transcript.** Take a discovery transcript from the cohort library and mark every sentence that describes a difficulty rather than a solution. Count how many are in the first quarter of the conversation.

> **What to notice.** The practice corpus records from field experience that the real difficulty is often mentioned once, in passing, inside a long conversation about solutions. If your marks cluster late, that is consistent with the observation. **It is a practitioner judgement and not a finding** — the corpus behind it measures nothing, records no failure that is not synthetic, and cites no external source anywhere.

## What this does not establish

**The techniques in this session have a practitioner literature behind them and no controlled evidence.** Asking for the last occurrence, refusing to zoom before the category is established, treating a feature request as a symptom — all three are diagnostic and procedural claims from books that report no measurement. Their direction is plausible; **no magnitude is known and none is asserted.**

**Two of the three books have edition problems that limit citation.** The copy of *The Mom Test* read for this repository carries no publisher, year or edition. The Portigal volume is the 2013 first edition only, and nothing from the later edition is used or implied.

**The corpus of professional practice behind this programme teaches the opposite of this session on the central point.** Its own discovery question bank asks the customer, in every section, to endorse the interviewer's solution category, and ships specimen answers alongside the questions. Neither is an occasional slip; both are the template. **This session teaches against its primary source on the strength of an argument rather than a finding.**

**Nothing here establishes that a better restatement produces a better engagement.** No source behind this programme measures that. What the session can defend is narrower: a restatement that admits only one mechanism has not been tested, and testing it is cheap.

**The documentation check tells you what a vendor says, not what is true.** The comparison material is first-party product documentation with a selection-guidance purpose. Its authority is definitional. It offers no adoption data, no failure-rate data and no evidence that its recommended mappings produce better outcomes, and it is the most perishable material in this repository.
