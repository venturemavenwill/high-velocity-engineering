# S007 · Who Is Not in the Room

**Module:** [M02](/wiki/modules/M02.md) · **Quarter:** [Q1](/wiki/quarters/Q1.md) · **Complexity class:** 2
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** Azure AI Search security trimming, and Microsoft Agent 365's tenant-wide tool consent and published agent-risk role matrix — the absent people whose rights and approvals decide what the system may return and who may investigate it
**Whitepaper:** [WP-007](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-007.md)
**Retrieves from:** S004, S005
**Entry state:** either

## The situation

At [S004](/wiki/seminars/S004.md) you read the people who were invited. This session is about the ones who were not, and about the specific question that almost no discovery instrument asks: **who is worse off if this succeeds?**

Some absences are accidents of scheduling. Some are a decision. The two look identical on an attendee list and they are not the same object.

There is a third kind, and it is the one this session's platform half is built on. Some people are absent from the room and present in the system anyway — as an access control list, a group membership, a sensitivity label. They never attend anything. Their rights are nonetheless the thing that decides what your system is permitted to say, and if you have not represented them the system will answer on their behalf without their permission and without your noticing.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught.

1. List everyone whose work changes if this succeeds. Now mark which of them were in the room.
2. Who can stop this after it is built, and were they consulted before it was?
3. Who loses time, status, headcount or discretion if it works?
4. Who owns the data you will need, has anybody asked them, and — separately — who is currently *forbidden* from reading it?
5. Of the absent parties, whose objection would be strongest? Write it at its strongest.

**Item 3 is asked in almost no discovery instrument in professional use**, including the one behind this programme — which is notable, because the same body of material makes a claim about headcount in its commercial section.

**Item 4 is the one this session is really about**, and most learners answer only its first half. The second half — who is currently forbidden — has an answer that already exists, in a system, today, and a retrieval system either honours it or silently replaces it.

## What you must produce

An **absence map**. Five parts:

1. **The affected-but-absent**, one row each, marked *late blocker*, *affected non-buyer*, or *source of evidence*.
2. **What each would say if asked**, in their terms — the strongest version, not the answerable one.
3. **One specific ask** that would bring the most consequential of them into the conversation, with a named person, a time and a thing you want from them.
4. **The enforcement row**: for every party whose access rights bear on what the system may return, the mechanism that would honour those rights, the trust boundary it sits behind, and the window during which a revocation would not yet have taken effect.
5. **The dropped list**: anything you considered and did not carry forward, with the reason. This is the most useful column on the page and the one nobody writes without being told to.

## The material

### Two kinds of absence with different remedies, and a third with no conversation attached

A **late blocker** can stop the work after it is built: a security function, a legal reviewer, a data owner, an external assessor. Consulting them late is expensive and recoverable. The remedy is a meeting with a defined purpose, and the failure mode is scheduling it after the design is settled.

An **affected non-buyer** will be worse off if the work succeeds. They usually hold no budget, are usually not introduced, and their objection is the one an engagement is least equipped to hear — because the person who introduced you to everyone else has no reason to introduce you to them. The remedy is harder and the session's only honest instruction is that their objection be recorded in their terms and not converted into a risk item with a mitigation.

The third kind has no conversation attached at all. **A person whose access rights constrain what a retrieval system may return is a stakeholder who will never attend anything.** Their interest is already encoded, in an access control list or a group membership or a label, and your system either reads that encoding or overrides it. This session treats that as a stakeholder problem rather than a security one, because at qualification it is: if the rights cannot be honoured, the engagement changes shape.

### The map you inherit is the map the sponsor drew

Discovery evidence is sampled, and the sampling frame is the sponsor's address book. That would be a minor problem if the omissions were random. They are not: the systematically absent parties are those whose interests diverge from the sponsor's, which is exactly the population whose evidence would change a qualification decision.

Kotter's account of organisational change puts the consequence of a missed blocker as strongly as anything in that literature — one well-placed blocker can stop an entire change effort, and his illustration is an executive who paid lip service to a transformation and did not alter his behaviour ([research/05-fde-craft/kotter-leading-change.md](/research/05-fde-craft/kotter-leading-change.md)). He is equally blunt about the limit of what a junior person can do about it: "If everyone in senior management is a cautious manager committed to the status quo, a brave revolutionary down below will always fail."

**Read that as a boundary on this session rather than as a counsel of despair.** Mapping absence tells you what an engagement depends on. It does not give you the standing to fix it, and a session that implied otherwise would be lying.

**The source's limits are unusual and should be stated whenever it is used.** Kotter's own 1996 preface records that the book contains no footnotes and endnotes, that he drew no examples or major ideas from any published source except his own writing, and that he did not try to cite evidence from other sources. Every case in it is anonymised and untraceable. The 2012 reissue adds a preface that corrects no error and retracts no claim. It is experienced practitioner counsel and it is not evidence.

### Awareness is not communication, and consultation is not consent

The change literature supplies one more mechanism worth holding, and it explains why a party who was told is not a party who was consulted.

Hiatt's model is explicit that sharing information does not by itself produce awareness: "Awareness is not achieved based on the messages sent, but rather how the messages are received and internalized by each person" ([research/05-fde-craft/hiatt-adkar.md](/research/05-fde-craft/hiatt-adkar.md)). He names five factors governing reception, of which the sharpest for this session is contestability — changes whose drivers are external and observable are easier to justify, and internally-motivated ones invite legitimate dispute, because "a reasonable person may debate the grounds for the change."

**That is a description of your absent party, not of a communication problem.** If your ask has only one possible answer, you have not asked; you have announced, and the announcement will be received by someone who is entitled to debate it and has been given no way to.

**Two limits, and the second one matters more than it looks.** The model is a registered commercial asset of the firm that publishes the book and sells the certification, every quantitative claim in it traces to that firm's own practitioner surveys with no methodology reproduced, and no independent evaluation of the model appears anywhere in it. And its scope is stated by its own author: the lifecycle "begins after a change has been identified", so **the model says nothing about whether the change is the right one** — which is precisely the question this stage exists to ask. Use it for how a stakeholder receives a message and not for whether the engagement should proceed.

### An index is a copy, and copies do not inherit their originals' access rules

Here the session becomes engineering.

Azure AI Search's documentation on document-level access control opens by stating that such control "is essential for building secure AI agentic systems grounding data, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) applications, and enterprise search solutions that require authorization checks at the document level" ([research/06-microsoft-platform/azure-ai-search-and-acl-patterns.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/azure-ai-search-and-acl-patterns.md)).

The reason retrieval raises the stakes is left implicit and is worth saying aloud. A conventional search interface that returns a forbidden document at least shows a link the user cannot open. **A retrieval system reads the document's contents into a generated answer. The leak is not a listing; it is the substance.** Access control therefore has to happen before generation, which means it has to happen in retrieval, which means the absent person's rights must live in or alongside the index.

The documentation offers four mechanisms rather than one blessed answer, and its selection rule is the transferable part: **reuse the authoritative permission system rather than reimplementing it.** Custom or non-Microsoft identity systems, and any index loaded by push, get **security filters** — a string comparison, described as being usable with any API version or package. Storage with existing native assignments gets **ACL and RBAC scopes**. Content already governed by enterprise classification gets **sensitivity labels**, which "reuses centralized classification and policy assignments". Content in the collaboration platform gets that platform's own ACLs.

**The security-filter route carries a word the documentation chose itself.** Contrasting it with the native pattern, the page notes that the permission filter metadata is recognised as Entra ID authentication, "whereas the security trimming workaround is simple string comparison". *Workaround.* A string filter is only as trustworthy as the code that supplies the string; nothing at the search service validates that the caller is who the filter says they are. **The security boundary sits in your application, not in the service** — which is a fact about who is accountable, and therefore a fact about a stakeholder.

The native pattern moves that boundary, and its two-stage structure is the design insight: first the service checks that the client application may read the index at all, and then, given the end user's token carried on a separate request header, it checks per-document user and group permissions and excludes anything that does not match. **Application authorisation and user authorisation are separate concerns evaluated separately.** The documentation also gives a governance recommendation worth carrying: "use group access over individual user access for ease of management."

### Two silent failures, and both produce a system that appears to work

**Unprojected chunks.** When a skillset chunks documents — which is standard practice — the permission metadata fields move from indexer field mappings to index projections, and without that projection, chunk-level references are not filtered. **An unprojected chunk is an unprotected chunk**, and nothing about the system's behaviour announces it.

**Stale permissions.** This is the sentence a qualification decision turns on. Enforcement evaluates the caller's claims against permission metadata *already stored in the index*, and permission changes in the source system are only reflected after that metadata has been synchronised. The preview terms say it more bluntly still: a timing lag occurs before the preview API recognises changes to access or permission restrictions. **A revocation is not immediate, and no latency figure is published anywhere** — so the size of the window is unknown and must be measured per deployment.

There is an asymmetry inside the collaboration-platform pattern that makes this worse and is easy to miss: changes on items with unique permissions refresh on each successful indexer run, while changes inherited from a parent scope require an explicit resynchronisation. **The inherited case is both the more common one and the one that does not self-heal.**

And there is a stated gap between what the source system enforces and what the index enforces: certain information-management policies that gate user access "aren't evaluated, ingested, or honored at query time". A control that exists upstream and is not honoured downstream is an absent stakeholder's right, quietly not represented.

### The privilege you must hold in order to respect a privilege

Integrating the collaboration platform's ACLs requires a Graph permission granting full control over all sites in order to read content and permissions, plus a directory-read permission when indexing list items and pages, in order to resolve email addresses into directory object identifiers.

**Requiring full control over everything in order to read who may see what is a real consideration and belongs on your absence map.** The security function that will have to approve it is a late blocker, and the conversation is much cheaper before an architecture depends on it.

**The whole source is highly perishable** and this must travel with every use of it: most of the page describes a preview API, the four approaches are compared on fit rather than on security strength, feature availability differs between API versions and SDK packages, and the terms place responsibility for compliance and geographic boundaries explicitly on the implementer.

### The person who decides your agent's reach, and never attends anything

There is a fourth kind of absence, and it is the sharpest one available at qualification because it has a named role, a documented power and no seat.

Approving a tool for an agent to use is not a project decision. The agent control-plane documentation records that granting agents access to a tool is a **tenant-wide consent**, and that only two roles may grant it: AI Administrator and Global Administrator ([research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-365-and-agent-identity.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-365-and-agent-identity.md)). Neither is typically a workshop participant. Neither has heard of your engagement. And the decision is binary — the tool is available to agents in that tenant, or it is not.

The Microsoft 365 signal layer makes the same point from the other direction: the tool exposing work data to an agent requires an Entra Global Administrator to consent to a named application permission before any agent can call it, and requires every calling user to hold a licence besides ([research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-iq-intelligence-layer.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-iq-intelligence-layer.md)). **The reach of the thing you are designing is set by somebody whose calendar you cannot see, at a scope larger than your project, for reasons that have nothing to do with your project.**

Read that as a stakeholder finding and not as a permissions detail. The consent-holder is a late blocker with an unusual property: they cannot be brought forward by a conversation about the design, because the object of their decision is the tenant rather than the design. **Naming them in writing before design proceeds is the entire available move**, and an engagement that has not identified them holds a dependency it cannot describe.

### The published role matrix breaks the runbook, and that is an absent-person problem

Now the finding worth more than any capability description in this session.

The same documentation publishes a table stating which administrative roles may investigate an agent risk in which product. It is not a table anybody would think to read, and it says this: **the AI Administrator — the role that can approve agents, assign owners and grant tenant-wide tool consent — cannot open the threat-detection or data-governance evidence behind the risks it is being asked to act on.** Security-oriented roles can open the threat-detection evidence. **And nobody in the published table can investigate the data-governance risks at all**; those require separate insider-risk roles, granted inside the governance product, by somebody the table does not mention.

The consequence is exact and testable on paper. **A single-operator runbook for agent risk triage cannot be written against these roles.** Any real process needs at least two people, and one of them holds a role appearing nowhere in the matrix that describes the process.

That is not a security topic. It is this session's topic. **A governance gap is a person-shaped hole** — the process assumes an actor who does not exist, and the absence is discoverable from a published table rather than from an incident. The right entry on an absence map is not "we will define roles later". It is the second and third people, named by role, with the observation that no single role completes the sequence.

**The limits, and they matter here more than usual.** The source was read on 2026-07-31, shortly after general availability, and large parts of the surface it describes are preview or sit behind a separate early-access programme. Twelve pages were fetched and read in full and roughly thirty more were read as search excerpts, so several structures are reconstructed. Its authority is definitional throughout: it states precisely what each role may do and **publishes no detection rate, no false-positive rate, and no measure of how often the triage it describes is completed at all.** No such figure appears here, and asserting one remains forbidden across this wiki.

### What gets dropped because it cannot be bound to a component

One last mechanism, from a different kind of source, and it is the reason your map needs a dropped column.

The chained threat-modelling pipeline recorded at [research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-agentic-threat-modelling.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-agentic-threat-modelling.md) — internal material, paraphrased here and never quoted — enforces a discipline that is genuinely good: a threat survives only if it can be bound to at least one named component and at least one numbered dataflow, and anything that cannot is discarded with a logged reason. That keeps the model about *this* system.

It also, structurally, removes everything about the operator, the supply chain, human process and out-of-band administration — each discarded with an entirely plausible justification that nobody reads. The evaluator downstream then declines to deduct for threats that are missing, listing them separately as unscored suggestions.

**The general form: a filter that keeps the analysis concrete removes exactly the participants who are not represented in the architecture.** People are not components. If your map has no dropped column, you have the same design, and you will not be able to tell either.

That artefact reports no observed result of any kind, no comparison against a human-authored model, and no coverage measurement. It is a specimen of a design pattern and it establishes nothing about frequency.

## The workflow

**Represent one absent party's access rights in a retrieval system, then break the representation and observe that nothing complains.**

You will use Azure AI Search. If you have no search service available, steps 1, 2, 6 and 7 are executable against the published documentation alone, and step 6 — writing down the revocation window you cannot measure — is the one this session most wants you to have done.

**Step 1 — name the enforcement question per party.** For every row on your absence map, write which of three things is true: their rights do not touch this system; their rights are enforced by a mechanism you can name; or their rights are enforced by your own application code. The third is the interesting one and learners routinely file it as the second.

**Step 2 — choose an approach and state the rule you used.** The documentation's selection logic is to reuse the authoritative permission system rather than reimplement it. Write down which system is authoritative for your content, and what you are giving up: the classification-label route, for instance, costs you autocomplete and suggestion behaviour on that index, single-tenancy, and general availability.

**Step 3 — configure a permission filter rather than a string field.** The difference between these two index shapes is the difference between an enforced boundary and a convention.

```jsonc
// Index definition (fragment). The permission field is what makes an absent
// person's rights enforceable by the service rather than by your own code.
{
  "name": "assessment-notes",
  "permissionFilterOption": "enabled",
  "fields": [
    { "name": "id",        "type": "Edm.String", "key": true },
    { "name": "chunk",     "type": "Edm.String", "searchable": true },
    { "name": "parent_id", "type": "Edm.String", "filterable": true },

    // Entra-recognised permission metadata — NOT a string your application supplies.
    // Groups, not individuals: the documentation recommends group access
    // "for ease of management", and an individual list is a maintenance liability.
    { "name": "groupIds",  "type": "Collection(Edm.String)",
      "permissionFilter": "groupIds", "filterable": true }
  ]
}
```

**Step 4 — send the end user's token, separately from your own.** Two tokens, two checks, two accountable parties.

```http
POST /indexes/assessment-notes/docs/search?api-version=<preview-api-version>
Authorization: Bearer <APPLICATION token — the app's own read role on the index>
x-ms-query-source-authorization: Bearer <the END USER's Entra token>
Content-Type: application/json

{ "search": "assessments returned for rework in the last quarter" }
```

Remove the second header and run the same query. Note what comes back, and note that nothing in the response tells you which of the two runs was the authorised one.

**Step 5 — chunk a document and forget to project the permission field.** Split a protected document with a text-split skill, leave the permission metadata in the indexer field mappings rather than carrying it onto the projected chunks, and query as the unauthorised persona. The chunks come back. **This is the single most instructive failure in the topic**, because the parent document is correctly protected and the system therefore looks correct from every angle except the one that matters.

**Step 6 — revoke, then time it.** Remove the user from the group in the source directory and re-run the query on a loop until the document stops being returned. Write the elapsed time into your absence map as the revocation window. If you cannot run this, write down that no latency figure is published and that the window is therefore unknown — **which is the honest entry, and it is a constraint on the engagement rather than a detail of the build.**

**Step 7 — write the enforcement row.** For each party: mechanism, trust boundary (service or your code), synchronisation route, revocation window, and one thing this arrangement does not honour.

**Step 8 — attack your own map.** Give the agent your map and ask it to argue that every strongest objection you wrote is a straw figure, and that every enforcement row's trust boundary is one level weaker than you claimed. Then ask it to name, for each, the observation that would settle it. Record what you kept and one row you changed.

**A second pass: the parties whose absence is a role rather than a person.**

Nothing in this pass requires a tenant. All of it is answerable from published documentation, and its output is two entries on the absence map that no conversation with the sponsor would ever have produced.

**Step 9 — name the consent-holder.** Establish who in the customer organisation holds AI Administrator and who holds Global Administrator. Record whether either has been engaged. If neither has, that is the blocker, and it is written down before design proceeds rather than discovered when the first tool approval fails.

**Step 10 — write one triage sequence, then assign every step to a role.** Take a single line from the published risk taxonomy — *an agent appears on the at-risk list* — and decompose it: see the risk, open the evidence behind it, decide, act. Put a role against each step, drawn from the published matrix rather than from your assumption about what an administrator can do.

**Step 11 — prove that no single role completes it.** This is the whole exercise, it is provable on paper, and the proof is the artefact.

```yaml
# absent-roles.yaml — parties whose absence is structural rather than accidental.
consent_holder:
  decision: "whether agents in this tenant may use a given tool"
  scope: tenant                      # not the project. This is why they are absent.
  granted_by: ["AI Administrator", "Global Administrator"]
  identified: false
  engaged: false
  blocker_recorded_on: null
  note: >
    Cannot be brought forward by a conversation about the design, because the
    object of the decision is the tenant. Name them, in writing, before design.

triage_sequence:
  trigger: "an agent appears on the at-risk list"
  steps:
    - step: "see the risk"
      role: "AI Administrator"
      can_complete: true
    - step: "open the threat-detection evidence behind it"
      role: "AI Administrator"
      can_complete: false            # the published matrix says no
      second_person_required: "a security-oriented role"
    - step: "open the data-governance evidence behind it"
      role: "every role in the published matrix"
      can_complete: false            # nobody in the table can
      second_person_required: "insider-risk roles granted inside the governance product"
    - step: "act on the finding"
      role: "AI Administrator"
      can_complete: true
  single_operator_possible: false
  finding: >
    The process assumes an actor who does not exist. The gap is discoverable from
    a published table rather than from an incident, and it belongs on the absence
    map as two named people rather than as a note to define roles later.

silences:
  detection_rate: none
  false_positive_rate: none
  triage_completion_measure: none
  read_on: 2026-07-31
```

**Step 12 — attack the role file.** Give the agent `absent-roles.yaml` and require it to argue that a single operator *can* complete the sequence. Then require it to name the page that would settle the question. **The failure of that argument is the finding**, and a learner who cannot make the agent fail has not read the matrix.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That an absent person's rights are a runtime property with a latency, not a policy statement; that the boundary between "the service enforces this" and "we enforce this" is invisible in every response the system returns; that the commonest way to lose somebody's rights is not to violate them but to chunk the document they were attached to; and that **a published process can require an actor nobody has hired**, which is legible from a role table and from nothing else.

## What good looks like

- At least one listed party bears a cost and holds no purchasing power. **Those are the ones a map assembled from the sponsor's contacts will never contain.**
- The strongest objection is written in that party's terms, and it is one you cannot immediately answer.
- Each party who could stop the work later is marked, with the stage at which they would do it.
- The ask is specific — a named person, a time, and what you want from them — and it has a possible answer other than yes.
- Every party whose access rights bear on the system has an enforcement row naming a mechanism, a trust boundary and a revocation window, with "unknown" stated where it is unknown.
- The dropped column is not empty, and at least one entry in it is a person rather than a topic.
- Somewhere on the page, a right that the upstream system enforces and the retrieval system would not honour is named.

## How it goes wrong

Seven failure modes, each with the warning sign that precedes it.

- **The buyer's map** — only people with budget or authority appear, because those are the people the sponsor introduced you to. *Warning sign:* nobody on your map is worse off if the work succeeds.
- **Charitable ventriloquism** — you state each absent objection in the form you are best placed to answer. *Warning sign:* every objection on your page has a rebuttal beneath it.
- **The consulted formality** — the absent party is to be informed rather than asked. *Warning sign:* your ask has no possible answer other than agreement.
- **Deferred consultation** — the data owner, the security reviewer or the operator is scheduled after the design is settled. *Warning sign:* the phrase "we will bring them in once we know what we are building".
- **The convention mistaken for a boundary** — a string filter supplied by your own application is recorded as enforcement. *Warning sign:* you cannot say what would happen if the string were wrong, because nothing would.
- **The unprojected chunk** — permissions are correct on the document and absent on the pieces of it that are actually retrieved. *Warning sign:* your protection was verified by querying for whole documents.
- **The instantaneous revocation** — the system is assumed to honour a permission change as soon as it is made. *Warning sign:* your map has no revocation window, or it has one and nobody measured it.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The map is carried into [S010](/wiki/seminars/S010.md), where a refusal resting on an excluded party must show that the exclusion was established rather than assumed.

**1 · Rewrite three asks so they can be refused.** Each of these is an announcement. Rewrite it as an ask.

- *We will be sharing the design with the security function next month.*
- *Operations will be consulted during rollout.*
- *We wanted to let the assessors know this is coming.*

> **Model answers.** *Can you give us forty minutes before the design is fixed, to tell us which of these three data sources you would refuse and why?* · *Which two of your team's current tasks would you want protected from this, and what would we have to show you to believe it?* · *If we brought you the first ten outputs, what would you look for, and what would make you say stop?* **The test is whether the person can say no and the engagement changes as a result.** If a refusal would leave your plan intact, you sent a notification.

**2 · State the trust boundary.** Using the workflow above, take one retrieval design and write the sentence naming who enforces the access decision — the search service against a directory token, or your own application code against a string it constructed. Then write what happens if that party is wrong.

> **What you should find.** The documentation calls the string-comparison route a workaround, in those words, and contrasts it with permission metadata recognised as directory authentication. **If your answer is "our application filters the results", the correct enforcement row records that your application is the security boundary** — which is a legitimate design, and a different one from the one most teams believe they have built.

**3 · Go back to your own item 4.** Find the second half of what you wrote to opening item 4 — who is currently forbidden from reading the data. Most learners will find they answered only the first half. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then rewrite it as an enforcement row.

> **Criterion.** A passing answer names a population, the system that currently holds that population's rights, the mechanism that would carry those rights into retrieval, and the window during which a revocation would not yet be honoured. "We would apply appropriate access controls" fails on all four. "The field engineers' notes are readable by their own regional group only, that grouping lives in the directory, it would be carried as permission metadata evaluated against the caller's token at query time, and a revocation would not take effect until the index resynchronises — for which no published latency exists" passes.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Break it, then explain why nothing complained.** Run step 5 of the workflow — chunk a protected document without projecting its permission metadata — and write one paragraph explaining, to somebody who has not seen it, why every test they would have thought to run passes.

> **What to notice.** The parent document is correctly protected. A permission audit on the source passes. A spot check on document retrieval passes. **The failure is only visible if you query for the unit the system actually returns**, which is the chunk, and almost nobody writes that test because the design document does not mention chunks.

**5 · Find the silence in the room.** Somebody who attended every meeting and said nothing is also not in the room. Write down one such person from your own working life, and what it would have cost them to speak.

> **Scoring criterion.** A passing answer describes a calculation rather than a personality. Edmondson's account of silence is that it is a risk assessment made quickly and usually below conscious attention, and that the payoffs are asymmetric: voice benefits the organisation later and with low certainty, silence benefits the individual immediately and with high certainty ([research/04-professional-formation/edmondson-psychological-safety.md](/research/04-professional-formation/edmondson-psychological-safety.md)). She notes that silence "rarely announces itself" — the moment passes and nobody is the wiser except the person who held back. **If your answer names a trait, rewrite it as a payoff.**

**6 · Name the dropped.** Closed book: what does a threat-modelling filter that requires every finding to be bound to a named component and a numbered dataflow systematically remove?

> **Answer.** Everything about the operator, the supply chain, human process and out-of-band administration — because people are not components, and a filter that keeps the analysis concrete removes exactly the participants who are not represented in the architecture.
>
> **Then compare.** Look back at your answer to opening item 1, where you listed everyone whose work changes. How many of them would survive that filter? **The ones that would not are your map's real content**, and if you have none, your map was drawn from the architecture rather than from the organisation.

**7 · Write the triage runbook and watch it fail.** Using the workflow's second pass, write the runbook for *an agent appears on the at-risk list*. Assign every step to a role from the published matrix. Then answer one question: how many people does this runbook need?

> **Answer key.** More than one, and the reason is published rather than inferred. The role that governs agents — the one that can approve them, assign owners and grant tenant-wide tool consent — **cannot open the threat-detection or data-governance evidence behind the risks it is being asked to act on.** Security-oriented roles can open the threat-detection evidence and cannot open the data-governance evidence. **Nobody in the published table can investigate the data-governance risks**, which need separate insider-risk roles granted inside the governance product, by somebody the table does not name.
>
> **Scoring criterion.** A passing runbook names at least two roles and states which step forces the handover. A runbook whose step reads "the administrator investigates the risk" fails, and it fails against a published table rather than against an opinion. **A learner who writes "escalate to security" without naming what security can and cannot see has moved the gap rather than found it** — the data-governance evidence is outside that role too.

## What this does not establish

**Nothing here shows that consulting absent parties improves an engagement.** The corpus behind this programme is silent on the question in a specific and diagnosable way: it has no data block at all, no question about who may see the data or whether anyone consented, and no question asking who loses if the work succeeds. That silence is an absence in a source, not a finding about practice, and this session is built on it.

**The change literature establishes mechanisms, not outcomes, and one of its two sources cites nothing.** Kotter's own preface states that he drew no examples or major ideas from any published source except his own writing and did not attempt to cite evidence; every case in the book is anonymised and uncheckable. The ADKAR material is published by the firm that owns the trademark and sells the certification, contains no independent evaluation of the model, and its author's own scope statement excludes the question this stage asks — whether the change is the right one.

**The psychological-safety material is cited for a mechanism and its strongest evidence is elsewhere.** The book read here is a trade account by the construct's originator; its literature review has no search protocol and reports no disconfirming study, its case studies are secondary and selected after the outcomes were known, and no trial of its leadership framework is reported. The quantitative synthesis of that literature is a separate source and is used at [S010](/wiki/seminars/S010.md).

**The platform material is first-party documentation describing mostly preview functionality.** It compares its four approaches on fit and not on security strength — a course must supply that analysis, and this one only begins it. It publishes **no synchronisation-latency figures**, so the size of the revocation window is genuinely unknown and none is asserted here. It offers no failure-rate data and no measurement of how often any of this goes wrong in practice.

**That access control is a stakeholder question rather than a security question is this design's framing**, not a claim from any source. The retrieval failure taxonomy this programme uses does not centre access control at all ([research/02-technical-foundations/barnett-rag-failure-taxonomy.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/barnett-rag-failure-taxonomy.md)) — which is itself worth noticing, since that taxonomy is an experience report from three deployed systems.
