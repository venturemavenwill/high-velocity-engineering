# S028 · The Architecture You Are Not Allowed to Build

**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** Microsoft Foundry basic versus standard setup — the prototyping topology that lacks network isolation, and the production topology compliance actually requires
**Whitepaper:** [WP-028](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-028.md)
**Retrieves from:** S026, S027
**Entry state:** this-programme-only

## The situation

Your register from [S026](/wiki/seminars/S026.md) is nearly all frozen.

The design that would actually solve this is not available to you. The one that is available is worse, and you know exactly how much worse.

**Answer these in writing before reading further.**

1. Describe the system you would build if nothing were frozen. One paragraph.
2. Which single constraint, removed, gets you closest to it?
3. **What is the smallest thing in this space you can change without anyone's permission?**
4. What would you have to demonstrate before anybody would seriously consider unfreezing the constraint from item 2?
5. If nothing moves, what do you build?

**Item 3 is the session and most people answer it with "nothing".** That answer is almost always false, and this session exists to make it expensive to give.

## What you must produce

Two artefacts. They are produced together and neither is acceptable alone.

- **The unavailable design** — the system you would build, written down properly, with the single constraint that forecloses it named. Not a complaint. Written well enough that somebody could build it if the constraint moved.
- **The available change** — the smallest thing you can move now, without permission, chosen for **what it would demonstrate about the frozen constraint** rather than for being easy.

Plus the argument for unfreezing, addressed to the person your register names, written in the terms of that person's own interest.

## The material

### The engineer's judgement is the thing being wasted, and its waste is invisible

When a design is foreclosed by a constraint, the analysis that produced the design does not go anywhere. It is not written down, because writing down a design you cannot build feels like self-indulgence. It is not communicated, because the audience for it is the person who holds the constraint and approaching them feels like escalation. **So it evaporates**, and what remains is a diminished design with no record that a better one existed or what it would have required.

The consequence appears later and is attributed elsewhere. When the delivered system underperforms, the constraint is cited — correctly, and unfalsifiably, because nobody wrote down what the alternative was or what it would have cost. **The explanation cannot be checked because the evidence for it was never produced.**

The rule this session is built on comes from the practice corpus behind this programme: even in an almost entirely frozen space something can be changed, and constraint is not an excuse for doing nothing ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md)). **No instance of a frozen space being opened is recorded anywhere in that material, no engagement is described in which the rule was applied, and nothing in it is cited, sourced or measured.** Worse, and this is taught rather than hidden: **the same body of material elsewhere explains disappointing delivery outcomes by pointing at the customer's organisational constraints** — the exact move the rule forbids. Hold both, and notice which one is being used when.

### The sharpest platform lesson in the stage, and the wrong reading of it

Microsoft Foundry offers two setup topologies, and the documentation describes them with unusual candour. The **basic setup** is **"for rapid prototyping and individual development when speed and ease of access are priorities"** and **"lacks network isolation"**. The **standard setup** provides **"fine-grained control over data, security, and networking"**, with public networking for non-confidential workloads needing enterprise data controls and private networking for confidential workloads under strict compliance requirements ([research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md)).

**A vendor stating plainly that its fast option has no network isolation is good documentation and should be read closely for that reason.**

Now the reading that matters, because almost everybody gets it wrong. **The lesson is not "always choose the controlled option".** It is that **prototyping topology and production topology are different things, and the transition between them is a deliberate act rather than a late discovery.** The basic setup lacking network isolation is a *feature of prototyping*, not a defect of the product. What is a defect is a team that builds for a term in one topology, assumes the move to the other is a configuration change, and finds out at a compliance review that it is a redesign.

That is why this platform sits in this session rather than in a deployment module. **Your register says the production topology is frozen. It is — and the prototyping topology is not**, and the gap between them is the largest available change most learners have and the last one they think of. You will not get a private-networking exception this term. You can stand something up in the prototyping topology, run the evidence the exception argument needs, and hand somebody a result instead of a request.

**The limits are severe and must travel with the claim.** This is first-party documentation with a selection-guidance purpose; its authority is definitional. **It offers no adoption data, no failure-rate data, no cost comparison and no evidence that its recommended mappings produce better outcomes.** It compares one vendor's options against each other and not against alternatives. It was read through search-returned excerpts rather than full page fetches, several comparison tables were truncated in the excerpts read, and the material is **highly perishable** — product names and capability boundaries are actively changing, and a limitation recorded today may be closed tomorrow. **Teach the distinction between the two topologies. Do not teach either topology's current feature list as permanent.**

### What the production topology actually asks for, and why that is a checklist rather than a mood

The posture-management material supplies the concrete content of "production topology", and it is short enough to memorise. Its four infrastructure-as-code checks for a hosted model endpoint are: **use private endpoints; restrict endpoints; use managed identity; use identity-based authentication** ([research/06-microsoft-platform/purview-dspm-for-ai-and-defender-ai-spm-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/purview-dspm-for-ai-and-defender-ai-spm-docs.md)). **Three of the four are about eliminating keys and network exposure**, which is the same pair of ideas the gateway material is built on.

The same source names the reason a compliance function will not simply take your word for the individual pieces. **Attack path analysis** exists because of **"toxic combinations"** — individually acceptable configurations that compose into a breach — with grounding and fine-tuning named as the two moments a model touches proprietary data and therefore the two moments it can leak by construction. **A constraint that looks arbitrary at the level of a single setting is frequently rational at the level of a composition**, and this is the single most useful thing you can put into an unfreezing argument: evidence about the composition, not about the setting.

**Its limits.** One consulted page is explicitly the classic version and states it has been superseded; the companion material was read through search excerpts; agent-level capability moves to a separate licence on a stated date; and **no detection rate, false-positive rate or coverage measurement of any kind is published.** None is asserted here.

### The smallest thing you can change is usually a boundary, and boundaries are cheap

The gateway material is where most learners find their available change, because a gateway is a component you can put in front of something without changing the something.

Its mechanisms are the standard production-topology answers in policy form. **Managed identities** authenticate to services **"so you don't need API keys for authentication"** — the elimination of a long-lived secret rather than better storage of one. A **token-limit policy** enforces per-consumer limits keyed on an arbitrary counter key, with prompt tokens precalculable at the edge so that an over-limit request never reaches the backend. And **dimensioned token metrics** make consumption attributable per interface and per user rather than merely visible in total ([research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md)). The page also carries a distributed-systems lesson dressed as a caveat: scaling the gateway does not scale the backends behind it, which must be scaled and co-located separately.

Why this belongs in a session about frozen constraints: **most of what a compliance function is actually worried about is attribution and blast radius**, and both are addressable at a boundary you own. An available change that produces per-consumer attribution where there was none is evidence about the risk the constraint guards against — which is the only kind of available change this session accepts. **First-party, definitional, no benchmarks and no cost measurements; capability varies by service tier, so confirm what yours supports before promising anything.**

### The fallback is a production system and must be designed as one

The production-systems literature supplies the discipline that stops a fallback becoming a protest. Its **optimising metric and gating metric** pair is the transferable form: **declare the constraint before you start adding capability, and stop at the constraint rather than at the point of diminishing returns** ([research/02-technical-foundations/crowe-machine-learning-production-systems.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/crowe-machine-learning-production-systems.md)). A fallback with a declared gating metric is a design. A fallback that is simply the good design with pieces removed is a grievance with a diagram.

The same source's rule for the boundary between experiment and product is worth carrying into your available change: **ship pipelines, not models, once a thing has users — and do not build a pipeline for an experiment that has none.** Your available change has no users. Build it accordingly, and say so, because an available change that acquires users without sanction is the shadow build this session warns about. **That book is a practitioner survey with no comparative evidence for any recommended practice, a non-neutral toolchain, and figures that are properties of particular systems; none is reproduced here.**

### Arguing from a standard rather than from a preference

A constraint is a position, and positions are held for reasons that are usually good from where the holder stands. Arguing from your own preference produces a contest of wills. **Arguing from a standard independent of both parties gives the holder something they can tell the people they must answer to**, which is the mechanism the negotiation literature identifies ([research/05-fde-craft/fisher-ury-getting-to-yes.md](/research/05-fde-craft/fisher-ury-getting-to-yes.md)). The same source's caution applies with particular force here: a criterion is not independent merely because it is numerical, and the parties must examine who selected it, what it omits, and whether it applies to this workload.

**Its limits.** A practitioner framework; its cases illustrate mechanism without isolating causes; **it reports no controlled comparison of anything**, and it licenses no claim about how often such an argument succeeds. **None is made.**

## The workflow

**Stand part of the unavailable design up in the topology you are allowed to use, and produce the evidence the exception argument needs.**

You will use Microsoft Foundry's setup options with a gateway in front. **Steps 1, 2, 6 and 7 need no subscription.** If you can provision nothing at all, do those four, and treat the provisioning refusal itself as the constraint whose authority you name — which is the session's subject arriving in person.

**Step 1 — write the topology difference down before you touch a portal.** Two columns: what the prototyping setup gives you and what it does not; what the production setup adds and what it costs to operate. Mark which of the four baseline checks — private endpoints, restricted endpoints, managed identity, identity-based authentication — each topology satisfies. **The row where they differ is your foreclosing constraint, stated as a property rather than as a mood.**

**Step 2 — declare the gating metric of the fallback before you build anything.** One sentence: *this design must clear X, and capability added beyond X is not the point.* Written now, it prevents the fallback becoming a sulk. Written later, it is a justification.

**Step 3 — provision the prototyping topology and record explicitly that it lacks network isolation.** Put that sentence in your own notes, in your own words, next to the date. **You are deliberately building in a topology that would not pass the review, and the value of the exercise depends on your never forgetting it.**

**Step 4 — build the smallest thing that produces evidence about the frozen constraint**, then put a gateway in front of it so the risk becomes observable. Not the whole unavailable design — the one component whose behaviour the constraint's holder is actually worried about.

```xml
<!-- Illustrative. Policy names and attributes change; verify against current documentation. -->
<inbound>
  <!-- The constraint's stated worry: an unbounded consumer, a long-lived key, no attribution. -->
  <!-- The available change: per-consumer accounting at the edge, and no key anywhere.        -->
  <authentication-managed-identity resource='https://your-endpoint.example' />

  <llm-token-limit counter-key='@(context.Subscription.Id)'
                   tokens-per-minute='SET_FROM_YOUR_OWN_SIZING'
                   estimate-prompt-tokens='true'
                   remaining-tokens-variable-name='remainingTokens' />

  <llm-emit-token-metric namespace='unfreeze-evidence'>
    <dimension name='Component' value='@(context.Api.Id)' />
    <dimension name='Consumer'  value='@(context.Subscription.Id)' />
    <dimension name='Topology'  value='prototyping-no-network-isolation' />
  </llm-emit-token-metric>
</inbound>
```

**The `Topology` dimension is the honest part of that fragment.** Every number you produce this term is labelled with the fact that it came from a topology the review would reject, so nobody — including you — can later present it as a production result.

**Step 5 — run it and collect the evidence, per consumer, over a fixed period.** You are answering one question and only one: **is the risk the constraint guards against larger or smaller than the holder assumes, and by what observation?** Collect nothing else. An available change that produces a general demonstration produces nothing usable.

**Step 6 — write the unfreezing argument from the other side of the table.** Open with what the holder is accountable for, in their words. Name the standard you are arguing from — an existing policy, a precedent inside their own organisation, a published requirement, an obligation they already carry — and only then state what you want. **Include what your evidence does not cover**, because the composition argument is the one they will make and you should make it first.

**Step 7 — attack your own argument.** Two prompts. First: *argue that this constraint should stay exactly where it is, using my own evidence as its support.* Second: *name the toxic combination my change creates — what does this compose with that neither of us has looked at?* Record what you kept and what changed. **The second prompt is the one that finds things**, because a composition failure is invisible from inside any single component.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That the topology you are allowed to use and the topology you must eventually ship into are two different systems, and that discovering this at the end was a choice somebody made at the beginning. You will provision one, read the sentence saying it lacks network isolation, and understand in ten minutes something that usually takes a failed compliance review to learn.

## What good looks like

- **The unavailable design is buildable.** Somebody could pick it up. It has a shape, a boundary, and a reason. A paragraph of grievance is not a design.
- **The foreclosing constraint is named singly.** Not a fog of circumstances — one constraint, one holder, one thing that would have to change. If you cannot narrow it to one, you cannot make a case, and the fog is doing the work of an excuse.
- **The available change is genuinely available.** You could start it this week and you would not have to ask. If you would have to ask, it is not the available change and you have not found it yet.
- **The available change was chosen for its evidential value.** It demonstrates something about the frozen constraint — that the risk it guards against is smaller than assumed, that the alternative works, that the cost is bearable. **Choosing the easiest available change instead of the most informative one is the commonest way to satisfy this session and learn nothing.**
- **The unfreezing argument is written in the holder's interest, not yours.** What they are accountable for, what they would have to explain to somebody who was not in the room, and what would make that explanation easy.
- **The fallback design is not sulking.** It is designed to be good at what it is, with its compromises acknowledged rather than resented, and with a declared gating metric so that what it does *not* do is a decision rather than a shortfall. **A design produced under protest is a design nobody will maintain.**
- **Every result you produce is labelled with the topology that produced it.** A number from the prototyping setup, presented without that label, is a claim you cannot defend and a habit you cannot afford.

## How it goes wrong

- **Learned helplessness** — everything is frozen, therefore nothing is attempted, and the eventual disappointment is attributed to the constraints. It is convenient, unfalsifiable, and usually partly true. *Warning sign:* your answer to item 3 was "nothing", and you did not spend long on it.
- **The shadow build** — you build the unavailable design anyway, in a corner, without sanction. It works, people start depending on it, and it is now an unowned dependency inside somebody else's estate. *Warning sign:* you are pleased that nobody has noticed.
- **The protest design** — the fallback is deliberately unimpressive so that everyone can see what the constraint cost. *Warning sign:* you would not put your name on it.
- **The constraint fog** — item 2 has no answer because it is *the whole situation*. Nothing can be argued against a situation. *Warning sign:* your unfreezing argument has more than one addressee.
- **The demonstration nobody asked for** — the available change produces evidence about a constraint nobody was ever going to reconsider. *Warning sign:* you cannot name who would read the result.
- **The unfreezing argument written as a complaint** — it is correct, it is well reasoned, and it makes the recipient look negligent. It will not be read twice. *Warning sign:* it would embarrass its addressee if forwarded.
- **The prototype presented as production** — evidence gathered in a topology with no network isolation is offered as though the topology were irrelevant to it. *Warning sign:* none of your charts says where the numbers came from.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed**, and no specification item at [S030](/wiki/seminars/S030.md) marks either artefact this session produces. That omission is deliberate and [WP-028](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-028.md) §7 records it as a cost rather than defending it.

**1 · Sort six candidate changes into available and not.** For each, answer one question: would you have to ask anybody?

- *Add per-consumer token accounting at a gateway you already operate.* · *Move the workload to the private-networking topology.* · *Write a test that fails when an adverse outcome reaches an endpoint without an identity.* · *Ask the platform team for a new subscription.* · *Replace the shared key with a managed identity in your own component.* · *Publish the design you cannot build to the customer's wiki.*

> **Model answers.** Available: the accounting, the test, and the managed identity within your own component — each is inside something you already own. Not available: the topology move and the new subscription, both of which need sanction. **The last one is the trap.** Publishing the unavailable design looks free and is not: it is a communication with an audience, and doing it without agreeing who reads it is the escalation you were trying to avoid, arriving unannounced. **If your answer to any of these did not name whose permission is or is not required, you sorted by effort rather than by authority.**

**2 · Read the topology statement and write what it forecloses.** Find the setup-options documentation for the platform you are using. Quote the sentence that states what the fast option does not provide, record the date you read it, and write the one requirement in your engagement that the sentence forecloses.

> **What you should find.** A statement that the rapid-prototyping setup lacks network isolation, alongside a controlled setup offering fine-grained control over data, security and networking. **The correct conclusion is not that you should have chosen the controlled setup.** It is that the two are different systems and that moving between them is work somebody has to schedule. **If your documentation no longer contains such a sentence, that is a finding about the documentation** — record the absence and the date rather than assuming the property went away.

**3 · Go back to your own item 3, then break it.** Find what you wrote for opening item 3 — the smallest thing you can change without permission. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then choose again.

> **Criterion.** An available change passes on four counts: you could start it this week; you would not have to ask; it produces an observation about the *foreclosing* constraint rather than a general improvement; and you can name the person who would read the result. "Tidy up the logging" fails on the third and fourth. "Emit per-consumer token metrics so that the sponsor's stated worry about one team's spend becomes a number they can look at" passes on all four.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Find the toxic combination in your own available change.** List every other component your change now touches, and name one pair whose individual configurations are acceptable and whose combination is not.

> **What to notice.** You will find it at an identity or a network edge, and it will be a pair neither owner has ever looked at together — which is the reason the documentation names composition analysis as a separate capability rather than folding it into the individual checks. **Naming your own toxic combination before the constraint's holder does is the single strongest move available in an unfreezing argument**, because it demonstrates that you are reasoning at the level they are accountable at.

**5 · Rewrite one paragraph from the other side.** Take the strongest paragraph of your unfreezing argument and rewrite it so that it opens with what the holder is accountable for, cites a standard neither of you owns, and would not embarrass them if forwarded to their own manager.

> **Scoring criterion.** The rewrite passes if the addressee could forward it unchanged and be helped rather than exposed by it. **A correct argument that makes its recipient look negligent will not be read twice**, and being right is not a defence against that.

**6 · Name the contradiction.** Closed book: the practice material this session's rule comes from contradicts that rule elsewhere in itself. State both positions, and say which one you would rather be judged by.

> **Answer.** The rule says constraint is never an excuse for doing nothing. Elsewhere the same material explains disappointing delivery by pointing at the customer's organisational constraints, alongside a readiness rubric composed almost entirely of customer dispositions. **A practice cannot hold both**, and this programme declines to resolve it for you because asserting a resolution the source does not contain is the exact failure this repository teaches you to detect. **The contradiction is handed over intact.**

## What this does not establish

**The rule this session is built on is an assertion, and it has no evidence behind it whatsoever.** The practice material behind this module states that even in an almost entirely frozen space something can be changed, and that constraint is not an excuse for doing nothing. **No instance of a frozen space being opened is recorded anywhere in that material. No engagement is described in which the rule was applied. Nothing in it is cited, sourced or measured.**

There is worse, and it is taught rather than hidden. **The same body of material, elsewhere, explains disappointing delivery outcomes by pointing at the customer's organisational constraints** — the very constraints this session tells you to work inside instead of blaming. A practice whose method says constraint is not an excuse, and whose prepared answers use constraint as the explanation, has not read itself. **That contradiction is not resolved in the source and it is not resolved here.** Hold both and notice which one is being used when.

The negotiation material behind the unfreezing argument is case-based, establishes no effect by its authors' own statement, and licenses no claim about how often such an argument succeeds. **None is made.**

**The platform material is first-party throughout and definitional rather than empirical.** The setup-options guidance offers no adoption data, no failure-rate data, no cost comparison and no evidence that its recommended mappings produce better outcomes; it was read through search excerpts rather than full page fetches, and several of its comparison tables were truncated in the excerpts read. The gateway documentation argues for a gateway and does not discuss the latency, cost or operational burden the gateway itself adds. The posture-management material publishes **no detection rate, no false-positive rate and no coverage measurement of any kind**, one of its consulted pages is explicitly superseded, and agent-level capability moves to a different licence on a stated date. **All of it is highly perishable, and a limitation recorded today may be closed tomorrow.**

**The production-systems material is a survey by its own description**, offers no comparative evidence for any recommended practice, and its toolchain is not neutral. Every figure in it is a property of a particular system and **none is reproduced here.**

**Nothing in this session shows that unfreezing arguments succeed, or that learners who write them fare better than learners who do not.** The design's claim is narrower and is still untested: that an engineer who has written the unavailable design has produced a record that can be checked later, and one who has not has produced an explanation that cannot.
