# S083 · Momentum, and Why It Decays

**Module:** [M17](/wiki/modules/M17.md) · **Quarter:** [Q9](/wiki/quarters/Q9.md) · **Complexity class:** 6
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** Provisioned throughput commitments meeting a workload that changed shape after the commitment was made
**Whitepaper:** [WP-083](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-083.md)
**Retrieves from:** S009, S064, S078, S082
**Entry state:** this-programme-only

## The situation

A proposal has been drafted on your side: a recurring review cadence with the operator, chargeable, described as protecting the investment.

It is probably the right diagnosis. The person who wrote it has a utilisation target that it satisfies. **Both of those sentences are true and neither disposes of the other.**

There is a second thing decaying in the same estate, and nobody has attached a proposal to it. Six months ago the workload was sized and a capacity commitment was made against it. The traffic has since changed shape — different prompts, different response lengths, a caching layer somebody added, a second application sharing the endpoint. **The commitment did not change, because a commitment is a document and the workload is a population of behaviours.**

Answer these in writing before the session opens.

1. What, specifically, do you expect to decay first — and what would you see when it did?
2. Write the version of your remedy that earns your organisation nothing. What is in it?
3. What does the chargeable version add that the free version cannot deliver, stated as a capability rather than as reassurance?
4. Who inside the customer would have to own the free version, and do they have the authority?
5. If your proposal is declined, what happens? Name the consequence, and say who bears it.

**Item 2 is the session's whole method.** Any diagnosis can be dressed as a need for your continued presence. The test of whether you believe the diagnosis is whether you will write the remedy that does not involve you.

## What you must produce

**A reinforcement design in two parts, a decay prediction with a named first sign, and a capacity re-derivation.**

1. **The unpaid version.** What the customer would do, with the people and authority they already have, to keep the change in place. Named owner, named cadence, named artefact, named trigger for noticing that it has stopped.
2. **The conflict-of-interest statement.** What your organisation gains from the paid version, in plain words, written by you, in the document. **Not a disclosure paragraph at the end — a section the reader will reach.**
3. **The decay prediction.** What decays first, what the first visible sign is, and roughly when relative to a fixed external event that does not move for anybody's convenience.
4. **The capacity re-derivation.** The traffic model the commitment was sized on, the traffic as it now is, and the input that moved most.
5. **The degradation design.** What happens at the moment the commitment stops covering the workload — stated as a configured behaviour rather than as an intention.
6. **A single recommendation**, and it may be either version.

## The material

### Momentum decays because the conditions that produced it were specific, and the world moved

That is the durable claim, and stating it in that form removes the mysticism.

Momentum is not a substance that drains. It is a set of conditions holding simultaneously: somebody senior kept asking, the alternative was still painful, the people who built it were reachable, the workload still looked like the workload the thing was designed for. **Each of those has its own half-life, and they are not correlated.** The engagement ends and the reachable people leave. A reorganisation moves the person who kept asking. A caching layer changes what the traffic costs. **Nothing decayed. Four independent things changed, and the conjunction that produced the momentum stopped holding.**

The consequence for a reinforcement design is direct and unwelcome: **a design that reinforces "the change" in general reinforces nothing**, because there is no such object. A design that names one condition, says who holds it, and says what its failure looks like is a design that can be executed by somebody who was not there.

### The concrete instance: a capacity commitment meeting a workload that changed shape

The clearest available worked example is not organisational at all, and that is why it is used here — it removes the temptation to treat decay as a fact about people.

Provisioned throughput on the model platform holds "a fixed amount of processing capacity exclusively for your deployment's use, whether or not requests are being made" ([research/06-microsoft-platform/provisioned-throughput-for-foundry-models.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/provisioned-throughput-for-foundry-models.md)). Everything else follows from that clause: capacity held whether or not it is used must be paid for whether or not it is used, which is why the billing unit changes from tokens to time, and why the deployment becomes a capacity-planning decision rather than a configuration setting.

**Sizing rests on three inputs and every one of them is a fact about last quarter's traffic.** *Request shape* — expected requests per minute, average prompt size, average response size. *Output-to-input ratio* — because "output tokens require more processing capacity than input tokens". *Cache rate* — the fraction of input tokens served from prompt cache, where **"cached tokens don't consume PTU capacity, so a higher cache rate reduces the PTUs required."** Those combine into a single normalised figure, divided by a per-model divisor, to give the number of units.

Read that as a decay mechanism rather than as arithmetic. **A team that adds a caching layer has changed the third input. A team that switches the assistant from summarising to drafting has changed the second. A team that onboards a second application has changed the first.** None of them told the person who made the commitment, because none of them knew the commitment existed. The commitment is now sized for a workload that no longer exists, in either direction — over-provisioned and expensive, or under-provisioned and throttling — and **nothing in the platform emits an event when a sizing assumption stops being true.**

The most transferable idea on that page is the distinction that makes the failure sharp. **Quota is "a policy limit enforced by Azure, and it has no associated cost"** — permission. **Capacity is "the actual amount of PTUs per model version that's available to be deployed"** — availability. The documentation states the consequence in a call-out: **"Having PTU quota doesn't guarantee that capacity is available. If capacity in the region is insufficient for the requested PTU count, the deployment fails."** And availability "changes throughout the day based on customer demand across all regions and models", while releasing capacity offers **"no guarantee the same capacity is available"** later.

**So the escape hatch is a one-way door that looks reversible**, which is the exact shape of most organisational momentum decisions and is worth carrying out of the platform entirely.

The billing guidance closes with the sentence this session takes as its motto, and it is a procurement lesson rather than a technical one: **"Reservations don't guarantee capacity. First create deployments to confirm that capacity is available, then purchase the reservation to lock in the discounted rate."** Deploy, verify, then commit. **The intuitive sequence — commit, then deploy — is exactly wrong**, and it is exactly the sequence a reinforcement proposal follows when it is signed before anybody has checked that the customer can execute it.

**Its limits.** First-party product documentation, definitional rather than empirical: it states how the platform behaves and makes no performance claim that could be independently checked. It is single-vendor and does not situate its capacity model against alternatives. Concrete per-model ratios, minimum deployment sizes and prices live on companion pages that were not fetched and are not reproduced here. **Product names, defaults, ratios and portal paths change; nothing in it may be assessed as fact.** And the choice architecture is the vendor's, which is worth remembering in a session about proposals with an interest attached.

### Graceful degradation is a configured behaviour, not an intention

The same platform supplies the model for what a reinforcement design should contain at the moment its assumption fails.

**Spillover** redirects requests to a standard deployment when a provisioned deployment is saturated and returns a non-success response, "helping reduce disruptions during traffic bursts". Architecturally this is graceful degradation with reserved capacity for the predictable load and shared capacity for the tail — **and the trigger is an error response rather than a prediction.** The system does not forecast that it is about to be overwhelmed; it discovers it, and has already decided what to do.

The gateway layer completes it. A load balancer over configured backends supports priority-based routing, and the documentation names the exact case: define priorities "to ensure optimal utilization of specific Microsoft Foundry endpoints, particularly those purchased as PTU instances" ([research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md)). You paid for dedicated capacity whether or not you use it, so route to it first and overflow elsewhere. The circuit breaker alongside it "features dynamic trip duration, applying values from the `Retry-After` header provided by the backend" — **letting the failing service state its own recovery time rather than being guessed at.**

**Transfer that shape to the organisational design and the session's standard writes itself.** A reinforcement design should say what happens when the cadence is missed, triggered by an observable state rather than by somebody noticing, with the fallback stated in advance and the recovery interval set by the party that knows it. **A plan whose failure mode is "somebody will notice" has no failure mode.**

**Limits:** policy names, tier availability and preview status change frequently; individual policy reference pages were not fetched; and the gateway page does not discuss the privacy consequences of the logging it recommends.

### Declaring victory is itself the mechanism of regression

The change literature is unusually direct about the moment this session sits in, and its claim is about *celebration* rather than about neglect.

**"Whenever you let up before the job is done, critical momentum can be lost and regression may follow"**, and "Three years of work can come undone with remarkable speed" ([research/05-fde-craft/kotter-leading-change.md](/research/05-fde-craft/kotter-leading-change.md)). The mechanism named is not entropy but people: irrational and political resistance "never fully dissipates", and instead of changing or leaving, opponents "will often sit there waiting for an opportunity to make a comeback." The sharpest formulation is the one to carry: **"short-term wins are essential to keep momentum going, but the celebration of those wins can be lethal if urgency is lost."**

The same source describes a coalition of the sincere and the cynical producing the failure together — "a combination of idealistic change initiators and self-serving change resisters often creates this problem" — which is worth holding beside your own closing ceremony. **The people most pleased by a declared success and the people most relieved by it are not the same people, and both applaud.**

**Its limits are stated by the author himself.** The book contains no footnotes and cites no published source except his own writing; every extended case is pseudonymous or unnamed, so **no case in it can be traced or checked by any reader**; and its best-known figures are derived by inspecting his own diagrams. **Direction and mechanism only, and no figure from it appears here.**

### Reinforcement attached to no accomplishment does damage rather than nothing

The other change source contributes the constraint that makes most reinforcement designs fail on their own terms.

Reinforcement must be **meaningful to the recipient**, **tied to a real accomplishment** — "If no accomplishment has been made, any attempt at rewards or recognition can backfire" — **free of countervailing negative consequences** such as peer pressure, and **backed by accountability systems**, which the source calls one of the strongest forms of reinforcement ([research/05-fde-craft/hiatt-adkar.md](/research/05-fde-craft/hiatt-adkar.md)).

Its worked failure is the one to remember, because it is an engineering failure rather than a motivational one. A bank deployed a quality programme well and then disbanded the implementation team; within a year courses had been cancelled and the programme was dead. **The diagnosis offered is the useful part: "The first mistake the implementation team made was to assume that managing change is essentially completing activities in a project plan."** A plan completed is not a change sustained, and the completion is precisely what triggers the disbanding.

**The warrant is thin.** A practitioner model published by the consultancy that owns its trademark and sells the training in it; all quantitative claims come from that firm's own surveys; **no independent evaluation of the model is cited anywhere**; its ordering claim is argued from the meanings of its own terms. **Direction and mechanism only.** And **one correction travels with it**: the widely repeated claim that effort past the blocking element is wasted is not in the book, and this repository withdrew it on reading. Use the conditional form.

### The corpus asserts the decay once, and sells the remedy

The internal practice material behind this programme states, in a single line of practitioner judgement, that **momentum decays without a deliberate reinforcing rhythm after the initial engagement** ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md)). No case is attached. No observation. No measurement. And the collection reports **no post-deployment record for anything it describes** — whether any delivered system survived, was maintained, or was still running months later is not reported once.

**The organisation asserting that momentum decays also sells the cadence that prevents it.** That is not an accusation; it is the ordinary structure of a delivery business, and it is why this session's method exists. **A diagnosis you are paid to make is not thereby wrong. It is a diagnosis whose author cannot check it**, and the only available correction is to write the version that earns nothing and see whether you still believe it.

**Its limits:** access-controlled and unverifiable by any public reader; paraphrased and never quoted; it contradicts itself in at least a dozen places; and its stated prerequisites are waived in the same breath as they are stated.

## The workflow

**Re-derive one capacity commitment against the workload as it now is, and configure what happens when it stops covering it.**

You will need read access to a Foundry resource with a provisioned deployment, or a sandbox in which you can create one, and read access to the gateway metrics in front of it. Where you have neither, run the procedure against the published sizing method with recorded assumptions and say that you did.

**Step 1 — recover the original traffic model, and date it.** Find what the commitment was sized on: requests per minute, average prompt size, average response size, assumed output-to-input ratio, assumed cache rate. **If nobody can produce it, that is the finding** and it goes at the top of the document. A commitment whose sizing assumptions were never written down cannot be re-derived by anybody, ever, and will be renewed on sentiment.

**Step 2 — measure the workload as it now is.** From the gateway's token metrics, take the same five quantities over a recent window. Use the group dimension from [S082](/wiki/seminars/S082.md) so that you can see whether a *new* consumer arrived rather than an existing one growing. **Those two look identical in a total and require different responses.**

**Step 3 — re-derive the requirement and name the input that moved most.** Recompute the normalised throughput figure with today's inputs and divide by the per-model figure the documentation publishes for the model you are actually running. **Then do the sensitivity: move each input across its plausible range and see which one the answer follows.** In most workloads it is the cache rate or the output ratio, and both are changed by engineering decisions nobody reports to the person holding the commitment.

**Step 4 — separate permission from availability.** Check your quota, then check regional capacity for the model version you would need. **Quota is a policy limit and costs nothing. Capacity is what is deployable right now and moves through the day.** Record both, separately, with the time you looked. This is the step that catches the plan that would have worked in a spreadsheet.

**Step 5 — write the drift register.** One file, beside the commitment.

```yaml
# commitment-drift.yaml  — SHAPE, NOT SCHEMA.
# Sizing inputs, unit names, model divisors and portal paths are facts about
# ONE date and ONE model version. Re-derive them; never copy them forward.

commitment:
  made_on: "<YYYY-MM-DD>"
  sized_against:                  # the traffic model as it was ASSUMED to be
    requests_per_minute: "<n>"
    avg_prompt_tokens: "<n>"
    avg_response_tokens: "<n>"
    output_to_input_ratio: "<as published for the model, on the date checked>"
    assumed_cache_rate: "<fraction>"
  sizing_record_found: true       # if false, STOP. This is the finding.

workload_now:
  measured_over: "<window, with dates>"
  source: "gateway token metrics, dimensioned by receiving group"
  requests_per_minute: "<n>"
  avg_prompt_tokens: "<n>"
  avg_response_tokens: "<n>"
  observed_cache_rate: "<fraction>"
  new_consumers_since_commitment: ["<group>"]   # growth and arrival look the
                                                # same in a total and are not
                                                # the same problem

drift:
  input_that_moved_most: "<one of the five>"
  caused_by: "<the engineering decision that moved it, and who made it>"
  told_the_commitment_holder: false             # usually false, and not a fault

permission_vs_availability:       # these are DIFFERENT things
  quota_units: "<policy limit — costs nothing, guarantees nothing>"
  capacity_checked_at: "<timestamp — availability moves through the day>"
  capacity_sufficient: "<yes|no>"
  note: "Releasing capacity is a one-way door that looks reversible."

degradation:                      # what happens when the commitment stops covering
  trigger: "<the error response that fires it — an OBSERVED state, not a forecast>"
  fallback: "spillover to a standard deployment in the same resource"
  routing: "priority-first to reserved capacity, overflow elsewhere"
  recovery_interval: "taken from the backend's own Retry-After, not guessed"

decay_prediction:
  what_decays_first: "<one named thing>"
  first_visible_sign: "<what a person would SEE, not what they would feel>"
  relative_to: "<a fixed external event that does not move for our convenience>"

conflict_of_interest:
  what_my_organisation_gains_from_the_paid_version: "<plain words, written by me>"
  would_i_still_recommend_it_unpaid: "<yes|no|I do not know>"
```

**Step 6 — configure the degradation rather than describing it.** Set spillover on the provisioned deployment and priority routing at the gateway so that reserved capacity is used first and overflow is served rather than refused. **Then break it on purpose**: drive the deployment past its limit and watch the fallback take the traffic. A degradation design that has never been triggered is a paragraph.

**Step 7 — write part one before part two, and finish it.** The unpaid version, complete, with a named owner who is employed by the customer. **The order is the exercise.** Written in the other order, the unpaid version becomes an argument for the paid one and you will not be able to tell that it has.

**Step 8 — size the decline.** You did this at [S009](/wiki/seminars/S009.md) for doing nothing at the start of an engagement; this is the same act at the other end. What does the operator lose if nothing is reinforced, who notices first, and when — relative to an external event that does not move for anybody's convenience.

**Step 9 — use the agent to attack your own proposal, not to improve it.** One prompt: *argue that every element of my paid version is achievable by the customer alone, and that my conflict-of-interest statement understates what my organisation gains — then find the one element that genuinely is not achievable without us.* Read it for the last clause. **That element, and only that element, is what justifies a charge.** Record what you kept and what you rejected, and record that the instrument shares a model family with the tooling under discussion and is therefore not independent of it.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** The drift, as a number you derived rather than a worry you have.

Nobody argues productively about whether momentum decays. Everybody can see that the cache rate assumed six months ago and the cache rate measured this week are different, that the difference was caused by a named engineering decision, and that **nobody told the person holding the commitment because there is no mechanism by which they would have.** The organisational version of the same failure is invisible; the capacity version is arithmetic. **A learner who has once watched a sizing assumption go stale without anybody being at fault stops looking for someone to blame in the organisational case.**

## What good looks like

- The decay is predicted specifically. Not *engagement will fall away* but *this person will stop reading this log, and the first visible sign will be that*.
- **The unpaid version is a real design.** It has an owner who exists, a cadence somebody could keep, and an artefact that is produced whether or not anyone is watching.
- The chargeable version is justified by a capability the customer demonstrably lacks — established at [S079](/wiki/seminars/S079.md) by observation, not asserted here.
- **The capacity re-derivation names the input that moved and the decision that moved it**, and says whether anybody told the person holding the commitment.
- Permission and availability are recorded separately, with the time each was checked. **A quota figure presented as capacity is the whole error in one cell.**
- The degradation is configured and has been triggered at least once. **A fallback that has never taken traffic is a paragraph.**
- Reinforcement is attached to something that actually happened. **A recognition for an accomplishment nobody made does damage rather than nothing.**
- The design says what happens when the reinforcement conflicts with something else the organisation rewards, because the countervailing pressure is the ordinary case and it wins by default.
- The recommendation is one sentence and it commits.

## How it goes wrong

- **The diagnosis that is also the sale** — the analysis and the proposal were written in one pass and the conclusion was available before the analysis. *Warning sign:* your unpaid version is a paragraph and your paid version is a schedule.
- **The plan mistaken for the change** — the engagement completed its activities, so the change is treated as done. *Warning sign:* your evidence that it worked is a list of things you did.
- **Victory declared** — the close was celebrated, the matter is considered settled, and the people who never agreed with it have been waiting for exactly this. *Warning sign:* nobody in the customer has raised an objection for some time.
- **Commit before verify** — a commitment was made against a plan rather than against a confirmed deployment. *Warning sign:* your sequence was decide, sign, then check.
- **Quota mistaken for capacity** — the allocation exists and the thing cannot be deployed. *Warning sign:* your evidence that you have the capacity came from a limits page rather than from a deployment.
- **The stale sizing model** — the workload changed shape and the commitment did not, because no mechanism connects them. *Warning sign:* nobody can produce the traffic model the commitment was sized against.
- **Reinforcement with no accomplishment** — recognition, reporting or a review ceremony attached to nothing that was achieved. *Warning sign:* you could run your cadence unchanged in a month where the system did nothing.
- **Degradation described rather than configured** — the design says what should happen and nothing implements it. *Warning sign:* the trigger in your plan is a person noticing.
- **The remedy that needs your authority** — the design only works if somebody from your organisation is in the room. *Warning sign:* the owner named in part one is not employed by the customer.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The reinforcement design and the drift register are carried to [S084](/wiki/seminars/S084.md) and to [S085](/wiki/seminars/S085.md), where the claim about what improved either survives the drift or does not.

**1 · Sort six statements into permission, availability and commitment.** For each, say what it licenses.

- *We have an allocation for this many units in this region.* · *We deployed it this morning and it succeeded.* · *We bought a reservation last quarter.* · *The model version we need had insufficient regional capacity at the time we checked.* · *We released the deployment over the weekend to save money.* · *Our commitment covers the workload.*

> **Model answers.** The first is **permission** — a policy limit that costs nothing and guarantees nothing. The second and fourth are **availability**, and both are time-stamped facts that may already be false. The third is a **commitment** — a financial discount on a meter, which the documentation states explicitly does not guarantee capacity. The fifth is a **one-way door dressed as reversible**, because there is no guarantee the same capacity is available on return. The sixth licenses nothing at all until the workload it was sized against is produced. **The general rule: permission is what you are allowed to ask for, availability is what exists right now, and a commitment is a price. None of the three implies either of the others**, and almost every capacity failure in this industry is two people using words from different columns.

**2 · Re-derive one commitment and run the sensitivity.** Using the workflow, recompute the requirement with today's inputs, then move each input across its plausible range and record which one the answer follows.

> **What you should find.** That the answer follows an input somebody changed for an unrelated reason — a caching layer, a prompt template, a new consumer — and that **the change was correct engineering.** Nobody was careless. There is simply no mechanism by which a person who improves a cache hit rate learns that a capacity commitment was sized against the old one. **Write that sentence in the register**, because it is the finding, and because a register that reads as an accusation will be ignored by the people who could fix it.

**3 · Go back to your own item 2, then break it.** Find the unpaid remedy you wrote before the session. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then write the honest version.

> **Criterion.** An unpaid version passes if it names **an owner employed by the customer, a cadence they could keep without you, an artefact produced whether or not anyone is watching, and a trigger that is a state change rather than somebody remembering.** All four, or it fails. *"The customer maintains the review rhythm"* fails on all four. *"The operations lead reviews the drift register at each monthly change board; the register is regenerated by the pipeline; if it is absent from the pack, the board's own agenda check raises it"* passes.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Break the fallback on purpose.** Drive the deployment past its limit and watch the overflow take the traffic. Record what the caller saw.

> **What to notice.** That the user-visible behaviour *changed* — different latency, possibly a different model version, certainly a different capacity guarantee — and that nothing in the design as written said so. **Graceful degradation is not invisible degradation**, and a design that has not stated what the user experiences during overflow has deferred that decision to whoever is on call. Note also that the trigger is an error response rather than a forecast: the system discovers saturation and has already decided what to do, which is the property you are being asked to transfer to the organisational design.

**5 · Write the sentence that costs you the work.** Take your conflict-of-interest statement and reduce it to one sentence stating what your organisation gains, in plain words, with no mitigating clause.

> **Scoring criterion.** A pass names the commercial gain in the first clause. A fail begins with the customer's benefit and reaches the gain in a subordinate clause. **The grammar is the exercise.** Then answer the only question the room asks: *would you still recommend this if your organisation were not paid for it?* **The answer *yes* is available and defensible. The answer *I do not know* is the most honest thing said in most rooms where this comes up, and it is worth more than a confident one.**

**6 · Find the celebration.** Look at how your engagement closed — the demonstration, the summary, the thanks. Write one sentence naming what that event told the people who never agreed with the change.

> **What to notice.** That a closing ceremony is a signal with two audiences, and that the second one hears *it is over*. **The people most pleased by a declared success and the people most relieved by it are not the same people, and both applaud.** This exercise is uncomfortable in proportion to how well the engagement went, which is the correct direction.

## What this does not establish

**That momentum decays, or that reinforcement prevents it.** The practice corpus behind this programme asserts the decay once, as a line of practitioner judgement, with no case, no observation and no measurement attached — in a body of work that reports no post-deployment record for anything it describes.

The change-management sources are no better and their warrants differ. One is a practitioner model published by the firm that owns its trademark and sells training in it, supported by that firm's own survey work with no independent evaluation cited. The other states in its own preface that it cites no external source and reports what its author has seen and concluded, with every case anonymised past a reader's ability to check and its counts recalled rather than counted. **Both license direction and mechanism only. No rate, no proportion, no magnitude and no timescale may be taken from either, and this session asserts none.**

**One correction travels with the first of them.** The widely repeated claim that effort past the blocking element is wasted is not in the book, and this repository withdrew it on reading. The conditional form is the only form permitted.

**The capacity material is definitional, not empirical.** First-party product documentation states how the platform behaves and makes no performance claim that could be independently checked. It is single-vendor and does not situate its capacity model against alternatives. **Every per-model ratio, minimum deployment size and price lives on a companion page that was not fetched, and none is reproduced here.** Product names, defaults and portal paths change; nothing in it may be assessed as fact; and its choice architecture is the vendor's, which matters in a session about proposals with an interest attached.

**The analogy between capacity drift and organisational decay is this design's own and is doing real work, so it should be doubted.** A sizing assumption going stale is a mechanical fact with a re-derivable arithmetic. Momentum in an organisation is not that, and treating it as though it were may be exactly the false precision this programme warns about elsewhere. **The analogy is used because it makes an invisible failure visible, not because the two are the same kind of thing**, and a learner who begins computing organisational half-lives has taken it further than it goes.

**And the specific tension this session is about is not resolved anywhere.** No source behind this programme separates the observation that a change needs reinforcing from the commercial interest of the party observing it, and none reports an engagement in which the unpaid remedy was recommended. **This session's method is an argument, not a practice, and it is class 3 throughout.**
