# S044 · Review Under Delivery Pressure

**Module:** [M09](/wiki/modules/M09.md) · **Quarter:** [Q5](/wiki/quarters/Q5.md) · **Complexity class:** 3
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** GitHub Copilot code review alongside the published engineering playbook's review practice, with the playbook's four unsupported figures named and none reproduced
**Whitepaper:** [WP-044](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-044.md)
**Retrieves from:** S007, S042, S043
**Entry state:** either

## The situation

The open playbook's reviewer guidance is the best single page on that site and it contains one instruction that nobody in industry obeys under pressure: **read every line changed.** No sampling. It is stated flatly, alongside a two-pass structure — a first pass for shape and intent, a second for detail — and alongside the norm that makes review survivable between colleagues: what matters is a bug caught, not who made it, not who found it, not who fixed it.

The same document then declines, on two separate pages, to say when a change is too large to read. *It is not a matter of setting a code line limit, but rather using common sense.* And a third page in the same section makes the observation that makes the refusal expensive: **large changes get biased review** — the senior engineer's approved unread, the junior engineer's not reviewed at all.

Answer these before instruction.

1. On the last change you reviewed, what proportion of it did you actually read? Say the honest answer.
2. What do you do when the change is too large to read and the delivery date is not moving?
3. If your team's rate of production changes and its review capacity does not, what breaks first?
4. Who reviews the reviewer?
5. What would you refuse to have reviewed by a machine, and on what grounds?

**Item 3 is the session's subject and it has no answer in any source behind this programme.** The delivery model this stage teaches rests entirely on expert human validation, states that the tooling amplifies the operator rather than levelling the field, and requires a team member to be somebody you would have trusted on a purely human team. **Across approximately ninety internal documents it never asks what happens when the volume arriving at the reviewer changes.** Review capacity is the binding constraint on the whole model and it is not discussed once.

## What you must produce

Two artefacts, and the second is the one that is new.

**A review record** for one real change, under a stated time budget. What you read line by line, what you read for shape only, what you did not open at all, what you raised, what you let through, and what you would want to know if it failed in production.

**A capacity statement.** The rate at which your slice is producing reviewable change; the review effort that rate demands under the every-line rule; who supplies it; and what you did when the demand exceeded the supply. **If it never exceeded the supply, say so and say why** — that is a finding about your rate of production, not an absence of finding.

## The material

### Read every line changed, and the refusal to say when that stops being possible

The playbook's reviewer page is the richest single page on that site, and it is worth holding close to verbatim.

It prescribes a two-pass structure — a first pass for shape and intent, a second for detail — and it does not permit sampling: **"Read every line changed"**. It states the norm that makes review survivable between colleagues, **"What matters is a bug caught, not who made it, not who found it, not who fixed it"**, and it closes the commonest loophole outright: **"Tests should always be committed in the same PR as the code itself ('I'll add tests next' is not acceptable)."** The author page closes the matching loophole from the other side — **"If the requested change is within the scope of the task, 'I'll do it later' is not an acceptable reason!"** — and requires reviewers **from both organisations** on a joint engagement ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md), pages read 2026-07-30).

Then the same document declines, on two separate pages, to say when a change is too large to read. **"It's not a matter of setting a code line limit, but rather using common sense."** And a third page in the same section makes the observation that turns the refusal from a stylistic choice into an expensive one: **large changes get biased review** — the senior engineer's approved unread, the junior engineer's not reviewed at all.

Read those three together. The document requires exhaustive reading, refuses to say when exhaustive reading has become impossible, and records that the failure mode when it becomes impossible is stratified by author seniority rather than by risk. **A rule that cannot fail visibly will be broken silently, and the breakage will be distributed along whatever social gradient the team already has.** That is the session, and no source states it.

The same section is unusually good on the social conditions. The inclusion page names non-inclusive behaviours directly, treats impostor phenomenon head-on, and is the only place on the site that reasons about who is being reviewed rather than about what is being reviewed. Take it seriously: it is doing the work the rest of the section assumes has already been done.

### The four figures, and the register they are written in

This is the session's most useful twenty minutes, and it is aimed at the programme's own source.

The playbook's code-review evidence-and-measures page proposes branch-policy verification as evidence — **"Verification of the policies on the main branch in Azure DevOps… may be sufficient evidence that a project team is conducting code reviews"** — which is evidence that review was **required**, not that it was **done well**. It offers three measures and concedes they are gathered by hand, "e.g. in an Excel sheet". And then it closes by asserting that **"the cost of doing code reviews is actually negative!"** — with no measurement, no baseline, no comparison and no source anywhere on the page.

**A complete reading of the site found the same failure in four separate places, and the pattern is the finding rather than any one instance.** The four are: the code-review claim above; a collaboration page asserting that pairing and swarming produce "a higher sprint velocity", offered with no evidence either; a unit-testing rationale page asserting a cost multiple for defects found in production, attributed to a 2008 study **through a link that cannot be followed in any current browser**; and sustainability material carrying vendor efficiency figures in bands wide enough to be unfalsifiable, from a document that was already years old when it was read. **This repository records all four as specimens and reproduces none of their magnitudes anywhere.**

Now the part that transfers. The playbook's **tone is uniform and its warrant is not.** Its testing section carries the densest bibliography on the site — Fowler, the Google SRE book, the Pact specification, OWASP. Its observability section cites the W3C Trace Context recommendation and the OpenTelemetry compliance matrix. Its code-review section cites Google's engineering practices and a published peer-review study. And its non-functional-requirements capture guide carries dozens of specific numeric thresholds **with no citation of any kind**, in exactly the same flat declarative voice. Its design section outside the diagram pages contains a single quantitative claim, and it is a wide-banded vendor figure from a years-old document. One page's entire reference list is four social-media posts and a blog article.

**Nothing on the page tells you which kind of page you are holding.** That is the skill this session installs and it is the most transferable thing the source can teach: **a document that writes a reasoned practice and a repeated number in the same register is not lying, and it is not reliable either.** Every engineer this programme sends into industry will meet a document like this, usually internal, usually written by people they respect. Reading it — checking whether a page has content before relying on it, checking a footer date, noticing that a page cites nothing, noticing that two adjacent pages disagree — is a professional skill, and this source teaches it better than a constructed exercise could, precisely because it is the source this programme itself draws on.

**Hold the site's other limits alongside.** Page footer dates run across four years with nothing in the navigation to indicate which is which; roughly half the site is stub, pure link list, tool catalogue or version-pinned recipe; it contradicts itself in at least a dozen places and never notices. **Cite it by page and by the date that page was read, never as a whole.**

### Review under pressure is a voice problem before it is a process problem

Every mechanism above assumes somebody says something. Under delivery pressure, the thing that fails first is not the process. It is the sentence.

The construct that names this defines it as **"the belief that the work environment is safe for interpersonal risk taking"**, where the risks being avoided are appearing "ignorant, incompetent, or disruptive" ([research/04-professional-formation/edmondson-psychological-safety.md](/research/04-professional-formation/edmondson-psychological-safety.md)). Two of its ideas bear directly on a review queue.

The first is the **asymmetry of voice and silence**: voice benefits the organisation, later, with low certainty; silence benefits oneself, immediately, with high certainty. Hence the observation that **"no one was ever fired for silence"**. Under a date that is not moving, that asymmetry sharpens, because the delay voice imposes is immediate and visible while the defect it prevents is neither.

The second is the set of **implicit theories of voice** — taken-for-granted rules about when it is appropriate to speak upward: do not criticise something the boss may have helped create; do not speak unless you have solid data; do not say anything negative in a group. Read that list next to *large changes get biased review, the senior engineer's approved unread* and the two accounts describe the same event from different sides. **The reviewer who does not open the senior engineer's files is not lazy. They are running a risk calculation, and the calculation is correct about their own interests.**

The construct is also carefully bounded, and this session takes the bounds seriously. It is **not immunity from consequences**. It is orthogonal to performance standards rather than traded against them, giving the two-by-two whose dangerous cell is high standards with low safety. Sanction is inside the model rather than an exception to it: **"psychological safety is reinforced rather than harmed by fair, thoughtful responses to potentially dangerous, harmful, or sloppy behavior."** And it is not sufficient — "it's not the fuel that powers the car".

**The limits are substantial and this repository states them rather than borrowing the construct's popularity.** The book is a trade-press statement, not a study; its literature chapter is a narrative review with no search protocol, no inclusion criteria and no negative finding reported across a hundred-plus articles, in a literature where its author is the most-cited figure; its case studies are secondary throughout and selected after the outcomes were known; and **no trial of its leadership framework is reported anywhere in it.** For the state of the evidence, the aggregate synthesis is the source to use: it pools many independent samples, ranks antecedents against each other rather than listing them, and — the sceptic's test — examines whether the construct predicts anything **over and above** positive leader relations and work engagement ([research/04-professional-formation/frazier-psychological-safety-meta-analysis.md](/research/04-professional-formation/frazier-psychological-safety-meta-analysis.md)). **That note was written from the official abstract because the publisher version is closed; the abstract reports no numerical effect sizes, so no correlation for any antecedent or outcome may be quoted from it.** The pooled literature is also overwhelmingly cross-sectional and self-report, so causal direction is not established.

### The binding constraint nobody in the sources discusses

Here is the claim the session is built on, and it is this design's rather than any source's.

The delivery model this stage teaches is explicit that it is **not democratisation**: the tooling is claimed to amplify the operator, so that an experienced engineer gets usable assets out of it and an inexperienced one generates noise. The consequence the corpus draws is that reviewer competence is load-bearing — a team member must be somebody you would have trusted on a purely human team ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md), **internal, access-controlled, paraphrased and never quoted**).

**The model therefore rests entirely on expert human validation. Across approximately ninety internal documents it never asks what happens when the volume arriving at the reviewer changes.** Review capacity is the obvious binding constraint on the whole arrangement and it is not discussed once.

The inference is an argument rather than a finding and the session says so. But it is a strong argument, and there is one piece of adjacent evidence worth holding beside it. In a randomised trial on real backlog items in the developers' own repositories, **measured and perceived speed diverged, and direct experience did not correct the perception** ([research/04-professional-formation/metr-ai-assisted-productivity-study.md](/research/04-professional-formation/metr-ai-assisted-productivity-study.md)). If a team cannot feel its own throughput accurately, it cannot feel its own review debt accurately either — and the capacity statement exists because the feeling is not admissible evidence.

**Limits, which are severe:** very small developer sample; expert maintainers in codebases they know intimately, close to a worst case for assistance and not representative of software work generally; self-reported time; one fixed early tooling generation; an explicit non-claims list published by the authors that must be cited with the headline; **the research group now labels the result out of date and has redesigned the study**, because participants will no longer reliably work without the tooling, and a later run pointed the other way and is judged unreliable by its own authors. **No magnitude from any of it is reproduced here.**

One more source is worth naming for the disposition rather than the finding. The error-management literature's core move is to frame errors as informative and then **give no further assistance when one occurs** — and its most operationally consequential moderator is that the task must supply interpretable feedback of its own, because where feedback cannot be readily read the method may be worse than instruction ([research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md](/research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md)). **A review comment that names a defect is interpretable feedback. "I would have done this differently" is not**, and that is the operational reason the style review appears in this session's failure list rather than merely in its etiquette.

### A machine reviewer's findings are input to a review, not a review

The published agent-configuration corpus this programme reads is the clearest available worked example of a review subsystem built as an artefact, and it is more useful for what it declares about its own limits than for its mechanisms.

It decomposes review into four parts: **factual orientation separated from judgement; depth scaled to risk; a fixed set of lenses a reviewer applies; and a severity taxonomy that normalises findings across reviewers** — with findings persisted under stable identifiers ([research/06-microsoft-platform/hve-core-repository.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/hve-core-repository.md), read at a pinned commit on a named date). Two of its declarations are load-bearing for this session.

**"Execution completion and acceptance outcome are different results."** A pass that ran to completion has not thereby been accepted. That distinction is exactly what a machine reviewer's output is: a completed execution, not an acceptance.

**An agent may not record a human's approval.** The corpus prohibits an agent from checking human-review boxes and requires a workflow to stop when a required human review is absent. It is a declaration rather than an enforcement, and the corpus says so.

The corpus is also candid about the limits of its own gates in a way that transfers directly to any automated reviewer you are handed. Its behavioural checks are **regex-graded and test output shape rather than correctness**, by its own documentation. A check can be **advisory** at one tier and **authoritative** at another, so the same finding has different consequences depending on where it ran. And the summary judgement is the one to carry: **the existence of a control is not evidence that the control works.**

**The limits are unusually specific and unusually honest.** It was read at one pinned commit, and **that revision was not all-green** — one reported check was failing. Its prose uses causal and outcome vocabulary — "ship faster", "prevents", "proven" — and **none of those readings is established by anything in the repository**: no controlled study, no calibrated baseline, no defect-rate comparison, no human-rated outcome dataset. Its evaluation machinery produces artefacts at run time and **stores none of them**; the results directory is empty. Its file paths, artefact kinds and package versions are vendor detail that **moved while it was being read**.

Which gives you the operating rule for any automated reviewer, including the one attached to your pull requests: **its findings are input to your review. Accepting them is a review decision you made, and you must be able to say which ones you verified.**

### The one refusal in ninety documents

Opening item 5 asks what you would refuse to have reviewed by a machine, and there is exactly one worked example available.

Across approximately ninety internal documents, **there is a single place where the answer to a customer's request is no.** Replacing human reviewers with a machine in a legal-review process was judged irresponsible, because those reviewers catch issues with legal consequences. **That refusal is the most trustworthy content in the collection, and it is trustworthy precisely because it costs the author something.** Everything else in the corpus is a claim in the direction of its own commercial interest; that one is not.

Write your own version for your own slice. The form that works is a named process, a named consequence, and a named person who would bear it.

## The workflow

**Review one real change under a stated clock, then write the sentence about capacity that you do not want to write.**

**Step 1 — state the time budget before you open the change, and write it down.** A budget derived afterwards is a description of what you did.

**Step 2 — run the two passes and record the boundary of what you read as you go.** Shape and intent first, then detail. **Reconstructing the boundary afterwards produces a flattering boundary**, every time, and the record is the artefact.

**Step 3 — point the specification at the change before you point yourself at it.** Run the executable specification from [S043](/wiki/seminars/S043.md). At least one of your findings must be *this does not do what the specification requires* rather than *I would have written this differently*.

**Step 4 — run the automated reviewer, and record its findings as input rather than as output.** Then verify a subset by hand and record which ones.

```yaml
# reviews/2026-07-31-slice-a.review.yaml
change: "PR 214 — adjudication drafter, escalation path"
time_budget_minutes: 45          # STATED BEFORE OPENING. Actual: 71.
pass_1_shape_and_intent: complete
pass_2_detail:
  read_line_by_line:
    - src/adjudicate/escalate.py
    - src/adjudicate/cite.py
  read_for_shape_only:
    - tests/test_escalate.py      # skimmed; assumed to match the specification
  not_opened:                     # SAY IT IN THESE WORDS. Do not soften.
    - infra/bicep/main.bicep
    - src/adjudicate/_generated_client.py
findings:
  - id: RV-001
    severity: high                # normalised taxonomy, so two reviewers are comparable
    lens: specification           # from S043, not from taste
    text: "Escalates without stating which paragraph was checked. Spec S3 requires it."
  - id: RV-002
    severity: low
    lens: scope                   # out of scope: MAY NOT block this change (S042)
    text: "Retry policy belongs to a new item. Recorded, not blocked."
    disposition: new_backlog_item
let_through_knowingly:
  - what: "Unbounded retry in the generated client."
    why: "Not opened. Date did not move."
    would_matter_if: "The backend rate-limits us in production and the client hides it."
machine_reviewer:
  findings_returned: 9
  verified_by_hand: [3, 5, 8]     # I can name these. The other six I accepted unverified.
  accepted_unverified: 6          # THIS NUMBER IS THE POINT OF THE FIELD
  note: "Findings are input to this review. Accepting them was my decision."
capacity_statement:
  reviewable_change_per_week: "about four changes of this size"
  review_effort_demanded: "more than one person's week under the every-line rule"
  supplied_by: ["me", "nobody else"]     # if this is the honest answer, write it
  customer_side_reviewer: none            # the playbook requires one. We have none. Say so.
  when_demand_exceeded_supply: "Files listed under not_opened were not opened."
  would_i_sign_this: pending
```

**Step 5 — write the capacity statement before you are comfortable with it.** This is the part that matters and the part learners soften. The honest version is frequently *our rate of production already exceeds what our review can absorb, and here is what we have been letting through.* **That sentence is a professional finding, not a confession**, and this session is the safest room in the programme to write it in.

**Step 6 — read the playbook's own measures page as a specimen.** Put it in front of you and ask what would have to be measured for its closing assertion to be true. Then find the other three unsupported figures on the site and ask what the four have in common. **Do not carry any of their magnitudes out of the room.**

**Step 7 — turn the instrument on your own review rather than on the code.** Give the automated reviewer your review record — not the diff — and instruct it to argue that your review was a sampled read presented as a read; that your severity ratings track author seniority rather than risk; and that your `let_through_knowingly` entry was written to make the record look honest rather than to record a decision. Keep what is better than your own reasoning; record what you rejected. **An instrument that praised your thoroughness was pointed at your diff, not at your record.**

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That the every-line rule has a rate attached to it, and that the rate is discoverable in one afternoon by anybody willing to write down what they did not open. **You will also see the biased-review effect in your own record if you review two changes by two authors of different seniority**, which is the fastest available route from an uncomfortable published observation to an uncomfortable personal one.

## What good looks like

- **The record says what was not read**, in those words, without softening.
- **The time budget is stated before the review, not derived after it.**
- **Something was let through knowingly**, and the record says what it was and what would have to be true for that to matter.
- **The capacity statement names a supplier of review**, and if the answer is *nobody in particular* the statement says so.
- **A reviewer from outside your own organisation appears**, or the record explains why the engagement has none.
- **At least one raised item is declined as out of scope** and recorded rather than absorbed, per [S042](/wiki/seminars/S042.md).
- **The specification from [S043](/wiki/seminars/S043.md) is used as a review instrument**, so that at least one finding is *this does not do what the specification requires* rather than *I would have written this differently*.
- **The machine reviewer's findings are recorded as input, with the verified subset named.** A record that cannot say which findings were checked has delegated the judgement and called it a review.
- **The refusal from item 5 is written down**, naming a process, a consequence and a person who would bear it.

## How it goes wrong

- **The rubber stamp** — approval given on the strength of who wrote it. *Warning sign:* your review time correlates with the author rather than the change.
- **The sampled read presented as a read** — you read the interesting parts and approved the whole. *Warning sign:* you could not say which files you did not open.
- **The style review** — every finding is a preference and none is a defect. *Warning sign:* nothing you raised would have caused an incident.
- **The scope fight in the thread** — the boundary is litigated in the comments while the change waits. *Warning sign:* the longest exchange is about whether something belongs.
- **The capacity denial** — review is treated as free and therefore unplanned. *Warning sign:* your capacity statement contains no hours belonging to anyone but you.
- **The delegated judgement** — a machine reviewer's findings are accepted as the review rather than as input to it. *Warning sign:* you cannot say which of the findings you verified.
- **The completed pass mistaken for an acceptance** — every gate ran, everything is green, and nobody has said the change is acceptable. *Warning sign:* your evidence that the change was reviewed is that a pipeline finished.
- **The borrowed number** — a figure from a respected internal document enters your own reasoning because the page it sat on read like every other page. *Warning sign:* you can state the claim and not its baseline.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The review record and the capacity statement are carried into [S045](/wiki/seminars/S045.md), where the record must still say what was not read and the capacity statement must arrive unrevised.

**1 · Mark four pages by how much you would rely on them.** Take four pages from four sections of the playbook with their footers hidden: one that cites Fowler and the Google SRE book; one that cites only the documentation of the tool it configures; one whose entire reference list is social-media posts; and one that cites nothing while asserting dozens of numeric thresholds. Rank them.

> **What you should find.** That **the register is identical across all four and only the bibliography differs.** The site's tone is uniform and its warrant is not, and nothing in the navigation, the layout or the prose tells you which page you are holding. Note what this does *not* license: the playbook is openly published, repairable by pull request, and better than most internal standards. **The lesson is not that it is bad. The lesson is that authority of tone is not evidence, and you have just calibrated on the document this programme itself draws on.**

**2 · Name the four unsupported figures and say what they have in common.** Find them, state each as a claim, and state what would have to be measured for each to be true. **Write none of the magnitudes down.**

> **Model answer.** The four are: the assertion that the cost of doing code reviews is negative; the assertion that pairing and swarming produce a higher sprint velocity; a defect cost multiple attributed to a 2008 study through a link that cannot be followed in any current browser; and vendor efficiency figures in bands wide enough to be unfalsifiable, from a document already years old when it was read. **What they have in common is that a reasoned practice and a repeated number are written in the same flat register, so the document does not distinguish between something it has thought about and something it has repeated.** For the first to be true you would need the cost of review time, the cost of the defects it caught, and a comparison against a team not doing it — none of which the page has, or claims to have. **This repository records all four as specimens and reproduces none of them, which is the behaviour the exercise is teaching.**

**3 · Go back to your own item 3, then break it.** Find what you wrote for opening item 3 — what breaks first when your rate of production changes and your review capacity does not. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference.

> **Criterion.** The answer this session teaches is that **review capacity is the binding constraint, and that what breaks first is not quality but the record of what was read.** Nobody announces that they have started sampling. The every-line rule is not repealed; it is quietly unmet, and because the source refuses to say when a change is too large to read, there is no threshold at which the failure becomes visible. **If you wrote that quality drops, you named a consequence and not a mechanism** — quality drops later, and by then the review record already said *approved*. If you wrote that the team would hire more reviewers, say who pays for them and out of which budget, because the corpus this stage rests on never asks.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is. **School, university and laboratory evidence; no adult-professional study in its reference list.**

**4 · Review two changes by two authors and compare your own time.** One from somebody senior to you, one from somebody junior. Same instrument, same record format.

> **What you should find.** The published observation is that **large changes get biased review, the senior engineer's approved unread and the junior engineer's not reviewed at all** — and the observation is easier to accept about other people than about yourself. If your record shows the pattern, note that you were not being lazy: you were running a risk calculation about speaking upward, and the literature on voice describes exactly that calculation and its taken-for-granted rules. **The corrective is structural rather than moral — fix the record format so that not opening a file has to be written down — because a rule that cannot fail visibly will be broken silently.** The voice construct's own evidence base is a trade book with a narrative review carrying no search protocol and no negative finding, and secondary case studies selected after the outcomes were known; the aggregate synthesis is where the state of the evidence lives, and this repository read it from an abstract that reports no numerical effect sizes.

**5 · Write your refusal, then have it challenged.** Name one thing in your slice you would not have reviewed by a machine, with the process, the consequence and the person who would bear it. Give it to a colleague whose job is to argue that you are being precious.

> **Scoring criterion.** It passes if the refusal names a consequence that is not *it would be embarrassing*, and if it survives one round of challenge without expanding to cover everything. **A refusal that covers everything is not a refusal, it is a policy of distrust**, and it will be overridden by the first person under a date. Compare yours with the only worked example available: across approximately ninety internal documents there is exactly one place where the answer to a customer's request is no, and it refuses to replace human reviewers with a machine in a legal-review process because those reviewers catch issues with legal consequences. **That refusal is trustworthy precisely because it cost its author something**, which is the property yours needs.

**6 · Ask one person whether they would sign your capacity statement.** Not whether it is accurate. Whether they would put their name to it in front of the people who set the date.

> **What to notice.** A statement nobody will sign will not survive the conversation it was written for, and finding that out here is cheaper than finding it out there. Notice which clause they refuse to sign: it is usually `supplied_by`, because naming a supplier of review makes an absence concrete. **If your honest answer is that nobody in particular supplies it, that is the finding**, and it belongs in the pack at [S045](/wiki/seminars/S045.md) unrevised. Notice also whether the playbook's requirement of reviewers **from both organisations** is met on your engagement. If it is not, say why rather than omitting the field.

## What this does not establish

Nothing here shows that reviewing every line produces better software than sampling it.

The playbook states the rule and **supplies no comparative evidence**, in common with every practice on that site. Its own evidence-and-measures page proposes three measures it concedes are gathered by hand, and then makes an unwarranted claim about cost that this repository records as a specimen and adopts nowhere. **No figure from any of the playbook's four unwarranted magnitudes is repeated anywhere in this stage.**

The internal corpus supplies the amplification claim — that the tooling raises what an expert produces and what a novice produces differently — and **supplies no measurement of it whatsoever**, in a collection that reports approximately thirty quantitative assertions of which almost all measure activity rather than outcome.

**The claim about validation capacity is this design's, not the sources'.** The sources establish that the model depends on expert validation; the inference that validation is therefore the binding constraint when production accelerates is an argument. It is a strong argument and it is untested, and no cohort running this session will test it either — [S066](/wiki/seminars/S066.md) and [S070](/wiki/seminars/S070.md) are where a measurement of it would have to live, and the programme predicts none will exist.

**The voice literature is cited for mechanism and not for magnitude, and its strongest popular statement is the weakest evidence in it.** The trade-press book supplying the definition and the voice asymmetry is not an empirical study; its literature chapter is a narrative review with no search protocol, no inclusion criteria, no quality appraisal and **no negative finding reported across a hundred-plus articles**, written by the field's most-cited author; its organisational cases are secondary throughout and were selected after the outcomes were known; and **no trial of its leadership framework is reported.** The aggregate synthesis is the appropriate source for the state of the evidence, and **this repository read it from its official abstract because the publisher version is closed** — the abstract reports no numerical effect sizes, so **no correlation for any antecedent or outcome may be quoted from it here.** The pooled literature is overwhelmingly cross-sectional and self-report, so causal direction is not established by it.

**The agent-configuration corpus establishes an architecture and warrants no benefit.** It was read at one pinned commit, on a named date, at a revision that was **not all-green**; its prose uses causal vocabulary that nothing in it establishes; its evaluation machinery stores no results at all; and its paths, artefact kinds and package versions moved while it was being read. Its most useful sentence is its own: **the existence of a control is not evidence that the control works.**

**The error-management evidence is relative and laboratory-bound.** Every study compared one training method against another rather than against no training; **no study measured on-the-job performance**; and the moderator this session leans on — that feedback must be interpretable — received, in the authors' own words, only limited support. **No effect size from it appears here.**

## Further reading

**The review practice, and the four specimens**

- [research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md) — read every line changed; the two-pass structure; the bug-caught-not-who norm; tests in the same change; the out-of-scope and no-deferral rules; reviewers from both organisations; the refusal to give a size limit; the observation that large changes get biased review; and the four unsupported magnitude claims across the site. **Pages read 2026-07-30; cite by page and date, never as a whole. Footer dates span four years with nothing in the navigation to indicate which is which; roughly half the site is stub, link list or version-pinned recipe; it contradicts itself in at least a dozen places; the tone is uniform and the warrant is not. All four unsupported figures are recorded here as specimens and none of their magnitudes is reproduced anywhere in this wiki.**
- [research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md) — the amplification claim and its consequence that reviewer competence is load-bearing; the absence of any discussion of validation capacity across the collection; and the single recorded refusal to replace human reviewers in a legal-review process. **Internal and access-controlled, not publicly reachable, not citable externally, paraphrased and never quoted; the amplification claim carries no measurement whatsoever; roughly thirty quantitative assertions of which almost all measure activity rather than outcome, and none appears anywhere in this wiki.**

**Why the sentence fails before the process does**

- [research/04-professional-formation/edmondson-psychological-safety.md](/research/04-professional-formation/edmondson-psychological-safety.md) — interpersonal risk; the asymmetry of voice and silence; implicit theories of voice; the orthogonality of standards and safety; and sanction as part of the model rather than an exception to it. **A trade-press book, not a study; its narrative review has no search protocol, no inclusion criteria and reports no negative finding across a hundred-plus articles in a literature where its author is the most-cited figure; its cases are secondary and selected after the outcomes were known; no trial of its framework is reported.**
- [research/04-professional-formation/frazier-psychological-safety-meta-analysis.md](/research/04-professional-formation/frazier-psychological-safety-meta-analysis.md) — the aggregate synthesis, its ranking of antecedents against each other, its incremental-validity test against positive leader relations and work engagement, and its test of homology across individual and group levels. **Read from the official abstract because the publisher version is closed; the abstract reports no numerical effect sizes and none may be quoted; the pooled evidence is overwhelmingly cross-sectional and self-report, so causal direction is not established.**
- [research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md](/research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md) — error framing with no assistance offered when an error occurs, and the requirement that the task supply interpretable feedback. **All effects are relative to another training method, never to no training; no study measured on-the-job performance; the feedback-clarity moderator received only limited support by the authors' own statement. No effect size appears here.**

**Why felt throughput is not admissible evidence**

- [research/04-professional-formation/metr-ai-assisted-productivity-study.md](/research/04-professional-formation/metr-ai-assisted-productivity-study.md) — measured and perceived speed diverging on real backlog items in the developers' own repositories, with direct experience failing to correct the perception. **Very small developer sample; expert maintainers on codebases they know intimately; self-reported time; one fixed early tooling generation; an explicit non-claims list that must be cited with the headline; the research group now labels the result out of date and has redesigned the study; the later run is judged unreliable by its own authors and is not a reversal. No magnitude from it is reproduced here.**

**The automated reviewer, and the limits it declares about itself**

- [research/06-microsoft-platform/hve-core-repository.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/hve-core-repository.md) — review decomposed into orientation, risk-scaled depth, fixed lenses and a normalised severity taxonomy; execution completion distinguished from acceptance outcome; the prohibition on an agent recording a human's approval; advisory versus authoritative gates; and the statement that the existence of a control is not evidence that the control works. **Read at one pinned commit on a named date, at a revision that was not all-green; its causal and outcome vocabulary is unwarranted by anything in it; its evaluation machinery stores no results; its paths, artefact kinds and package versions are vendor detail that moved during the reading.**

**The pedagogical design**

- [research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md) — why exercise 3 requires comparison rather than rereading, and why the opening items precede instruction. **School, university and laboratory evidence; no adult-professional study in its reference list.**
