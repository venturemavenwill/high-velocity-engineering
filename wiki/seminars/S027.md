# S027 · Extending What Already Runs

**Module:** [M06](/wiki/modules/M06.md) · **Quarter:** [Q3](/wiki/quarters/Q3.md) · **Complexity class:** 3
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** MCP and OpenAPI composition beside the Bot Framework to Agents SDK migration — extension by protocol rather than replacement, and an inherited system whose unsupported list is published and whose end of support is not
**Whitepaper:** [WP-027](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-027.md)
**Retrieves from:** S008, S021, S026
**Entry state:** either

---

## The situation

There is already a system. It has been running for years. It works badly, people depend on it, and somebody built it who is still employed here.

Your design has to meet it somewhere.

**Answer these in writing before reading further.**

1. What already runs here that your design will touch?
2. Who maintains it, and what would they say about your plan?
3. Which of its concepts will you adopt, and which will you translate?
4. What does translation cost, **every time, for as long as both systems exist**?
5. Is there a version of this where you do not integrate at all?

**Item 5 is the one nobody writes down.** Integration is expensive and its cost is paid continuously, while its benefit is usually asserted once, in a slide, on the grounds that two things being related in a story means they must be connected in a system.

## What you must produce

A **boundary decision** for every existing system your design meets. Three options are available and all three are real:

- **Adopt its model.** Use its concepts and its vocabulary inside your work, and accept its constraints as yours.
- **Translate at the boundary.** Keep your own model, and build and maintain a layer that converts between the two.
- **Do not integrate.** Accept the duplication or the manual step, and say what that costs.

Each decision carries its cost, its owner, and **the condition under which you would change it.**

Plus a **map** of the systems your design meets and the relationships between them — describing what is actually there, including the organisational and political contours, not the arrangement you would prefer.

Plus, for the largest boundary, **the translation written out concept by concept**, including what has no equivalent on the other side and what happens to a case that does not fit.

## The material

### The default response to an existing system is not a decision

It is a sequence of small avoidances. Reasoning about the old system is unpleasant, its maintainers are hard to reach, its documentation is stale, and the fastest way to make progress this week is to build the piece you need. Repeated for a quarter, that produces a new system that partially duplicates the old one, connected by whatever was convenient at the time, with no boundary anywhere and no statement of which model governs.

The practice corpus behind this programme names the pattern and its version is sharper than the general observation: **reinventing rather than building on what the customer already runs** is one of a small set of named failure patterns, given with warning signs observable in advance — nobody has spoken to the existing system's maintainers, the plan describes a replacement nobody asked for, the demonstration runs on invented data rather than the customer's own ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md)). **That is practitioner judgement with warning signs and no outcome data**, from access-controlled material in which no engagement is reported to have gone badly.

### Adoption is refused for emotional reasons, and the literature says so out loud

The domain-modelling literature is unusual in defending conformity properly. Evans gives it a section headed **"Following Isn't Always Bad"**, argues that a good existing model has **"knowledge crunched into its design"** and **"may well be much more advanced than your own understanding"**, so that **"you could be dragged into a better design"** — and then names the reason it is refused anyway: conformity **"is very unappealing emotionally, which is why we choose it less often than we probably should"** ([research/05-fde-craft/evans-domain-driven-design.md](/research/05-fde-craft/evans-domain-driven-design.md)).

That sentence is the reason adoption is listed first in your artefact rather than last. Order matters when a list is a decision procedure: **adoption listed last is adoption never reached**, because by the time you get there the design is drawn and the cost of unwinding it is your own work.

### A translating layer is a maintained system, and it is costed once and paid continuously

The same source defines the isolating layer precisely, and the precision matters: it is **"a mechanism that translates conceptual objects and actions from one model and protocol to another"** — translation of *concepts*, not transport of messages. A layer that moves fields between two schemas without changing the model is a serialiser. A layer that lets your model stay yours while somebody else's stays theirs is a different and much larger object.

Evans costs it with a parable about a wall that **"bankrupted at least one dynasty"**, and states the moral as a rule: **"The benefits of isolation strategies must be balanced against their costs."** The failure in practice is not that teams do not know the layer costs something. **It is that the estimate is attached to the build**, while the expenditure is attached to every subsequent change on either side, for as long as both models exist.

### Not integrating is a real option, and it is almost never on the list

The strongest sentence in this session's source material is a scope-discipline argument: **"Integration is always expensive. Sometimes the benefit is small... Just because features are related in a use case does not mean they must be integrated."** Two things being related in a story is not evidence that they must be connected in a system, and a duplicated record with a person who reconciles it weekly is sometimes cheaper than the layer that would have removed them.

**Its limits, and they bite harder here than anywhere else in the module.** That source makes **no quantitative claim of any kind** and declares two prerequisites flatly — that development is iterative, and that engineers have a close continuing relationship with the people who understand the domain — stating that without them it claims nothing. **Integration engagements are exactly the case where the second prerequisite is least likely to hold**, because the people who understand the existing system are frequently in another organisation, contractually unavailable, or gone. The book was read in full from a locally held copy carrying no publisher, date or edition metadata.

### The map describes reality, including the parts of it that are political

A context map is a descriptive instrument first. Evans's rule is **"Map the existing terrain. Take up transformations later"**, and he repeats the operational form of it: **"don't change the map until the change in reality is done."** He also gives the speech test that shows the map has landed — people stop saying *George's team's stuff is changing* and start saying *the Transport Network model is changing, so we have to change the translator for the Booking context*.

And he is explicit about what the boundaries actually follow: **"In practice, political relationships between teams often determine how systems are integrated."** A boundary drawn on technical similarity that cuts across a team's responsibility produces a permanent coordination cost nobody costed. **A boundary drawn on ownership is usually uglier and usually cheaper.**

### False cognates survive every review, because the word is familiar on both sides

The two failure signatures at a boundary are duplicate concepts and **false cognates** — **"two people who are using the same term (or implemented object) think they are talking about the same thing, but really are not."** This is the glossary discipline from [S021](/wiki/seminars/S021.md) applied at a larger scale, and it is the same instrument.

The production-systems literature supplies the version of this failure that has no words in it at all. **Training–serving skew** occurs when a transformation is computed one way in one place and another way in another, and its defining property is that it **produces no error, no exception and no obviously wrong output** ([research/02-technical-foundations/crowe-machine-learning-production-systems.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/crowe-machine-learning-production-systems.md)). It is a false cognate expressed in data rather than in vocabulary, and it is the clearest available demonstration that **silent failure is the characteristic failure mode of a system boundary.** That book is a practitioner survey; it offers no comparative evidence for any recommended practice, its toolchain is not neutral, and **every number in it is a property of a particular system and none is reproduced here.**

### Extension by protocol, and the vendor's own refusal of the single-platform question

Here is the platform lesson, and it is unusually direct.

The comparison documentation for agent build paths does something vendor documentation rarely does: it declines the question it exists to answer. **"These options aren't mutually exclusive. For example, a serverless agents runtime app can expose tools through MCP that Foundry agents or Copilot Studio agents consume"** ([research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md)). **The choice is not a platform for a system. It is a platform per component, composed through protocols** — which is the boundary decision restated in the vendor's own vocabulary.

The same family names three routes by which an agent reaches an external system: a **tool catalogue**, **MCP**, and **OpenAPI specifications**. Those are the three shapes your translating layer can take, and choosing between them is choosing how much of somebody else's model you let across. It also names the fork that governs everything else — **who owns the orchestrator** — and records that owning it determines model choice, extensibility and lifecycle tooling together. One consequence is worth memorising because it is a boundary decision that binds at the end of a project from a choice made at the start: **publishing route is constrained by build choice**, with only certain toolchains able to publish to the commercial store.

The gateway documentation shows the same idea one layer down, and it is the closest thing to an anticorruption layer sold as a product. The AI gateway manages **remote MCP servers and agent-to-agent APIs** as first-class endpoint types alongside model APIs, and its preview **unified model API** exposes **"multiple backends through a single OpenAI-compatible endpoint"**, handling format translation automatically so that governance policy is applied once across all of them ([research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md)). That is translation-at-the-boundary with an owner, a bill and an operator — which is exactly what the drawn box on your diagram is missing. The same page insists it **"extends API Management's existing API gateway; it's not a separate offering"**, which is itself the session's thesis: **extension of what already runs, rather than replacement of it.**

The internal platform synthesis behind this strand makes the durable version of the claim: **MCP and A2A are open protocols rather than products**, and the argument that agents need standard ways to reach tools, data and other agents is architecturally sound and vendor-independent ([research/06-microsoft-platform/03-accelerate-agentic-ai.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/03-accelerate-agentic-ai.md)). **That document is internal, is not publicly accessible, cannot be cited externally, and is the most perishable artefact in this repository**; its quantitative claims carry no methodology and none is repeated here. Where a claim from it is taught, the public documentation is the citable source.

**All the platform material in this session is highly perishable.** Product names, tooling names and capability boundaries are actively changing; several of the comparison tables were truncated in the excerpts this repository read; the pages were read through search results rather than full fetches; and the gateway's Foundry integration is preview. **Teach the axes. Discard the product names.**

### Can it migrate, or must it be rebuilt? The boundary decision in its most concrete form

Your artefact offers three options, and there is a fourth question hiding underneath the first one. **Adopt the incumbent's model — fine. Can the incumbent come with you at all?**

That question is usually answered by vibes, in a meeting, by somebody estimating. There is one case in this platform where it can be answered by enumeration instead, and it is worth working through because the *shape* of the answer transfers to every inherited system you will ever meet.

The predecessor conversational SDK is superseded ([research/06-microsoft-platform/agents-toolkit-and-sdk-family.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/agents-toolkit-and-sdk-family.md), read 2026-07-31). What makes the case teachable is how much has been published about the move: **a general guidance page and two language-specific guides**, carrying package mapping tables, namespace find-and-replace instructions, configuration changes, troubleshooting sections and closing checklists. The framing is repeated in both language guides and is the right framing: **"Treat this work as a modernization effort, not only a package swap."**

Then the part with real planning consequence — a published list of what **is not supported** in the successor, introduced with the sentence that makes it decisive: bots requiring these functionalities **"can't migrate without implementing alternatives"**. It names adaptive dialogs; an expression library that is present but not actively supported; the legacy telemetry approach; a command-line tool whose commands are all deprecated; deprecated activity types; the scaffolding generators, superseded by the toolkit; the inspection tooling; language generation; language understanding; **the previous generation of understanding services, whose online services "are already disabled"**; a queue-storage integration; streaming connections; and a template manager. Dialogs survive under a new namespace, described as "a practical bridge during migration".

**Read that as an instrument rather than as a list.** It converts *can we migrate this?* from a judgement into an inventory: walk the incumbent's dependencies against it, mark each one migrate, replace or drop, and total the replacements honestly. For some systems the total comes back **cannot migrate without a rebuild**, and that is precisely the answer this session exists to make sayable in week two rather than in month five. **An enumerable answer arriving early is worth more than a confident answer arriving late**, and it costs an afternoon.

**Now the silence, and it is the reason this material belongs here rather than in a migration module.**

**No dated end of support was found anywhere for the predecessor SDK. No support policy. No migration deadline. And no migration-effort estimate for a bot of any size.** So the decision has an **enumerable scope** and an **unbounded schedule**, which is an unusual and uncomfortable combination: you can say exactly what must be rebuilt and nothing at all about when it must be. The only dated commitment in the whole family attaches to a different component — a predecessor toolkit library carries a stated community-only support window with an end date on it — which sharpens rather than softens the point, because it shows the vendor does publish such dates when it chooses to.

The consequence for your artefact is exact. **A migration with a published breaking-change list and no published end date is a translating layer whose end condition you have to invent yourself**, which is the `change_condition` and `end_date` fields from step 2 arriving with real weight. The honest value for `end_date` here is one *you* chose and can defend, not one you were given — and writing `null` means you have accepted a permanent tax, which is a legitimate decision and must be a stated one.

**One more property changes what the migration actually is**, and missing it is the commonest error in this area. The successor is **transport, state and identity — not orchestration.** Its own documentation is unusually blunt: **"The Agents SDK isn't an AI model, an orchestration engine, or a no-code builder. The Agents SDK doesn't decide what an agent says."** So migrating moves the plumbing and leaves the question of what the thing says entirely untouched. The orchestration library composes **inside** it rather than competing with it — layers, not alternatives — which is the same non-exclusivity claim you met one paragraph ago, arriving one level down and settling an argument teams routinely have about which of the two to pick.

And one sentence from that same family belongs beside your non-integration option, because it is the strongest available statement of the position from a party with no incentive to make it: **"If you can write a function to handle the task, do that instead of using an AI agent."**

**Its limits.** Read 2026-07-31. The migration guidance and the unsupported table were read **through search-returned excerpts rather than full page fetches**, so the list above is what the excerpts returned and **is not guaranteed complete**; a team advising a real migration must read the current guides in full. Every absence recorded here — the end-of-support date, the support policy, the deadline, the effort estimate — **was found by searching for the thing and not finding it, not stated by any source**, and absence at one reading is not proof of absence in the product. **Names decay faster than behaviour in this family**: every principal name in it has changed at least once, one package has changed name twice, and one component ships today under two live package names with two commands, so a course that picks one will be wrong for some readers. **No adoption data, no failure-rate data, no cost comparison and no evidence that migrating produces a better outcome than not migrating appears anywhere in it**, and the surrounding ergonomics claims are marketing register with no measurement behind them.

### One more discipline, from an unexpected place

The openly published engineering playbook this stage borrows its artefact conventions from contains a single sentence that settles the commonest argument at a boundary: **"We do not recommend making a breaking change to a service that predates these guidelines simply for the sake of compliance."** ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md), page read 2026-07-30.) A standards body declining to apply its own standard retroactively is a precedent worth having in your pocket. **A precedent is not evidence**, and that playbook offers no comparative evidence that any of its practices outperform alternatives — it is also uneven, with page dates spread across several years and roughly a fifth of it stubs or link lists. Cite it by page and by the date that page was read, never as a whole.

## The workflow

**Expose one existing system to your design through a protocol rather than a rewrite, and write the translation down as the artefact it is.**

You will use MCP and an OpenAPI specification, with the AI gateway in front if you can reach one. Steps 1, 2, 3 and 7 need no cloud subscription. If the existing system is unreachable, model it from its own documentation and **record on the map that you did**, which is itself the honest outcome of step 6.

**Step 1 — find out what the existing system already speaks.** An OpenAPI document, a database view, a scheduled file drop, or nothing. **A system that already publishes a contract has already made a boundary decision, and adopting that contract is the adoption option in concrete form.** Record the answer, and record the date you checked, because stale specifications are the norm.

**Step 2 — write the boundary decision file before writing any code.** One entry per system your design meets. The `change_condition` field is the one that stops a temporary bridge becoming a standing tax by default.

```yaml
# docs/boundaries/decisions.yaml
- system: "Claims adjudication service (2014, team: Claims Platform)"
  decision: translate           # adopt | translate | do-not-integrate
  why_not_adopt: "Their 'claim' spans an entire policy year; ours is one event."
  route: mcp                    # tool-catalogue | mcp | openapi | none
  standing_cost:
    build: "two weeks"
    per_year: "one engineer-week of change absorption on either side, plus on-call"
    owner: "our team, until the change condition fires"
  change_condition: "If Claims Platform publishes a per-event contract, we adopt it and delete this layer."
  end_date: 2027-06-30          # null means you have chosen a permanent tax; say so
  translation:                  # concepts, not fields
    - theirs: "claim"          ours: "policy_period"   note: "one-to-many; ours is finer"
    - theirs: "adjuster"       ours: null              note: "NO EQUIVALENT — dropped on ingest"
    - theirs: "status=OPEN"    ours: "state=awaiting_evidence | state=in_review"
  unfitting_case: "A reopened claim. Their model reuses the identifier; ours cannot. Rejected at the boundary and queued for a person."
```

**Step 3 — expose the translation as an MCP tool server, and name the tools in *your* vocabulary.** This is the whole exercise in one instruction. A tool called `getClaim` has let their model across your boundary; a tool called `fetch_policy_period_for_event` has not. **The tool name and its description are the anticorruption layer's public surface**, and a model reading them will reason in whichever vocabulary you chose.

**Step 4 — where the existing system publishes an OpenAPI document, import it rather than hand-writing a client.** Then read the imported operations and mark every one whose name means something different on your side. Those are your false-cognate candidates, and the list is usually longer than expected because the imported names are all plausible.

**Step 5 — put the gateway in front and make the boundary observable.** Attach a token-limit policy keyed on a counter key so that one consumer of the boundary cannot starve the others, emit token metrics with a dimension identifying the consuming component, and authenticate with a managed identity so that the boundary carries no long-lived secret. **A boundary you cannot attribute traffic across is a boundary you cannot cost**, and cost is the field step 2 demanded.

**Step 6 — show the map to somebody who works on the existing system and ask one question: what is wrong with this?** Where you cannot reach such a person, write that on the map itself, in the place their confirmation would have gone. **An unconfirmed map is usable. An unconfirmed map that looks confirmed is not.**

**Step 7 — attack your own boundary.** Two prompts, in this order. First: *argue that we should not integrate with this system at all, and price the duplication we would accept instead.* Second, and harder: *read these tool names and descriptions and find every place where the same word means two different things on the two sides.* Record what you kept and what you rejected. **An agent asked to improve your integration will add to it. Asked to remove it, it has to find a reason, and locating is a harder operation than adding.**

**Step 8 — inventory the incumbent against the published unsupported list, and total it honestly.** One row per dependency, one verdict each. **Do not estimate anything until the inventory is finished**, because an estimate produced halfway through an inventory is an estimate of the part you have looked at.

```yaml
# docs/boundaries/incumbent-migration.yaml
# The question is not "how long will this take". It is "does the successor
# support this at all". Answer that first; it is enumerable and cheap.

incumbent: "Claims triage bot (2019, team: Contact Centre Platform)"
successor_supported_list_read: 2026-07-31   # date, because the list moves
source_depth: "search excerpts, not a full page fetch — treat as incomplete"

dependencies:
  - name: "dialog framework"
    verdict: replace          # migrate | replace | drop
    note: "the newer dialog style is unsupported; the older namespace survives as a bridge"
  - name: "language understanding service"
    verdict: replace
    note: "the online service is already disabled, so this is not a deadline, it is a fact"
  - name: "language generation templates"
    verdict: replace
  - name: "queue-storage integration"
    verdict: replace
  - name: "streaming connection to the desk client"
    verdict: drop
    note: "unsupported and nobody has used the desk client since 2023. Confirm with an owner."
  - name: "telemetry pipeline"
    verdict: replace
    note: "legacy approach unsupported; the replacement is a different pipeline, not a shim"

verdict_totals: { migrate: 0, replace: 5, drop: 1 }
honest_conclusion: >
  This is a rebuild wearing a migration's name. Say so now. The three options in
  decisions.yaml are still adopt, translate or do-not-integrate — and "rebuild"
  is not among them, so the real choice is between translating at a protocol
  boundary and not integrating at all.

# THE SCHEDULE IS NOT GIVEN TO YOU.
end_of_support_published: false      # none found for the predecessor SDK
migration_deadline_published: false
effort_estimate_published: false
our_end_condition: >
  We choose one, because nobody supplied one. State the trigger, not the date:
  "when the contact-centre team next reopens this codebase for any reason."
  A null here means a permanent tax has been accepted. Say that out loud.
```

**Step 9 — write the end condition the vendor did not give you, and put a name against it.** An inherited system with a published breaking-change list and no published deadline will be migrated when somebody decides to, and if nobody decides, never. **The trip wire from [S026](/wiki/seminars/S026.md) is the right instrument here** — an observation somebody other than you could notice, written before the pressure arrives.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That the translating layer is not a box on a diagram — it is a file with named concepts, a list of things that have no equivalent, a rule for the case that does not fit, an owner, an annual cost and a date. **You will write the `unfitting_case` field last and it will take longer than everything above it**, which is the cheapest available demonstration that the exceptions are where the maintenance goes. **Steps 8 and 9 add the other half**: that *can this move at all* is an inventory rather than an estimate, that the inventory can be finished in an afternoon, and that finishing it frequently returns an answer the room did not want in week two and would have accepted much worse in month five.

## What good looks like

- **Adoption was genuinely considered.** Conforming to somebody else's established model is refused far more often than it should be, and the reason is emotional rather than technical: it feels like a loss of authorship. A model that has survived years of production has knowledge crunched into it and may be better than yours. **The register should show adoption being rejected for a stated reason, not never appearing.**
- **Translation is costed as a standing cost**, not as a one-off build. A translating layer is real engineering and it is maintained for as long as both models exist. Cost the wall before you build it.
- **Non-integration is on the list as a real option**, with what it costs written next to it. Sometimes the right answer is two systems and a person who reconciles them once a week.
- **The map describes reality.** It includes which teams own what, who reports to whom, and where the boundaries follow organisational lines rather than technical ones — because they usually do. **Do not change the map until the change in reality has happened.**
- **The maintainers of the existing system are named** and, where possible, have seen the map. Where that was not possible, the map says so.
- **The vocabulary at each boundary is checked for false cognates.** Two systems using the same word for different things is the failure that survives every review, because the word is familiar on both sides.
- **The route is named** — tool catalogue, MCP, OpenAPI or none — and the choice is defended by how much of the other model it lets across, not by which was easiest to configure.
- **Every translating layer has an end condition**, or an explicit statement that a permanent tax has been accepted. The second is a legitimate decision. Silence is not.

## How it goes wrong

- **The greenfield reflex** — the plan replaces the existing system because reasoning about it is unpleasant. **Reinventing rather than building on what the customer already runs is a named failure in the practice material behind this stage, with observable warning signs.** *Warning sign:* nobody has asked the existing system's maintainers anything.
- **The free translator** — the translating layer is drawn as a box and never costed. *Warning sign:* the layer has no owner and no line in anybody's plan.
- **The wall you cannot afford** — the isolation is correct in principle and larger than the thing it protects. The benefit of isolation has to be balanced against its cost, and the cost is usually understated because it is paid later. *Warning sign:* the translation layer has more concepts in it than either model it sits between.
- **The map redrawn before reality** — the map shows the arrangement you have proposed rather than the one that exists, so everyone reasons about a system that is not there. *Warning sign:* somebody argues about the map instead of about the system.
- **The compliance rewrite** — an existing service is changed to conform to your new standard, purely for the sake of compliance, with no other benefit. **The published playbook this stage draws its artefact discipline from explicitly declines to require this of its own older services.** Page read 2026-07-30. *Warning sign:* the justification for the change is the standard itself.
- **The absent maintainer** — the boundary is designed without anyone who has operated the existing system in the room. *Warning sign:* your design assumes a behaviour nobody has confirmed.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** One property of this session's output is checked at [S030](/wiki/seminars/S030.md): where the design meets an existing system, the boundary decision is named and its standing cost is stated.

**1 · Classify six boundaries.** For each, say whether the correct answer is adopt, translate or do not integrate, and name the cost you are accepting.

- *A mature identity provider your customer already operates.* · *A reporting database whose "customer" is a billing account and whose owners have left.* · *A partner's REST service with a published contract and a support agreement.* · *An internal tool with one maintainer and no tests.* · *A regulator's file format.* · *A colleague's spreadsheet that four people update.*

> **Model answers.** Adopt the identity provider and the regulator's format — both are models with knowledge crunched into them and neither is yours to improve. Translate the partner service, because a published contract with support is precisely the case where a thin, owned layer is affordable. **Do not integrate the reporting database or the spreadsheet**, at least at first: the first has no owner to negotiate the model with and the second has four, and in both cases duplication plus a person is cheaper than a layer nobody will maintain. The internal tool is the genuinely hard one and the answer depends entirely on whether its maintainer will be in the room. **If your answer to any of the six did not include a cost, you classified rather than decided.**

**2 · Build the smallest real composition.** Using the workflow, expose one operation of an existing system as an MCP tool named in your own vocabulary, and have an agent call it. Record the tool name, the underlying operation, and the concept that changed between them.

> **What you should find.** That the translation is not in the code but in the name and the description, and that a model reasons in whichever vocabulary you gave it. **Note also what the documentation lets you do here:** it states in its own words that these build options are not mutually exclusive and gives protocol composition as the example, so a design that mixes platforms is following published guidance rather than working around it. **The names, the routes and the products are all perishable; the fact that a boundary is a vocabulary decision is not.**

**3 · Go back to your own item 5, then break it.** Find what you wrote for opening item 5 — whether there is a version of this where you do not integrate at all. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then write the non-integration option properly, with its cost.

> **Criterion.** A non-integration entry passes if it states what is duplicated, who reconciles it, how often, and what breaks first when the duplication drifts. "We considered not integrating but the systems need to share data" fails — it restates the problem. "Two records of the same customer, reconciled weekly by the operations lead, drifting first on address changes, accepted until the volume exceeds one person's Friday" passes. **Then compare that cost against the standing cost you wrote for translation.** If you have never written both numbers next to each other, you have never actually chosen.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Find one false cognate, in a system you did not build.** Take the imported operations from workflow step 4 and find a term that means something different on the two sides. Write both definitions.

> **What to notice.** You will find it in a noun, not a verb, and it will be a word so ordinary that neither side has ever defined it — *account*, *customer*, *active*, *complete*, *owner*. **A false cognate is invisible precisely in proportion to how familiar the word is**, which is why the check has to be mechanical rather than intuitive.

**5 · Cost the wall.** For your largest boundary, write the build cost and the annual cost separately, and name who pays the second.

> **Scoring criterion.** The entry passes if the annual figure names a person or a team and a unit of their time, and if it survives the question *what happens to this number when their model changes?* **The isolation argument in the source material is costed with a parable about a wall that bankrupted a dynasty, and the moral is that the benefit of isolation must be balanced against its cost** — which is impossible unless somebody wrote both down.

**6 · Name the prerequisite you do not have.** Closed book: the source supplying this session's boundary vocabulary declares two conditions without which it claims nothing. Name both, and say which one your engagement violates.

> **Answer.** Iterative development, and a close continuing relationship between engineers and the people who understand the domain. **On integration work the second is the one that fails**, because the people who understand the existing system are frequently in another organisation or gone. Saying so is not a disclaimer — it changes what you may claim for the resulting design.

**7 · Turn "can we migrate this?" into an inventory, and notice what the inventory cannot tell you.** Take one inherited system and one published list of what its successor does not support. Walk the dependencies, mark each **migrate**, **replace** or **drop**, total the verdicts, and write the one-sentence honest conclusion. **Then write down separately what the published material does not give you.**

> **Criterion.** A passing inventory has a verdict against every dependency, a total, a conclusion that follows from the total, and a dated record of which version of the unsupported list was walked. **A conclusion of "this is a rebuild" is a passing answer**, and reaching it in an afternoon rather than in a quarter is the entire point.
>
> **What you should find on the second half.** That the scope is enumerable and the schedule is not. **No end-of-support date, no support policy, no deadline and no effort estimate** are published for the superseded component — while a different component in the same family does carry a dated support window, which proves the vendor publishes such dates when it chooses to. **A dependency with a known breaking-change list and no known deadline does not migrate itself**, so the end condition is one you invent, defend and attach a trip wire to. Writing `null` there is a legitimate decision and an explicit one: it means a permanent tax has been accepted, by you, on somebody's behalf.
>
> **And notice what the inventory has not answered.** The successor is transport, state and identity — not orchestration — and says so in its own documentation. **Every dependency you marked `migrate` moves the plumbing and settles nothing about what the system says**, which is a second migration nobody has scoped and which does not appear on any published list.
>
> **A caution on the exercise.** The unsupported list available to this programme was read through search excerpts rather than fetched in full, so **treat your inventory as incomplete by construction** and record the reading depth beside the date. That is the same admission [S026](/wiki/seminars/S026.md) demands of an instrument's scope, arriving here about a document rather than a scan.

## What this does not establish

The boundary vocabulary in this session comes from a source whose evidence is **named, followed-up personal experience across identified projects** — including two that failed and one where the author's own design was replaced by a successor team, which he reports and approves of. That is unusually honest evidence of its kind and it is still not a study.

**That source makes no quantitative claim of any kind and none should be attributed to it.** It also declares two prerequisites without which it claims nothing at all: that development is iterative, and that engineers have continuing close access to the people who understand the domain. **Where either is absent — and on many integration engagements the second is — this session's discipline is being used outside the conditions its source claims for it.** Say so rather than assume it carries.

The claim that reinventing rather than extending is a common and costly failure is **practitioner judgement from the internal corpus, stated with warning signs and no outcome data.** No engagement in which it happened is described, and the corpus reports no engagement that went badly at all.

**The production-systems material is a survey and says so.** It offers no comparative evidence for any recommended practice, its toolchain is not neutral — most of its authors work at one vendor and both its worked pipelines use that vendor's stack — and every figure in it is a property of a particular system. **No number from it is reproduced here.**

**The platform material is the most perishable content in this session and is first-party throughout.** It offers no adoption data, no failure-rate data, no cost comparison and no evidence that its recommended mappings produce better outcomes; it compares one vendor's options against each other and not against alternatives; several of its comparison tables were truncated in the excerpts this repository read; the pages were read through search results rather than full fetches; and the gateway's integration into the agent platform is preview. **One consulted artefact is internal, is not publicly accessible and cannot be cited externally at all** — where a claim from it is taught here, the public documentation is the source given.

**The published playbook is cited by page and by the date that page was read, never as a whole.** Its pages carry footer dates spread across several years, roughly a fifth of the site is stubs or link lists, it contradicts itself in at least a dozen places, and it contains four unsupported magnitude claims that this repository records as specimens and reproduces nowhere.

**The migration material establishes what is unsupported and nothing about what migrating costs.** The guidance and the unsupported table were read through search-returned excerpts rather than full page fetches on 2026-07-31, so **the list this session works from is what those excerpts returned and is not guaranteed complete**; a team advising a real migration must read the current guides in full rather than this page. **The absence of an end-of-support date, a support policy, a migration deadline and an effort estimate was established by searching for each and not finding it** — no source states that they do not exist, and absence at one reading is not proof of absence in the product. **No adoption data, no failure-rate data, no cost comparison and no evidence that migrating produces a better outcome than not migrating appears anywhere in that material**, and the surrounding claims about the toolchain "streamlining" and "simplifying" work are marketing register with no measurement behind them and carry no weight here. **Names in that family decay unusually fast** — every principal name has changed at least once and one component currently ships under two live package names with two commands — so any inventory built against it must record the date and the version it was built against, and this session assesses the inventory rather than the list.
