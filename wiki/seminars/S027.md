# S027 · Extending What Already Runs

**Module:** [M06](/wiki/modules/M06.md) · **Quarter:** [Q3](/wiki/quarters/Q3.md) · **Complexity class:** 3
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** none
**Whitepaper:** [WP-027](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-027.md)
**Retrieves from:** S008, S021, S026
**Entry state:** either

## The situation

There is already a system. It has been running for years. It works badly, people depend on it, and somebody built it who is still employed here.

Your design has to meet it somewhere.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught.

1. What already runs here that your design will touch?
2. Who maintains it, and what would they say about your plan?
3. Which of its concepts will you adopt, and which will you translate?
4. What does translation cost, **every time, for as long as both systems exist**?
5. Is there a version of this where you do not integrate at all?

**Item 5 is the one nobody writes down.** Integration is expensive and its cost is paid continuously, while its benefit is usually asserted once, in a slide, on the grounds that two things being related in a story means they must be connected in a system.

## What you must produce

A **boundary decision** for every existing system your design meets. Three options are available and all three are real:

- **Adopt its model.** Use its concepts and its vocabulary inside your work, and accept its constraints as yours.
- **Translate at the boundary.** Keep your own model, and build and maintain a layer that converts between the two.
- **Do not integrate.** Accept the duplication or the manual step, and say what that costs.

Each decision carries its cost, its owner, and **the condition under which you would change it.**

Plus a **map** of the systems your design meets and the relationships between them — describing what is actually there, including the organisational and political contours, not the arrangement you would prefer.

## What good looks like

- **Adoption was genuinely considered.** Conforming to somebody else's established model is refused far more often than it should be, and the reason is emotional rather than technical: it feels like a loss of authorship. A model that has survived years of production has knowledge crunched into it and may be better than yours. **The register should show adoption being rejected for a stated reason, not never appearing.**
- **Translation is costed as a standing cost**, not as a one-off build. A translating layer is real engineering and it is maintained for as long as both models exist. Cost the wall before you build it.
- **Non-integration is on the list as a real option**, with what it costs written next to it. Sometimes the right answer is two systems and a person who reconciles them once a week.
- **The map describes reality.** It includes which teams own what, who reports to whom, and where the boundaries follow organisational lines rather than technical ones — because they usually do. **Do not change the map until the change in reality has happened.**
- **The maintainers of the existing system are named** and, where possible, have seen the map. Where that was not possible, the map says so.
- **The vocabulary at each boundary is checked for false cognates.** Two systems using the same word for different things is the failure that survives every review, because the word is familiar on both sides.

## How it goes wrong

- **The greenfield reflex** — the plan replaces the existing system because reasoning about it is unpleasant. **Reinventing rather than building on what the customer already runs is a named failure in the practice material behind this stage, with observable warning signs.** *Warning sign:* nobody has asked the existing system's maintainers anything.
- **The free translator** — the translating layer is drawn as a box and never costed. *Warning sign:* the layer has no owner and no line in anybody's plan.
- **The wall you cannot afford** — the isolation is correct in principle and larger than the thing it protects. The benefit of isolation has to be balanced against its cost, and the cost is usually understated because it is paid later. *Warning sign:* the translation layer has more concepts in it than either model it sits between.
- **The map redrawn before reality** — the map shows the arrangement you have proposed rather than the one that exists, so everyone reasons about a system that is not there. *Warning sign:* somebody argues about the map instead of about the system.
- **The compliance rewrite** — an existing service is changed to conform to your new standard, purely for the sake of compliance, with no other benefit. **The published playbook this stage draws its artefact discipline from explicitly declines to require this of its own older services.** Page read 2026-07-30. *Warning sign:* the justification for the change is the standard itself.
- **The absent maintainer** — the boundary is designed without anyone who has operated the existing system in the room. *Warning sign:* your design assumes a behaviour nobody has confirmed.

## Do it

Draw the map as it is. Put the team names on it. Mark which boundaries exist because of technology and which exist because of who reports to whom.

Then take the largest boundary and write the translation you would have to maintain: the concepts on each side, what converts to what, what has no equivalent, and **what happens to a case that does not fit.** The cases that do not fit are the ones that will consume the maintenance.

Then write the date. **A translating layer with no end condition is a permanent tax**, and the decision to accept a permanent tax is a different decision from the one to build a temporary bridge. Say which one you are taking.

## Show it

Show the map to somebody who works on the existing system. Ask them one question: **what is wrong with this?**

If you cannot reach such a person, write that on the map itself, in the place where their confirmation would have gone. **An unconfirmed map is usable. An unconfirmed map that looks confirmed is not.**

## What this does not establish

The boundary vocabulary in this session comes from a source whose evidence is **named, followed-up personal experience across identified projects** — including two that failed and one where the author's own design was replaced by a successor team, which he reports and approves of. That is unusually honest evidence of its kind and it is still not a study.

**That source makes no quantitative claim of any kind and none should be attributed to it.** It also declares two prerequisites without which it claims nothing at all: that development is iterative, and that engineers have continuing close access to the people who understand the domain. **Where either is absent — and on many integration engagements the second is — this session's discipline is being used outside the conditions its source claims for it.** Say so rather than assume it carries.

The claim that reinventing rather than extending is a common and costly failure is **practitioner judgement from the internal corpus, stated with warning signs and no outcome data.** No engagement in which it happened is described, and the corpus reports no engagement that went badly at all.
