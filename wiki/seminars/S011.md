# S011 · What a Workshop Is For, and What It Is Not

**Module:** [M03](/wiki/modules/M03.md) · **Quarter:** [Q2](/wiki/quarters/Q2.md) · **Complexity class:** 2
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** none
**Whitepaper:** [WP-011](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-011.md)
**Retrieves from:** S003, S007
**Entry state:** either

## The situation

The engagement was qualified. Somebody has now booked two days, a room, a projector and eleven people from the customer's organisation, and has asked you to "run a discovery workshop".

Nobody has said what the two days are supposed to produce.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught. You will be held to your answers at [S015](/wiki/seminars/S015.md).

1. The workshop ends. Everyone thanks you warmly and says it was the best session they have had this year. Nothing else happens. **Did it work?**
2. Name the single decision this workshop exists to make. Who in the room has the authority to make it?
3. What observable thing, present at the end of the second day, would tell you the workshop had failed?
4. Somebody proposes holding it at your offices because the rooms are better. What information do you need before you can agree?
5. If the answer to item 1 is *no*, what were the other people in the room being asked to give up?

**Most people answer item 1 with a qualified yes.** They will say it built relationships, or created alignment, or opened the door. Hold that answer; the session is going to argue against it, and the argument is not obvious.

## What you must produce

A one-page **workshop charter**. It carries four things and nothing else:

- **The decision** the workshop exists to produce, stated as something that will be either taken or not taken.
- **The people** whose presence is necessary for that decision, named by the authority they hold rather than by seniority.
- **The failure condition** — the observable state, at the end, that would mean this did not work.
- **The venue consequence** — where the session must be held, derived from who must be present, derived from where the difficulty actually lives.

## What good looks like

- The decision is one the customer takes, **not one you take.** "We will agree the architecture" is your decision. "They will commit named engineers to a pilot on their own data" is theirs.
- The failure condition is something you could actually observe on the day, without waiting a quarter to find out.
- **The failure condition can fire.** If your charter's only failure state is total collapse, you have written a decoration, and [S001](/wiki/seminars/S001.md) already made this mistake once.
- The necessary-people list has somebody on it who is inconvenient to invite. If everyone on it was going to be there anyway, the list is a description rather than a requirement.
- The venue argument runs in the right direction: the difficulty determines who must be in the room, and who must be in the room determines where the room is. **Not the other way round.**

## How it goes wrong

- **The demonstration** — the session degrades into a walkthrough of what your tooling can do. The customer enjoys it and learns nothing about themselves. *Warning sign:* the customer asks for a tool overview, and you agree because it seems like a reasonable warm-up.
- **The alignment claim** — the stated output is agreement, shared understanding or a common language. None of these can be observed at the end of a session and all of them can be asserted afterwards. *Warning sign:* your charter's success condition uses a word that has no failure state.
- **The room of watchers** — attendance is decided by seniority and interest rather than by authority and hands. *Warning sign:* the invitation list grew and nobody was removed from it.
- **The prepared agenda** — the structure was settled before anybody understood the customer's situation, so the session can only produce answers the structure already allows. *Warning sign:* you could run the same two days for a different customer without changing a slide.
- **The convenient venue** — the location was chosen on cost or comfort, and the people who could have answered the hard question are the ones who could not travel. *Warning sign:* the venue was decided before the difficulty was named.

## Do it

Work from the supplied engagement brief in [Q2](/wiki/quarters/Q2.md). Write the charter.

Then take the contradiction the practice corpus leaves open and resolve it for your own charter. **The same organisation states, as a prohibition, that design work must be interleaved into the engineering cadence and never run as a separate workshop — and its case studies' proudest achievement is a compressed multi-day workshop.** Nothing in the corpus reconciles them.

You must take one of two positions in a paragraph attached to your charter:

- **The workshop is a concentrated instrument**, justified only when the interleaved cadence has failed to establish something specific. Then name that thing. If you cannot name it, you have argued yourself out of the workshop.
- **The prohibition is right**, and the appropriate response to this brief is a sequence of shorter engagements inside the customer's existing cadence. Then say what you lose by not having everybody in one room, and why that loss is acceptable.

**A charter that does not take a position on this has not noticed the problem.**

## Show it

Give your charter to one other person with a single instruction: **strike out every line that could be true after a session that produced nothing.**

Most charters lose most of their lines. What remains is the charter you actually wrote.

## What this does not establish

Nothing here shows that workshops with charters produce better outcomes than workshops without them.

The corpus this session draws on **reports no workshop that failed.** Every case study succeeds; the single failure narrative in the whole collection is a synthetic composite and is fictional by the material's own admission. There is no post-engagement record, no sustainment data and no comparison against any other way of working. The claim that thanks-without-commitment is failure is a **position the corpus takes and does not evidence**, and this session teaches it as a position.

**The venue ordering claim has the same status.** It is a practitioner judgement, stated once, and it is repeated here because it is non-obvious and cheap to test, not because anybody has tested it.

## Perishable content in this session

This stage has no platform anchor, so the second column records organisational and tooling instances rather than a version-bound service.

| Durable claim | Perishable instance |
|---|---|
| An instrument needs a stated output before it needs an agenda | The particular antipattern list the corpus currently publishes, which is a stratum still being revised |
| A success condition with no failure state is not a condition | Present conventions about session length, hybrid attendance and remote participation |
| The difficulty determines the people, and the people determine the venue | The cost and friction of travel that make the wrong ordering tempting |
| A polished prepared agenda suppresses the customer's own account | The near-zero cost of producing polished material, which is a property of current tooling |
