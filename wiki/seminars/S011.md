# S011 · What a Workshop Is For, and What It Is Not

**Module:** [M03](/wiki/modules/M03.md) · **Quarter:** [Q2](/wiki/quarters/Q2.md) · **Complexity class:** 2
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** Copilot Studio topic and trigger design — an elicitation protocol made explicit, where the ordering of questions is a designed artefact rather than a habit
**Whitepaper:** [WP-011](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-011.md)
**Retrieves from:** S003, S007
**Entry state:** either

## The situation

The engagement was qualified. Somebody has now booked two days, a room, a projector and eleven people from the customer's organisation, and has asked you to "run a discovery workshop".

Nobody has said what the two days are supposed to produce.

You have been sent an agenda. It is good. It has timings, a break, a lab, a wrap-up. It was written before anybody in your organisation had spoken to anybody in theirs, and it could be run at a different customer next month with one logo changed.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught. You will be held to your answers at [S015](/wiki/seminars/S015.md).

1. The workshop ends. Everyone thanks you warmly and says it was the best session they have had this year. Nothing else happens. **Did it work?**
2. Name the single decision this workshop exists to make. Who in the room has the authority to make it?
3. What observable thing, present at the end of the second day, would tell you the workshop had failed?
4. Somebody proposes holding it at your offices because the rooms are better. What information do you need before you can agree?
5. If the answer to item 1 is *no*, what were the other people in the room being asked to give up?

**Item 5 is the one this session is really about.** Items 1 to 4 can be answered from good instincts. Item 5 requires you to have a theory of what a workshop extracts from the people attending it, and most learners discover while writing it that they do not have one — which is why the warm thanks felt like enough.

**Most people answer item 1 with a qualified yes.** They will say it built relationships, or created alignment, or opened the door. Hold that answer; the session is going to argue against it, and the argument is not obvious.

## What you must produce

Two artefacts.

**A one-page workshop charter**, carrying four things and nothing else:

1. **The decision** the workshop exists to produce, stated as something that will be either taken or not taken.
2. **The people** whose presence is necessary for that decision, named by the authority they hold rather than by seniority.
3. **The failure condition** — the observable state, at the end, that would mean this did not work.
4. **The venue consequence** — where the session must be held, derived from who must be present, derived from where the difficulty actually lives.

**A working elicitation topic**, built on the assigned platform, in which:

5. The ordering of your opening questions is written down as configuration rather than carried as a habit.
6. The trigger — the condition under which this line of questioning is the right one — is stated explicitly and is separable from the questions themselves.
7. One question you would have asked has been removed, and the reason for removing it is recorded.

## The material

### An instrument is defined by its output, not by its procedure

A workshop is a machine. It consumes two days of eleven people's attention and produces something. The question this session is built on is what.

The convention in delivery organisations is to answer it with the procedure: a discovery workshop is two days of structured discovery activities. That answer is circular and it is load-bearing, because a machine described by its procedure cannot be observed to have failed. It ran. Everybody attended. The activities were completed.

The practice corpus behind this stage is unusually good on this point and states the position more sharply than most published material does. **A session that ends in thanks and good feelings with no committed next step has failed, and awareness and enthusiasm are named as unacceptable outcomes** ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md)). That is a commercially uncomfortable thing for a delivery organisation to write down, which is a point in its favour.

**Its limits, and they bound everything drawn from it in this module.** It is access-controlled internal material held here only as a white-labelled paraphrase; it reports no failed workshop anywhere, its only failure narrative is a synthetic composite that is fictional by its own admission, and it contains no post-engagement or sustainment record for any case. **A corpus in which nothing ever fails cannot establish that anything in it works.** It is being used here as a position worth holding, not as a finding.

### The output with no failure state

The customary output of a discovery workshop is alignment, shared understanding or a common language. Test each against one question: what would you observe, at the end of the second day, if it had not happened?

There is no answer. Every candidate observation — the room disagreed, somebody looked confused, two people used a term differently — is compatible with alignment having occurred and is also compatible with it not having occurred. **A condition that cannot be found unmet is not a condition; it is a description with a success mood attached.**

This matters more than it looks, because the alignment claim is not a lie. Alignment is real, it is valuable, and organisations genuinely need it. The complaint is narrower and it is the one to carry: **alignment cannot serve as a specification, because a specification is a thing somebody else can check.**

### Commitment is identified by what somebody gave up

If the output is not alignment, what is it? The most usable answer available comes from the customer-conversation literature, which distinguishes **commitment** — a stakeholder gives up something they value — from **advancement**, in which the relationship moves to a defined next stage ([research/05-fde-craft/fitzpatrick-the-mom-test.md](/research/05-fde-craft/fitzpatrick-the-mom-test.md)). The currencies named are meaningful time, reputational risk and money.

That gives you a test with teeth. A scheduled session with a purpose, sustained trial use, an introduction to a decision maker, a named engineer's time, representative data, a public case study — each costs the giver something. "We'll definitely look at getting some time booked" costs nothing, and the source names the pattern precisely: a friendly contact who repeatedly meets and praises without committing or rejecting is a **zombie lead**, and the process failure is leaving with neither advancement nor rejection. **A clear no is a result. A warm nothing is not.**

The source's limits: a founder-oriented practitioner handbook with no controlled comparison, no sampling framework and no measured effect. Its rules are memorable heuristics, its conversation counts are anecdotal, and it warns itself that a stakeholder without authority may be unable to commit despite a genuine need. **Take the distinction; leave the numbers, of which it has none worth taking anyway.**

### The decision is one the customer takes

The charter's first line is where most of the work happens, and it fails in a predictable way: the learner writes a decision they themselves will take.

"We will agree the target architecture" is your decision. "They will commit two named engineers to a pilot on their own data, for six weeks, starting before the end of the quarter" is theirs. The first can be achieved by you writing a document. The second cannot be achieved by you at all, which is exactly why it is worth writing down.

This is the same move the design-research literature makes when it insists that project scope begins with the organisation's actual tolerance for change, and that the focus is expressed as people doing activities in contexts rather than as a technology or a product component ([research/05-fde-craft/beyer-holtzblatt-contextual-design.md](/research/05-fde-craft/beyer-holtzblatt-contextual-design.md)). Its formulation of the constraint is the one to memorise: **the data collected bounds the design conclusions available later.** A workshop that gathers nothing costly from the customer has bounded its own conclusions at zero.

**Its limits.** A method text grounded in the authors' long professional practice and accumulated project examples. It offers procedural rationale and cases, **not a controlled comparison demonstrating that the full method outperforms alternatives**, and its timings, sample sizes and sequencing advice are practitioner heuristics. Its own caution is explicit: do not convert its examples or prescriptions into effect-size claims.

### The difficulty determines the people, and the people determine the venue

The corpus supplies one non-obvious ordering claim and it is worth teaching precisely because it is cheap to test and nobody has tested it: the pain point determines which engineers must be present, and that determines where the workshop is held.

Run it the other way and you get the ordinary outcome. The venue is chosen on cost and comfort, and the people who could have answered the hard question are the ones who could not travel. **The venue decision is taken first in almost every organisation, and it silently decides the attendance list.**

The same source names four antipatterns with warning signs that are observable *in advance*, which is what makes them usable: the engagement degrading into a product demonstration; under-preparation producing generic content; insufficient technical depth so the method is dismissed as theory; and reinventing rather than building on what the customer already runs. The advance signs include a customer asking for a tool overview, a room of managers with few hands-on engineers, no sandbox requested, an agenda finalised before the customer is understood, and labs running on sample data rather than the customer's own scenarios. **One of those antipatterns is contradicted by the corpus's own workshop recipe, which hands teams a sample product idea and a prepared starter repository.**

### The contradiction the corpus does not notice

Here is the thing this session hands you unresolved, because it is unresolved.

The same organisation states, as a repeated prohibition, that design activity must be interleaved into the engineering cadence and never run as a separate workshop. Its case studies' proudest achievement is a compressed multi-day workshop. **Nothing in the corpus reconciles them, and as far as this repository can tell nobody has noticed.**

You do not get to leave that alone, because whoever books your room will decide it for you if you do.

### The ordering of questions is a designed artefact

Now the part that transfers to the platform, and it is the reason this session has an anchor at all.

An elicitation protocol is a sequence of questions with a trigger. In a room, the trigger is your judgement and the sequence is your habit, and neither is inspectable — including by you. On the assigned platform, both become configuration. A topic is a named unit of conversation with declared **trigger phrases** and an explicit ordered body; the platform's own comparison material describes low-code authoring through a drag-and-drop interface "without coding", with "hundreds of prebuilt connectors" ([research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md)).

The durable idea underneath is the one to keep: **separating the trigger from the body forces you to say when this line of questioning is the right one**, which is the charter question at the scale of a single conversation. The perishable part is everything about the product.

The same documentation is unusually candid about that product's envelope, and the sentence to hold is that this tool is "designed for small-scale deployments, such as department-level solutions". It also states plainly a "limited ability to fine-tune API calls, actions, and response formatting compared with a pro-code environment", that Adaptive Cards are "limited to schema 1.6 and earlier", and that feature availability is slower. **A tool used outside its design envelope fails through governance and lifecycle rather than through function**, which is the failure shape nobody notices until it is expensive.

**The documentation's limits.** First-party vendor selection guidance, read through search-returned excerpts rather than full page fetches, comparing Microsoft options only against each other. **It offers no adoption data, no failure-rate data and no evidence that its recommended mappings produce better outcomes**, and product names in this family are actively in flux. Teach the axes; discard the products.

## The workflow

**Build your charter's opening as an inspectable elicitation protocol, then use the agent to attack it.**

You will use Copilot Studio's topic authoring surface. Steps 1 to 4 require only the maker portal; step 6 requires a publish to the test canvas. If no tenant is available, steps 2, 3 and 6 can be done against the exported YAML alone, and step 6 is the one this session most wants you to have done.

**Step 1 — write the charter first, on paper, before you open anything.** Four lines. If the charter takes more than one page you will not consult it under pressure, and [S015](/wiki/seminars/S015.md) will find that out.

**Step 2 — create one topic per charter line, not one topic for the workshop.** Name them after what they establish: `establish-decision-owner`, `establish-current-workaround`, `establish-cost-of-doing-nothing`. A topic that cannot be named after a thing it establishes is an agenda item wearing a topic's clothes.

**Step 3 — write the trigger before the body.** Trigger phrases declare the condition under which this line of questioning is correct. Writing them first is the discipline; it is the charter question one level down.

**Step 4 — order the questions deliberately and record the ordering rule.** Breadth before depth: establish that the problem category is active and consequential before you investigate anything inside it. Then export the topic and read the ordering as text.

```yaml
# establish-current-workaround.yaml — illustrative only.
# Copilot Studio's authoring schema changes; verify shape against current
# documentation before reuse. Adaptive Cards here are constrained to
# schema 1.6 and earlier, which is a documented limit of this product.
kind: AdaptiveDialog
beginDialog:
  kind: OnRecognizedIntent
  intent:
    triggerQueries:
      - How do you handle it today
      - What happens now when that fails
      - Walk me through the last time this came up
  actions:
    # BREADTH FIRST. Do not move to detail until the category is established.
    - kind: Question
      id: last_occurrence
      prompt: When did this last happen? Take me to that specific day.
      variable: Topic.LastOccurrence
      entity: StringPrebuiltEntity
    - kind: Question
      id: what_was_done
      prompt: What did you actually do about it?
      variable: Topic.Workaround
      entity: StringPrebuiltEntity
    - kind: Question
      id: who_paid
      prompt: Whose time did that take, and how long?
      variable: Topic.Cost
      entity: StringPrebuiltEntity
    # REMOVED, deliberately, and the removal is the artefact:
    #   "Would a tool that did X help you?"
    #   It asks the customer to endorse the solution the asker sells.
    #   Recorded here rather than deleted, so the decision stays visible.
```

**Step 5 — remove one question and record why.** Every question that invites the customer to endorse your solution category comes out. So does every question that ships with example answers attached, because appended examples narrow the response before it is given.

**Step 6 — attack your own protocol with the agent.** In the test canvas, play the customer badly on purpose: answer every question with a compliment, then with a generic future promise, then with a feature request. Then give the agent your charter and your topic export and instruct it to produce the strongest case that **this workshop should not be run at all**, and to name the single observation that would settle it. Record what you kept and what changed.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That your question order is a decision. Exported as text, an ordering you have used for years becomes a thing with alternatives, and the questions that only work because you were in the room stop working. It also makes the endorsement question visible: **written down, next to a trigger, a question that asks the customer to agree your product would help is obviously what it is**, and spoken aloud in a warm room it is not.

## What good looks like

- The decision is one the customer takes, **not one you take.**
- The failure condition is observable on the day, without waiting a quarter to find out.
- **The failure condition can fire.** If your charter's only failure state is total collapse, you have written a decoration, and [S001](/wiki/seminars/S001.md) already made this mistake once.
- The necessary-people list has somebody on it who is inconvenient to invite. If everyone on it was going to be there anyway, the list is a description rather than a requirement.
- The venue argument runs in the right direction: difficulty determines people, people determine venue. **Not the other way round.**
- Every topic is named after something it establishes, and its trigger is written separately from its body.
- The removed question is recorded with its reason, and the reason is about the question's form rather than about time.

## How it goes wrong

- **The demonstration.** *Warning sign:* the customer asks for a tool overview, and you agree because it seems like a reasonable warm-up. The session degrades into a walkthrough of what your tooling can do; the customer enjoys it and learns nothing about themselves.
- **The alignment claim.** *Warning sign:* your charter's success condition uses a word that has no failure state. The stated output is agreement, shared understanding or a common language — none observable at the end, all assertable afterwards.
- **The room of watchers.** *Warning sign:* the invitation list grew and nobody was removed from it. Attendance is decided by seniority and interest rather than by authority and hands.
- **The prepared agenda.** *Warning sign:* you could run the same two days for a different customer without changing a slide. The structure was settled before anybody understood the situation, so the session can only produce answers the structure already allows.
- **The convenient venue.** *Warning sign:* the venue was decided before the difficulty was named. Cost and comfort chose the location, and the people who could have answered the hard question could not travel.
- **The endorsement question.** *Warning sign:* a question in your protocol contains the name of a product category you sell. You have asked the customer to validate your solution and will record their politeness as evidence.
- **The polished protocol.** *Warning sign:* your topic is elaborate and you have not run it against a hostile respondent. Detail added early drags the discussion onto irrelevancies, and polish suppresses honest feedback.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed here.** All of them are assessed indirectly at [S015](/wiki/seminars/S015.md), where the charter is one of five submitted artefacts.

**1 · Strike out the charter.** Give your charter to one other person with a single instruction: strike out every line that could be true after a session that produced nothing.

> **What you should find.** Most charters lose most of their lines. What remains is the charter you actually wrote. **A line that survives a session which produced nothing was never carrying information**, and the commonest survivor is the stated output.

**2 · Take a position on the contradiction.** The corpus prohibits running design activity as a separate workshop and celebrates a compressed multi-day workshop. Write one paragraph taking one of two positions, and attach it to your charter.

> **Model answers.** Either: *the workshop is a concentrated instrument, justified only when the interleaved cadence has failed to establish something specific* — then name that thing; if you cannot name it, you have argued yourself out of the workshop. Or: *the prohibition is right, and the correct response to this brief is a sequence of shorter engagements inside the customer's existing cadence* — then say what you lose by not having everybody in one room, and why that loss is acceptable.
>
> **A paragraph that acknowledges both readings and commits to neither has not noticed the problem.** The requirement to name what is lost by the position you did not take is what makes the fence uninhabitable.

**3 · Sort eight closing sentences.** For each, say whether it is a commitment, an advancement, or neither, and name the currency spent.

- *"This has been really valuable, thank you."*
- *"Let's get something in the diary for the new year."*
- *"I'll ask Priya to spend Thursday with you on the ingest path."*
- *"Send me the deck and I'll circulate it."*
- *"We can give you a subset of the claims data under the existing NDA."*
- *"I'll raise it at the architecture board on the eighth — you should come."*
- *"We're not going to do this. The compliance exposure is too high."*
- *"Everyone's excited."*

> **Model answers.** Commitments: Priya's Thursday (time, and somebody else's), the claims subset (reputational risk, and real work by a data owner), the architecture board slot (the sponsor's own standing). Advancement without commitment: circulating the deck, at best. Neither: thanks, excitement, and the diary sentence, which has no person, no date and no cost. **The refusal is the second most valuable sentence in the list**, because it ends a zombie lead and tells you something true. A learner who marks it as a failure has marked the customer.

**4 · Go back to your own item 5.** Find what you wrote to opening item 5 about what the room was being asked to give up. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then rewrite it.

> **Criterion.** A passing answer names a specific currency — a named person's time, a named dataset, a public association with the work, a budget line — held by a named party who had the authority to spend it, and says what it would have cost them. "Their engagement" and "their buy-in" fail: neither is anybody's to give.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: presenting a correct answer beside a learner's wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group **instructed to compare them** learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference is. **That source's limits: school, university and laboratory evidence, with no adult-professional study in its reference list, and its own author concedes that the design fails when badly implemented.**

**5 · Run the topic against a hostile respondent.** Using the platform, drive your own elicitation topic while answering only in compliments, generic promises and feature requests. Record which of your questions still produced a fact.

> **What to notice.** The questions that survive are the ones anchored to a specific past event. The ones that collapse are the ones asking what the respondent thinks, wants or would use. **You are watching the difference between a question about the world and a question about an opinion**, and you are watching it in a transcript you can re-read, which the room does not give you.

**6 · Name the envelope.** Closed book: what does the platform documentation say about the scale this tool is designed for, and what follows for a charter that names an enterprise-wide decision?

> **Answer.** It positions the product for small-scale, department-level deployments. What follows is that a protocol built here is a prototype of an elicitation design, not the delivery vehicle for an enterprise programme — and that a tool used outside its envelope fails through governance and lifecycle rather than through function, so the symptoms arrive late and look like process problems rather than tooling ones.

## What this does not establish

Nothing here shows that workshops with charters produce better outcomes than workshops without them.

The corpus this session draws on **reports no workshop that failed.** Every case study succeeds; the single failure narrative in the whole collection is a synthetic composite and is fictional by the material's own admission. There is no post-engagement record, no sustainment data and no comparison against any other way of working. The claim that thanks-without-commitment is failure is a **position the corpus takes and does not evidence**, and this session teaches it as a position. It is also access-controlled, so no reader outside this repository can check the paraphrase against its source.

**The venue ordering claim has the same status.** It is a practitioner judgement, stated once, and it is repeated here because it is non-obvious and cheap to test, not because anybody has tested it.

**The commitment-and-advancement distinction is class 2.** It comes from a practitioner handbook with no controlled comparison, no sampling framework and no measured effect; its author's own conversation counts are anecdotal and its rules are heuristics. It is taught here because it converts an unobservable outcome into an observable one, not because anybody has shown that teams using it do better.

**The design-research material establishes mechanism, not outcome.** Its own text states that it offers procedural rationale and cases rather than a controlled comparison demonstrating that the method outperforms alternatives, and instructs readers not to convert its examples into effect-size claims. None is converted here.

**The platform documentation describes what the product does and supplies no evidence that doing it produces better results.** It compares Microsoft options only with each other; it was read through search excerpts rather than full page fetches, so page structure is reconstructed; it publishes no adoption, cost or failure-rate data; and its product names are actively in flux. **The scale positioning quoted in this session is a design statement by the vendor, not a measured limit**, and a limitation recorded today may be closed tomorrow.

**No effect size is asserted anywhere in this session**, including for the learning mechanism behind the opening questions. Direction and mechanism only.
