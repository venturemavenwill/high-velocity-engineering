# S009 · The Cost of Doing Nothing

**Module:** [M02](/wiki/modules/M02.md) · **Quarter:** [Q1](/wiki/quarters/Q1.md) · **Complexity class:** 2
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** Azure API Management AI gateway and provisioned throughput — sizing token cost and capacity commitment for the do-nothing case as well as the do-something case
**Whitepaper:** [WP-009](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-009.md)
**Retrieves from:** S005, S008
**Entry state:** either

## The situation

Everything in this stage so far has made it easier to decline. This session makes it expensive.

If the work does not happen, something continues. Somebody keeps paying for it, usually in time and usually without anyone recording that they are. Until that cost is on the page next to the cost of proceeding, a refusal is a preference.

There is a second cost, and the programme has been quiet about it until now. **Refusing is not free to the person who refuses.** The benefit of speaking up accrues to the organisation, later, with low certainty. The cost falls on you, immediately, with high certainty. A programme that teaches refusal as a skill and never sizes that asymmetry has handed you a capability and concealed its price.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught.

1. If this work does not happen, what specifically continues to happen? To whom, and how often?
2. Who is currently paying that cost, and do they know they are?
3. What is the baseline, and who measured it?
4. If the engagement succeeds, who will establish that the change was caused by it?
5. What does declining cost you — not the customer, you — and what would make that cost survivable?

**Item 4 has no answer in almost any engagement, and it is not a hard question.** It is simply one that nobody has been assigned.

**Item 5 is the one this session is really about**, and it is the one the programme owes you. [WP-001](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-001.md) conceded that this programme teaches refusal as an individual skill when the research says speaking up is conditional on the environment. **That concession is discharged here and at [S010](/wiki/seminars/S010.md), by pricing the refusal rather than by promising you it will go well.**

## What you must produce

A **do-nothing statement**. Six parts:

1. **What continues** — an event, with a frequency and a bearer.
2. **Who pays it now**, and whether they know they are.
3. **The baseline**, with the instrument that produced it — or an explicit declaration that there is none, with the person who would have to produce one and what that would cost.
4. **The named person or function who would establish that any observed change was caused by the work.** If nobody can be named, write "nobody" and treat that as the finding it is.
5. **The cost of proceeding**, sized in the same units as everything else, including what is paid when nothing is happening.
6. **The cost of the refusal** — to the customer, and separately to you — with what would make yours survivable.

## The material

### The alternative is an option, and options have prices

A refusal is a choice between two futures, and only one of them normally gets costed.

The negotiation literature has a precise instrument for this and it is the correct one to borrow. Fisher and Ury's **BATNA** — the best alternative to a negotiated agreement — is the course of action actually available if no agreement is reached, and it is the standard against which any proposed package is accepted or rejected ([research/05-fde-craft/fisher-ury-getting-to-yes.md](/research/05-fde-craft/fisher-ury-getting-to-yes.md)). Their sharpest point is that **an unknown alternative is dangerous in both directions**: a party who has not developed one may accept less than it was worth, or reject more.

Two further devices from the same source belong in this session. A **trip wire** is a deliberately formulated early-warning threshold, worse than the hoped-for result and better than the alternative, which triggers a pause before drift becomes commitment — it is explicitly not a rigid bottom line, and its function is to create time to reassess. And a **micro-BATNA** is what you do if *this particular meeting* ends without agreement, prepared in advance, with an exit line written.

**The source's limits are recorded and are not small.** It is a practitioner framework from the Harvard Negotiation Project, developed through analytic distinction and worked cases rather than controlled comparison. Its historical cases illustrate possibility and mechanism and do not isolate causes. **It reports no evaluation of the complete method, and no effectiveness claim for any teaching use of it is warranted by it.**

### The cost side is engineered and the return side is elicited

Here is the structural asymmetry that makes delivery proposals feel more rigorous than they are.

Consumption is forecast by engineers, instrumented by engineers, and refined against telemetry from their own system. Return is chosen by the customer from a menu, measured by the customer using instrumentation they may not possess, over a period nobody specifies, with causation assumed. **A ratio whose denominator is well instrumented and whose numerator is not is not a well-instrumented ratio, and it will feel like one** because both arrive in the same document with the same typography.

The value framework recorded at [research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md) is this repository's worked specimen. It instructs the reader to establish a baseline and never exhibits one being established. It catalogues metric definitions and never reports one measured. Its case-study section is reserved and empty. Several of its metric definitions define themselves as the change attributable to the system, which presupposes exactly the thing that needed establishing. And no party anywhere in it is assigned responsibility for establishing causation.

**A framework that generates numbers while asserting none is harder to check than one that over-claims**, because there is no proposition to check the numbers against. That diagnosis rests on a complete reading and is the strongest thing this session stands on.

### An instrument that could not have detected the difference has not told you there was none

The measurement literature supplies the general form, and it is worth holding because it applies to your baseline as much as to anybody's benchmark.

Miller's argument is that an evaluation is an experiment, and that the decisive quantity for reading a null result is the **minimum detectable effect** — the smallest true difference an instrument of that size and design could reliably register ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md)). His practical consequence is exactly the sentence a customer needs before committing: **"we measured and saw no difference" is not a finding until you can say what you could have seen.** He also shows, in a worked comparison carried through his whole paper, that a correct analysis can reverse the apparent winner rather than merely widening its interval.

Transfer that to a do-nothing statement. If nobody has counted how often records come back for rework, then after the engagement nobody will be able to say whether it changed. **The absence of a baseline is not a gap in your paperwork. It is a decision, already taken, that the outcome will be unknowable** — and it is usually taken by nobody, silently, before you arrive.

**The source's limits.** It is analytical rather than empirical; most of its illustrative magnitudes rest on stylised assumptions; it addresses comparing scores rather than choosing what to score; and its machinery assumes questions drawn from a well-defined population, which a hand-picked set of examples is not. **No number from it appears here.**

### Capacity held whether or not it is used

The platform half of this session exists because the do-something side has a cost structure that most engineers meet for the first time in production, and it is unusually legible in documentation.

In a standard deployment, inference capacity is shared and throughput varies with demand. In a provisioned deployment, the platform "holds a fixed amount of processing capacity exclusively for your deployment's use, whether or not requests are being made" ([research/06-microsoft-platform/provisioned-throughput-for-foundry-models.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/provisioned-throughput-for-foundry-models.md)). Everything follows from that clause. **Capacity held whether or not it is used must be paid for whether or not it is used**, which is why the billing unit changes from tokens to time, and why the decision becomes a capacity-planning commitment rather than a configuration setting.

The single most transferable idea in that material is the distinction between **quota** and **capacity**. Quota is a policy limit enforced by the platform, scoped per subscription, per region and per deployment type, and it has no cost. Capacity is the amount actually available to deploy right now. The documentation states the consequence in a call-out: having quota does not guarantee that capacity is available, and a deployment fails if regional capacity is insufficient. Capacity also fluctuates through the day with demand across all customers, and releasing it offers no guarantee of getting it back. **Permission is not availability**, and that is a general lesson about shared infrastructure that this instance makes concrete.

Sizing is a three-input calculation and its structure is the lesson rather than its arithmetic: the expected shape of a request, a ratio expressing that output tokens consume more capacity than input tokens, and a cache rate, since cached input tokens consume no capacity at all. **It is a capacity model, not a pricing model** — it converts a description of traffic into a quantity of machine, and every simplification in the traffic description propagates into the answer.

Two further facts belong in a cost statement. The documentation explicitly advises against scaling provisioned deployments up and down with traffic, for two stated reasons: capacity might not be available when you need to scale back up, and continuous hourly billing at high utilisation typically exceeds reservation pricing. And a reservation is a discount applied to a billing meter rather than to a deployment — **reservations do not guarantee capacity, and the correct sequence is deploy, verify availability, then commit.** The intuitive sequence, commit then deploy, is exactly wrong.

**Its limits.** First-party documentation, definitional rather than empirical. The concrete ratios, minimum deployment sizes, prices and default allocations live on companion pages that this repository did not fetch, and none is asserted here. Names, defaults and portal paths change.

### Fairness under contention, and why it only appears with the second consumer

The gateway material supplies the other half of the cost picture, and its central argument is a general one dressed as a product caveat ([research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md)).

Tokens are the scarce resource, providers assign quota as tokens per minute, and that must be distributed across consumers — applications, teams, departments. With one application, a limit set directly on the deployment suffices. As the portfolio grows, the documentation's framing is that you need to make sure one application does not consume the whole quota and block others. **That is a noisy-neighbour problem, and it generalises: a shared finite resource with no per-consumer accounting will be consumed by whichever consumer is least careful.**

The mechanism is a token-limit policy enforcing a rate or a longer-period quota, keyed on any counter — a subscription, an address, or an arbitrary expression — with prompt tokens estimated at the gateway so that a request already over the limit never reaches the backend. **Reject at the edge rather than paying to discover the rejection downstream.**

Two more mechanisms matter for a cost statement. Emitting token metrics with custom dimensions is what makes spend attributable: total consumption tells you the bill, and consumption per API per consumer tells you whom to talk to — **and attribution is what makes governance possible.** And priority-based load balancing exists specifically to saturate paid-for dedicated capacity before overflowing to shared capacity, which is the direct operational consequence of having bought capacity you are paying for at rest.

**The gateway documentation's limits.** It argues for a gateway and does not discuss the latency, cost or operational burden the gateway itself adds. Capability availability varies by service tier. Several capabilities are preview. **It publishes no performance data and no cost-saving measurement of any kind**; the benefits it describes are plausible mechanisms rather than measured outcomes. It is also silent on the privacy consequences of logging prompts and completions, which it recommends for billing and auditing.

### The cost of the refusal, which is not the customer's

Now the part this module owes you.

Edmondson's account of silence is that withholding is a quick risk calculation with an asymmetric payoff structure: voice benefits the organisation later and with low certainty, while silence benefits the individual immediately and with high certainty ([research/04-professional-formation/edmondson-psychological-safety.md](/research/04-professional-formation/edmondson-psychological-safety.md)). Her observation that "no one was ever fired for silence" is the compressed form of it.

**Read that as a costing instruction rather than as encouragement.** If you are going to decline, the price is yours and it arrives first. A do-nothing statement that prices the customer's alternative and says nothing about your own has priced one side of a two-sided decision, and it is the side you do not pay.

This is where the refusal gets a BATNA of its own. What is your alternative if you decline and the engagement proceeds without you? What is it if you decline and are overruled? What would make the cost survivable — a written record, a named ally, a trip wire agreed in advance with somebody senior? **Those are not comforts; they are the assets that decide whether the refusal at [S010](/wiki/seminars/S010.md) is available to you at all.**

**The source's limits are substantial and this session does not lean on it for anything load-bearing.** The book read here is a trade account by the construct's originator; its literature chapter is a narrative review with no search protocol and no reported disconfirming finding; its cases are secondary and selected after the outcomes were known; and no trial of its framework is reported. The mechanism is used; no magnitude is taken.

**And the honest closing note: refusal is not the sophisticated default.** A learner who leaves this module believing that declining is the mature answer has misread it. The module makes refusal expensive precisely so that it becomes a judgement rather than a posture, and a refusal that costs the customer more than the work would have cost them is a bad refusal, however well delivered.

## The workflow

**Size both futures in the same units, including the one where you say no.**

**Step 1 — write the do-nothing column before anything else.** An event, a frequency, a bearer. If you cannot supply a frequency, write down who could and what it would cost to ask them. **A column containing the word "nothing" is almost never true** and is the first thing your reviewer will test.

**Step 2 — size the do-something case structurally, not numerically.** Take the requirement from [S008](/wiki/seminars/S008.md) and walk the three sizing inputs: the shape of a typical request, the fact that output tokens consume more capacity than input tokens, and the fraction of input that would be served from cache and therefore consume no capacity at all. Write down which of those three you are guessing. **Every simplification in your traffic description propagates into the capacity answer, and the guesses are where the estimate actually lives.**

**Step 3 — separate permission from availability in writing.** State whether the deployment you are proposing has quota, whether it has capacity, and how you would find out. These are different questions with different answers and different failure modes, and a plan that conflates them has assumed the second because it checked the first.

**Step 4 — write the commitment sequence down in the right order.** Deploy, verify that capacity is actually available, then commit financially. If your plan commits first, mark it and say why.

**Step 5 — put per-consumer accounting in front of it.** A cost you cannot attribute is a cost nobody will own. Add a gateway policy fragment to your statement — not to deploy today, but so that the counter key is a decision you made rather than a default you inherited.

```xml
<!-- Inbound policy fragment. The counter key is the decision on this page:
     it decides who is protected from whom when the quota runs out.
     Keyed on subscription here; a team, a workload or a tenant may be
     the honest unit instead. Say which, and why. -->
<inbound>
  <base />

  <llm-token-limit
      counter-key="@(context.Subscription.Id)"
      tokens-per-minute="{{tpm-per-consumer}}"
      estimate-prompt-tokens="true"
      remaining-tokens-variable-name="remainingTokens" />

  <!-- Dimensions are what make a metric answerable. Total spend tells you
       the bill. Spend per consumer per workload tells you who to talk to. -->
  <llm-emit-token-metric namespace="llm-metrics">
    <dimension name="Consumer" value="@(context.Subscription.Name)" />
    <dimension name="ApiId"    value="@(context.Api.Id)" />
    <dimension name="Workload"
               value="@(context.Request.Headers.GetValueOrDefault(&quot;x-workload&quot;,&quot;unattributed&quot;))" />
  </llm-emit-token-metric>
</inbound>
```

**Step 6 — write the statement itself, in a fixed shape.** Create `do-nothing.yaml`. The refusal block is not optional.

```yaml
do_nothing:
  continuing_event: "Assessment records are returned by the assessor and reworked."
  frequency: "Unknown — nobody has counted. See baseline."
  bearer: "Two named back-office staff, plus the originating field engineer."
  do_they_know: "The field engineers do not; the rework is absorbed downstream."

baseline:
  state: absent                     # measured | absent
  who_would_produce_one: "Operations lead, from the case-management export."
  what_it_would_cost: "One person, part of a week, plus an export nobody has requested before."
  minimum_detectable_change: >
    Unknown. With no count, no later measurement could distinguish an improvement
    from a quiet month. This is a decision that the outcome will be unknowable,
    and it has already been taken by nobody.

attribution:
  who_would_establish_causation: "Nobody. Recorded as the finding it is."
  metric_definitions_that_presuppose_it:
    - "Any metric defined as the change attributable to the system."

cost_of_proceeding:
  build: "From the sufficiency finding at S008."
  at_rest: >
    If dedicated capacity is chosen, it is paid for whether or not requests
    are being made, and releasing it does not reserve it for later.
  evaluation: "Open-ended: validation is feasible only during operation."
  attribution_mechanism: "Per-consumer token accounting at the gateway."

cost_of_refusal:
  to_customer:
    event: "The continuing event above, for at least another planning cycle."
    who_bears_it: "The same two named staff."
  to_me:
    immediate: "I am the person who said no to work the sponsor has approved."
    certainty: "High and immediate, against a benefit that is later and uncertain."
    what_would_make_it_survivable:
      - "A written record of the observation, dated before the decision."
      - "One named person senior to me who has seen the same observation."
      - "A trip wire agreed in advance: what I would escalate, and to whom."
    my_alternative_if_overruled: "State it. If you cannot, you have no BATNA."
```

**Step 7 — attack your own statement.** Give the agent your `do-nothing.yaml` and ask it to argue that the do-nothing cost is overstated, that the baseline absence is an excuse, and that the refusal cost to you is imaginary. Then require it to name, for each, the observation that would settle it. Record what you kept and one line you changed.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That both futures can be written in the same units, and that when they are, the asymmetry appears on the page: one side is engineered from telemetry and the other is elicited from opinion. It also makes the at-rest cost visible, which is the single item most often missing from a proposal — because a capacity commitment consumes money on a schedule that has nothing to do with whether anybody is using the system.

## What good looks like

- The continuing cost is an event with a frequency and a bearer, **not a category with an adjective attached.**
- The baseline is either measured, with its instrument named, or explicitly declared absent — and declaring it absent, with the person who would have to produce one, is a complete answer.
- Somebody is named as responsible for attribution. If nobody can be, that is written down as the finding it is.
- The cost of proceeding includes what is paid when nothing is happening, and says whether the capacity model was chosen or inherited.
- Permission and availability are separated, and the commitment sequence is stated in the order that verifies before it commits.
- The counter key for per-consumer accounting is a named decision with a reason, not a default.
- **The refusal has a price on the page, in two columns**, and the learner's own column names at least one thing that would make it survivable.

## How it goes wrong

Seven failure modes, each with the warning sign that precedes it.

- **The unowned number** — a figure appears with no instrument behind it. *Warning sign:* you cannot say who would produce it again next quarter, or how.
- **Attribution by assumption** — the benefit is defined as the change attributable to the work, which presupposes exactly the thing that needed establishing. *Warning sign:* your definition contains the word "attributable" and no method.
- **The costless refusal** — declining is treated as the zero option. *Warning sign:* your do-nothing column is empty, or contains the word "nothing".
- **Baseline by recollection** — a figure somebody remembered in a meeting becomes the reference point. *Warning sign:* the source of the baseline is a person rather than an instrument, and nobody has noticed.
- **Quota mistaken for capacity** — a plan assumes that permission to deploy means the deployment will succeed. *Warning sign:* your capacity line names an approval rather than a check.
- **The unattributed bill** — consumption is measured in total and never per consumer. *Warning sign:* your metrics have no dimensions, and the first cost conversation will be with everybody at once.
- **The heroic refusal** — the cost of declining is priced for the customer and not for the person declining. *Warning sign:* your statement reads as though saying no were free to you, and you did not write down what would make it survivable.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The statement is carried into [S010](/wiki/seminars/S010.md), where element 4 requires the cost of the refusal to the customer to appear inside the refusal itself.

**1 · Turn four categories into events.** Each of these is a cost with an adjective. Rewrite each as an event with a frequency and a bearer.

- *Significant manual effort.*
- *Poor data quality.*
- *Reduced staff satisfaction.*
- *Missed opportunities.*

> **Model answers.** *Records returned for rework — count them for four weeks, borne by two named staff.* · *Fields left blank at submission — count per hundred submissions, borne by whoever later chases them.* · *Voluntary exits from the team in twelve months, and exit-interview reasons, borne by the hiring budget.* · *The last three deferred requests, named, with who asked and when.* **The test is whether somebody could go and count it.**

**2 · Separate permission from availability.** Using the platform material, write two sentences for one proposed deployment: one about quota and one about capacity. Then state how you would find out about each, and what happens if the second is unavailable.

> **What you should find.** Quota is a policy limit, scoped per subscription, per region and per deployment type, and costs nothing. Capacity is what is actually available to deploy now, fluctuates through the day with demand across all customers, and is released without any guarantee of return. **The documentation states plainly that having quota does not guarantee capacity and that a deployment fails when regional capacity is insufficient** — so a plan that treats an approved quota as a deployable capability has assumed the second because it checked the first.

**3 · Go back to your own item 5.** Find what you wrote to opening item 5 about what declining costs *you*. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then rewrite it, including your alternative if you are overruled.

> **Criterion.** A passing answer names a cost that lands on you, says whether it is immediate or deferred and certain or uncertain, names at least one asset that would make it survivable, and states what you would do if the work proceeded without you. "It might be awkward" fails on all four.
>
> **Why this exercise exists at all.** [WP-001](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-001.md) concedes that this programme teaches refusal as an individual skill when the research treats speaking up as conditional on the environment, and names this session as where the concession is discharged. **It is discharged by pricing, not by reassurance** — the programme cannot make your environment safe and it can stop pretending the price is zero.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Choose the counter key and defend it.** Using the policy fragment in the workflow, decide what your token limit should be keyed on — a subscription, a team, a workload, a tenant — and write two sentences on who that choice protects and who it exposes.

> **Scoring criterion.** A passing answer notices that the counter key is a fairness decision rather than a technical one. Keying on a subscription protects tenants from each other and does nothing about two workloads inside one tenant. Keying on a workload does the reverse. **A shared finite resource with no per-consumer accounting will be consumed by whichever consumer is least careful**, and the key decides who that sentence applies to.

**5 · Write the trip wire.** Following the negotiation material, write one threshold — worse than what you hope for and better than your alternative — that would trigger a pause and a reassessment rather than a decision.

> **What to notice.** A trip wire is not a bottom line, and the distinction is the point: a bottom line blocks learning and prevents you from considering packages that meet your interests in an unexpected shape, while a trip wire creates time. **If yours reads like a refusal condition, you have written a bottom line and called it something softer.**

**6 · Name the sequence.** Closed book: in what order should capacity be verified and a financial commitment made, and why?

> **Answer.** Deploy first to confirm that capacity is genuinely available, then purchase the commitment to lock in the discounted rate. A commitment is a discount applied to a billing meter and does not reserve anything.
>
> **Then compare.** Look back at your answer to opening item 3, about the baseline and who measured it. Did you treat an approved figure as a measured one anywhere? **The two errors have the same shape** — a permission or an approval standing in for an observation — and noticing that is worth more than either answer.

## What this does not establish

**The framework this session is built against is not exaggerated; it contains no magnitudes at all.** It instructs the reader to establish a baseline and never exhibits one being established, catalogues metric definitions and never reports one measured, and recommends a method for quantities it never quantifies. **A framework that generates numbers while asserting none is harder to check than one that over-claims**, because there is no proposition to check the numbers against. That diagnosis is recorded from a complete reading and is the strongest thing this session rests on.

**Everything else here is direction and mechanism at best.** That pricing the alternative improves decisions, and that a named attributor changes anybody's behaviour, are design reasoning with no external warrant.

**The negotiation material reports no controlled comparison of anything.** BATNA, trip wires and micro-BATNAs are analytic devices with worked cases, and the cases illustrate mechanism without isolating causes. No effectiveness claim for teaching them is warranted by that source.

**The platform documentation is definitional and its concrete quantities are absent from this page by design.** Per-model capacity ratios, minimum deployment sizes, prices, default allocations and regional availability all live on companion pages this repository did not fetch, and **none is asserted here.** The gateway documentation publishes no performance or cost-saving data of any kind, does not discuss the cost and latency the gateway itself adds, and does not address the privacy implications of the prompt logging it recommends.

**The psychological-safety mechanism is used and its evidence is not.** The account of silence's asymmetric payoffs is taken from a trade book whose literature chapter has no search protocol, reports no disconfirming study, and selects its cases after the outcomes were known. **The quantitative synthesis of that literature is a different source and is used at [S010](/wiki/seminars/S010.md), where more weight rests on it.** Nothing here establishes that writing down the cost of a refusal makes the refusal more likely, or safer.
