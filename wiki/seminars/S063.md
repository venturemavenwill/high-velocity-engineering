# S063 · The Incident You Did Not See

**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** none

## The situation

Two things happened last month and you know about neither.

The first is an incident that produced no signal, because nothing was watching the thing that broke. The second is an incident that produced a signal, sent it to somebody, and that somebody has a mail rule.

**Both are the same failure wearing different clothes**, and the second is more common. An alert set grows by accretion: every incident adds one, nothing removes one, and the estate arrives at a condition where everything pages and therefore nothing does. **Alert fatigue is real** — the openly published playbook says so in those words — and the discipline that answers it is deletion, which is uncomfortable because every deletion is a decision to stop seeing something.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught.

1. What is the minimum service quality your customer would actually accept — not what you intend to deliver?
2. Which of your alerts fired most recently, who received it, and what did they do?
3. Name a signal your system produces that no downstream process consumes.
4. What would tell you an incident had happened if nobody reported it?
5. Who is woken up by your alerts, and have you asked them?

**Item 1 is the session's instrument and almost everybody answers it wrong the first time**, because the honest answer is lower than the answer a delivery team wants to give.

## What you must produce

**A service level, an alert set derived from it, and a register of what nobody is watching.**

- **The service level**, stated as the minimum viable service quality acceptable to the customer, with a sentence saying explicitly that it is not what the team intends to deliver.
- **The alert set**, in which every alert names the action it expects and the person expected to take it — and a deletion record for everything that expected neither.
- **The unconsumed-signal register**: for every output the system produces, the process that consumes it, or a recorded decision that nothing does and why.
- **One incident account** — real or reconstructed — of something that happened and produced no useful signal, naming the detection that was missing and whether you have added it.

## What good looks like

- **The service level is a floor, not an aspiration.** It is what the customer would accept, and the document says so in a sentence a reader cannot miss. **An objective set at what the team hopes for produces no budget to spend and turns every ordinary variation into a breach.**
- **Perfect availability is not targeted**, and the document says why not rather than treating the omission as modesty.
- **Every alert names an expected action and an expected actor.** An alert expecting no action is deleted or downgraded.
- **The deletions are recorded**, each with what it means you will now miss. **Deleting an alert is a decision to stop seeing a class of event, and it is only defensible in writing.**
- **The unconsumed-signal register is complete over the system's outputs**, and each entry names a consumer or a decision. A good report that reaches no queue is on this register.
- **The incident account is about the system rather than the person.** The question is how this is prevented in future, never how somebody could possibly have done that.
- **Your detection line is stated**, and so is what you expect it to miss.

## How it goes wrong

- **Alert fatigue** — everything pages, so nothing does. *Warning sign:* somebody has a mail rule, and it is not a secret.
- **The aspirational service level** — the objective is what the team hopes to deliver, set by the team, never put to the customer. *Warning sign:* your service level was agreed in a room the customer was not in.
- **The orphan analyser** — a scanner or report produces genuine findings that no process consumes. *Warning sign:* the reports are good, and no work item anywhere references one.
- **The blameful account** — the remedy for an incident is that somebody should be more careful. *Warning sign:* your corrective action is training.
- **The unreported incident** — nothing was reported, so nothing happened. *Warning sign:* your incident count is a function of customer patience rather than of system behaviour.
- **The target of total availability** — no error budget, so every change is a breach and the objective is quietly ignored. *Warning sign:* nobody has ever cited the objective in a decision.
- **The undeleted alert** — you agreed it expects no action and you kept it anyway. *Warning sign:* your deletion record is empty and your alert count went up.

## Do it

**First, get the service level from the customer, not from the team.** Ask what quality of service would be unacceptable rather than what would be good. **The minimum viable quality is the number you can defend**, and it is lower than the one you would like to write.

**Second, derive the alert set from it.** An alert is legitimate when a breach of the level, or a credible path to one, requires somebody to do something. Everything else is a graph.

**Third, delete.** Take every alert that survives the derivation and ask what the receiver is expected to do. Where the answer is nothing, remove it — and **write down what you will now miss**, because that sentence is the whole of the accountability for the deletion.

**Fourth, go and ask the person who is woken up.** They know which alerts are noise and nobody has asked them. This is the cheapest evidence available in the session and it is the piece most learners skip.

**Fifth, walk the outputs.** Every report, scan, analyser and export the system produces. For each, name the process that consumes it. The ones with no consumer go on the register, and the register is the artefact that names the incident you have not had yet.

**Sixth, write the incident account.** Choose something that happened, or reconstruct a plausible one from the pre-mortem you ran at [S032](/wiki/seminars/S032.md). Name the detection that was missing. Then say whether you added it, and if not, why the cost was not worth paying — **which is a legitimate answer and an indefensible silence.**

## Show it

Two moves, in this order.

**Give your alert set to the person who receives the alerts.** Ask them to mark the ones they have stopped reading. Do not defend any of them while they do it.

**Then give your unconsumed-signal register to somebody who did not write it and ask them to find one more.** The one they find is the interesting one, because it is the output you had stopped seeing as an output.

**A finding that reaches no queue was not found by the organisation, only by the instrument.** That sentence is what both moves are testing.

## What this does not establish

**Nothing here shows that a pruned alert set catches more incidents than a large one**, or that a service level expressed as a floor produces better operational outcomes than one expressed as an aspiration. The openly published playbook states both rules and supplies no comparative evidence for either, and the page carrying its service-level definition is among the oldest on its site.

**The detection line this session asks you to draw has no principled position.** Delete too little and the alerts are ignored; delete too much and the incident count becomes a function of who complains. **Nothing in any source available to this programme says where the line falls**, and this session does not either — it requires you to state yours and to name what you expect it to miss, which is a discipline rather than an answer.

**The unconsumed-signal register is this design's invention.** The observation behind it is verified: in the pipeline this stage learns from, three auxiliary analysers produce good standalone reports, none emits an identifier the rest of the chain uses, and no stage consumes their output — **so a real vulnerability found by the code analyser never becomes a threat, never becomes a work item and never reaches a backlog.** Turning that observation into a required register is a design decision with no warrant behind it, and it has not been tested on anybody.

## Perishable content in this session

| Durable claim | Perishable instance |
|---|---|
| A service level is the minimum quality the customer accepts, not what you intend to deliver | Whatever objective your platform's tooling proposes by default |
| An alert that expects no action is a graph | The current alerting product and its routing rules |
| A signal no process consumes is not a signal | This year's set of scanners and reports and where their output lands |
| An incident count can be a measure of customer patience | Present expectations about what customers report and how quickly |
