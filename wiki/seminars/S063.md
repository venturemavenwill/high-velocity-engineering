## The situation

Two things happened last month and you know about neither.

The first is an incident that produced no signal, because nothing was watching the thing that broke. The second is an incident that produced a signal, sent it to somebody, and that somebody has a mail rule.

**Both are the same failure wearing different clothes**, and the second is more common. An alert set grows by accretion: every incident adds one, nothing removes one, and the estate arrives at a condition where everything pages and therefore nothing does. **Alert fatigue is real** — the openly published playbook says so in those words — and the discipline that answers it is deletion, which is uncomfortable because every deletion is a decision to stop seeing something.

There is a third, and it is the one this session is named after. **An incident occurred, your posture tooling was running, and it produced nothing** — and you have no way to establish whether that is because nothing happened, because the tool cannot see that class of thing, or because the thing it saw fell below a threshold nobody has ever examined.

**The vendor publishes no detection rate and no false-positive rate for its posture tooling.** Not a poor figure — no figure. So the inference from *no alert* to *no incident* has a strength that is **genuinely unknown**, rather than merely unstated. That is not a complaint about the product; it is the working condition, and it is this session's teaching object.

**Answer these in writing before reading further.** Item 4 is the session's centre and the one most people cannot answer without help they are not going to get.

1. What is the minimum service quality your customer would actually accept — not what you intend to deliver?
2. Which of your alerts fired most recently, who received it, and what did they do?
3. Name a signal your system produces that no downstream process consumes.
4. No alert fired last month. Write down exactly what that licenses you to believe.
5. Who is woken up by your alerts, and have you asked them — and who would tell you about an incident nobody was paged for?

Most people answer item 1 too high, because the honest answer is lower than a delivery team wants to write. **Item 4 is where the session lives**, and the answers divide cleanly: some learners write a sentence about the system, and some write a sentence about the instrument. Only the second kind is defensible.

## What you must produce

**A service level, an alert set derived from it, and two registers of what nobody is watching.**

1. **The service level**, stated as the minimum viable service quality acceptable to the customer, with a sentence saying explicitly that it is not what the team intends to deliver.
2. **The alert set**, in which every alert names the action it expects and the person expected to take it — and a deletion record for everything that expected neither, each deletion naming what you will now miss.
3. **The unconsumed-signal register**: for every output the system produces, the process that consumes it, or a recorded decision that nothing does and why.
4. **The detection-capability statement**: for each class of incident you care about, what would have to be true for your instruments to see it, and what you expect them to miss. **This is the artefact that converts silence from reassurance into evidence with a stated strength.**
5. **The instrument-resolution line**: for every posture or scanning tool you rely on, its scan scope, its cadence and its detection latency — and what falls outside all three.
6. **One incident account** — real or reconstructed — of something that happened and produced no useful signal, naming the detection that was missing and whether you have added it.

## The material

### The absence of an alert is not evidence of the absence of an incident

This is the session's hardest claim and it is epistemological rather than technical, so it is worth stating in its exact form. **A monitoring system's silence is informative only in proportion to what that system is capable of detecting.** If you know an instrument reliably detects a class of event, its silence is strong evidence that the event did not occur. If you do not know that, its silence is evidence of nothing at all — and, worse, it *feels* like evidence, because a quiet dashboard and a healthy system are indistinguishable from the operator's chair.

The posture tooling this session anchors on is a good instrument and it is worth deploying. **It publishes no effectiveness data of any kind: no detection rates, no false-positive rates, no coverage measurements** ([research/06-microsoft-platform/purview-dspm-for-ai-and-defender-ai-spm-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/purview-dspm-for-ai-and-defender-ai-spm-docs.md)). That absence is not carelessness and it is not unusual — almost no security product in this class publishes such figures. **It is a norm, and a norm is precisely what a curriculum should make visible**, because an engineer who believes their tools arrive with characterised error rates will make worse decisions than one who knows they do not.

So the operational rule is a prohibition with a check attached. **Do not write "no incidents occurred". Write "no incident of a class our instruments can detect was detected, at the resolution recorded below."** The second sentence is longer, uglier, and the only one you can defend.

### What the documentation does publish is the resolution of the instrument

This is where the same source becomes unusually useful, because it is candid about its own bounds in a way that lets you state yours. The documentation records the **scope** of its assessments — a sample of the estate's most-used locations rather than the whole of it; item-level scanning restricted to a small number of sites; one major storage service excluded from item-level scanning altogether; a cap on items per location, with a further caveat that counts become unreliable above a stated size. It records the **cadence**, which is weekly for the default assessment. And it records the **latency**: policies take time to produce data, custom assessments take longer, the first assessment does not display for several days, and custom results expire.

**Read those three as the resolution of the instrument rather than as small print.** A tool that samples part of an estate on a weekly cadence with multi-day latency gives you a posture *estimate*, not a real-time control — and an engineer who cannot state its scope, its cadence and its latency does not know what their controls actually cover. **That triple is the most transferable thing in the whole source**, and it applies to every scanner, every analyser and every dashboard you will ever be handed.

Three further facts from the same documentation belong in the detection-capability statement.

**Oversharing is amplified rather than created by AI deployment.** The documentation states it directly: because AI can proactively surface content that might be obsolete, over-permissioned or lacking governance controls, **"generative AI amplifies the problem of oversharing data."** Organisations have had over-permissioned document estates for two decades and suffered little, because nobody read them. **A retrieval system reads all of them, instantly, on behalf of anyone who asks.** The latent misconfiguration becomes an active leak on the day a copilot is switched on — which generalises to a rule worth carrying: **deploying capable retrieval converts every dormant permissions error in an organisation into a live one.**

**Auditing that an interaction happened is not auditing what was said.** Without an explicit policy, some first-party AI surfaces audit the event and not the prompts and responses. A team that believes it has a record often has only a timestamp.

**Attack path analysis exists because individually acceptable configurations compose into breaches** — what the documentation calls **"toxic combinations"** — and it names grounding and fine-tuning as the moments a model touches proprietary data, and therefore the two moments data can leak by construction rather than by attack. The threat list on the same surface names an AI-native failure mode that classical security models do not cover: a **wallet attack**, which drives cost rather than downtime, and which follows directly from usage-based billing.

**The source's limits are extreme and must travel with every use of it.** One of the two pages consulted is explicitly the **classic** version and states that improvements will not be backported; the other was read through search excerpts rather than a full fetch, so its structure is reconstructed. Agent-level discovery and posture move to a separate licence on a stated date. Substantial licensing and tenant-administrator prerequisites apply. And **no effectiveness data of any kind is published** — which is the session's subject rather than its footnote.

### An absence is a measurement, and measurements have power

The discipline that makes item 4 answerable does not come from security at all. It comes from the statistics of evaluation, and it transfers exactly.

**An evaluation is an experiment.** The statistical treatment of language-model evaluation supplies the apparatus: report the standard error beside the mean, report the item count, report the cluster count where items arrive in related groups, and — the part that matters here — **use power analysis to establish, in advance, the smallest true difference your instrument could reliably detect** ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md)). The paper's own framing of the failure it targets is the same one this session targets in a different domain: results are reported with a *highest number is best* mentality and without any test of whether the difference is distinguishable from noise.

**The transfer is direct.** "We ran the evaluation and saw no difference" becomes a statement about what the evaluation was capable of detecting, and the same conversion applies to "no alert fired". **A null result from an underpowered instrument means nothing**, and the honest response is not to become more confident but to state what would have had to happen for the instrument to speak. The source's clustering result reinforces the point in the direction that matters: where observations arrive in related groups rather than independently, treating them as independent **overstates precision**, so your instrument is usually less discriminating than it looks.

**Its limits:** an analytical paper rather than an empirical one; most of its illustrative magnitudes rest on stylised assumptions and **none is reproduced here**; and statistical validity says nothing about construct validity — a tight interval around a meaningless measure is still meaningless.

### Degradation that produces no error at all

The production machine-learning literature supplies the mechanism for the incident that generates no signal because there is nothing to signal. **Training–serving skew** — preprocessing performed differently at training and at serving — is described as a common production failure whose errors are **difficult to find because the model continues to return plausible answers** ([research/02-technical-foundations/crowe-machine-learning-production-systems.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/crowe-machine-learning-production-systems.md)). No exception, no error, no failed check. The same source separates **drift**, which compares one source over time, from **skew**, which compares two sources such as training against serving, and splits drift into data drift, a change in the inputs, and concept drift, a change in the relationship between inputs and labels, **which invalidates the mapping the model learned rather than merely moving the inputs.**

The operationally decisive detail is about detection rather than about failure. **Drift detection splits into supervised techniques that require fresh labels and unsupervised techniques that do not** — and the unsupervised family is the practical default, because labelling is slow and expensive. Which means the instrument you will actually be running is the weaker one, and the stronger one is unavailable for the reason that makes the whole problem hard: **you do not have ground truth for what is happening now.**

The same source's observability framing supplies the two properties an alert must satisfy — **alertability**, a threshold that makes failure unambiguous, and **actionability**, enough signal to find a cause — and its two named sensitivities explain why an alert often fires nowhere near the cause. **Entanglement**: changing one thing changes everything. **Configuration**: a small error in versions, parameters or feature lists produces radically different behaviour that ordinary software tests do not catch.

**Its limits:** practitioner guidance from a commercial publisher, not research; no study design, no comparison group, no measurement that its practices outperform alternatives; a single-vendor default toolchain; some foundational claims rest on **anonymous practitioner testimony**; and every quantity in it is a property of a particular system after a particular run. **No figure from it appears here.**

### The incident that was seen, and not reported

There is a class of incident your instruments could have seen and did not, because a person decided not to raise it. **An incident count therefore measures the reporting climate as well as the system**, and a team congratulating itself on a quiet quarter may be measuring how expensive speaking up has become.

The organisational-behaviour literature on this is precise about the mechanism. **Silence is invisible**: the moment passes, and nobody is the wiser except the person who held back ([research/04-professional-formation/edmondson-psychological-safety.md](/research/04-professional-formation/edmondson-psychological-safety.md)). The cause is not personality but a calculus, and the calculus is asymmetric — **voice benefits the organisation, after a delay, with low certainty; silence benefits oneself, immediately, with high certainty.** Hence the observation that carries the whole argument: **"no one was ever fired for silence."** Alongside it sit the *implicit theories of voice* the same research reconstructs: do not criticise something the boss may have helped create; do not speak unless you have solid data; do not say anything negative about the work in a group.

The constructive half matters as much. The same work's failure taxonomy separates **preventable** failures, which are deviations from a known process; **complex** failures, which are novel combinations of events; and **intelligent** failures, which are forays into new territory — and it insists that the response must differ by type, because treating all three the same is what teaches people to stop reporting. It also records a gap between the proportion of failures managers say *should* be treated as blameworthy and the proportion they concede *are* treated that way, and **the gap runs in the direction that suppresses reporting.** Sanction is inside the model rather than an exception to it: a fair, thoughtful response to genuinely dangerous behaviour is described as reinforcing rather than harming the climate.

**Its limits:** the work read in full is a trade book by the construct's originator; its case studies are drawn almost entirely from secondary sources — published teaching cases, journalism, official investigation reports — and the foundational empirical paper it rests on was **not read** for this repository, so claims traceable only to that paper are held at the level of direction and mechanism. **No magnitude from any of it is reproduced here.**

### The gateway sees traffic the posture tools do not

The second half of the anchor is there because the two instruments look at different things and the seam between them is where incidents hide. The gateway sits in the request path and can log prompts, completions and token usage **"for billing and auditing"**, emit token metrics with **custom dimensions** that make consumption attributable per interface and per consumer, and break the circuit on an unhealthy backend using a **dynamic trip duration taken from the backend's own `Retry-After` header** ([research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md)).

Two consequences for this session. **The wallet attack named on the posture surface is visible on the gateway surface**, because a cost-driving attack is a traffic pattern before it is a bill — and it is visible only if the dimensions that make consumption attributable were emitted before the attack, which is [S061](/wiki/seminars/S061.md)'s point arriving as a security property. And **the gateway's own logging is a privacy surface the documentation recommends and never discusses**, so an incident register that treats prompt capture as free has an undeclared exposure of its own.

**Its limits:** first-party product documentation, fetched and read in full, with a commercial frame; capability availability varies by service tier; parts are preview; **no performance or cost data of any kind**; and the privacy implications of logging prompts and completions are not addressed at all.

### The quiet queue that a control produced

Everything above treats silence as an unfortunate property of an instrument. **This subsection is about silence produced deliberately, by you, as a documented consequence of doing the right thing** — and it is the strongest specimen this session has.

The estate-governance documentation states it plainly: **where a blocking rule covers an agent, near-real-time alerts are not generated for that agent.** The events do not vanish; they move into the behaviour telemetry, which is a different surface, queried differently, watched by different people, and usually watched by nobody at all ([research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-365-and-agent-identity.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-365-and-agent-identity.md)).

**Read that sequence slowly, because the order is what makes it dangerous.** A team identifies a risk. The team enables blocking, which is the strongest available control and unambiguously the correct decision. The alert volume for those agents falls to nothing. **Every observable the team has says the situation improved, and the situation may have improved, and the observable would look identical if it had not.** Nobody lied, nobody was careless, and no attacker was involved. **A control was switched on and the instrument that was watching stopped reporting**, exactly as documented, on a page somebody would have had to read.

This is the session's own thesis in its most compressed form. Silence is a joint property of the world and the instrument — and here **the instrument's sensitivity was changed by the person reading it, in the same act that changed the world.** You cannot even hold one side still. A before-and-after comparison across that boundary is comparing two different instruments and calling the difference an outcome.

**The second half is worse and is easy to miss.** **The agent is not informed that it was blocked.** It continues from a step that did not happen. So the agent's own logs record no failure, no exception and no retry; whatever it does next is built on an action it believes succeeded. **There is therefore no signal at either end** — the security surface has stopped alerting by design, and the application surface never had anything to report. **Only somebody who goes and looks at the enforcement telemetry knows anything happened**, and looking is a thing nobody is paged to do, because the paging is what was turned off.

**So the register needs a row that most registers do not have: a control whose activation changed what an instrument reports.** Not a blind spot you inherited — one you created, for good reasons, and which will outlive your memory of creating it. The countermeasure is not to avoid the control. **It is to write the compensating observation into the same change** — the query against the behaviour telemetry, with an owner and a cadence — so that enforcement and observation move together rather than trading against each other silently.

**Two related boundaries from the same source belong in the register beside it.** Coverage of the real-time protection layer stops at the sanctioned tool path: **agents that do not travel it are not covered, and the documentation says so** rather than leaving it to be discovered. And the aggregated risk view lags the event by a published interval — **the figure is not reproduced here and does not matter; the mechanism does**, because a count read before the pipeline has finished arriving looks complete at every moment and is not.

**What this establishes and what it does not.** It establishes a mechanism, in the vendor's own words, by which a correctly configured estate goes quiet. **It establishes nothing about how often anything is caught by anything.** **No detection rate and no false-positive rate is published for any signal in that material, and none is asserted here or anywhere in this wiki.** **No coverage measure exists either** — nothing states what proportion of the agents actually present in a tenant are discovered, so an estate that looks fully instrumented is fully instrumented for the population that emits.

**Its limits are severe and unusually recent.** First-party documentation for a surface that reached general availability within months of being read, **substantially preview and in places behind a further preview programme again**; three announced capabilities carry a stated future date and were not re-verified; the announcement page it partly rests on arrived heavily fragmented, with two capability tables reduced to headers; and several central pages — including the schema of the telemetry table a compensating query would target — were **not read at all.** **Verify the behaviour before relying on it, and record the date you checked.**

## The workflow

**Derive the alert set from a floor, then write down what your instruments cannot see.**

**Step 1 — get the service level from the customer, not from the team.** Ask what quality of service would be unacceptable rather than what would be good. **The minimum viable quality is the number you can defend**, and it is lower than the one you would like to write.

**Step 2 — derive the alert set from it.** An alert is legitimate when a breach of the level, or a credible path to one, requires somebody to do something. Everything else is a graph.

**Step 3 — delete, and pay for each deletion in writing.** Take every alert that survives and ask what the receiver is expected to do. Where the answer is nothing, remove it and **write down what you will now miss**, because that sentence is the whole of the accountability for the deletion.

**Step 4 — go and ask the person who is woken up.** They know which alerts are noise and nobody has asked them. This is the cheapest evidence available in the session and it is the piece most learners skip. Ask them separately whether there is anything they have stopped raising, and **do not defend anything while they answer.**

**Step 5 — state the resolution of every instrument you rely on.** For each posture tool, scanner or analyser: scan scope, cadence, detection latency, and what falls outside all three. The documentation for the anchored tooling states all three for itself; most tools do not, and **an instrument whose resolution you cannot state is one whose silence you cannot read.**

**Step 6 — write the silence register, and make it the artefact that carries the epistemology.**

```yaml
# operate/silence-register.yaml   <- SHAPE, NOT SCHEMA. Verify any product keys separately.
reporting_period: "2026-06-01 to 2026-06-30"

headline: >
  NO INCIDENT OF A CLASS OUR INSTRUMENTS CAN DETECT WAS DETECTED,
  at the resolutions recorded below. THIS IS NOT "no incidents occurred".

instruments:
  - name: "posture management (AI security posture)"
    scope: "sampled: most-used locations only; item-level scanning limited to a small number of sites; one major storage service excluded"
    cadence: "weekly default assessment"
    latency: "multi-day before first results display; custom results expire"
    published_detection_rate: "NONE PUBLISHED"          # <- the teaching object
    published_false_positive_rate: "NONE PUBLISHED"
    therefore: "silence from this instrument has UNKNOWN strength as evidence"

  - name: "gateway telemetry"
    scope: "all traffic traversing the gateway; NOT traffic that bypasses it"
    cadence: "continuous"
    latency: "near-real-time for metrics; batch for logs"
    blind_to: "anything reaching a backend directly, and anything upstream of the correlation identifier"

detection_capability:
  - incident_class: "a document surfaced to a reader who should not see it"
    would_be_seen_if: "the retrieval path emits an access decision AND the reader is identifiable"
    expected_to_miss: "any surfacing inside a session that predates the sampling window"
    confidence_in_silence: low

  - incident_class: "cost-driving abuse (wallet attack)"
    would_be_seen_if: "token metrics carry a consumer dimension emitted BEFORE the abuse"
    expected_to_miss: "abuse routed through a consumer identity we do not distinguish"
    confidence_in_silence: medium

  - incident_class: "silent quality degradation (skew or concept drift)"
    would_be_seen_if: "we had fresh labels"
    expected_to_miss: "EVERYTHING, in practice: we run unsupervised detection only"
    confidence_in_silence: none          # <- state this rather than omitting the row

unreported:
  channel: "<how somebody raises an incident nobody was paged for>"
  asked_this_period: true
  what_came_back: "<verbatim, including 'nothing'>"
  note: "an incident count measures the reporting climate as well as the system"

unconsumed_signals:
  - output: "<analyser or report>"
    consumer: null
    decision: "<why nothing consumes it, and who decided>"
```

**Step 7 — walk the outputs.** Every report, scan, analyser and export the system produces. For each, name the process that consumes it. The ones with no consumer go on the register, and **a finding that reaches no queue was found by the instrument and not by the organisation.**

**Step 8 — write the incident account, about the system rather than the person.** Choose something that happened, or reconstruct a plausible one from the pre-mortem you ran at [S032](/wiki/seminars/S032.md). Name the detection that was missing. Then say whether you added it, and if not, why the cost was not worth paying — **which is a legitimate answer and an indefensible silence.**

**Step 9 — list every control whose activation changed what an instrument reports, and pair each with a compensating observation.** This is the row most registers do not have, and it is not a blind spot you inherited — it is one you created. Walk every enforcement control in the estate and ask the awkward question: **did switching this on reduce the risk, reduce the reporting, or both, and how would I tell the difference?** Where a documented rule says alerting stops for the covered population, the answer is *both*, and the register must say so.

```yaml
# operate/silence-register.yaml  <- appended to the block in step 6. SHAPE, NOT SCHEMA.

controls_that_silenced_an_instrument:
  - control: "blocking rule covering <agent set>"
    enabled_on: "<date>"
    enabled_by: "<named person>"
    documented_effect: >
      near-real-time alerts are NOT generated for a covered agent;
      the events move to the behaviour telemetry instead.
    second_effect: >
      the agent is NOT informed it was blocked. it continues from a step that
      did not happen, so its own logs show no failure, no exception, no retry.
    therefore: >
      NO SIGNAL AT EITHER END. the alert queue is quiet by design and the
      application log is quiet because nothing was raised. a fall in alert
      volume across this date is NOT a measurement of anything.
    compensating_observation:
      query: "<the behaviour-telemetry query that replaces the alert>"
      owner: "<a named person, not a rota>"
      cadence: "<how often, and what happens when it is missed>"
      shipped_with_the_control: true      # <- if false, the control shipped blind
    verified_on: "<date you checked this behaviour against current documentation>"

  - control: "<the next one. there is always a next one.>"
```

**Step 10 — use the agent to attack the register, not to populate it.** One prompt: *for every row where I claimed confidence in silence, argue that my instrument could not have detected that class at all, and name what would show it.* A second, for the step-9 block: *for each control I listed, argue that my compensating observation is watched by nobody, and name the week in which somebody last read it.* Keep what beats your own reasoning, record what you rejected, and note that this instrument shares a family with the systems you are reasoning about and is therefore not independent of them. **An agent that agreed your coverage was adequate was not used as an instrument.**

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That writing the confidence column forces a verdict you have been avoiding — for at least one incident class, the honest entry is *none*, and seeing it in a table beside classes you can detect is different from knowing it in the abstract. **You will also observe that the resolution triple is unavailable for most of your tools**, because most vendors do not publish scope, cadence and latency the way the anchored documentation does, and that unavailability is itself the finding.

## What good looks like

- **The service level is a floor, not an aspiration.** It is what the customer would accept, and the document says so in a sentence a reader cannot miss. **An objective set at what the team hopes for produces no budget to spend and turns every ordinary variation into a breach.**
- **Perfect availability is not targeted**, and the document says why not rather than treating the omission as modesty.
- **Every alert names an expected action and an expected actor.** An alert expecting no action is deleted or downgraded.
- **The deletions are recorded**, each with what it means you will now miss. **Deleting an alert is a decision to stop seeing a class of event, and it is only defensible in writing.**
- **Every instrument carries its scan scope, its cadence and its detection latency**, and names what falls outside all three. Where the vendor does not publish them, the register says so rather than leaving the row blank.
- **The detection-capability statement names at least one incident class the instruments cannot see at all**, with the confidence in silence recorded as none rather than omitted.
- **No sentence anywhere in the artefact reads "no incidents occurred".** The permitted form names the class, the instrument and the resolution.
- **The unconsumed-signal register is complete over the system's outputs**, and each entry names a consumer or a decision. A good report that reaches no queue is on this register.
- **Somebody has been asked what they have stopped raising**, and what came back is recorded verbatim, including "nothing".
- **The incident account is about the system rather than the person.** The question is how this is prevented in future, never how somebody could possibly have done that.

## How it goes wrong

- **Silence read as safety** — no alert fired, so nothing happened. *Warning sign:* your report contains the sentence "no incidents occurred" and no statement of what your instruments can detect.
- **The uncharacterised instrument** — a posture tool trusted without its scope, cadence or latency being known. *Warning sign:* you cannot say what fraction of the estate it looked at, or when.
- **Alert fatigue** — everything pages, so nothing does. *Warning sign:* somebody has a mail rule, and it is not a secret.
- **The aspirational service level** — the objective is what the team hopes to deliver, set by the team, never put to the customer. *Warning sign:* your service level was agreed in a room the customer was not in.
- **The orphan analyser** — a scanner or report produces genuine findings that no process consumes. *Warning sign:* the reports are good, and no work item anywhere references one.
- **The blameful account** — the remedy for an incident is that somebody should be more careful. *Warning sign:* your corrective action is training.
- **The unreported incident** — nothing was reported, so nothing happened. *Warning sign:* your incident count is a function of customer patience rather than of system behaviour, and nobody has been asked what they stopped raising.
- **The undifferentiated failure** — a deviation from a known process, a novel combination of events, and a deliberate excursion into new territory all handled the same way. *Warning sign:* your response to every incident has the same shape, and reporting is falling.
- **The target of total availability** — no error budget, so every change is a breach and the objective is quietly ignored. *Warning sign:* nobody has ever cited the objective in a decision.
- **The undeleted alert** — you agreed it expects no action and you kept it anyway. *Warning sign:* your deletion record is empty and your alert count went up.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The service level, the alert set and both registers are defended at [S065](/wiki/seminars/S065.md), where every alert must name its expected action and actor and every deletion must record what will now be missed.

**1 · Sort four silences by what each licenses.** For each, say what you may conclude: *the posture tool reported nothing this week* · *no customer complained this month* · *the unit tests all passed* · *the on-call phone did not ring*.

> **Model answers.** The first licenses almost nothing on its own, because **no detection rate and no false-positive rate is published for that class of tooling** and its scan scope, cadence and latency bound it further — it looked at part of the estate, some days ago. The second measures customer patience and the friction of your complaint channel, both of which vary independently of the system. The third establishes that the cases somebody thought to write behaved as that person expected. The fourth establishes that no rule in your alert set matched, which is a statement about the alert set. **The general rule: a silence is a claim about an instrument, and it becomes a claim about the world only in proportion to what that instrument can detect — which for most security tooling is unpublished, and therefore unknown rather than merely unstated.**

**2 · State the resolution of one instrument.** Pick a posture tool, scanner or analyser you rely on and find its scan scope, its cadence and its detection latency.

> **What you should find.** For the anchored posture tooling, all three are documented and are narrower than teams assume — a sample of the estate, a weekly default cadence, and multi-day latency before results display, with item-level scanning restricted further still. **For most other tools you will find none of the three**, and that absence is the finding: record it, because a control whose resolution you cannot state is a control whose coverage you cannot claim. **Do not infer a resolution from a marketing page.**

**3 · Go back to your own item 4, then break it.** Find what you wrote for opening item 4 — what the absence of an alert last month licenses you to believe. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then write the permitted form for one real incident class in your own system.

> **Criterion.** A pass is a sentence about the *instrument*: it names the incident class, names what would have had to be true for the instrument to see it, and states the confidence in the silence — including *none*, where that is honest. A fail is a sentence about the *system*: "nothing happened", "we were clean", "the month was quiet". **The hardest pass is the row where the honest confidence is none**, and most systems have at least one, because supervised drift detection requires fresh labels and almost nobody has them.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Ask the person who is woken up.** Take your alert set to them and ask which ones they have stopped reading. Then ask the second question: is there anything they have stopped raising?

> **Scoring criterion.** A pass records both answers verbatim, including "nothing", and records whether you defended any alert while they spoke. **The second question is the one that matters and it is the one people flinch from**, because its honest answer is a statement about the team rather than about the tooling. The mechanism behind it is well characterised: **silence rarely announces itself**, the moment passes, and the calculus is asymmetric — voice benefits the organisation, later, uncertainly; silence benefits the individual, immediately, certainly. **An incident count therefore measures the reporting climate as well as the system.**

**5 · Classify one incident by failure type, then check your response.** Take your incident account and classify it as a deviation from a known process, a novel combination of events, or a deliberate excursion into new territory. Then look at what you actually did about it.

> **What to notice.** That the three call for different responses — retraining and system redesign, analysis from several perspectives, and designing the next experiment — and that most organisations apply one shape to all three. **Applying the preventable-failure response to a novel one is the specific move that teaches people to stop reporting**, because it reads as blame for something nobody could have known. Note also that sanction is inside the model rather than an exception to it: a fair, considered response to genuinely dangerous behaviour is described as reinforcing the reporting climate, not damaging it.

**6 · Find an incident class the gateway can see and the posture tooling cannot.** Use the gateway's traffic view and the posture tooling's estate view side by side.

> **What you should find.** At least one, and the cheapest example is cost-driving abuse — a **wallet attack** is a traffic pattern before it is a bill, and it is visible per consumer only if the dimension that makes consumption attributable was emitted before the abuse began. **That is [S061](/wiki/seminars/S061.md)'s argument arriving as a security property.** Record which instrument sees which class, and record the seam: traffic that reaches a backend without traversing the gateway is invisible to both, and that path is where an incident will eventually live.

**7 · Explain a quiet alert queue to a reader who will otherwise mistake it for safety.** Take a scenario in which a blocking rule is enabled over a set of agents. Write the paragraph that goes in the monthly report. Then write the compensating observation that should have shipped in the same change, with an owner and a cadence.

> **Scoring criterion.** A pass says three things. That **alert volume for the covered agents fell because near-real-time alerts are not generated for a covered agent** — a documented consequence of the control, not a measurement of the estate. That the events **moved** to the behaviour telemetry rather than ceasing, so the observation exists and somebody must go and make it. And that **the agent is not told it was blocked**, so it continues from a step that did not happen and its own logs record no failure, no exception and no retry — **which means there is no signal at either end.** A fail reports the fall in alerts as an improvement, which is the sentence the exercise exists to prevent, and a near-miss reports it as ambiguous without naming the mechanism. **The strongest answer notices that the before-and-after comparison is not available at all**: the instrument's sensitivity changed in the same act that changed the world, so there is no held-still side to compare against. **Verify the behaviour against current documentation before you write the paragraph, and record the date** — this is precisely the class of published rule that moves.

## What this does not establish

**Nothing here shows that a pruned alert set catches more incidents than a large one**, or that a service level expressed as a floor produces better operational outcomes than one expressed as an aspiration. The openly published handbook states both rules and supplies no comparative evidence for either, and the page carrying its service-level definition is among the oldest on its site.

**The central absence is the session's subject and it is worth stating flatly.** No detection rate and no false-positive rate is published for the posture tooling this session anchors on. **Not a poor figure — no figure.** So the strength of the inference from *no alert* to *no incident* is unknown rather than merely unstated, and no exercise in this session closes that gap. What the session does instead is convert the gap into a recorded property of your artefact, so that a downstream reader inherits the uncertainty rather than the reassurance.

**The posture documentation's other limits are severe.** One of the two pages consulted is explicitly the **classic** version and states that improvements will not be backported to it; the other was read through search excerpts rather than a full page fetch, so its structure is reconstructed rather than verified. Agent-level discovery and posture move to a separate licence on a stated date, which changes what is available under which plan. Coverage is bounded in ways that are easy to miss — sampling of most-used locations, item-level scanning restricted to a small number of sites, one major storage service excluded — and substantial licensing and tenant-administrator prerequisites apply that a teaching environment may not satisfy. **No coverage measurement of any kind is published**, so the scope statements bound the instrument without characterising it.

**The statistical apparatus this session borrows is analytical, not empirical, and it is about the wrong half to be a rescue.** It tells you whether a reported absence means anything; it does not tell you whether the thing you chose to watch was worth watching. Its illustrative magnitudes rest on stylised assumptions and **none is reproduced here.** A well-quantified null result about an irrelevant class is still irrelevant.

**The production-monitoring literature is practitioner guidance, not research**, with no study design, no comparison group and no measurement that its practices outperform alternatives; some of its foundational claims rest on anonymous practitioner testimony; and every quantity in it is a property of a particular system. **No figure from it appears here.**

**The organisational-behaviour material read for this session is a trade book by the construct's originator**, whose case studies are drawn almost entirely from secondary sources, and whose foundational empirical paper **was not read** for this repository. Claims traceable only to that paper are held at the level of direction and mechanism, and **no magnitude from any of it is reproduced here.** Nothing in it establishes that a better reporting climate produces fewer incidents; it establishes a mechanism by which incidents go unreported, which is a different and narrower claim.

**The detection line this session asks you to draw has no principled position.** Delete too little and the alerts are ignored; delete too much and the incident count becomes a function of who complains. **Nothing in any source available to this programme says where the line falls**, and this session does not either — it requires you to state yours and to name what you expect it to miss, which is a discipline rather than an answer.

**The silence register, the detection-capability statement and the unconsumed-signal register are this design's inventions.** The observation behind the last is verified: in the pipeline this stage learns from, several auxiliary analysers produce good standalone reports, none emits an identifier the rest of the chain uses, and no stage consumes their output — **so a real vulnerability found by the code analyser never becomes a threat, never becomes a work item and never reaches a backlog.** Turning that observation into a required register is a design decision with no warrant behind it, and it has not been tested on anybody.

**One honest closing hazard.** A session built on a documented absence is fragile in a specific direction: **if a detection rate is ever published, this session's central argument must be rewritten rather than defended.** That is the correct kind of fragility for a design tied to a documented state of the world, and it is why the absence is recorded with a date.

**And the enforcement-silence material establishes a mechanism, not a frequency.** It is documented, in the vendor's own words, that a blocking rule stops near-real-time alerting for the agents it covers and that the agent is not told it was blocked. **Nothing establishes how often that produces a missed incident, how many teams have hit it, or whether anybody has ever noticed.** No detection rate, no false-positive rate and no coverage measure is published for any signal in that material — **none is asserted here** — and nothing states what proportion of the agents present in a tenant are discovered at all, so the population your compensating query watches is the population that emits. **The source is also the newest and least settled in this repository**: general availability within months of the reading, substantial preview surface, three announced capabilities carrying a future date and unverified, an announcement page that arrived fragmented with capability tables reduced to headers, and several central pages including the relevant telemetry schema not read. **This is a published rule to verify before relying on, not a fact to memorise.**
