## The situation

This is the stage's second assessment occasion and it does not assess the same thing as [S035](/wiki/seminars/S035.md).

There, the finding's form and disclosure were marked. **Here the price is marked.** You assembled a body of evidence, somebody committed something to it, and the question the assessor holds is whether you could have obtained the same commitment for less.

There is a second price, and most packs arrive without it. The proof ran on somebody's infrastructure, consumed somebody's capacity, and — if the commitment converts — obliges somebody to hold capacity whether or not it is used. **A proof presented without its running cost is a proof presented as free, and nothing in this stage was free.**

Answer these in writing before you begin, and submit them.

1. What did the whole proof cost — your time, other people's time, and anything consumed?
2. Which single piece of evidence did the commitment actually turn on?
3. What did you produce that nobody used?
4. Design the cheaper proof. Same commitment, less spent. What is it?
5. What is still unproven, and what will it cost to find out?

**Item 3 is the assessment's centre of gravity.** Almost every proof contains work that was interesting, competent and irrelevant to the decision, and the willingness to name your own is the professional disposition the stage exists to build.

## What you must produce

A **proof pack**, assembled from the stage's work and defended in front of an assessor who has read your [S031](/wiki/seminars/S031.md) charter and your [S039](/wiki/seminars/S039.md) proposal and nothing else.

1. **The question the stage answered**, and the decision that turned on it.
2. **The feasibility finding** from [S035](/wiki/seminars/S035.md), unaltered, with its bound and its cost.
3. **The data reality record** from [S034](/wiki/seminars/S034.md), with any blocking absence still marked blocking.
4. **The concept artefact and its stripping record** from [S036](/wiki/seminars/S036.md), including your position on the costless-polish problem.
5. **The failure-first run** from [S037](/wiki/seminars/S037.md), with its instrument, its criteria diff, and any stakeholder who did not accept the framing.
6. **The test record** from [S038](/wiki/seminars/S038.md), populations unmerged, with the initiation log.
7. **The commitment**, or the refusal, with what it cost the giver.
8. **The cost ledger** for the whole stage, in the units it was paid in, **including consumed capacity and the traffic model the capacity figure was inferred from.**
9. **The cheaper proof** you can now design, and **the unproven list** — everything a reader might assume you established and did not.

## The material

### The stage's question has a cost clause in it, and the clause is the assessed half

The stage asked whether the thing is feasible **and how cheaply you could find out.** Almost every candidate answers the first half well and treats the second as an aside, and the reason is structural rather than lazy: **a proof's price is mostly other people's attention**, and other people's attention does not appear on any invoice. The maintainer who found the data for you, the users who sat with the artefact, the sponsor's hour, the platform team's afternoon — none of it is in your timesheet, and a practice that counts only its own hours will always conclude that it was cheap.

The practice corpus behind this stage is a specimen of exactly that failure, which is why it is worth naming. Its organising thesis is excellent: **everything short of realised business value is activity rather than impact**, including the team, the architecture, the fundamentals and the deployment. And then **no cost side appears anywhere in it** — every claim is a gross benefit, with staffing, compute, the cost of the discovery activity itself and opportunity cost all absent ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md)). **A body of work whose central claim is that only measurable benefit counts, supporting itself with measures of activity, is the failure this occasion exists to make you incapable of repeating.** Its limits: access-controlled, never quoted or named, no evidence base of any kind, no recorded failure, no exclusion criteria.

### Capacity you hold is capacity you pay for, whether or not you use it

The platform makes this concrete in a way no lecture can, and the sentence that carries it is short. A provisioned deployment **"holds a fixed amount of processing capacity exclusively for your deployment's use, whether or not requests are being made"**, in contrast to a standard deployment where **"inference capacity is shared across customers and throughput can vary with demand"** ([research/06-microsoft-platform/provisioned-throughput-for-foundry-models.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/provisioned-throughput-for-foundry-models.md)).

Everything else follows from that clause. Capacity held whether or not it is used has to be paid for whether or not it is used, **which is why the billing unit changes from tokens to time.** And that changes the kind of decision you are making: a deployment stops being a configuration setting and becomes a capacity-planning commitment, where a wrong estimate costs money continuously rather than in proportion to traffic.

The four deployment categories are a clean trade space and worth reading as one. **Standard** bills per token, offers no latency service-level agreement, and suits **"development, testing, and production with variable or unpredictable traffic"**. **Priority processing** bills at a priority token rate with a defined per-model latency target, for latency-sensitive work **"without a long-term commitment"**. **Provisioned** bills per unit per hour or via reservations, with a defined latency target, for **"mission-critical, high-scale production workloads requiring guaranteed throughput and consistent latency"**. **Batch** bills at a discounted rate, asynchronously, with no service level. **The structure is the lesson: latency guarantees cost money, and the currency in which you pay changes with how much predictability you want.**

The unit itself has four properties that are individually dull and jointly decisive. Units are **model-independent** — **"You don't buy PTUs for a specific model"**. They are **region-specific** — **"Quota in East US doesn't carry over to West Europe."** Their throughput **varies by model**, so a heavier model needs more of them for the same throughput. And **each model has a minimum below which a deployment cannot be created**, which puts a floor under the cost of using a large model at all. **What you buy is machine, not service**, and how much service it yields depends entirely on what you run on it.

### Permission is not availability, and the correct sequence is the counter-intuitive one

This is the most transferable idea in the whole platform layer and it is the one that decides whether your pack's commitment is real.

**Quota** is **"a policy limit enforced by Azure, and it has no associated cost"**, scoped per subscription, per region and per deployment type. **Capacity** is **"the actual amount of PTUs per model version that's available to be deployed"**, allocated at deployment time and held for the deployment's lifetime. The documentation states the consequence in a call-out: **"Having PTU quota doesn't guarantee that capacity is available. If capacity in the region is insufficient for the requested PTU count, the deployment fails."**

Two further facts sharpen it. Capacity availability **"changes throughout the day based on customer demand across all regions and models"**, so the answer to *can I deploy this* is time-dependent. And **"deleting or scaling down a deployment releases its capacity back to the region pool. There's no guarantee the same capacity is available if you re-create or scale the deployment up later"** — so releasing capacity looks reversible and is not.

Then the sentence this whole stage has been walking towards. **"Reservations don't guarantee capacity. First create deployments to confirm that capacity is available, then purchase the reservation to lock in the discounted rate."** The correct sequence is **deploy, verify, then commit** — and the intuitive sequence, commit then deploy, is exactly wrong.

**Read that as a statement about proofs rather than about billing.** It is the same discipline this stage has been teaching in a different register: you establish that the thing is available before you buy the right to it. A candidate who has understood [S039](/wiki/seminars/S039.md) will recognise their own commitment proposal in it, and the recognition is the point — **the vendor's own procurement guidance and this programme's account of evidence-before-commitment are the same rule, arrived at independently.**

### A price is an inference from a traffic model, and yours came from a handful of people

Sizing is presented as a three-input calculation, and the inputs are where your honesty is tested. **Request shape** is expected requests per minute with average input and output sizes. The **output-to-input ratio** captures that **"output tokens require more processing capacity than input tokens"**. The **cache rate** is the fraction of input tokens served from the prompt cache, and **"cached tokens don't consume PTU capacity, so a higher cache rate reduces the PTUs required."** Those combine into a normalised throughput figure divided by a per-model divisor.

**This is a capacity model, not a pricing model.** It converts a description of traffic into a quantity of machine, and **every simplification in the traffic description propagates into the answer.** Your traffic description came from the [S038](/wiki/seminars/S038.md) test record, which had a small number of participants over a short window, on one shift pattern, with the non-invokers excluded by definition. **Say so in the ledger.** A cost figure whose provenance is a handful of sessions, presented without that provenance, is the same defect as a finding presented without its bound — and [S035](/wiki/seminars/S035.md) already assessed you on the finding.

**The limits: first-party documentation, definitional rather than empirical.** It makes no performance claim that could be independently checked and offers no comparison against other vendors' capacity models. **Per-model ratios, minimum deployment sizes, default quota allocations and prices live on companion pages that were not fetched and are not reproduced here.** Perishable: names, defaults, ratios and portal paths change.

### Attribution is what turns a total into a price

A total tells you the bill. **A price tells you which part of your proof was expensive**, and that requires per-consumer accounting that does not exist until somebody builds it.

The gateway argument is stated cleanly enough to teach verbatim in structure. Tokens are the scarce resource; providers assign quota as tokens per minute; those tokens must be distributed across **"different applications, developer teams, or departments within the company"**. With one application a limit on the deployment suffices. As soon as there is a second, **"you need to make sure that one app doesn't use the whole TPM quota and block other apps"** ([research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md)). That is the noisy-neighbour problem, and the general form is worth keeping: **a shared finite resource with no per-consumer accounting will be consumed by whichever consumer is least careful.**

Five mechanisms matter for a proof pack. The **token limit policy** enforces a limit or quota over a chosen period, keyed on **"any counter key, such as subscription key, originating IP address, or an arbitrary key defined through a policy expression"** — and it can **precalculate prompt tokens at the gateway**, so a request that would exceed the limit never reaches the backend. That is fail-fast: **reject at the edge rather than paying to discover the rejection downstream.** The **`llm-emit-token-metric`** policy attaches custom dimensions to emitted metrics, and **dimensions are what make a metric answerable** — total consumption tells you the bill, consumption per interface per population tells you who to talk to. **Priority load balancing** exists specifically **"to ensure optimal utilization of specific Microsoft Foundry endpoints, particularly those purchased as PTU instances"** — the direct operational consequence of having paid for dedicated capacity is to route to it first and overflow elsewhere. **Spillover** completes the pattern from the capacity side, redirecting requests to a standard deployment when a provisioned one is saturated, triggered by an error response rather than by prediction. And the **circuit breaker** takes its trip duration from the backend's `Retry-After` header, recovering on the failing service's schedule rather than a guessed one.

Two cautions belong to this session and neither is in the documentation. **Semantic caching trades correctness for cost, and the similarity threshold is the dial** — set it loosely and the system returns an answer to a question that was not asked, silently. And **logging prompts and completions creates a privacy surface the source does not discuss at all**, in the same page that recommends logging for billing and auditing. **Both cautions are the research note's own analysis rather than claims from the source, and are recorded as such.**

**The limits: first-party documentation with a commercial frame, definitional rather than empirical. No benchmarks, no latency figures, no cost-reduction measurements and no comparison against other gateway products.** Capability varies by service tier; the Foundry integration and the unified model API are **preview**; and policy names and attributes drift between revisions, so treat every fragment as illustrative.

### Why the assessment is a defence of price, and how the judgement is made

The form of this occasion is not arbitrary. It is an entrustment decision, and the assessment literature is specific about what one requires.

An entrustable professional activity is a bounded unit of work a supervisor could hand over whole, and **an entrustment decision implies the acceptance of risk, because it can never be fully predicted what the person will encounter** ([research/08-assessment-epas/ten-cate-epa-framework.md](/research/08-assessment-epas/ten-cate-epa-framework.md)). That is why the defence contains **what-if questions** rather than a walkthrough: retrospective explanation shows you understood the case you met, and **counterfactual questioning is the only available evidence about the cases you did not.** It is also why the assessor proposes a cheaper proof of their own — that is the what-if in its most useful form.

The same source names three professionalism characteristics in behavioural terms, and all three are visible in this pack rather than assertable about you: **integrity**, truthfulness in what you report to a supervising professional; **reliability**, stable and conscientious work; and **humility**, the ability to observe your own limitations and ask for help. **The unproven list is a humility instrument with a page number.**

Why the outcome is recorded as reasoned narrative rather than a mark comes from the other side of that literature. Assessors reading real evidence **relied on narrative to form the judgement and used scores only to confirm impressions already formed from prose**; assessors **anchored early and tended to hold their initial judgement even against seemingly disconfirming evidence**; and identical verdicts frequently rested on **entirely different reasoning** ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/govaerts-narrative-assessment.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/govaerts-narrative-assessment.md)). The consequences for this occasion are direct: **the assessor writes a reason rather than a score, high-stakes judgements should not be made by one person alone, and the reasoning is recorded so that divergence is discussable rather than invisible.** **Limits: medical education, one institution, one portfolio type; the transfer to engineering assessment is this programme's argument.**

One last discipline, from the evaluation practitioner literature, applies to every threshold in your pack: **"pass rate is a product decision"** ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/husain-your-ai-product-needs-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/husain-your-ai-product-needs-evals.md)). If your pack reports a threshold, it must report who decided it and why. **A single case study, no control condition, no measured improvement, tooling dated to early 2024.**

## The workflow

**Attach the price, make it attributable, and then attack the pack.**

**Step 1 — write the ledger before you write anything else**, and include the time of people who were not on your team. **A proof's price is mostly other people's attention.**

**Step 2 — find the load-bearing evidence.** The one piece the commitment actually turned on. Everything else is context, insurance or interest, and you now know which. **This is the only compounding mechanism the stage possesses**, because the corpus it draws on supplies none.

**Step 3 — build the traffic model from what actually happened, and state every simplification.** Requests per minute observed in your [S038](/wiki/seminars/S038.md) window; typical input and output sizes; an assumed cache rate. Write the three inputs and, beside each, the population it was inferred from and the population it excludes. **The non-invokers are not in your traffic model, and if the commitment converts, they will be in the traffic.**

**Step 4 — decide the commitment shape and apply deploy-verify-commit.** Shared capacity for a proof, dedicated capacity only where the latency guarantee is the thing being bought — and never buy the discount before you have confirmed the capacity exists, because a reservation discounts a meter and holds nothing. **Do not present a dedicated-capacity plan as an autoscaling strategy**; the documentation advises against scaling provisioned deployments with traffic and gives two reasons, one of which is that the capacity may not be there when you scale back up.

**Step 5 — put the gateway in front and make the spend attributable per population.** A total is not a price. Emit token metrics with dimensions that match the populations in your test record, so the pack can say which part of the proof was expensive rather than only what the proof cost.

```xml
<!-- apim/policies/proof-pack.inbound.xml  — ILLUSTRATIVE. Policy names and
     attributes drift between revisions; confirm against your own tier. -->
<inbound>

  <!-- Fail fast at the edge. Precalculating prompt tokens means an over-budget
       request never reaches the backend, so you do not pay to discover the
       rejection. Set the budget yourself: it is a policy decision, not a fact. -->
  <llm-token-limit
      counter-key="@(context.Request.Headers.GetValueOrDefault("x-proof-population","unattributed"))"
      tokens-per-minute="SET_THIS_YOURSELF"
      estimate-prompt-tokens="true"
      remaining-tokens-variable-name="remainingTokens" />

  <!-- Dimensions are what make a metric answerable. Total spend tells you the
       bill. Spend per population tells you which half of the proof was
       expensive - and these populations are the ones from the S038 record,
       deliberately, so the ledger and the test record can be reconciled. -->
  <llm-emit-token-metric namespace="proof-pack">
    <dimension name="Population"
               value="@(context.Request.Headers.GetValueOrDefault("x-proof-population","unattributed"))" />
    <dimension name="Artefact"   value="@(context.Api.Id)" />
    <dimension name="Stage"      value="S036-S039" />
  </llm-emit-token-metric>

</inbound>

<!-- NOTE FOR THE PACK, NOT FOR THE GATEWAY: logging prompts and completions is
     recommended in the documentation for billing and auditing, and the
     documentation does not discuss the privacy surface that creates. State in
     the pack what was logged, who could read it, and for how long. -->
```

**Step 6 — design the cheaper proof properly.** Not a shorter version — a different one. Which instrument would you not run? What would you have accepted on testimony? Which population would you have skipped, and what would that have risked? **A cheaper proof that risks nothing has not been designed, it has been trimmed.**

**Step 7 — write the unproven list.** Every claim a reader could reasonably infer from the pack that you did not establish: feasibility at scale, behaviour on data you did not see, adoption by people you did not test, cost under real traffic. **It is the pack's most valuable page and it is the one that gets deleted before circulation**, which is why it is an assessed element here.

**Step 8 — attack the pack with the model.** Ask it to argue that the commitment should be withdrawn: that the evidence does not support the ask, that the traffic model is inferred from a population that excludes the eventual users, and that a cheaper or better-targeted proof would have supported a different commitment. Then ask it to name the page a reader would cite back at you in six months. Keep what is better than your own reasoning; record what you rejected and why. **An agent that endorsed your pack was not used as an instrument**, which is [S001](/wiki/seminars/S001.md)'s claim closing the stage where it opened the programme.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That a price and a total are different objects — you can see the same spend resolve into two populations, one of which turns out to have consumed most of it. And that capacity, unlike quota, is a thing that either exists this afternoon or does not, which converts *we could scale this* from an assurance into a check somebody can run.

## What good looks like

These are the assessed elements. Each is checked as present and adequate, or not.

- **The cost of the whole proof is stated**, in the units it was paid in, including other people's time and anything consumed.
- **Any capacity or spend figure names the traffic model it was inferred from**, and the population that model excludes.
- **Spend is attributable rather than total.** The pack can say which part of the proof was expensive, not only what the proof cost.
- **Any commitment of capacity follows verify-then-commit.** A plan that buys a discount before confirming availability is a failed element, whatever the arithmetic says.
- **The cheaper proof is designed and specific.** A named instrument, a named omission, and what it would have risked. *We could have moved faster* is a failed element.
- **Something is named as having been unnecessary**, and the pack says what made it look necessary at the time.
- **The commitment is described in its currency**, and the pack says what the giver could have refused.
- **A refusal, a non-acceptance or an unconverted stakeholder appears somewhere in the pack.** A stage in which everybody agreed with everything is submitted with that stated as its finding.
- **The unproven list is longer than the proven one**, or the pack argues explicitly why it is not.
- **Populations, promises and inspections are still separated** in the assembled pack, not only in the records they came from.
- **Nothing in the pack asserts a magnitude that was not measured by a named instrument.**

**Nothing else is assessed.** Whether the proof succeeded is not marked. Whether the commitment was obtained is not marked. The quality of the prototype is not marked. Whether the platform work is elegant is not marked. The number of participants, spikes or iterations is not marked. The pack's presentation is not marked. Whether your cheaper proof would in fact have worked is not marked.

## How it goes wrong

- **The thorough pack** — everything was investigated, nothing was skipped, and the price is enormous. *Warning sign:* you are proud of the coverage.
- **The unpriced proof** — the pack reports findings and never what they took. *Warning sign:* your cost ledger contains only your own hours.
- **The total mistaken for a price** — one spend figure, unattributed, so nothing in the pack can say which half was expensive. *Warning sign:* your metrics have no dimensions on them.
- **The confident traffic model** — a capacity figure derived from a handful of sessions and presented without its provenance. *Warning sign:* your cost estimate is more precise than your participant count.
- **Commit before verify** — the plan buys reserved capacity to secure a rate, before anybody confirmed the capacity exists. *Warning sign:* the pack treats a discount as a guarantee.
- **The retrospective necessity** — everything you did turns out, on inspection, to have been essential. *Warning sign:* item 3 is blank.
- **The smoothed pack** — the blocking absence, the unconverted sponsor and the compliment-only test have all become clean lines. *Warning sign:* the pack reads better than the fortnight went.
- **The generous cheaper proof** — your cheaper design removes only things that were obviously optional. *Warning sign:* it does not risk anything.
- **The proven-by-assembly effect** — the pack is large, and its size is doing the persuading. *Warning sign:* your unproven list is shorter than your contents page.

## Exercises

**These are preparation and none of them is assessed.** The assessed objects are the proof pack and the defence, against the elements listed above and nothing else. Do these before the occasion; bring the outputs, and expect to be asked about them rather than marked on them.

**1 · Separate quota from capacity, then find the analogue in your own pack.** Write the difference in one sentence each, then name the place in your engagement where you had permission for something that was not actually available.

> **Answer key.** **Quota** is a policy limit enforced by the platform, scoped per subscription, region and deployment type, and it **costs nothing and guarantees nothing**. **Capacity** is what is actually deployable right now, allocated at deployment time, held for the deployment's lifetime, and **it changes through the day.** The documented consequence is that having quota does not mean a deployment will succeed. **The analogue in almost every engagement is access**: you were granted permission to the data in week one and received it in week five, and the pack that recorded the grant rather than the receipt recorded permission as availability. **If you could not find an analogue, check whether you recorded any approval as though it were a resource.**

**2 · Order the three procurement steps and defend the ordering.** Deploy, verify, commit — or commit, deploy, verify? Write which the documentation states and why the other one is tempting.

> **What you should find.** The documentation states it directly: reservations do not guarantee capacity, so **create deployments to confirm availability first, then purchase the reservation** to lock in the rate. The reverse is tempting because a commitment feels like it secures the thing it is a commitment to, and because the discount is the visible object while the capacity is not. **A reservation discounts a meter; it holds nothing.** Then write the same sentence about your [S039](/wiki/seminars/S039.md) proposal: what did you ask somebody to commit to before you had verified it was available?

**3 · Go back to your own item 3, then break it.** Find what you wrote about what you produced that nobody used. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference.

> **Criterion.** A passing answer names **a specific artefact, and what made it look necessary at the time.** The second clause is the whole exercise. *We over-documented* fails, because it names a habit rather than a decision. *The second spike on the retrieval path, which looked necessary because the charter named retrieval and nobody had yet established that the log lacked the entries entirely* passes, because it identifies the belief that justified the spend and the later finding that dissolved it. **The commonest failing answer is a blank item 3**, and the second commonest is an answer that names something the candidate had already decided was optional — which costs nothing to admit and demonstrates nothing.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Attribute one spend figure to two populations.** Using the gateway policy from step 5 of the workflow, split one consumption figure across the populations in your [S038](/wiki/seminars/S038.md) record and say which one surprised you.

> **What to notice.** Dimensions are what make a metric answerable, and the split is almost always uneven in a way that is invisible in the total. **The population that consumed most is frequently the one you tested least**, because heavy use and willingness to sit in a session are different dispositions. If you cannot split the figure at all, that is the finding: the proof ran without per-consumer accounting, and **a shared finite resource with no per-consumer accounting is consumed by whichever consumer is least careful.**

**5 · Write the cheaper proof and cost the risk you added.** One named instrument you would not run, one named omission, and what each would have risked.

> **Scoring criterion.** The cheaper proof passes if **removing the item creates a specific way the commitment could have been wrong.** *We could have run fewer sessions* fails. *We could have skipped the failure-first run and accepted the maintainer's account of the log, which would have risked funding a pilot on a log that cannot carry causal entries* passes. **A cheaper proof that risks nothing has been trimmed, not designed**, and trimming is the answer the assessor sees most often.

**6 · Rehearse the defence against a rival proof.** Have a peer read only your charter and your commitment proposal, then propose a cheaper proof of their own and ask what it would have missed.

> **What to notice.** This is the defence's actual shape and it is a what-if question rather than a walkthrough, because retrospective explanation only shows that you understood the case you met. **A candidate who cannot say what the rival proof would have missed has priced their work without understanding it. A candidate who concedes that nothing would have been missed has just designed the better instrument in the room, and that is a pass** — the occasion marks whether the price was counted, understood and reduced, never whether it was right.

## What this does not establish

This assessment shows that you can price a body of evidence, identify its load-bearing part and design a cheaper alternative. **It does not show that the cheaper alternative would have worked**, and it cannot.

**Nothing in the material behind this stage establishes what a proof should cost.** The practice corpus contains **no cost side anywhere** — every claim in it is a gross benefit, and staffing, compute, the cost of the discovery activity itself and opportunity cost are all absent. The open playbook offers **no comparative evidence** for any of its practices. The customer-conversation handbook **establishes no effect and no threshold and says so.** There is no benchmark against which a proof's price can be judged, and this session does not invent one: it assesses whether the price was counted, understood and reduced, never whether it was right.

**The platform layer establishes how capacity is bought and nothing about whether any of it is architecturally best.** Both sources are first-party documentation, definitional rather than empirical, single-vendor, with a commercial frame. The capacity page **makes no performance claim that could be checked independently**, and its concrete ratios, minimum deployment sizes, quota defaults and prices live on companion pages that were not fetched and are **not reproduced here**. The gateway page offers **no benchmarks, no latency figures, no cost-reduction measurements and no comparison against other gateway products**; its capabilities vary by service tier; and two of the features it describes are **preview**. **The correctness risk of semantic caching and the privacy surface created by logging prompts and completions are the research note's own analysis and are not claims the documentation makes** — the documentation does not discuss either.

**The claim that naming the load-bearing evidence makes the next engagement cheaper is design reasoning with no external warrant.** It is the stage's only compounding mechanism and nobody has tested it. [S086](/wiki/seminars/S086.md) and [S089](/wiki/seminars/S089.md) revisit it, at which point a cohort will have enough packs to find out.

**The assessment form is borrowed from a clinical literature and the borrowing is an argument.** Entrustment presupposes real work, a legally responsible supervisor and a licensure endpoint; a programme constructs the stakes, and the construction is the hard part. The primer is **guidance from an advocate reporting adoption rather than effectiveness**, and its full evidence standard — multiple trained observers producing documented, deliberated judgements — is **more than this occasion implements**, which is stated here rather than glossed. The narrative-assessment findings come from **two studies at one medical school with one portfolio type**, using think-aloud methods that cannot capture fast or automatic processing.

**No magnitude from any of it is asserted anywhere in this wiki. Direction and mechanism only.**
