# S039 · Requirements as Negotiated Constraint

**Module:** [M08](/wiki/modules/M08.md) · **Quarter:** [Q4](/wiki/quarters/Q4.md) · **Complexity class:** 3
**Strands:** PE · SY · MS
**Whitepaper:** [WP-039](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-039.md)
**Depends on:** S029, S034, S038
**Platform anchor:** Foundry model-deployment quota and the published quota-increase criterion · API Management `llm-token-limit` as the enforced constraint · **MCP servers:** Azure MCP Server (write; monitor and quota namespaces) · Microsoft Learn MCP Server

---

## What this day is for

A requirement is not a fact about the world. It is **an agreement between two parties about what will be built, arrived at under constraints neither fully controls, and it can be renegotiated.**

Students treat requirements as given, which produces two failures. The first is silent over-delivery: the engineer builds to a constraint that was never load-bearing, at a cost nobody asked for. The second is silent failure: the engineer accepts a constraint that cannot be met and discovers this late.

Both are avoided by the same move — **finding out which constraints are actually immovable, which is rarely the set that is stated as immovable.**

**Today the constraint is a real one and the other party has a published criterion.** The substrate's users are refused because a token limit says so; the token limit is what it is because a model-deployment quota says so; and that quota can be increased by asking. The party who decides publishes what will persuade them: **quota increase requests are processed in the order received, and priority goes to customers who actively use their existing allocation — requests that do not meet that condition may be denied.** That sentence is the day's material. It states the constraint's purpose in the words of the party enforcing it, it names the evidence that moves it, and it means the negotiation is a research act rather than a rhetorical one.

The day's professional demand: negotiate one constraint successfully, using evidence from the module's own four days.

## The delivery standard on this day

This day carries **principle 3 of the [FDE Delivery Standard](/wiki/program/14-FDE-Delivery-Standard.md): extend what the customer already runs before introducing anything new**, in that page's wording rather than as a quotation. The published criterion at phase 4 point 9 is that principle written by the other party to the negotiation — priority goes to those actively using the allocation they already hold. Here, extending what exists is not merely the preferred move; it is the evidence that moves the constraint at all.

**What the principle costs here is the interesting design.** The architecture you are not allowed to build is usually the better one, and a student who has only ever built greenfield has not met the condition that defines this work. It also costs step 5's hour: establishing what is already held, and how it is actually consumed, is slower and less rewarding than proposing something new — and it is the whole of the trade, because a request made without it is the one the criterion says may be denied.

**The playbook artefact that belongs here is the trade study** — requirements first, then criteria, then candidates, then evaluation against the stated criteria. It carries a negative rule that is phase 4 point 8 in a second register: **a trade study should be avoided where there is a clear solution choice**, so a study run on a settled question is a defect rather than diligence. **What the form does not supply is point 5's requirement** that the comparison be evaluable by the party who has to agree to it; that is this design's addition and the source is silent on it. **Neither source reports any outcome for the practice**, and [WP-039](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-039.md)'s evidence status says so.

**Amended 2026-07-30, on a reading of the playbook in full. Two further forms belong here and were previously invisible, because neither is reachable from the site's navigation as a template.**

The first is **the trade study template**, which is a different page from the trade study guidance quoted above — and **the guidance page carries no required fields at all.** The template does: who conducted it, against which backlog work item, in which sprint, the decision and the decision makers; then desired outcomes, evaluation criteria, optional key metrics and constraints, an accessibility section, solution hypotheses; then **one block per candidate, each pairing an experiment with the evidence it produced**; then results, decision and next steps. **The pairing is the form's argument** — a candidate earns its comparison by what you did to it and what that produced, not by what you think of it — and it is what makes step 6's trade something the other party can check rather than something they must accept.

The second is **the four design-review templates**, taught at point 12, and they are why this day now asks for design writing at the scope of the decision rather than in one format for everything.

## Phase 1 · Pretest and prediction (25 min)

Five items. A real substrate requirement is supplied with five stated constraints.

1. Which of these five are immovable?
2. For each you called movable, what would you offer in exchange?
3. Which one, if moved, would most change what you build?
4. Predict: how many of the five will turn out to be movable?
5. **Copilot prediction, sealed.** You will shortly ask the agent to assemble the utilisation evidence that supports your case. Write down what it will produce, and **name the one thing about your own consumption it will get wrong or omit**. Sealed with items 1 to 4 and opened at phase 8.

Item 4's answers are low. Most stated constraints move.

**Warrant.** Pretesting on constraint mobility before it is discussed. The prediction-to-actual gap is the day's opening finding and is checkable against the substrate's own history. Item 5 is sealed at phase 1 per instrumentation rule 8, and it is the day's only item whose answer depends on reading a metric definition rather than on judging a person.

## Phase 3 · Contrasting cases and generation (45 min)

**Executed, not read.** Groups receive **one requirement and four negotiation transcripts**, each between an engineer and the same requester — and, for each transcript, the evidence pack that engineer took into the room. The transcripts are read. **The evidence packs are rebuilt**: groups reproduce each one against the substrate's real metrics, through the Azure MCP Server's monitor tooling, and find out what each engineer could actually see.

- **Transcript A** — the engineer accepts everything. The requirement is met and costs four times what it should. Their pack is empty.
- **Transcript B** — the engineer refuses a constraint and offers nothing. The requester escalates. Their pack is a cost figure with no denominator.
- **Transcript C** — the engineer offers a trade the requester cannot evaluate, and the requester agrees without understanding. The problem surfaces later. Their pack is technically correct and unreadable.
- **Transcript D** — the engineer moves a constraint by supplying evidence that the constraint's purpose is served another way. Their pack shows consumption against allocation, split by outcome, over a period long enough to establish a pattern.

Groups judge each and commit, then rebuild, then the outcomes and costs are supplied.

Rebuilding produces a finding the reading does not. **Pack D is the only one that splits its request counts by status code**, which is the difference between "we are at ninety per cent of our allocation" and "we are at ninety per cent of our allocation and turning away one caller in six." The other three could have done it — the dimension is there — and none did.

Task after: **state what transcript D's engineer knew that the others did not.**

The answer is: what the constraint was *for*.

**Demonstrated rather than operated.** The quota increase request itself is shown — the form, the published criterion, a real submission and its response — and is **not operated by students**. The programme does not submit speculative capacity requests on a real subscription, and the outcome would not be in the programme's gift in any case. **It is not assessed.** What is operated is everything that would go into one, and the negotiation that is operated is the internal one at phase 5.

**Warrant.** Contrasting cases across negotiation stances with real cost outcomes. Transcript C remains the subtler payload — agreement without comprehension is worse than refusal and looks better. **The rebuild is what converts the phase's lesson from a judgement about people into a checkable property of an artefact**: three engineers had the same telemetry available and one of them queried it.

## Phase 4 · Consolidating instruction (50 min)

1. **A requirement is an agreement,** not a fact. It was arrived at by people, under constraints, and it can be revisited.
2. **Every constraint has a purpose,** and the purpose is often served by something cheaper. Ask what it is *for* before asking whether it can move.
3. **Four kinds of constraint:** external and genuinely fixed, organisational and negotiable with the right person, technical and dependent on a choice you could revisit, and inherited with no remaining purpose. **This list omits constraints that exist to protect an interest nobody will state, which are common, are not negotiable by evidence, and are S082's material. Safe when the requester can tell you what a constraint is for; unsafe when they cannot or will not, where treating the constraint as an evidence problem will fail and may damage the relationship.**
4. **What you bring to a negotiation:** evidence about cost, evidence about what the constraint is for, and an alternative that serves the purpose.
5. **The trade must be evaluable by the other party** — transcript C's failure. If they cannot assess what they are giving up, agreement is not consent.
6. **Over-delivery as a failure,** and why it is invisible: nobody complains about a system that does more than required, and the cost falls on time that was not spent elsewhere.
7. **Recording the agreement,** in S034's three-part form: what each party may assume, what they will observe, what happens if it is not met.
8. **When not to negotiate.** Sometimes the constraint is right and the answer is to build it.
9. **A constraint whose owner publishes its purpose.** Model-deployment quota is allocated per subscription, per region, per model, per deployment type, in tokens per minute. It is **a policy limit and explicitly not a guarantee that capacity exists**, which means it is the second kind of constraint and not the first — organisational and negotiable, wearing the costume of a physical bound. And the party who decides has published what persuades them: **priority goes to those actively using their existing allocation.** That converts point 2 into a research task with a known target. **Point 4's three components are, in this case, literally specified by the other side.**
10. **A default the platform chose for you, and one it will not let you choose.** Quota is allocated in **units of capacity**, and each unit carries a fixed pairing of requests per minute and tokens per minute that **varies by model** — for some models a unit is one request per minute and ten thousand tokens; for others it is six requests and one thousand. **You do not have granular control over the two as independent values.** So a requester who says "we need two hundred requests per minute" has named a quantity that is not purchasable on its own, and the engineer who agrees to it has agreed to something they cannot buy. That is not a negotiating failure; it is a failure to find out what the currency is before trading in it — **and it is the platform's version of transcript C, with the engineer on the receiving end.**
11. **Perishable and durable, separated.** The quota mechanics, the capacity-unit ratios, the published criterion's exact wording and every figure attached to them are **perishable**, and the criterion in particular is a commercial policy that can change without notice. Durable: a constraint has a purpose; the purpose is sometimes published by the party enforcing it; the currency of a trade is not always the quantity that was named. **Safe** for negotiating this constraint with this party this quarter, and for the general move of looking for a published criterion before assembling evidence. **Unsafe** as a claim that constraints generally come with published criteria — almost none do, and a student who expects one will stop looking when they find none.
12. **Design writing has a scope, and moving up one scope changes exactly one thing.** *(Added 2026-07-30.)* The playbook supplies four design-review templates rather than one, and the difference between them is the whole lesson. A **task** review is short: problem, goals, non-goals, **proposed options**, technology choices, open questions, resources — note that it asks for options rather than for a design, because at task scope the work is choosing. A **feature or story** review replaces the options with a proposed design and adds technology, non-functional requirements, dependencies, risks and mitigations. A **technical spike** review is a different genre again — goal, method, evidence, conclusions, next steps — and its own page states the constraint that keeps it honest: the goal of a spike is fact-finding, not recommendation. And a **milestone or epic** review is the story template **plus exactly one heading, `Operationalization`, and nothing else.** That is the day's most precise available statement of what scope means: **a design crosses from story to milestone at the point it acquires a runtime owner**, and everything else about the document is unchanged. All four require a draft marker in the title while the document is open, which is the same move as the decision record entering review as a proposal rather than arriving settled. **The failure this replaces is one essay format for every decision**, which over-writes small choices and under-writes the ones that will be operated by somebody who is not in the room.
13. **A non-functional requirement has eight fields and two of them are assessable.** *(Added 2026-07-30.)* The playbook's capture form asks for a name, a description, a priority, a **measurement or metric**, a **verification method**, constraints, an owner, and dependencies. **Only the fourth and fifth are marked here, and the reason is that a requirement which cannot say how it would be verified has not been written** — it is a preference with a heading over it. The other six are useful and are not evidence of anything. **The same page supplies a catalogue of example thresholds — uptime, response time, concurrency, complexity, coverage, completion time — and cites nothing whatever for any of them.** You are given that catalogue on this day, explicitly labelled sourceless, precisely so that you can watch what it does to you: numbers in a table written in a confident voice read as benchmarks, and these are illustrations somebody typed. **Use it as an example bank. Never as a benchmark, and never as your justification.** Your own numbers are yours to defend, and point 5 applies to them — a threshold the other party cannot evaluate is not a requirement they have agreed to.

**Warrant.** Consolidation indexed to phase 3. Point 2 is the day's method. Point 3 carries the strong-form scope statement. Point 5 is the ethical core and connects to S013's unresolved concealment question. Point 8 exists to prevent the day producing engineers who negotiate everything, which is the module's characteristic overreach in its final form. Points 9 and 10 are the day's platform-specific content; point 10 satisfies the [Copilot Practice Standard](/wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md)'s default requirement and is chosen because the constraint it names is one the room will otherwise carry unexamined into phase 5. **Points 12 and 13 were added 2026-07-30 and both are artefact specifications rather than arguments**, which is why they sit at the end of the consolidation and are short: point 12's single-heading escalation is a fact about the source that students can check in a minute and will remember longer than a paragraph about scope, and point 13's two-field restriction is the cheapest available answer to a requirement written as an aspiration. **Point 13 also carries the day's second unsourced-magnitude specimen**, in the same register as the four the [FDE Delivery Standard](/wiki/program/14-FDE-Delivery-Standard.md) refuses — and it is the more instructive one, because the catalogue is not making a claim at all and will still be read as one.

## Phase 5 · Whole-task learning task (90 min)

**Class 3. The requirement is the student's to negotiate. This phase produces a build, and the build is an instrument rather than a remedy.**

Students take the module's running need, now characterised through S036 to S038, and:

1. **State the requirement** as currently understood, in the S038 vocabulary. **Where it is non-functional, state it in the eight-field form at point 13**, and expect only two of those fields to be marked.
2. **List its constraints** and classify each by point 3's four kinds.
3. **For each, state what it is for** — and mark the ones where you do not know.
4. **Identify the constraint whose movement would most change the outcome.**
5. **Build the evidence pack.** Using Copilot in agent mode through the Azure MCP Server, assemble consumption against allocation for the deployment behind the constraint, over a period long enough to establish a pattern, **split by outcome** so that refusals are visible rather than averaged away. State the pack's own floor: the finest available sampling interval is one minute, so any behaviour faster than that is not in your evidence and you say so.
6. **Prepare the trade:** the cost evidence, the purpose evidence, and the alternative — **written at the scope of the decision.** Name which of point 12's four scopes this decision sits at, and use that template. If you claim the milestone scope, `Operationalization` is present and filled; if you claim the story scope, say who will operate the result and why that does not yet need its own section. **Choosing the scope is the assessed judgement, not filling the headings.**
7. **Negotiate it** — with the requester and the substrate's capacity owner, present for the last thirty minutes.
8. **Record the agreement** in S034's three-part form, or record the refusal and why.

**The requester and the capacity owner are present for step 7.** Each student gets six minutes.

**The artefact carries three further elements**, per the [Copilot Practice Standard](/wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md).

- **Prompt record.** What you asked the agent for at step 5, in order, including what you abandoned.
- **Intervention record.** Every point at which you corrected it. **An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that the pack's own design would have caught an intervention-worthy error — a stated floor on the sampling interval, or an assertion that the query's status-code split accounts for the whole request count, are both accepted, and both are the check that would have caught the phase 6 failure.
- **Frontier statement.** One thing the agent did faster than you could have, and one thing it did badly that you had to catch.

**Fading:** step 3 is worked with the room; step 6's trade form is supplied. Step 5 is unsupported beyond the tooling.

**Warrant.** Whole-task at class 3 with live negotiation. Step 3's do-not-know marks are the day's most useful output and are frequently more numerous than students expect. **Step 5 is the day's practical turn and it is deliberately placed before step 6 rather than inside it**: a trade assembled from evidence you gathered is a different object from one assembled from evidence you were given, and the difference is visible in what the student is willing to concede.

## Phase 6 · Error clinic (40 min)

Instructor first: something built on the substrate to a constraint that turned out to have no purpose, discovered two years later — with attention to **who would have been able to say so at the time, and why nobody asked them**.

Then the agent case: a Copilot-built utilisation pack that was **confidently wrong and passed review**, and which was used to argue against a requester who was telling the truth. Asked to show whether the service had been available to a team that was complaining about failures, the agent chose the metric named availability, wrote a correct query, and reported 100% over the whole period. The team had been refused hundreds of times. **The metric subtracts server errors from total calls, and server errors are responses of 500 and above** — so a refusal that returns 429 does not appear in it at all, by definition, and the definition is published.

The pack went into a meeting. The requester was told their evidence did not match the system's. They had no way to argue, and they were right.

Attention goes to why review did not catch it: **two engineers checked that the query was correct and it was.** Nobody read the metric's definition, because the metric's name was the word the requester had used. This is the same failure the room saw at S037 in a lower-stakes setting, arriving here with a person on the other side of it.

Then students: something you built because you were told to, or a number you took into a conversation today without checking what it counted.

**Warrant.** Error management targeting inherited constraints, which are the fourth kind and the one with no living owner. The agent case is the [Copilot Practice Standard](/wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md)'s required addition and it is placed here rather than at S037's lower stakes because **the module's argument is that a measurement error becomes an ethical failure at the moment it is used to overrule somebody** — which is point 5's evaluable-trade requirement seen from the other end.

Pairs. Each student states their step 8 agreement, and the partner — playing the requester's manager, who was not in the room — must decide whether to accept it.

They may ask one question.

## Phase 8 · Calibration and commitment (20 min)

- Items 1 to 4 resolved against the substrate's actual history of that requirement.
- The room's step 3 do-not-know counts shown as a distribution.
- **Item 5 opened.** The sealed predictions about what the agent would get wrong in the evidence pack are read against what it produced, and the cohort's hit rate is recorded.
- **The requester and the capacity owner state, in the room, which negotiation they found most useful and why.**
- **What was simulated and what was not, said plainly.** The negotiation you just conducted had a real constraint, a real requester and real evidence, and it had none of a real negotiation's pressure: nobody in the room lost anything, nobody was defending a budget, and nobody will be accountable for the outcome. **The easiest part of a real negotiation has been simulated and the hardest part has not.** A student who succeeded today has learned that preparation works, which is true and is not the same as learning that it is sufficient. S085 is where the rest arrives.
- Each student names one constraint they are currently working under whose purpose they cannot state.

## Assessment

Specification item in PE201. Checked elements: the requirement in the S038 vocabulary, **and in the eight-field form where it is non-functional**; constraints classified by kind; a purpose statement or an explicit do-not-know for each; the identified highest-leverage constraint; **the evidence pack, with its outcome split and its stated sampling floor**; the prepared trade with all three components, **written at a named scope in the matching design-review template**; the negotiation outcome; the agreement or refusal recorded in three-part form; **the prompt record; the intervention record; the frontier statement.**

**Two of those elements were added 2026-07-30 and both are deliberately narrow.** Where the requirement is non-functional, **only its measurement or metric and its verification method are marked** — the other six fields must be present and are not assessed, because a form marked field by field becomes a form filled field by field. And the scope element marks **whether the scope was chosen correctly and defended**, not whether the headings were completed: a story-scope design that should have been a milestone-scope design fails on the choice, and a well-populated template at the wrong scope fails with it.

Per the [Assessment Architecture](/wiki/program/03-Assessment-Architecture.md)'s rule 3, **the phase 7 outcome must be recorded in the artefact** — whether the absent manager accepted, and if not, what they asked.

**A constraint list containing no do-not-know marks fails the specification.**

**An evidence pack that reports an availability or success figure without stating which response codes it excludes fails the specification.**

**A non-functional requirement with no verification method fails the specification.** A requirement that cannot say how it would be checked has not been written.

**A threshold justified by the playbook's example catalogue fails the specification.** The catalogue carries no source and is handed to you under that label; borrowing a number from it is not a justification, it is a citation of somebody's illustration.

**An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that the pack's own design would have caught an intervention-worthy error.

Nothing else is assessed, including whether the negotiation succeeded.

## What to take from this day

**The claim to hold.** A requirement is an agreement between two parties, not a fact about the world. **Every constraint has a purpose, and you find it before you ask whether the constraint moves** — and the currency of a trade is not always the quantity that was named.

**What the sources license, and what they do not.** Requirements as negotiated agreements, constraint-purpose inquiry and over-delivery as an invisible cost are grounded in [research/05-fde-craft/fisher-ury-getting-to-yes.md](/research/05-fde-craft/fisher-ury-getting-to-yes.md) and [research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md). **Direction and mechanism only; no magnitude is asserted.** The published quota criterion states a priority and no turnaround, no approval rate and no definition of what counts as actively using an allocation — so this programme makes no claim about whether any particular request would be granted.

**Guidance for your own work.**

- **Mark the constraints whose purpose you cannot state.** Those marks are the most useful thing the exercise produces, and there are usually more than you expect.
- **Split your evidence by outcome.** An average hides the refusals, which are the thing the other party has to see.
- **State the trade so the other party can evaluate it.** Agreement without comprehension is not consent.
- **Say what your figure excludes, in the same sentence as the figure.** On the [Agent Configuration Standard](/wiki/program/13-Agent-Configuration-Standard.md) a query result is an *observed result* bounded by what the metric counts; carrying it into a room as proof that somebody is mistaken is *unsupported inference*.
- **Write the design at the scope of the decision.** Four templates exist because four scopes exist, and the one thing the milestone scope adds is `Operationalization` — so the question that decides your scope is *who operates this*, not *how big does it feel*.
- **Never justify a threshold from somebody's example table.** The catalogue you were handed is a set of illustrations with no source, written in the same confident voice as a benchmark. Your number is yours to defend, and the field that makes it a requirement is the one naming how it would be verified.

**Where this is easy to get wrong.** Taking a number into a negotiation without reading what it counts. Two engineers checked the query and it was right, the requester was right too, and they had no way to argue.
