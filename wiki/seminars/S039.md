# S039 · From Prototype to Commitment

**Module:** [M08](/wiki/modules/M08.md) · **Quarter:** [Q4](/wiki/quarters/Q4.md) · **Complexity class:** 3
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** Microsoft Foundry basic setup to standard setup — the documented transition from a prototyping topology without network isolation to a production one, treated as a deliberate act
**Whitepaper:** [WP-039](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-039.md)
**Retrieves from:** S009, S019, S037, S038
**Entry state:** this-programme-only

---

## The situation

The prototype has done its work. Somebody now has to give something up, or the stage produced an interesting fortnight and nothing else.

This is the point at which a proof either converts or becomes a story people tell warmly for months. **The warm version is the worst available outcome**, because it looks like progress and consumes the same calendar as progress.

There is also a sentence forming in the room, and you should notice it before it is said out loud: *so we just move it to production.* It is the most natural thing anybody will say today and it is wrong in a way you can prove with a documentation page.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught. Nobody is going to help you.

1. What are you asking for, from whom, and what does it cost them?
2. Write the refusal you would accept. What does it sound like, and what do you do next?
3. Somebody says they are very keen and asks you to keep them posted. What has been given?
4. What does the prototype entitle you to ask for, and what does it not?
5. Your ask succeeds and the engagement proceeds. What did you promise that you have not proved?

**Item 2 is the one candidates skip.** An ask with no acceptable refusal is not an ask; it is an announcement with a question mark. Item 4 is the one they answer optimistically, and the platform section below is where that optimism is priced.

## What you must produce

A **commitment proposal**, one page, addressed to a named role.

1. **The ask** — what specifically is being given up, by whom, when.
2. **The currency** — meaningful time, reputational exposure, or money. Say which, and say why that one.
3. **What it buys them**, in terms they hold rather than terms you hold.
4. **The evidence it rests on**, with a plain statement of what the prototype did and did not establish — including what your [S037](/wiki/seminars/S037.md) run could not have detected.
5. **The transition it actually funds** — what changes between the topology that produced the proof and the topology that could carry the work, named item by item rather than as *productionising*.
6. **The refusal path** — what you will do if they decline, stated so that declining is a real option, together with the threshold at which you will stop even if nobody refuses.
7. **The next state** — what changes about the relationship if they say yes, expressed as a thing that happens on a date rather than as a feeling.

## The material

### The failure is not being refused

Fitzpatrick's distinction is the one to hold. **Commitment** means a stakeholder gives up something they value. **Advancement** means the relationship moves to a defined next stage. The currencies are **meaningful time, reputational risk and money**, and the evidence strengthens as the cost to that particular person becomes more real ([research/05-fde-craft/fitzpatrick-the-mom-test.md](/research/05-fde-craft/fitzpatrick-the-mom-test.md)).

Two of his observations do most of the work today. **A trial is not automatically a commitment**: opening a free account may cost nothing, while onboarding a team, supplying real data, changing a workflow or accepting a case study may cost a great deal. And **a clear rejection is useful**, because it ends a relationship that was consuming your attention and supplies negative evidence you can act on. The genuine process failure is neither of those. It is **avoiding the ask and leaving with neither advancement nor rejection** — the friendly contact who repeatedly meets and praises and never commits.

**The limits: a practitioner handbook with no controlled comparison, no measured effect and no validated threshold, whose local copy carries no publisher, year or edition.** Its warrant is diagnostic and procedural. **Direction and mechanism only.**

### An ask needs a refusal its author could receive

The negotiation literature gives the structure for that, and it is more useful than the folk version of it.

A **bottom line** offers psychological protection and does damage: it hides information and prevents you from considering packages that meet your interests in unexpected ways. The better protection is a **BATNA** — the best action actually available to you if no agreement is reached — and the point about it that people skip is that **it is developed rather than named**. You generate the actions you could take without agreement, improve the most promising, and select the best. **An unknown BATNA is dangerous in both directions**: you may accept less than the alternative, or reject more ([research/05-fde-craft/fisher-ury-getting-to-yes.md](/research/05-fde-craft/fisher-ury-getting-to-yes.md)).

Three further instruments transfer directly. A **trip wire** is a threshold formulated in advance — less favourable than what you hope for, better than your BATNA — whose function is to force a pause before you drift into an unattractive commitment. A **micro-BATNA** is what you do if *this meeting* ends without agreement, and preparing an exit line for that case is explicitly recommended. And a **framework agreement** is an agreement-shaped working draft with the unresolved terms left visibly open, which is a far better artefact than a proposal that pretends everything is settled.

The source also names the design rule that most improves an ask's chance of being accepted for the right reasons: **make their decision easy to take and easy to justify to people who are not in the room.** A sponsor is rarely refusing you; they are frequently unable to explain the yes to somebody else. And it supplies the vocabulary for pressure tactics you should recognise and not use — **calculated delay, escalating demands after apparent progress, the hardhearted absent partner, and take-it-or-leave-it presented as though no process choice remained.** A deadline used as leverage is on that list, which is why *the window closes on Friday* is a warning sign in your own proposal rather than a strength.

**The limits, and one of them is a genuine constraint on this session.** The framework is conceptual and case-based: it defines failure modes, proposes practices and works through examples, and it is **not a controlled evaluation of negotiation methods.** Historical cases illustrate mechanism and do not isolate causes. **The note's translation of the framework into delivery work is explicitly marked as course-design inference, not a claim the authors make.** And the hardest caution for engineers: **not every constraint is negotiable.** A regulator, an accountable risk owner or a system owner may hold legitimate decision authority, and clarifying interests is not the same as having shared decision rights.

### Promotion is a re-architecture, and the vendor documents the seam

Here is where the natural reading of this session — the prototype gets promoted — stops being true, and you can check it rather than believe it.

Microsoft Foundry's setup options are not two settings on one system. The **basic setup** is documented as being **"for rapid prototyping and individual development when speed and ease of access are priorities"**, and the documentation states plainly that it **"lacks network isolation"**. The **standard setup** provides **"fine-grained control over data, security, and networking"**, with public networking for non-confidential workloads that need enterprise data controls and private networking for confidential workloads with strict compliance requirements ([research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md)).

**Read the first one again.** Every property that made the prototype fast is named as a property of the prototyping topology: speed, ease of access, and the absence of network isolation. Those are not accidental shortcomings you forgot to fix. **They are the thing you bought**, and buying the opposite is a different purchase. When you move, the set of things the system can reach changes, the set of people who can reach it changes, and a body of provisioning that did not exist has to be created and owned by somebody. **That is a re-architecture, and it is what your ask is actually for.**

The documentation is unusually candid in stating this, and the correct lesson is not *always choose standard*. It is that **prototyping topology and production topology are different things, and the transition between them is a deliberate act** rather than a configuration detail discovered at the end of a delivery.

Two further seams sit in the same family and both are decisions made early that bind late.

**The hosting fork.** Foundry's **prompt agents** use declarative configuration and are **"managed by Foundry with no agent compute"**; **hosted agents** run **"your code (containerized)"** on managed container compute. The line between them is whether you are running code, and therefore whether somebody is paying for and operating compute. A proof built as the first and a commitment sized for the second are not the same request.

**The distribution constraint.** The documentation states that **"only agents built with the Teams SDK, Microsoft 365 Agents SDK, or Foundry can be published to the Microsoft Commercial Store with the Agents Toolkit."** Distribution is constrained by build choice — **a decision taken at the start binds at the end**, and if your ask includes a route to market you did not check, the refusal you get later will not be from the sponsor.

And the same rapid-versus-controlled trade appears once more at the integration layer: a Foundry agent reaches Microsoft 365 either by direct publish, which **"automatically provisions Azure Bot Service and Entra ID"**, or through an Agents Toolkit proxy app for **"advanced customization, debugging, and multi-environment deployment"**. **The fast path provisions things on your behalf that somebody will later have to own**, and the ask should name who.

**The limits travel with every use.** First-party selection guidance whose authority is definitional; **no adoption data, no failure-rate data, no cost comparison and no evidence that its recommended mappings produce better outcomes**; one vendor's options compared against each other; **read through search excerpts rather than full page fetches, with several comparison tables truncated**; and **highly perishable** — setup names and their boundaries are exactly the kind of thing that moves between releases, so re-verify the seam before you cost it.

### The currency may not be the one you think, and the prototype may be dissolving resistance it has not earned

The practice corpus behind this stage supplies two observations that cut in opposite directions, and holding both is the professional move.

The first is an advantage. **Working software is hard to argue with**, and political resistance dissolves in front of a running prototype in a way it does not in front of a document. The second is a diagnosis: **resistance originates in ownership of a process rather than in technical incapacity** ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md)).

Put them together and the warning is sharp. If resistance is about process ownership, then a demonstration that dissolves it has not answered the objection — **it has made the objection socially expensive to state.** You may now have a commitment against evidence you do not hold, which you will be delivering against in [Stage 5](/wiki/quarters/Q5.md), and the person whose process you are taking has not stopped believing what they believed.

It also tells you the currency. If your ask requires somebody to stop doing the thing that makes them useful, you are not asking for time. **You are asking for standing**, and standing is the most expensive of the three currencies and the least often named. Price it and say so in the proposal.

The negotiation frame supplies the repair, and it is the position-versus-interest move. *We need it on-premises*, *no production data*, *build us a dashboard* are proposed answers. The interests underneath may be residency, auditability, contractual exposure, reversibility, or a fear of uncontrolled access — and surfacing those can reveal a smaller pilot, a synthetic-data phase, a read-only view or a staged approval that meets the interest at a fraction of the ask. **This does not license you to override an authorised decision. It helps you present better options to the person who owns it.**

**Both corpus observations are practitioner judgement from access-controlled material with no evidence base of any kind, no recorded refusal and no cost side.**

### What the evidence entitles you to ask for

The last discipline is the one that makes the proposal survivable when it is quoted back at you in three months.

An evaluation is an experiment and inherits every limit of one, and the limit that matters in a proposal is **power**: a run that could not have detected the difference it was looking for has established nothing, and nothing in the result will say so ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md)). **Your proposal must therefore distinguish *we tested and found no problem* from *we could not have found one*.** The first is evidence. The second is a sentence that will be read as the first by everybody who was not there.

**That source is analytical rather than empirical, its illustrative magnitudes rest on stylised assumptions, and no number from it appears here.**

This is also where the ask is sized. A prototype tested with one population does not entitle you to a programme; a failure-first run that removed one design option entitles you to fund the removal of the next one. **Proportion is the whole of professional credibility here**, and it is checked in one place: whether the thing you are asking for is bigger than the thing you established.

## The workflow

**Write the refusal, price the transition, then write the ask.**

**Step 1 — write the refusal first, and write it as something you could receive.** What does the no sound like, in their words? What do you do the following morning? If either answer is punishment or absence, you have not designed an ask.

**Step 2 — develop the BATNA rather than naming it.** List what you would actually do without this commitment: a smaller pilot with a different sponsor, a synthetic-data phase, a narrower claim, a withdrawal. Improve the best one. **An ask made without this is made from a position you have not checked.**

**Step 3 — pick the currency, and check whose process you are asking somebody to give up.** Time, reputation or money — and if the answer is really standing, say so.

**Step 4 — price the transition against the documented seam.** Open the setup guidance. For your own artefact, write what changes between the topology that produced the proof and the topology that could carry the work: network isolation, who can reach it, what has to be provisioned, who owns what the fast path provisioned for you, and whether you are moving from no agent compute to somebody operating a container. **Date the page you read it on.**

**Step 5 — write the proposal as a framework agreement with the unresolved terms visible.** Leave the open brackets in. A proposal that pretends everything is settled invites a yes that means nothing.

```yaml
# docs/commitment/handover-pilot.yaml
addressed_to: "Operations manager, line 3"      # can actually give the thing

ask:
  what: "Two named shift leads, half a day a week, for six weeks, on their own cases."
  currency: standing                            # NOT time. See note.
  currency_note: >
    The handover is how the outgoing lead demonstrates they ran a good shift.
    Automating it takes something from them that is not hours. Priced as standing.
  what_it_buys_them: "A handover the night agency staff can act on without ringing."

evidence:
  established: "S037 run removed one design option: the log lacks causal entries."
  did_not_establish:
    - "That the log CAN carry them. Untested."
    - "Adoption. S038 exposure and engagement were different populations."
  could_not_have_detected: >
    The S037 set had two cases from one line on one shift pattern. It could not
    have detected a difference between lines, and 'we found no difference' and
    'we could not have found one' are the same sentence for that comparison.

transition_funded:                              # NOT "productionising"
  read_on: 2026-07-31                           # re-verify: this material moves
  from: "basic setup — documented as rapid prototyping, speed and ease of access, LACKS NETWORK ISOLATION"
  to:   "standard setup — fine-grained control over data, security and networking"
  deltas:
    - "Network isolation now exists, so every reach the prototype had must be re-declared."
    - "Identity and bot resources auto-provisioned by the fast path acquire a named owner."
    - "If we move from a no-agent-compute shape to our own container, somebody operates it."
  open: "[ WHO OWNS THE PROVISIONED RESOURCES AFTER WEEK SIX — UNRESOLVED ]"

refusal_path:
  what_no_sounds_like: "'Not this quarter, and not with those two.'"
  what_i_do_next: "Narrow to one lead on the worst line; re-ask after the audit."
  is_declining_survivable: true                  # if false, this is coercion

trip_wire: >
  If by week three no real handover has been written by anybody other than us,
  stop and re-scope. Better than the BATNA, worse than success, and it exists
  to force a pause before drift.

micro_batna: "If this meeting ends undecided: ask for one observed handover, on a date."

next_state: "First observed handover, Tuesday. A date, not a disposition."
```

**Step 6 — state what you have not proved, unprompted, in the proposal itself.** Include everything from the [S034](/wiki/seminars/S034.md) record still marked blocking. **Unprompted disclosure is the only version of this that is worth anything**, because the same sentence extracted under questioning reads as a concession rather than as evidence of how you work.

**Step 7 — attack the proposal with the model playing the person, briefed with their interests and instructed to refuse.** Give it their position and their constraints, **not your proposal's framing**, and require it to decline three times for three different reasons. **Keep the refusals you had no answer to.** Those are the parts of the ask that were being carried by your enthusiasm, and they will not survive a real room either. Then ask it to name the sentence in your proposal that would be hardest for the sponsor to repeat to *their* manager, which is the make-their-decision-easy test in operational form.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That *move it to production* is a list of named changes with owners rather than a verb — you will produce the list in about twenty minutes and it will be longer than the room expects. And that an ask sized to a switch and an ask sized to a re-architecture are different asks addressed to different people.

## What good looks like

- **The ask costs the giver something they would notice.** Named specialist time, access to real data, a named review owner, a change to somebody's workflow, a public association, money.
- **The refusal is genuinely available.** You can describe what a no looks like and what you do the following morning, and neither answer is punishment.
- **A trip wire exists**, set before the ask, at a level better than your alternative and worse than success, and it names what forces the pause.
- **The transition is itemised rather than named.** *Productionising* is not a line. Network isolation, ownership of auto-provisioned resources and who operates any compute are lines.
- **The evidence is not oversold.** The proposal states what the prototype tested, on whose case, what remains unproven — including everything from the [S034](/wiki/seminars/S034.md) record still blocking — and **what the run could not have detected**.
- **The ask is proportionate to the evidence.** A prototype tested with one population does not entitle you to a programme.
- **The authority is right.** The person asked can actually give the thing, and can explain the yes to somebody who was not in the room.
- **The cost of doing nothing is stated**, from [S009](/wiki/seminars/S009.md), and it is stated honestly rather than as pressure.

## How it goes wrong

- **The unrefusable ask** — the request is framed so that declining is embarrassing. *Warning sign:* your refusal path is empty because you cannot imagine one.
- **Enthusiasm banked as commitment** — they were delighted, so you recorded a yes. *Warning sign:* the strongest thing you have is an adjective.
- **The friendly nothing** — repeated warm meetings, no decision, no rejection. *Warning sign:* your next state is *stay in touch*.
- **Promotion mistaken for a switch** — the ask funds turning something on, and the work is a re-architecture. *Warning sign:* your proposal contains the word *productionise* and no list of named changes with owners.
- **The orphaned provision** — the fast path provisioned identity and bot resources on your behalf and nobody was named to own them. *Warning sign:* you cannot say who administers the thing your demonstration ran on.
- **The prototype presented as a system** — you asked for a commitment proportionate to something you have not built. *Warning sign:* the proposal describes capabilities, not evidence.
- **The wrong authority** — the person can approve the sentiment and not the resource. *Warning sign:* they said yes immediately and cheerfully.
- **The deadline as leverage** — the ask is carried by an external date rather than by the evidence. *Warning sign:* your proposal's most persuasive sentence is about time running out.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The proposal, or the refusal it produced, is carried into [S040](/wiki/seminars/S040.md), where the whole proof is defended on price.

**1 · Sort six responses into commitment, advancement and nothing.**

- *We'll get you a sandbox account.* · *I'll put you in front of the operations director on the 14th.* · *Keep me posted.* · *Take Priya for half a day a week — I'll tell her team.* · *We're really excited about this.* · *We can't do it this quarter, and here's why.*

> **Answer key.** **Nothing** — a sandbox usually costs the giver nothing, and a trial is not automatically a commitment. **Advancement**, and a good one, because it has a person and a date. **Nothing**, and it is the zombie lead in its purest form. **Commitment**, in the currency of time *and* standing, because telling a team is reputational exposure. **Nothing** — an adjective. **The most valuable line in the set**: a clear rejection ends a relationship consuming your attention and supplies negative evidence you can act on. **If you ranked the last one lowest, you ranked the answers by how they felt to receive.**

**2 · Price the seam on the platform.** Using the Foundry setup guidance, list what changes for your own artefact between the prototyping topology and the production one. Date the page you read.

> **What you should find.** The documentation describes the basic setup as being for rapid prototyping and individual development when speed and ease of access are priorities, and states that it **lacks network isolation**; the standard setup provides fine-grained control over data, security and networking, in a public-networking and a private-networking variant. **Your list should contain at least one item that requires somebody who is not on your team.** If it does not, you have listed settings rather than a topology change. **If the setup names or their boundaries have moved since this page was written, that is the finding** — the note marks this material highly perishable, and a proposal costed against a stale seam is a proposal that will be corrected in public.

**3 · Go back to your own item 2, then break it.** Find the refusal you wrote you would accept. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference.

> **Criterion.** A passing refusal has three properties. It is **speakable by the other person** — written in their words, not as a category. It leaves **you with something to do the next morning that is not sulking or escalation**. And it is **survivable for the relationship**: you could work with them the following week. **The commonest failure is a refusal that is really a delay** — *they'd say not yet* — which is not a refusal at all but the friendly nothing wearing a no's clothing, and an ask whose only imaginable rejection is a postponement has not been designed to be declined. **If you wrote *they might say no*, you named a possibility rather than designing an exit.**
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Convert one position into three interests, then into a smaller ask.** Take a constraint you have been given — *no production data*, *it must run in our tenant*, *not until the audit* — and write three interests that could sit underneath it, plus one observation that would discriminate between them.

> **Model answer.** *No production data* may be about **residency**, about **contractual exposure to a third party**, about **an unresolved question of who is accountable if it leaks**, or simply about **a previous incident nobody wants to repeat**. The discriminating observation is usually cheap: ask what happened the last time somebody asked, and who said no. **The smaller ask follows from the interest, not from the position** — a synthetic-data phase answers residency and answers accountability not at all. **A learner who produced three restatements of the same interest has paraphrased a position rather than opened it.**

**5 · Set a trip wire and defend it.** Write the threshold at which you will stop even if nobody refuses, and say what it is better and worse than.

> **Scoring criterion.** A trip wire passes if it is **observable by somebody other than you**, and if it sits **above your alternative and below success**. *If it isn't going well* fails. *If no real handover has been written by anybody outside the team by week three* passes. **Its purpose is not to protect you from a bad deal; it is to create time to reassess before sunk cost, urgency and executive attention convert a poor commitment into the only visible path.**

**6 · Read the ask and the refusal path to a peer, in that order.** Their job is to answer one question.

> **The question, and what it catches.** *Could I say no to this and still work with you tomorrow?* If the answer is no, the ask is coercive regardless of your intent — **and coercion produces a commitment that is withdrawn at the first opportunity, usually silently, and usually during delivery.** Then ask the harder second question: *could you explain this yes to your own manager without me in the room?* If not, the proposal has been written to persuade a person rather than to be defended by one.

## What this does not establish

Nothing here shows that commitment-based asks produce better engagements.

The framework — costly behaviour as evidence, the three currencies, advancement as a defined next state, the friendly non-committer as the worst outcome — comes from a practitioner handbook that **reports no controlled comparison, no measured effect and no validated threshold, and says so in its own account of its method.** Its warrant is diagnostic and procedural, and its local copy carries no publisher, year or edition. **Direction and mechanism only. No magnitude is asserted.**

**The negotiation framework is conceptual and case-based and is not a controlled evaluation of negotiation methods.** Its historical cases illustrate that a move can work, not how often it will; the note records that its translation into delivery work is **course-design inference rather than a claim its authors make**; and it carries a caution this session must repeat, that **not every constraint is negotiable** — a regulator, an accountable risk owner or a system owner may hold legitimate decision authority, and clarifying interests is not the same as holding shared decision rights.

**The platform layer establishes that the two topologies differ and nothing about how the transition should be funded.** The setup descriptions are first-party documentation with definitional authority: **no adoption data, no failure-rate data, no cost comparison, no outcome evidence**, one vendor's options compared against each other, read through search excerpts with several tables truncated, and **highly perishable**. **The claim that promotion is therefore a re-architecture rather than a switch is this design's reading of that documentation**, not a statement the documentation makes.

The two claims taken from the practice corpus — that working software dissolves resistance a document cannot, and that resistance originates in process ownership — are **practitioner judgement from a body of work with no evidence base of any kind**, recorded from a complete reading of access-controlled material that no public reader can check. Both are plausible, neither is measured, and **the corpus records no engagement in which an ask was refused**, because it records no engagement that went badly.

The measurement source behind the *could not have detected* clause is **analytical rather than empirical**, and its illustrative magnitudes rest on stylised assumptions.

**The instruction to write the refusal before the ask is design reasoning with no external warrant.** It is held because an ask whose author cannot describe a survivable no has not designed one, and that is an argument rather than a finding.
