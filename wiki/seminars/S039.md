# S039 · From Prototype to Commitment

## The situation

The prototype has done its work. Somebody now has to give something up, or the stage produced an interesting fortnight and nothing else.

This is the point at which a proof either converts or becomes a story people tell warmly for months. **The warm version is the worst available outcome**, because it looks like progress and consumes the same calendar as progress.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught.

1. What are you asking for, from whom, and what does it cost them?
2. Write the refusal you would accept. What does it sound like, and what do you do next?
3. Somebody says they are very keen and asks you to keep them posted. What has been given?
4. What does the prototype entitle you to ask for, and what does it not?
5. Your ask succeeds and the engagement proceeds. What did you promise that you have not proved?

**Item 2 is the one candidates skip.** An ask with no acceptable refusal is not an ask; it is an announcement with a question mark.

## What you must produce

A **commitment proposal**, one page, addressed to a named role.

- **The ask** — what specifically is being given up, by whom, when.
- **The currency** — meaningful time, reputational exposure, or money. Say which, and say why that one.
- **What it buys them**, in terms they hold rather than terms you hold.
- **The evidence it rests on**, with a plain statement of what the prototype did and did not establish.
- **The refusal path** — what you will do if they decline, stated so that declining is a real option.
- **The next state** — what changes about the relationship if they say yes, expressed as a thing that happens on a date rather than as a feeling.

## What good looks like

- **The ask costs the giver something they would notice.** Named specialist time, access to real data, a named review owner, a change to somebody's workflow, a public association, money.
- **The refusal is genuinely available.** You can describe what a no looks like and what you do the following morning, and neither answer is punishment.
- **The evidence is not oversold.** The proposal states what the prototype tested, on whose case, and what remains unproven — including everything from the [S034](/wiki/seminars/S034.md) record that is still blocking.
- **The ask is proportionate to the evidence.** A prototype tested with one user does not entitle you to a programme.
- **The authority is right.** The person asked can actually give the thing.
- **The cost of doing nothing is stated**, from [S009](/wiki/seminars/S009.md), and it is stated honestly rather than as pressure.

## How it goes wrong

- **The unrefusable ask** — the request is framed so that declining is embarrassing. *Warning sign:* your refusal path is empty because you cannot imagine one.
- **Enthusiasm banked as commitment** — they were delighted, so you recorded a yes. *Warning sign:* the strongest thing you have is an adjective.
- **The friendly nothing** — repeated warm meetings, no decision, no rejection. *Warning sign:* your next state is *stay in touch*.
- **The prototype presented as a system** — you asked for a commitment proportionate to something you have not built. *Warning sign:* the proposal describes capabilities, not evidence.
- **The wrong authority** — the person can approve the sentiment and not the resource. *Warning sign:* they said yes immediately and cheerfully.
- **The deadline as leverage** — the ask is carried by an external date rather than by the evidence. *Warning sign:* your proposal's most persuasive sentence is about time running out.

## Do it

**Pick the currency first, because it determines everything else.** Time, reputation and money are the three things a stakeholder can actually give, and the evidence gets stronger as the cost to them becomes more real. **A free trial account is not a commitment; onboarding a team, supplying real data, changing a workflow or agreeing to be named is.** Decide which one your evidence has earned.

**Then write the refusal before you write the ask.** [S019](/wiki/seminars/S019.md) established that a commitment ask has succeeded when a commitment was made *or explicitly refused*, and this session makes the second half operational. **A clear rejection ends a relationship that was consuming your attention and produces negative evidence you can act on.** The failure mode is not being refused; it is leaving with neither advancement nor rejection, which is the outcome that requires no courage from anybody.

**Then state what you have not proved, in the proposal, unprompted.** The corpus behind this stage observes, correctly, that working software is hard to argue with — that political resistance dissolves in front of a running prototype in a way it does not in front of a document. **That is a warning as much as an advantage.** A prototype that resolves resistance it has not earned the right to resolve has bought you a commitment against evidence you do not have, and you will be delivering against it in [Stage 5](/wiki/quarters/Q5.md).

**Then check whose process you are asking somebody to give up.** The same corpus notes that resistance originates in ownership of a process rather than in technical incapacity. If your ask requires somebody to stop doing the thing that makes them useful, the currency is not time — it is standing, and you should price it accordingly and say so.

Finally, have an agent play the person you are asking, briefed with their interests rather than with your proposal, and instructed to refuse. **Keep the refusals you had no answer to.** Those are the parts of the proposal that were carried by your enthusiasm, and they will not survive a real room either.

## Show it

Read the ask and the refusal path to a peer, in that order.

**Their job is to answer one question: could I say no to this and still work with you tomorrow?** If the answer is no, the ask is coercive regardless of your intent, and coercion produces a commitment that will be withdrawn at the first opportunity — usually silently, and usually during delivery.

## What this does not establish

Nothing here shows that commitment-based asks produce better engagements.

The framework — costly behaviour as evidence, the three currencies, advancement as a defined next state, the friendly non-committer as the worst outcome — comes from a practitioner handbook that **reports no controlled comparison, no measured effect and no validated threshold, and says so in its own account of its method.** Its warrant is diagnostic and procedural. **Direction and mechanism only. No magnitude is asserted.**

The two claims taken from the practice corpus — that working software dissolves resistance a document cannot, and that resistance originates in process ownership — are **practitioner judgement from a body of work with no evidence base of any kind**, recorded from a complete reading of access-controlled material that no public reader can check. Both are plausible, neither is measured, and **the corpus records no engagement in which an ask was refused**, because it records no engagement that went badly.

**The instruction to write the refusal before the ask is design reasoning with no external warrant.** It is held because an ask whose author cannot describe a survivable no has not designed one, and that is an argument.
