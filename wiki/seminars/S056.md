# S056 · Can This Be Solved Without AI At All

**Module:** [M12](/wiki/modules/M12.md) · **Quarter:** [Q6](/wiki/quarters/Q6.md) · **Complexity class:** 4
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** The build-path comparison extended with the option the vendor does not sell — a deterministic system, evaluated on the same terms
**Whitepaper:** [WP-056](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-056.md)
**Retrieves from:** S003, S008, S033
**Entry state:** this-programme-only

---

## The situation

The governance regime this module is built on opens with a question, and it is the right one.

**Can this be solved without AI at all?**

You are five stages past the point where that was easy to ask. There is a running increment. There is a threat model. People have been paid. **The question is now expensive, and in most engagements it is never asked at any price**, because everyone in the room arrived selling the answer.

The reason it belongs in a responsible-AI module rather than in a procurement one is not sentimental. **The least harmful system is frequently the one that does not infer.** A system that ranks documents by term statistics cannot hallucinate a citation, cannot be prompted into disclosing its instructions, cannot drift when a supplier changes a model, and cannot be argued with about what it meant. Every harm this module goes on to assess is a harm of inference. Declining to infer removes the whole class, and it is the only intervention in the stage that does.

Answer these before instruction.

1. State your problem in one sentence containing no technology.
2. Design the non-AI solution. Not the current process — the one you would build from scratch, given everything except a model.
3. What does each option cost, including the things nobody counts?
4. What evidence would have made you choose the alternative?
5. Who benefits from the AI answer, and were they in the room when the problem was described?

**Item 4 is the session.** A rejection that no evidence could have reversed is not a rejection, and this programme has already said that once, in week one, about engagements. **Item 2 is where most people fail**, because they offer the status quo and call it an alternative. The status quo has no designer, no components and no operator you can name; it is a description of the present, and comparing your system against the present is comparing it against nobody's best effort.

## What you must produce

**A designed non-AI alternative**, at the same fidelity as the thing you are building, and a **rejection recorded where a later reader will find it.**

1. **The problem, stated without a technology in it.**
2. **The alternative, designed from scratch** — what it is made of, who operates it, what it cannot do.
3. **A frozen comparison set**, chosen before either system's output was seen, and the one instrument both are scored on.
4. **A comparison on cost**, carrying the categories the practice material behind this programme never carries: staffing, compute, the cost of the discovery activity itself, opportunity cost, and the cost of being wrong.
5. **The disconfirming evidence** — what you would have had to see to choose the alternative.
6. **The rejection**, dated, in a place somebody will read it in a year.

## The material

### The question is a capability question before it is an ethical one

There is a class of proposed systems that can be declined without any argument about harm, because the capability they assume does not exist in the component you were going to build them from.

The position paper this rests on is blunt. Auto-regressive language models **cannot, by themselves, do planning**, and they cannot self-verify either, "because self-verification is after all a form of reasoning" ([research/02-technical-foundations/kambhampati-llms-cant-plan.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/kambhampati-llms-cant-plan.md)). Its constructive half is more useful than its negative half: a model should be treated as a **universal approximate knowledge source** — broad coverage, no correctness guarantee — with correctness supplied by an **external verifier** and flexibility by the generator, and neither trusted with the other's job.

**If your proposal is that a model produce a plan and be relied on to know the plan is right, you do not need an ethics argument to decline it. You need an architecture argument, and you already have one.** That is a cheaper conversation and it lands better, because it concedes nothing about anybody's intentions.

**The paper's limits travel with it.** It is a position paper; its claims are arguments and have been contested; it is scoped to auto-regressive models as of early 2024, and models trained explicitly for reasoning postdate the argument. **The architectural discipline is what transfers**, and it remains correct whether or not model reasoning improves — which is precisely why it is the durable half.

### The comparison you were handed has no row for *no model*

The vendor's build-path material is unusually candid and it is the anchor for this session. It splits the options by service model — a platform-as-a-service path supporting declarative and pro-code development with your choice of models, a software-as-a-service path for low-code development by business teams, and an infrastructure path with maximum flexibility — and states the selection criteria as **"your organization's technical capabilities, timeline, and control requirements"** ([research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md)). It admits that the fast setup **"lacks network isolation"**. It states that one of its own products is **"designed for small-scale deployments, such as department-level solutions."** It says plainly that the options **are not mutually exclusive** and compose through protocols.

That is a good comparison, honestly written, and **every option in it contains a model.**

This is not a criticism. It is the structural fact the session exists to make visible. **A comparison whose every row contains the thing under question cannot answer whether the thing is needed**, and the vendor is not obliged to sell you the absence of its product. The absence is your row to add, and adding it is the whole workflow below.

**Its limits are heavy and stated.** First-party, single-vendor selection guidance, read through search-returned excerpts rather than full page fetches with several comparison tables truncated. **Highly perishable** — product names and feature boundaries are actively changing. It offers **no adoption data, no failure-rate data, no cost comparison and no evidence that its recommended mappings produce better outcomes.** And it compares the vendor's options against each other, not against anybody else's, and not against doing it without a model.

### A deterministic baseline is a real competitor, and the evidence is unambiguous

The most common reason the alternative loses is that nobody built one. The second most common is that the one built was a straw figure. Both are avoidable, because in the commonest applied case — finding the right document — there is a strong, cheap, well-understood competitor with decades of evidence behind it.

Lexical retrieval is **derived, not invented**. The probability ranking principle says to rank by decreasing probability of relevance; inverse document frequency **falls out of that derivation** rather than being assumed; the saturating response to repeated terms is justified by an eliteness argument, because a document using a term many times is more likely to be about it but not proportionally so; and length normalisation is exposed as a tunable parameter precisely because a long document may be verbose or may genuinely cover more ground, and real collections contain both ([research/02-technical-foundations/robertson-zaragoza-bm25-and-beyond.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/robertson-zaragoza-bm25-and-beyond.md)). **Every component answers a stated modelling question**, which means every component can be argued with — a property no embedding has.

**Its limits:** the monograph is paywalled and its full text was not retrieved in this repository, so the derivation is recorded from the framework as established in the wider literature rather than read from the source. It predates dense retrieval, rerankers and retrieval-augmented generation entirely. Its independence assumption is known to be false. It cannot bridge vocabulary mismatch on its own, and the conventional parameter values are conventions from past collections rather than optima.

The evidence that this matters in deployment comes from the heterogeneous zero-shot benchmark ([research/02-technical-foundations/thakur-beir.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/thakur-beir.md)), which assembled a deliberately dissimilar collection of retrieval datasets and evaluated systems spanning lexical, sparse, dense, late-interaction and re-ranking families on domains they were not trained for. **The lexical baseline is not the best method and it does not collapse on any domain.** The most expressive families achieve the best average, at high computational cost. The efficient neural families **often underperform**, which the authors characterise as considerable room for improvement in generalisation.

The sentence to carry into a governance argument is this. **A method whose worst case is acceptable is operationally different from a method whose average is higher but whose worst case is unknown**, and a governance artefact is a document about worst cases.

**That benchmark's limits are real too.** It is a snapshot of its own period and the specific rankings have moved. Its datasets are a sample of retrieval tasks and not the population, and their relevance judgements differ in density and construction, so per-dataset scores are not strictly comparable. And it has since become a target of optimisation in its own right, which weakens its zero-shot premise over time. **Read it for the methodology and the trade-off structure, not for which system led.**

### One side of the comparison has no price at all

The delivery practice behind this programme has an excellent rule and this session inverts it: **do not automate the existing workflow — ask how you would do this from scratch given the tooling.** Applied to the alternative, that rule is what stops you offering the status quo. A non-AI option that is the current process with better forms is not a design.

The same corpus supplies the reason the comparison is normally one-sided. **It contains no cost side anywhere.** Every claim in it is a gross benefit; staffing cost, compute cost, the cost of the discovery activity itself and opportunity cost are all absent. Its value framework is sharper still: **the cost side is engineered and the return side is elicited** — consumption forecast by engineers against their own telemetry, return chosen by the customer from a menu and measured with instrumentation the customer may not possess, over a period nobody specifies, with causation assumed and **no party assigned responsibility for establishing it** ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md)).

**A ratio whose denominator is well instrumented and whose numerator is not is not a well-instrumented ratio, and it will feel like one.** Your comparison will feel like one too unless you price both sides in the same units and name who establishes that any observed change was caused by the system.

**Limits, and they are severe.** That corpus is access-controlled, paraphrased here and never quoted. It carries no reference or citation of any kind across approximately ninety documents. It contradicts itself in more than a dozen places. **It records no failure, states no exclusion criteria, and its prepared answer to the objection that tooling improved while delivery did not reattributes the shortfall to the customer's own organisational constraints** — which arranges its evidence so that a disappointing result confirms rather than challenges its thesis. No figure from it is reproduced here or anywhere in this repository.

### *On the same terms* means one instrument, chosen first

An eval is an experiment, and most comparisons of two systems are run in a way that could not have produced a different answer.

The statistical treatment this session borrows makes three demands that a governance comparison should meet ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md)). **Compare on paired, item-level differences rather than on two headline summaries**, because the two systems find the same items easy and hard and the summaries throw that information away. **Do the power analysis before you run it**, so you know whether the comparison you are about to run is capable of detecting the difference you intend to claim. And treat the **Minimum Detectable Effect** as the honest report of what a null result means: an eval too small to see the difference produces the same output as a system with no difference, and **only one of those two is a finding.**

Its worked example carries the lesson better than any principle: a superficial reading of three benchmark comparisons gives one model the win, and a correct analysis reverses it. **Ordinary practice produced the wrong answer, not merely an imprecise one.**

**Its limits:** it is an analytical paper rather than an empirical one; most of its illustrative magnitudes rest on stylised assumptions and are worked examples rather than constants; **statistical validity says nothing about construct validity**, so a tight interval around a meaningless metric is still meaningless; and its power analysis needs variance estimates from prior runs that a first-time team does not have.

## The workflow

**Extend the vendor's build-path comparison with the row it does not contain, then make the two options answer the same question on the same frozen set.**

**Step 1 — record the comparison's axes, and the date you read them.** Open the build-path guidance and write down what it actually compares on: service model, who authors, what compute you operate, orchestration ownership, model choice, initiation, hosting, channels, distribution. **Do not paraphrase from memory. The tables move.**

**Step 2 — add the row.** A deterministic system: no model, no inference, no non-determinism. Fill in every axis the vendor's table has. Most cells will be trivially answerable, and **the triviality is the finding** — the axes that dominate the vendor comparison mostly stop mattering when there is nothing to orchestrate.

**Step 3 — build the alternative for real, at small scale.** For a retrieval problem this is a lexical index with a saturating term-frequency component and a length-normalisation parameter, plus whatever explicit rules your domain actually has. An afternoon is enough. **A described alternative loses every time; a running one does not.**

**Step 4 — freeze the comparison set before you look at either output.** Write the queries and the expected outcomes, commit them, and record the commit. If you write the set after seeing your system succeed, you have written a set your system passes.

**Step 5 — score both on one instrument, and record the unit of analysis.** Say what a single item is, say what counts as correct, and say it once for both systems. **A metric without its unit of analysis is not a metric**, and two systems scored under different units are not being compared.

**Step 6 — price both, in the same units, including the categories nobody carries.** Staffing to build. Staffing to operate. Compute. The cost of the discovery activity that produced the requirement. Opportunity cost. And the cost of being wrong, which differs between the options and is usually where the argument actually lives.

**Step 7 — write the necessity record where decisions live**, not in a slide.

```yaml
# decisions/necessity.md front matter  <- SHAPE, NOT SCHEMA. Confirm your own tool's keys.
question: "Can this be solved without AI at all?"
asked_on: "<YYYY-MM-DD>"
asked_by: "<name and role>"
still_open_at_time_of_asking: true   # false means this record is theatre; say so

problem_without_technology: >
  A named person experiences X. No product, model or capability appears in this sentence.

alternative:
  design: "Lexical index with explicit rules. Not the current process."
  operator: "<named role who runs it>"
  cannot_do: ["<stated limitation>", "<stated limitation>"]
  built: true                        # a described alternative is not an alternative

comparison:
  frozen_set_commit: "<sha>"         # committed BEFORE either output was seen
  instrument: "<one metric, defined once, applied to both>"
  unit_of_analysis: "<what one item is>"
  can_this_comparison_detect_the_difference_i_am_claiming: "<yes / no / unknown>"
  # 'unknown' is an acceptable answer. 'no difference' when the answer is 'no' is not.

cost:                                # same units both sides, or this section is advocacy
  ai_option:    { build: "", operate: "", compute: "", discovery: "", opportunity: "", being_wrong: "" }
  deterministic:{ build: "", operate: "", compute: "", discovery: "", opportunity: "", being_wrong: "" }
  causation_owner: "<who establishes that an observed change was caused by the system>"

disconfirming_evidence: >
  What I would have had to observe, during discovery or proof, to choose the alternative.
  Not 'if the model had not worked' — that is only available after the money is spent.

interest_declared: >
  Who gains commercially if the AI option is chosen, including my own organisation.

decision: "<proceed with model / proceed without model / defer>"
```

**Step 8 — attack the record, not the system.** One prompt: *argue that the deterministic option should have won, using only what is written in this record, and name the sentence that a hostile reader would not accept.* Then a second, harder one: *identify every claim here that would still be written exactly this way if the opposite were true.* Keep what beats your own reasoning; record what you rejected. **An agent that agreed with your choice was not used as an instrument**, which is [S001](/wiki/seminars/S001.md)'s claim arriving in the stage that needs it most.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That the axes dominating the vendor's comparison mostly collapse when the model is removed, which tells you how much of the decision was about operating a model rather than about solving the problem. That a lexical baseline built in an afternoon is rarely as bad as the room assumed, and that nobody in the room had checked. And that most of the AI option's price is not in the comparison you were handed, because the comparison was written to help you choose between products rather than to help you decide whether to buy one.

## What good looks like

- **The problem statement survives having every product name removed from it.** If it does not, you have written a solution.
- **The alternative is a design, not a description of the present.** It has components, an operator and a named failure mode — and it was built rather than described.
- **The comparison set was frozen before either output was seen**, and its commit is recorded.
- **One instrument scores both options**, with the unit of analysis stated once and applied to both.
- **Both options are priced in the same units**, and the AI option's price includes what it consumes rather than only what it saves.
- **The disconfirming evidence is something that could actually have appeared** during this engagement, at a moment when the decision was still open.
- **The rejection is findable and somebody's interest is named.** Filed where a decision record lives, and if the AI answer benefits your organisation commercially, the record says so.

## How it goes wrong

- **The straw alternative** — a non-AI option designed to lose. *Warning sign:* you enjoyed writing it.
- **The status quo as alternative** — you offered *carry on as now* and treated it as a design. *Warning sign:* your alternative has no components and no operator.
- **The unpriced comparison** — the alternative is costed and the AI option is described. *Warning sign:* one column has numbers and the other has adjectives.
- **The set written afterwards** — the comparison queries were chosen once one system's behaviour was known. *Warning sign:* your frozen set has no commit earlier than your first result.
- **The undetectable difference reported as no difference** — the comparison could never have seen the effect you are discussing. *Warning sign:* you wrote *no meaningful difference* and cannot say what difference the comparison could have detected.
- **The retrofitted question** — asked after the model was chosen, to be answered rather than to decide anything. *Warning sign:* nothing you could have written would have changed the plan.
- **The capability answer** — you justified the choice by what the technology can do rather than by what the problem requires. *Warning sign:* your justification would fit a different problem unchanged.
- **The unowned interest** — nobody in the record has anything to gain. *Warning sign:* your organisation is selling the thing you chose and the record does not mention it.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The necessity record is carried into [S060](/wiki/seminars/S060.md), where the assessment is defended on the work it produced rather than on its own completeness.

**1 · Strip the technology, twice.** Take your problem statement and delete every product, model, vendor and capability from it. Then delete every verb that presupposes inference — *predict*, *summarise*, *classify*, *understand*, *generate*. Write what is left.

> **Model answer shape.** *A duty manager cannot find the procedure that applies to an incident, so they either act on the wrong one or wait for somebody who knows.* Nothing in that sentence commits to a method. **If nothing survives the second deletion, you have been describing a solution since Stage 1**, and this session is the last cheap moment to discover it. Record that as the finding rather than reverse-engineering a problem to fit the system.

**2 · Find the missing row.** Using the workflow above, open the vendor's build-path comparison and record what it compares on and the date you read it. Then answer one question in writing: **where in that comparison is the option with no model in it?**

> **What you should find.** That the comparison is honest, well-constructed, explicit about its axes — and that every option in it contains a model, because it is a guide to choosing among the vendor's products rather than a guide to deciding whether to use one. **Record the absence as a finding, not as a criticism.** The documentation is answering the question it was asked. Note also that it publishes no adoption data, no cost comparison and no failure-rate data for any path, so even within its own scope it is a reasoned framework rather than a validated one, and it is **highly perishable** — cite the page and the date, never the product.

**3 · Go back to your own item 4, then break it.** Find what you wrote for opening item 4 — the evidence that would have made you choose the alternative. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then write the disconfirming evidence properly, and write the moment during this engagement at which it could have appeared.

> **Criterion.** Disconfirming evidence passes if it names an observation that (a) could have occurred during discovery or proof, (b) was available while the decision was still open, and (c) a second person could recognise. **It fails if it is only available after the money is spent** — *if the model had not worked* is not disconfirming evidence, it is a description of regret. It also fails if it could never occur: a condition that cannot fire is decoration, and this programme said so in week one about engagements.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Price the thing nobody prices.** Take the AI option and write down the cost of being wrong: what happens, to whom, how it is discovered, and who pays. Then do the same for the alternative.

> **What to notice.** The two are almost never symmetric, and the asymmetry is usually the real argument. A deterministic system that returns nothing is visibly unhelpful and gets escalated; an inferring system that returns something plausible and wrong is invisibly unhelpful and gets acted on. **The corpus this programme is built on carries no cost side at all** — no staffing, no compute, no discovery cost, no opportunity cost — so this column is yours to invent, and inventing it is the exercise. **State who establishes that any observed change was caused by the system.** A claim whose attribution is assigned to nobody is not a claim.

**5 · Ask what your comparison could have detected.** Before reporting any result, write one sentence: *this comparison, on this set, could have detected a difference of at least this size.* Then reread your conclusion.

> **Scoring criterion.** A pass states the smallest difference the comparison could distinguish and says whether the claimed difference exceeds it. **A fail reports *no meaningful difference* without knowing whether the instrument could have seen one** — those are the same output and only one of them is a finding ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md)). *Unknown* is an acceptable answer and is much better than a confident null. Note the source's own caution: this is statistical validity only, and a tight interval around a metric that does not measure what you care about is still meaningless.

## What this does not establish

**Nothing here shows that asking the question improves any outcome**, and no source available to this programme measures it.

**The regime that asks it publishes no evidence that any assessment ever changed a system.** The practice corpus around it is worse placed still: it arrives at every engagement selling an AI-accelerated delivery model, its readiness criteria are composed almost entirely of customer dispositions, and its prepared answer to the objection *your tooling improved and our delivery did not* reattributes the shortfall to the customer's own organisational constraints. **A model whose failure explanation is always the customer's readiness has arranged its evidence so that a disappointing result confirms rather than challenges the thesis**, and it will rarely conclude that the tooling was the wrong instrument. That corpus is also access-controlled, contains no citation of any kind, contradicts itself in more than a dozen places, records no failure and states no exclusion criteria.

**The claim that the deterministic option is less harmful is an argument, not a finding.** It follows from the observation that the harms this module assesses are harms of inference, and nobody has measured whether teams that build a deterministic alternative end up with safer systems. It is stated here because the reasoning is checkable, not because it has been tested.

**The retrieval evidence establishes something narrower than the session uses it for.** The benchmark shows that a lexical baseline generalises robustly across a heterogeneous collection of retrieval tasks it was not trained on. It does not show that a lexical system is adequate for *your* problem, and its authors say so — strong performance there is evidence of breadth, not a guarantee for any particular new domain. It is a snapshot of its own period, its datasets are a sample rather than a population, and it has since become an optimisation target in its own right.

**The capability argument rests on a contested position paper.** That models cannot plan and cannot self-verify is an argument, has been debated, and is scoped to a model generation that has since been added to. **The architectural discipline transfers; the empirical claim should not be presented as settled.**

**The platform anchor rests on documentation that publishes no outcomes.** The build-path comparison is first-party selection guidance read through search excerpts with tables truncated, carrying no adoption, cost, failure-rate or outcome data, and it is highly perishable. Its usefulness here is entirely structural — it shows you the shape of a decision and the row that shape omits.

**The one claim this session makes without hedging is definitional.** A comparison in which every option contains a model cannot answer whether a model is needed. That is true of the vendor's table, of your team's shortlist, and of the conversation in the room, and it is true regardless of which products exist next year.
