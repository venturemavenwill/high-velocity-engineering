# S047 · Criteria Drift and Validating the Validator

**Module:** [M10](/wiki/modules/M10.md) · **Quarter:** [Q5](/wiki/quarters/Q5.md) · **Complexity class:** 4
**Strands:** ME · SG · IS
**Whitepaper:** [WP-047](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-047.md)
**Depends on:** S029, S046
**Re-tests:** S042
**Platform anchor:** Foundry evaluator catalogue versioning; scheduled evaluations and continuous evaluation (preview) · **MCP servers:** Foundry MCP Server (preview) · Microsoft Learn MCP Server

---

## What this day is for

Yesterday's criterion was constructed carefully, from evidence, with its trade named. It will stop being right, and nobody will notice.

Three mechanisms do this. **The criterion is optimised against**, so the cheapest route to satisfying it diverges from the thing it stood for. **The world moves**, so the population, the use and the stakeholders change while the criterion does not. And **the criterion is adjusted** — each adjustment individually reasonable — until it no longer measures what it was built to measure.

The target: **build the check your own criterion could fail, run it, and act on the result.** S029 taught instrument validation; a criterion is an instrument, and this is the day where that costs something, because the instrument is one the student built yesterday.

**The third mechanism now has a data model.** A registered evaluator keeps a stable identifier across edits, by design: the documentation states that when you edit criteria and save as a new version you echo the existing `id`, and that the service does not reassign it. That is a sensible engineering decision and it is also cumulative drift's enabling condition, written into a schema. The name survives. The dimensions do not have to.

## Phase 1 · Pretest and prediction (25 min)

Five items on a criterion in use on the substrate for two years, held as **eleven registered versions of one evaluator** with its full adjustment history.

1. Does this criterion still measure what it was built to measure?
2. Here are the eleven adjustments made to it. Which one broke it?
3. What would have detected the break at the time?
4. Predict: is the break attributable to a single adjustment?
5. **Copilot prediction, sealed.** You will shortly ask the agent to *validate our evaluation criterion*. Write down what it will produce, and **name the one thing it will get wrong**. Sealed with items 1 to 4 and opened at phase 8.

Item 4's answer is no. No individual adjustment broke it and the sequence did.

Item 5's expectation is that the agent will produce a genuinely competent validation — a held-out set, a run, a report — and will validate the version currently registered against the judgement of the person who registered it, which is the one check the criterion cannot fail. The vendor's own guidance points the same way: it advises validating that a rubric's scores *align with your own judgment* before using it at scale.

**Warrant.** Pretesting with an attribution demand that has no answer. The absence of a culprit is the day's content and is discovered rather than asserted. Item 5 is administered here and may not migrate into phase 5, per instrumentation rule 8.

## Phase 3 · Contrasting cases and generation (45 min)

**Executed, not read.** Groups receive **one criterion at four points in time, as four registered evaluator versions**, and run all four over the same held response set, reading each version's scores side by side.

- **T1** — criterion and construct aligned.
- **T2** — the system has been optimised against the criterion; the score improved and the construct did not.
- **T3** — the user population has changed; the criterion still measures its construct and the construct is no longer the one that matters.
- **T4** — the criterion has been adjusted eleven times; each adjustment was defensible and the aggregate is unrecognisable.

Groups diagnose each and commit.

Then: **for each, state what check, running at T1, would have raised an alarm before the next point.**

T4 is the payload, and running it is what makes it bite. The four versions carry **the same `id`, the same display name and the same catalogue entry**. Every report ever written about this criterion names it identically. The scores from T1 and T4 sit in the same column of the same dashboard and are not measurements of the same thing, and nothing in the run output says so — because from the service's point of view nothing is wrong. No check that examines the criterion at a point in time detects it.

**Warrant.** Contrasting cases across drift mechanisms with a fixed criterion, now executed against registered versions rather than described. T4 requires a check that examines the criterion's *history* rather than its state, which is a different kind of instrument and is the day's hardest idea. Running the four versions is what converts *the aggregate is unrecognisable* from a claim into a column of numbers under one name.

## Phase 4 · Consolidating instruction (50 min)

1. **Three drift mechanisms:** optimisation against the criterion, movement in the world, and cumulative adjustment. **This list omits drift caused by a change in the measurement pipeline rather than in the criterion — a scoring library update, a sampling change — which is real, is common, and belongs with S068's detectability material. Safe as an account of why a criterion stops matching its construct; unsafe as a complete account of why a reported number moves, which requires the pipeline as well.**
2. **Why optimisation degrades a proxy,** worked. S026 point 4 at full length.
3. **Validating the validator:** a check the criterion could fail. Not a review, not an inspection — a procedure with a result the criterion might not survive.
4. **Three validation forms:** a held-back stakeholder judgement, an outcome the criterion should predict, and a case set the criterion should rank in a known order.
5. **Detecting T4,** which requires the criterion's own history as data. Adjustments logged with their reasons, examined as a sequence. On this platform the raw material is already there — versions are registered and retained — and what is missing is a *reason* per version, which no schema field requires.
6. **What to do when validation fails.** Reconstruct, restrict, or retire — and each has a cost to the parties from S046.
7. **The re-validation schedule** as part of the criterion's definition. A criterion without one is a criterion nobody has committed to checking.
8. **Defaults are opinions, and one of them is a check that cannot fail.** The rubric documentation's own validation advice is to *iterate until the rubric reliably distinguishes acceptable from unacceptable responses* and to *validate that the rubric scores align with your own judgment before using it at scale.* That is a sound engineering instruction and it is the wrong shape for a validation, because the party whose judgement the criterion is being aligned to is the party who wrote the criterion. It is point 3's review wearing a check's clothes, and it is in the vendor's documentation because it is what almost everyone actually does. Three more were chosen for you: continuous evaluation samples your traffic at **a rate you set and a default hourly run cap of 100**, above which **runs are skipped rather than queued**, so a criterion can go unchecked for an hour without anything reporting that it did; a scheduled evaluation runs a *pinned* version, so a criterion edited and not re-registered is not the criterion being run; and evaluator versions are deleted one version at a time, so the history is destructible by an ordinary maintenance action.
9. **What is perishable here and what is not.** The version schema, the hourly cap, the sampling controls, the preview status of scheduled evaluation and the exact wording of the vendor's validation advice are all perishable. **The durable claims are that a criterion drifts by three mechanisms, that only one of them has a culprit, that a check is distinguished from a review by whether the criterion could fail it, and that detecting cumulative adjustment requires history rather than state.** A platform that adds a mandatory reason field to every evaluator version next year has made this day's step 6 cheaper and has not changed a word of the claim.

**Warrant.** Consolidation indexed to phase 3's four points. Point 1 carries the strong-form scope statement. Point 3's distinction between a check and a review is the day's operative definition, and point 8 supplies a real, published, well-intentioned instance of the distinction being missed, which is more useful than any constructed one. Points 8 and 9 are the [Copilot Practice Standard](/wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md)'s required platform content.

## Phase 5 · Whole-task learning task (90 min)

**Class 4. The validation is the student's, on their own criterion, and this phase produces a build.**

Students use Copilot in agent mode, through the Foundry MCP Server, to **ship a working validation** of the criterion they registered yesterday. They:

1. **State the construct** your S046 criterion stands for.
2. **Design a check** the criterion could fail, from point 4's forms or another.
3. **State in advance what result would mean the criterion is broken**, and what you would do.
4. **Run it,** as a registered evaluation against a held set the criterion has not seen.
5. **Report the result,** including if it failed.
6. **Set the re-validation schedule** and the adjustment log's format — **naming which T4-style drift the log would make visible**, and what field carries the reason a version was created.
7. **State what your check cannot detect** — and if it cannot detect T4-style drift, say so.

**The artefact carries three further elements**, per the [Copilot Practice Standard](/wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md).

- **Prompt record.** The instructions you gave the agent, in order, including the ones you abandoned.
- **Intervention record.** Every point at which you corrected it, and what the correction was. **An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that the design could have detected an intervention-worthy error — a check that asserts it is running against the registered version it names, and fails loudly if it is not, is the obvious example and is accepted.
- **Frontier statement.** One thing the agent did faster than you could have, and one thing it did badly that you had to catch. Both specific, both from this build.

**Fading:** step 2's forms are supplied; step 4's registration mechanics are supplied just in time. **Step 3 is unsupported.**

**Warrant.** Whole-task at class 4 on the student's own artefact, now shipping rather than describing. Step 3 is the day's discriminator: deciding the failure threshold before the result exists is the only version that is not motivated, which is S040's discipline applied to a criterion. Step 6's added clause is [WP-047](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-047.md) §8's objection-1 repair — a log format assessed for presence rather than adequacy is the day's answer to its hardest problem checked as a box.

## Phase 6 · Error clinic (40 min)

Instructor first: a substrate criterion that was validated annually, passed every year, and was measuring the wrong thing throughout — with attention to **what the validation was actually checking**.

Then the agent case. A student asked Copilot to *tighten the criterion and re-run the evaluation*. The agent edited the rubric locally with a clean, well-reasoned diff — two dimensions sharpened, one weight raised — ran the evaluation, and reported that the pass rate had improved. **The diff was correct, the run succeeded, and the number described the previous version.** An evaluation run uses the registered version pinned in its configuration, and the local edit had not been registered, so nothing in the output was false and nothing in it was about the criterion the student had just written. Attention goes to why review did not catch it: the reviewer read the diff, which was good; read the result, which was real; and had no reason to ask whether the two referred to the same object.

Then students: a check you have run that could not have failed, or a place today where you accepted agent output you had not checked.

**Warrant.** Error management. A validation that cannot fail is the day's central failure and is indistinguishable from a rigorous one in any report. The agent case is the [Copilot Practice Standard](/wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md)'s required addition and is chosen because it has no incorrect step — the failure is entirely in an assumption about which version is which, which is the same shape as T4 at one adjustment rather than eleven.

## Phase 7 · Peer articulation (30 min)

Pairs. Each student's partner reads **the step 2 check and step 3 threshold**, and must construct a way the criterion is broken and the check still passes.

If they succeed, the pair decides whether to extend the check or to record the blind spot at step 7.

Per rule 3, the outcome is recorded in the artefact.

**Warrant.** Interactive-tier articulation with a defeat-the-check demand, and a forced trade. This is [S029](/wiki/seminars/S029.md) phase 7's dual-defeat protocol reused, which the [Seminar Day Design Pattern](/wiki/program/04-Seminar-Day-Design-Pattern.md)'s rule 5 now requires rather than permits.

## Assessment

Specification item in ME201. Per rule 1, the checked elements are: a stated construct, a designed check with its form, a pre-committed failure threshold, the check run, the result reported including failure, a re-validation schedule and adjustment log format **naming the T4-style drift the log would make visible**, a statement of what the check cannot detect, the phase 7 outcome, **the prompt record**, **the intervention record**, and **the frontier statement**. **Nothing else is assessed, including whether the criterion passed.**

**A check whose stated threshold could not have been reached fails the specification.**

**An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that the design could have detected an intervention-worthy error.

| Durable claim | Platform instance, perishable |
|---|---|
| A criterion drifts by three mechanisms | Eleven registered versions of one evaluator |
| Cumulative adjustment has no culprit | A stable `id` echoed across every edit, by design |

## What to take from this day

**The claim to hold.** A criterion drifts by three mechanisms — it is optimised against, the world moves, and it is adjusted — and **only one has a culprit**. A check is distinguished from a review by whether the criterion could fail it, and detecting cumulative adjustment requires history rather than current state.

**What the sources license, and what they do not.** Criterion drift, proxy degradation under optimisation and validation practice are grounded in [research/03-measurement-evaluation/shankar-evalgen.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/shankar-evalgen.md) and [research/03-measurement-evaluation/singh-leaderboard-illusion.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/singh-leaderboard-illusion.md), with the practitioner material held at folder level. **Direction and mechanism only; no magnitude is asserted.** Three silences carry into your work: no schema field records *why* a version was created, no cadence is published for re-validation, and no agreement with human judgement is published for any evaluator.

**Guidance for your own work.**

- **Pre-commit the result that would mean the criterion is broken**, and what you would then do. Decided afterwards, that judgement is motivated.
- **Log the reason each version was created.** The platform retains the versions; nothing requires the why, and the why is what makes drift visible.
- **State what your check cannot detect**, plainly, including if it cannot detect cumulative adjustment.
- **Pin the revision.** Per the [Agent Configuration Standard](/wiki/program/13-Agent-Configuration-Standard.md), a run uses the version pinned in its configuration — an edit you did not register produces a true number about a different object.

**Where this is easy to get wrong.** Validating a criterion against the judgement of the person who wrote it. That is a review wearing a check's clothes: it passes every year, it is what almost everyone does, and it is indistinguishable from rigour in any report.
