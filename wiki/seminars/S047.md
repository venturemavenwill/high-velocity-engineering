## The situation

The corpus behind this stage states a prohibition as a headline takeaway, and it is the best single rule in approximately ninety documents.

**Use the tooling to make each step better, not to skip steps.**

The same collection's customer-facing material then tells its proudest story, in which a prototype is generated on the first day from a template, with no discovery activity described at all. **Read against the collection's own method, the flagship story is step-skipping presented as acceleration, and neither document notices.**

Both of those things are in the same body of work, written by the same organisation, for different audiences. This session puts them on the same page — and then asks the question neither document asks: what, physically, would have stopped it?

Because the prohibition is a sentence. A sentence is a statement of intention, and **an intention is not a boundary.** You can hold it sincerely on Monday, hold it less sincerely on Thursday, and lose it entirely at the point in an engagement where losing it costs most. A mode that *cannot* edit is a different object from a mode you have decided not to let edit, and the difference does not show up until the day your decision would have gone the other way.

**Answer these in writing before reading further.**

1. Where is your current plan, and what happens to it when this window closes?
2. You changed your approach yesterday. Where is that recorded, and by what name?
3. What is the difference between a phase that completed and a phase that was accepted?
4. While you were planning yesterday, what physically prevented you from editing code?
5. Which planning step did the tooling let you skip this week, and what did skipping it cost?

**Item 4 is the session, and the honest answer for almost everybody is *nothing*.** Item 5's honest answer is often *none, because it did not cost anything* — and that answer needs the same scrutiny as its opposite, because a cost you did not observe is not a cost you did not pay.

## What you must produce

Two things, and the second is the one that makes the first true.

1. **A plan-only mode and an implement mode, held as configuration**, differing in what each is permitted to do rather than in what you intend to do in each. The permission list is the artefact; the description is commentary.
2. **The boundary test**: what you asked the plan-only mode to do that it should have refused, what it did, and the pin under which you observed it — commit or version, host, model, date.
3. A **plan for one increment of your slice**, durable on disk, with:


- **Stable identifiers** for each phase and each task within it, used consistently across the research record, the plan, the implementation evidence and the review.
- A **research record** stating what was established before planning and where it came from.
- **Blockers** stated as conditions rather than as feelings, each with what would clear it.
- **A return path**: the named circumstance under which implementation stops and goes back to planning, and who may declare it.
- **A completion-versus-acceptance line**: what will make each phase complete, and separately, what will make it accepted.
- **A skipped-step register**: every step of your own process the tooling let you omit, with what you did instead and what evidence would show the omission was safe.

## The material

### The prohibition, the anecdote, and the rule that contradicts them both

Start with the tension, because it is the durable content and the prohibition on its own is advice.

The collection behind this stage carries a second rule alongside the first: given a fixed time budget, run more iterations rather than fewer and more refined ones. **That rule and the prohibition pull in opposite directions and the collection never notices.** More iterations of a step is *making the step better*. Fewer, longer, more careful steps is what a disciplined engineer does when told not to skip anything. The two are reconcilable in principle and the reconciliation is never written down, which means every reader resolves it by temperament.

The collection's limits are heavy and travel with everything taken from it. **It is access-controlled and is paraphrased here rather than quoted.** It contains approximately thirty quantitative assertions of which exactly one names a measurement instrument, and no figure from it is reproduced anywhere in this repository. It offers no declined engagement, no non-synthetic failure narrative and no outcome data of any kind ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md)).

**Your plan has to say which of the two rules it implements.** That is the smallest honest response to a source that holds both.

### A boundary you configure and a boundary you intend

The pinned configuration corpus supplies the mechanism the prohibition needs, and it supplies it structurally rather than as advice ([research/06-microsoft-platform/hve-core-repository.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/hve-core-repository.md)).

It separates the work into **gated phases — research, plan, implement, review, follow-up — with explicit paths back to earlier phases rather than only forward.** The design's own commitment is that these are distinct activities with distinct exit conditions rather than a narrative order, and that a phase can be re-entered rather than only completed. It then joins the phases with **durable state on disk, declared authoritative over session memory**, under stable identifiers, with separate locations for the research record, the plan, implementation evidence, critique and review. The argument is provenance and restartability: an interrupted run resumes from files, and a decision made in one phase can be found by identifier in another.

One distinction inside that design is worth more than the rest. **Execution completion and acceptance outcome are different results**, and a phase that ran to completion has not thereby been accepted. Almost every team conflates them, and the conflation is invisible because both produce the same artefact: work that stopped.

**But every one of those separations is a declaration, and the corpus is candid that it is.** It ships no model and no safety classifier; host behaviour varies; human oversight remains necessary. A phase gate written into a workflow file is a request that the executor observe a gate. **A mode whose tool list does not include an edit capability is a different kind of object**, because the absence is enforced by something other than the executor's cooperation.

That is the session's durable claim and it generalises past this tooling entirely: **a boundary enforced by intention is not a boundary.** The version of this you already know is that a code freeze announced in a meeting is not a code freeze and a locked branch is.

**A limit on the platform half, recorded rather than hidden.** This repository holds **no research note covering the host's custom chat mode surface** — no note records its schema, its permission model, its defaults, or what a mode is actually prevented from doing. Everything in this session about that surface is therefore taught as *shape* and refused as *syntax*: the boundary is a configuration and its enforcement is an observation you must make on your own host, on the date you make it. **Do not take the fenced block below as a schema reference.**

### What "plan" means when the system does not plan

A mode called *plan* is doing something, and the honest description of what it is doing is the intellectual content of this session.

The position paper this rests on rejects both prevailing camps and states its negative claim without hedging: **auto-regressive language models cannot, by themselves, do planning** ([research/02-technical-foundations/kambhampati-llms-cant-plan.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/kambhampati-llms-cant-plan.md)). It then closes the most popular escape route in agent design, and the parenthetical is the part to carry: they likewise cannot do **self-verification**, *because self-verification is after all a form of reasoning*. **If a system cannot reliably plan, you cannot repair the plan by asking the same system to check it**, because checking is the same class of capability that was found wanting.

The constructive half is why the paper is useful rather than merely deflationary. Models should be viewed as **universal approximate knowledge sources** — the phrase repays attention. *Universal* captures breadth across domains. *Approximate* captures unreliability. *Knowledge source* captures the correct functional role: a good source of candidate structure, plausible decompositions and relevant constraints, none of them guaranteed correct. The proposed architecture pairs the model with **external model-based verifiers in a bi-directional interaction regime**: the model proposes, a verifier sound with respect to a formal model critiques, the critique returns, the model revises. **Correctness comes from the verifier and flexibility comes from the model, and neither is trusted to do the other's job.**

**Its limits are recorded and matter.** It is a position paper, not an experimental report; its central claims are arguments and have been contested. It targets auto-regressive models as of early 2024, and models explicitly trained for reasoning postdate the argument, so the empirical picture should be re-checked rather than assumed static. Sound verification requires a formal domain model, which is not always available; the framework mitigates that cost and does not remove it, and its verifier-construction step still depends on human review.

The consequence for your configuration is exact. **A plan-only mode is a generator of candidate structure that has been prevented from acting.** That is a genuinely valuable thing to have, and it is not what the word *plan* implies to a customer, a manager or a junior engineer. **Naming it honestly in your own documentation is part of today's artefact**, because a stakeholder who believes a plan was produced by planning will read its confidence differently from one who knows it was produced by proposal.

### Fact-finding is not decision-making, and the spike is where that is written down

The openly published handbook makes the same separation from the human side, and its wording is sharper than most internal standards manage.

On technical spikes it states the constraint directly: **"The goal of a spike should be fact-finding, not decision-making or recommendation"** ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md), pages read 2026-07-30). Its feasibility-spike page adds the constraint that governs what may be done with the output: **"the code written for the spikes is not going to serve as the code for the final solution"**. It opens a spike with a ninety-minute pre-mortem framed by one question — *imagine the project has failed; what problems and challenges caused this failure?* — and runs weekly sharing calls with the whole crew attending, so that findings do not stay with the person who found them.

Its async design-review page carries one further rule worth stealing: **"After two round trips of question/response, resort to synchronous communication for resolution"**. That is a stopping rule for a written exchange, and stopping rules are this module's recurring instrument.

**Cite that handbook by page and by the date the page was read, never as a whole.** Its pages carry footer dates spread across several years, roughly a fifth of the site is stubs or link lists, it contradicts itself in at least a dozen places without noticing, and it asserts four unsupported magnitude claims. **This repository records those four as specimens and reproduces none of them.**

### The vendor's own account, and the two things in it you must not repeat

The internal platform synthesis behind this strand states the industry problem correctly and in one sentence: demonstrating this technology in proofs of concept is relatively easy, and deploying into production while extracting sustainable return is the difficulty ([research/06-microsoft-platform/03-accelerate-agentic-ai.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/03-accelerate-agentic-ai.md)). **That is the most durable line in the document and it is the sentence this whole stage exists to answer.**

It also supplies the most transferable content in the platform strand: **five orchestration patterns — sequential, concurrent, group chat, handoff and Magentic — as a genuine taxonomy of multi-agent control flow** rather than a product feature list, four of whose names describe structure rather than branding. A plan-and-implement separation is the simplest instance of the first.

**And it is the best available specimen of the failure this programme exists to prevent.** It is a well-written, internally coherent vendor narrative containing a precise unverifiable performance figure with no benchmark, metric definition or interval; a maturity model with no causal evidence; and analyst projections presented as established fact. **It is internal, is not publicly accessible, cannot be cited externally, and is the most perishable artefact in this repository. None of its quantitative claims is repeated anywhere in this wiki**, and an engineer who cannot mark the unverifiable claims in a document of that kind will be persuaded by every architecture deck they are ever shown.

## The workflow

**Make the boundary a configuration, then try to cross it.**

**Step 1 — record the pin before anything else.** Host, model, extension or package version, date. Every observation below is a property of that combination.

**Step 2 — write two modes whose difference is a permission list, not a description.** The shape is a mode file with front matter naming the capabilities the mode may use. **This is the shape and not the schema** — check your host's current documentation for the keys it actually accepts, and record the version you checked.

```markdown
---
# .github/chatmodes/plan-only.chatmode.md   <- SHAPE, NOT SCHEMA.
# Verify every key against your host's current docs and record the version.
description: 'Research and plan. Produces a plan file. Cannot change the tree.'
tools: ['codebase', 'search', 'usages', 'fetch', 'problems']
---

You are in PLAN mode.

- Write the plan to `.plans/<increment>.md`. Give every phase an identifier
  of the form `P01`, and every task `P01-T01`. Reuse them downstream.
- For each phase state TWO lines: what makes it COMPLETE, and separately
  what makes it ACCEPTED, with who accepts it. They are different results.
- State the RETURN PATH: the named circumstance under which implementation
  stops and re-enters planning, and who may declare it.
- You propose. You do not decide, and you do not verify your own proposal.
  Where a step needs verification, name the external check that supplies it.
- If asked to edit, run or commit: refuse and say which mode is required.
```

**Step 3 — write the implement mode as a separate file** whose permission list includes editing and whose instructions require every change to cite the plan identifier it discharges.

**Step 4 — test the boundary rather than trusting it.** In plan-only mode, ask for the edit. Not a hypothetical — ask plainly: *make that change now.* Record three things: what it did, whether the refusal came from the permission list or from the prose, and how you could tell the difference. **If you cannot tell the difference, you do not yet have a boundary; you have a well-behaved mode**, and the distinction is the whole session.

**Step 5 — run one increment through the phases and preserve everything**, including the identifiers for work you abandoned. Then ask the question the corpus asks of its own record: what does this prove, and what does it not? It proves what was decided and when. **It proves nothing about whether the decision was right**, and a plan whose main effect is to make the work feel accountable is worse than no plan, because it is accountability theatre with a filing system.

**Step 6 — exercise the return path deliberately.** Take a task in the implementation phase, declare the return condition met, and go back to planning. Record what changed. **A return path nobody has used is a diagram.**

**Step 7 — use the agent to attack your own plan, not to improve it.** Two prompts, in this order. First: *argue that this plan was written after the work it describes.* A plan in which every phase completed as planned cannot survive that prompt, and if yours does, you have found something. Second: *produce a task that satisfies every completion criterion in this plan and delivers nothing.* Every criterion it defeats is a criterion that names a form rather than an outcome. Keep what is better than your own reasoning and record what you rejected. **An agent that endorsed your plan was not used as an instrument** — which is [S001](/wiki/seminars/S001.md)'s claim arriving again, and it is the claim this module keeps needing.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That a rule and a permission are different objects, and that you cannot tell which one you have until you try to break it. It also makes the completion-versus-acceptance distinction physical: **when the two lines are written separately, most phases turn out to have had no acceptor at all**, and nobody notices that while the phases are running.


## What good looks like

- **The two modes differ in a permission list, not in a description.** A reader can see what the plan mode cannot do without reading its prose.
- **The boundary was tested and the result recorded with its pin**, including the honest result *I could not tell whether the refusal came from the permission list or from the instructions*.
- **A decision made during planning can be located from the implementation record by its identifier**, and this is demonstrated rather than claimed.
- **The plan survives the end of the session.** Close everything, reopen, and continue from files.
- **Completion and acceptance are separate lines with different signatories**, and at least one acceptance is a human decision the executor may not record.
- **The return path names a circumstance, not a mood.** *If it is not working* is not a return path.
- **The skipped-step register is non-empty and specific**, and at least one entry names something the learner would defend skipping.

## How it goes wrong

- **The intended boundary** — the plan mode's restraint lives in its instructions and in your habits, and there is no permission that would stop it. *Warning sign:* your answer to item 4 is still *nothing*.
- **The conversation plan** — it exists in the exchange and nowhere else. *Warning sign:* you would have to scroll to answer item 2.
- **The plan written afterwards** — produced to accompany work already done. *Warning sign:* every phase completed as planned.
- **Completion read as acceptance** — a phase ran to the end and is therefore treated as approved. *Warning sign:* nobody said anything between the last task and the next phase.
- **The skipped plan** — generation is cheap, so implementation started before planning finished, and the plan was reconstructed to match. *Warning sign:* your first artefact of the day is code.
- **The unused identifier** — identifiers exist, nothing references them. *Warning sign:* you cannot trace a single decision forward.
- **The self-verifying plan** — the plan names the model as the thing that will check the plan. *Warning sign:* your verification step and your generation step have the same author.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The plan and the mode configuration are carried into [S050](/wiki/seminars/S050.md), where identifiers must resolve live from the implementation record.

**1 · Sort five restraints into configured and intended.** For each, say what would have to change for it to hold on a day when you did not want it to.

- *We do not edit while planning.* · *The plan mode's tool list contains no edit capability.* · *We always write the acceptance line.* · *The branch is locked and requires two reviewers.* · *We never let the agent commit.*

> **Model answers.** The tool list and the locked branch are configured; the other three are intended. **The test is not sincerity, it is what happens on the bad day** — an intention holds exactly as long as its holder wants it to, and the days when you would break it are the days it was for. Note also that *we never let the agent commit* can be made configured, and that noticing which of your intentions are convertible is more useful than the sorting.

**2 · Name what a plan mode is actually doing.** In two sentences, describe your plan mode to a customer without using the word *plan*.

> **What you should find.** Something close to: *it proposes candidate structure from broad but unreliable knowledge, and it is prevented from acting on the proposal until a person or an external check has looked.* **That is the honest description**, and it follows from the position that auto-regressive models cannot by themselves plan or self-verify, because self-verification is itself a form of reasoning ([research/02-technical-foundations/kambhampati-llms-cant-plan.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/kambhampati-llms-cant-plan.md)). **The paper is a position paper whose claims are arguments and have been contested, and it targets models as of early 2024** — so state the mechanism and do not present it as settled.

**3 · Go back to your own item 4, then break it.** Find what you wrote for opening item 4 — what physically prevented you from editing while planning. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then write the answer properly, and name the one restraint in your current practice you could convert from intended to configured this week.

> **Criterion.** An answer passes if it names something that would have stopped you on a day you wanted to proceed: a permission you do not hold, a branch you cannot push to, a mode whose capability list omits the action. **It fails if it names a decision, a habit, a team norm or a documented rule**, because all four are held by the person they constrain. *We agreed not to* fails. *The mode has no edit capability and I confirmed that by asking it to edit* passes.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Cross the boundary on purpose.** Run workflow step 4 and record which of the three outcomes you got: it refused and you can attribute the refusal to the permission list; it refused and you cannot attribute it; it complied.

> **What to notice.** The middle outcome is the commonest and the most instructive, because it is indistinguishable from the first while everything is going well. **A mode that refuses because it was asked nicely not to is a mode that will comply on the prompt you have not thought of yet.** Record the pin beside whichever outcome you got: this is a fact about your host and model on a date, and this repository holds no note characterising that surface, so nothing here licenses you to generalise it.

**5 · Put the prohibition and the anecdote side by side.** Write down which one your own week resembled, then complete the skipped-step register.

> **Scoring criterion.** A pass names the step, what was done instead, and what evidence would show whether the omission was safe. **A learner who finds their week resembled the anecdote has produced the session's most useful finding**, provided they can say which step was skipped. *I skipped nothing* is acceptable only with the second half attached: what you would have had to observe to know that, and whether you observed it.

**6 · Two round trips, then stop.** Take one open question in your plan and run it as a written exchange, applying the rule that after two round trips of question and response you move to a conversation.

> **What to notice.** How much earlier the rule fires than you expect, and how often the third round trip would have been the one that resolved it. **The rule is a stopping rule, not a claim about efficiency** — and like every stopping rule in this module, its value is that it converts an abandonment into a decision. The handbook that supplies it offers no comparative evidence for it and does not claim to.

## What this does not establish

Nothing here shows that phase separation produces better software, and the pinned corpus is unusually clear that it makes no such demonstration.

It contains distinct evaluation lanes, a schema validator, link and metadata checks, and behavioural graders — and **no committed result corpus.** A grader exists; results do not. Its own documentation records that its behaviour checks test output shape rather than correctness, that some checks are advisory by default and lack established calibration, and that human oversight remains necessary. **The revision this session pins is not all-green**, and that is part of the evidence rather than a blemish on it.

**Nothing here shows that a configured boundary produces better work than an intended one either.** The claim is narrower and definitional: an intention is held by the person it constrains and a permission is not, so the two fail differently. That the second failure mode is less common is an argument, not a finding, and no source in this repository measures it.

The internal corpus's prohibition against skipping steps is **a practitioner judgement with no measurement attached**, stated as a takeaway and contradicted by its own author's flagship story. This session teaches the contradiction because the contradiction is the durable content; the prohibition on its own is advice.

**The planning literature is a position paper and is contested.** Its negative claim about self-verification is an argument, its scope is auto-regressive models as of early 2024, and models explicitly trained for reasoning postdate it. **The correct use is the architectural discipline — generate, verify externally, critique, regenerate — which is correct whether or not model reasoning improves.**

**The handbook offers no comparative evidence for any of its practices and does not claim to.** Its pages contradict one another in at least a dozen places, and four of its assertions are unsupported magnitude claims recorded here as specimens and reproduced nowhere.

**And there is a hole in the platform half of this session that the design does not paper over.** This repository holds no research note on the host's custom chat mode surface. Everything you observe about it today is an observation under one pin, made by you, and it should be recorded as one.

**The strongest claim available is about provenance:** a record held under stable identifiers can answer a question asked later, and a conversation cannot. That is a property of files. It is not a claim about correctness and this session does not make one.
