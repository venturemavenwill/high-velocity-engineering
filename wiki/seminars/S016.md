# S016 · Questions That Invite Flattering Answers

**Module:** [M04](/wiki/modules/M04.md) · **Quarter:** [Q2](/wiki/quarters/Q2.md) · **Complexity class:** 2
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** Microsoft Agent Framework evaluations — building an evaluator for leading questions, and discovering it agrees with you more readily than it should
**Whitepaper:** [WP-016](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-016.md)
**Retrieves from:** S001, S003, S007
**Entry state:** either

---

## The situation

You have been handed a discovery question bank. It is long, it is organised by topic, it was written by experienced practitioners, and every question comes with example answers so you know what good looks like.

You are going to use it to interview a customer who knows you have come to sell them something.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught.

1. Write the question you would ask to find out whether a customer's code review is slow. Now write what a polite person says in response to it.
2. A question in the bank asks where the customer thinks an assistant could help their engineers. **Who benefits from the answer?**
3. Every question ships with example answers. Name one thing that changes about the response when the examples are read out.
4. What question would tell you this engagement should not proceed?
5. Who loses if this succeeds?

**Items 4 and 5 are the session.** They are not in the bank. They are not in any version of the bank.

## What you must produce

A **repaired question set** for your engagement, produced in three passes over a supplied bank.

- **Pass one — strip.** Remove every specimen answer. For each one, write what it would have made you expect.
- **Pass two — classify and rewrite.** Mark every question **behavioural**, **projective** or **evaluative**. Rewrite every evaluative question so that it asks about something that has already happened.
- **Pass three — add.** Write the three questions the bank does not contain: the data question, the disconfirmation question, and the question about who loses.

**The rewrite is the assessable work.** The classification is how you find what to rewrite.

## The material

### Nobody is lying to you

The interviewing literature's central claim is not that customers deceive. It is that a respondent can see which answer would please you, and producing it is the socially correct move. Fitzpatrick's formulation is that people will lie to you — not maliciously, but because you asked a question whose answer they can see would please you ([research/05-fde-craft/fitzpatrick-the-mom-test.md](/research/05-fde-craft/fitzpatrick-the-mom-test.md)).

The consequence is the one that matters and it is uncomfortable: **question quality is the interviewer's responsibility, not the respondent's honesty.** Social generosity is a predictable condition of the situation. A question that cannot survive it is a defective instrument, and blaming the customer for a flattering answer is blaming a thermometer for the weather.

Portigal makes the same point structurally rather than morally ([research/05-fde-craft/portigal-interviewing-users.md](/research/05-fde-craft/portigal-interviewing-users.md)). Ordinary conversation supplies defaults that are actively counterproductive in an interview: taking half the airtime, waiting for a turn to speak, offering a related experience, smoothing over silence, supplying possible answers, correcting an error. A relaxed tone does not make an interview casual in its operation. **Every one of those defaults is a way of putting words into somebody's mouth, and four of them feel like good manners.**

**Their limits, stated because they bound everything below.** Fitzpatrick's book is a founder-oriented practitioner handbook; the local copy read for this repository carries **no publisher, year or edition statement**, so it cannot be cited by edition, and it reports no controlled comparison, no measured effect and no validated threshold. Portigal is a **first edition only** — the later edition has not been read here — and is a first-person practitioner synthesis whose recommendations are craft claims; the note records that even its empathy claim rests on an analogy in a footnote rather than on a study of interviewing.

### Three contaminants, and the repair for each

Fitzpatrick's classification is the most immediately usable thing in the source, because you will meet all three in your first hour.

**Compliments** are socially pleasant and evidentially empty. The repair is to deflect and return to a fact or a commitment. **Fluff** — generic self-description, future promise, hypothetical possibility — is repaired by anchoring to a particular past episode and asking for the sequence, what was tried, and what happened. **Ideas and feature requests** are recorded but not obeyed; the repair is to dig for the job, constraint or purchasing condition beneath them. A requested integration may be an optional convenience, the repeated reason alternatives failed, or shorthand for a costly reporting task, and those three imply entirely different products.

Portigal adds the discipline that makes the repair survive contact with your notes: **mark observed and reported material separately from your interpretation, while you are writing it.** An interpretation recorded silently becomes an observation by the time it is summarised.

### Behavioural, projective, evaluative

This is the classification you will apply in pass two, and it is deliberately crude because it needs to be applied quickly.

A **behavioural** question asks about something that happened: *walk me through the last time this went wrong.* A **projective** question asks about a hypothetical self: *would you use a tool that did this?* An **evaluative** question asks whether something is good: *do you think this would help?*

**The test is whether the answer is a memory or a prediction.** Only the first is evidence. The other two are the respondent's present mental model dressed as a fact about the future, and the interviewing literature is consistent that statements about future use, purchase and price expose the model rather than forecast the behaviour.

### The specimens are anchors, not aids

The bank you are repairing ships example answers with nearly every question. They exist for a real reason — a question lands badly, the room goes quiet, and an example rescues it — and that reason is why stripping them feels like vandalism.

Two things make them the defect anyway. Appending options narrows the response before it is given, which is the mechanism Portigal names when he instructs the interviewer to ask the shortest clear question and stop rather than trailing examples to relieve the silence. And the specimens in this particular bank disproportionately name the frictions the method happens to address, which means **the instrument tends to elicit the problems its authors can solve.**

There is a third effect and it outlives the interview. Several specimen answers embed figures. They are unattributed and presented as the plausible range of customer responses, which makes them function as implicit norms: an engineer who has read them hears a real customer's number as high or low against a scale nobody validated. **No figure from that material is reproduced anywhere in this repository, and the mechanism is teachable without them.**

### The bank's best feature, and the four failures it cannot fix

Credit first, because the diagnosis is worthless if it is indiscriminate. The bank enforces a **mandatory breadth-first block before any deep dive.** That is an independent rediscovery of the rule against investigating detail inside a problem category before establishing that the category matters — Fitzpatrick's *premature zoom* — and it is enforced structurally rather than merely advised, which is stronger than the published treatment. Its prior-attempt questions are textbook, its threshold questions ask what would be good enough, and several of its questions are properly behavioural.

Now the four failures, each diagnosable against published method.

**It asks the customer to endorse the solution.** Every section closes by asking where the interviewer's own solution category would help — asked by the person who has come to deliver that solution. This is the central prohibition in the interviewing literature and it is present here as the template rather than as a slip. **Check your set for it twice**, because the shape survives paraphrase.

**Its specimens lead the witness**, for the reasons above.

**It contains no observation.** Nobody is asked to demonstrate a deployment, share a screen during a pipeline run, or set up an environment while being watched. That is [S017](/wiki/seminars/S017.md)'s repair and you should not attempt it here.

**It never consolidates**, and it ships an artefact that prevents consolidation — a lookup table converting one person's single remark into a prescribed intervention. That is [S018](/wiki/seminars/S018.md)'s repair.

And three absences. **No data block at all**: nothing about where data comes from, who may see it, whether it is correct, whether it is labelled, whether anybody consented. **No question about what result would show it had not worked.** **No question about who loses if it succeeds**, in a practice whose commercial material carries an explicit headcount claim ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md)). That note is a paraphrase of access-controlled internal material; **no public reader can check it**, and it records no outcome data of any kind.

### Writing the three additions

The **data question** is asked of the person who owns the data, which is rarely the person who invited you. Where does this come from, who may see it, is it correct, is it labelled, did anybody agree to this being used for this. Written as yes-or-no items for the sponsor it becomes a compliance ritual; written as an open reconstruction for a data owner it is discovery.

The **disconfirmation question** is the one to spend time on: *what result, six months from now, would make you conclude this had not worked?* The bank has no equivalent. Neither, in most cases, does the customer — and **the fact that they cannot answer is itself the finding**, because a sponsor with no failure condition has not yet decided what the system is for.

The **who-loses question** is [S001](/wiki/seminars/S001.md) item 3 returning, and it lands harder here. It is asked of somebody other than the sponsor, because the sponsor may be the answer. Ask a person whose work the system changes and who does not control whether it arrives.

### The reflexive turn: your instrument invites flattering answers too

This is the session's real subject and it is the reason the platform work exists.

You are about to build a check that scores your questions for leadingness, and you will build it out of a model. **A model asked to grade something is a respondent with the same social incentive you are trying to design out of your bank** — except that its agreeableness has been documented rather than guessed at.

The standard citation for judge validation names four systematic biases and supplies a mitigation for each ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/zheng-mt-bench-chatbot-arena.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/zheng-mt-bench-chatbot-arena.md)). **Position bias** — the judge favours a response for where it sits in the prompt. **Verbosity bias** — longer answers are preferred independent of quality. **Self-enhancement bias** — a judge prefers outputs from its own model family. **Limited reasoning** — a judge cannot reliably grade a problem it cannot itself solve. The paper's most transferable idea is the standard it sets: judge quality is reported against the rate at which humans agree with each other, never against an imagined perfect accuracy, because on a subjective task there is no ground truth to be accurate about. **Its limits:** the agreement result is for strong judges on that task family and does not transfer automatically to weaker judges or specialist domains, and its bias taxonomy has aged better than its specific figures, none of which is quoted here.

Now put those two facts side by side. Verbosity bias means a longer question scores better. Self-enhancement bias means an evaluator built from the same model family that helped you write the questions will like the questions. **You are constructing an instrument that will approve of your work for reasons that have nothing to do with your work**, which is precisely the failure your question bank has, arriving from a different direction.

### The criteria will move to fit what you have already produced

The second reflexive problem is worse because it feels like progress.

Shankar's finding is a genuine circularity rather than a workflow inefficiency: **users need criteria to grade outputs, but grading outputs is how users define criteria** ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/shankar-evalgen.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/shankar-evalgen.md)). Some criteria appear to be dependent on the specific outputs observed rather than definable in advance, which the authors say raises serious questions for approaches assuming the independence of evaluation from observation of model outputs.

Applied here: if you write your leadingness criterion after looking at your own rewritten questions, the criterion will drift until your questions pass it. **That evidence is qualitative** — the paper establishes that drift occurs and matters, not how often or how much — and its user study is small by construction. The practical defence is ordering: write the criterion and the labelled batch before you look at any score.

The evaluation ladder that makes the criterion cheap enough to write first comes from Husain: decompose the system into features and scenarios, turn each scenario into an assertion, run the cheap deterministic assertions constantly and the expensive judged ones on a cadence ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/husain-your-ai-product-needs-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/husain-your-ai-product-needs-evals.md)). His statistical warning is the part teams skip and the part you need: **raw agreement is misleading under class imbalance, so report precision and recall separately.** In a question set most questions are not leading, so the class you care about is rare and raw agreement will look reassuring. **His source's limits:** a practitioner essay resting on a single sustained case, with no control condition, no measured before-and-after and no released dataset; its tooling names are dated.

### Your instrument is unreliable in a direction you cannot feel

Two final warnings, both direction-only.

Capability in these systems is **jagged**: the boundary between what they handle well and what they do not is uneven and does not track how hard a task feels to a person ([research/04-professional-formation/dellacqua-jagged-technological-frontier.md](/research/04-professional-formation/dellacqua-jagged-technological-frontier.md)). Outside the boundary you do not get a refusal; you get a confident wrong answer in the same register as the correct ones. **That note was written from an abstract, so the shape of the finding is usable and no number in it is.**

And perceived and actual effect diverge. A study of experienced developers found them slowed by AI assistance while believing they had been sped up ([research/04-professional-formation/metr-ai-assisted-productivity-study.md](/research/04-professional-formation/metr-ai-assisted-productivity-study.md)). **Direction and mechanism only; no magnitude from it is asserted here or anywhere in this wiki.**

The operational consequence for today is one sentence. **An evaluator that agrees with your question set has not been used as an instrument** — which is the same claim you are making about a customer who agrees with your bank.

## The workflow

**Build an evaluator that scores your questions for leadingness, then measure how much it disagrees with you.**

You will use the Agent Framework's evaluation surface ([research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-framework-and-evaluations-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-framework-and-evaluations-docs.md)). Steps 1, 2, 3 and 6 run locally with no API calls, and step 6 is the one this session most wants you to have done. **The documentation is read through search excerpts rather than full page fetches, many evaluators are preview, one consulted page is a superseded classic variant, and API surfaces differ by language — verify names before you rely on them.**

**Step 1 — write the deterministic check first.** Decompose the way Husain does. Feature: *the question set*. Scenario: *a question that names a solution category*. Assertion: *no question contains a term from the solution-category lexicon*. This is a local check, it runs without a model, and it will catch the crude half of the problem.

**Step 2 — build the dataset before you look at any score, and label it yourself.** One row per question, with your own label attached. Do this while your memory of writing the question is fresh and before any evaluator has opined, because that ordering is the only defence you have against criteria drift.

```jsonl
{"id": "q07", "pass": "original", "question": "Where do you think an assistant could help your engineers most?", "my_label": "leading", "why": "names my solution category and asks for endorsement"}
{"id": "q07r", "pass": "rewrite", "question": "Tell me about the last change that took longer than you expected. What did you try?", "my_label": "not_leading", "why": "asks for a memory; every answer leaves me somewhere different"}
{"id": "q11", "pass": "original", "question": "Is your review process painful?", "my_label": "leading", "why": "evaluative, and only one answer is socially easy"}
{"id": "q23r", "pass": "rewrite", "question": "What result, six months from now, would make you conclude this had not worked?", "my_label": "not_leading", "why": "disconfirmation; the customer being unable to answer is itself the finding"}
```

**Step 3 — choose the unit of analysis deliberately and write it beside every number you report.** The framework requires a multi-turn conversation to be split into query and response halves, and the documentation is explicit that **how you split determines what you are evaluating.** Three strategies are supplied — last turn, full, and per-turn. Here the unit is a single question rather than a conversation, so the choice is yours to make and to state; the point is that you made it. **A metric without its unit of analysis is not a metric.**

**Step 4 — add the judged evaluator, and treat the defaults as opinions.** The cloud tier defaults to relevance, coherence and task adherence, adding tool-call accuracy automatically when tool definitions are present. None of those is what you want. You want a rubric evaluator carrying your own criterion, and you want its threshold justified rather than inherited — the threshold is a policy choice separable from the measurement, so moving it changes the pass rate without changing anything about your questions.

```yaml
# eval-config.yaml — illustrative. Several evaluators are preview and surfaces
# differ by language. Verify every name against current documentation.
dataset: evals/question-set.jsonl

split_strategy: full          # DECISION, not a default. State it with every result.
repetitions: 5                # one sample from a stochastic system is not a measurement

local_checks:
  - no_solution_category_term  # deterministic; runs on every edit, needs no model

judged_evaluators:
  - name: leadingness_rubric
    input: "{{item.question}}"
    criterion: >
      Score the question on whether a respondent who wished to please the
      interviewer could answer it flatteringly. Ignore length. Ignore politeness.
      A question passes only if at least one honest answer would leave the
      interviewer worse off than another.

thresholds:
  leadingness_rubric: { cut: "<justify here>", why: "<a sentence, not an inheritance>" }

# No agreement-with-human data is published for any evaluator on this platform.
# The disagreement rate you measure in step 6 is the only warrant your numbers have.
```

**Step 5 — run repetitions and report the spread.** Repetitions exist because a single sample from a stochastic system is not a measurement. A pass rate with no interval attached is not a result.

**Step 6 — compare the evaluator's labels against your own, and report precision and recall separately.** Not raw agreement. Your leading questions are the minority class, and raw agreement is exactly what is not recommended when the classes are imbalanced. **Note what you are doing: measuring an agreement rate the vendor documentation does not publish for any of its evaluators.** That absence is not an oversight you are correcting; it is a gap you are obliged to fill yourself, every time.

**Step 7 — attack your own question set.** Give the agent your rewritten set and ask it to write, for each question, the most flattering answer a polite customer could give while telling the truth. Then ask it which of your questions it could not do this for. Record which questions survived and which you rewrote again. **Do not ask it to improve your questions** — an instrument asked to improve something adds; asked to attack it, it must locate a weakness, and locating is the harder operation.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That your check has a false-negative rate you can measure, and that the rate is worst on exactly the questions your rewrite was subtlest about. It also makes the reflexive point unforgettable rather than merely stated: **you will watch a judge approve a question you know is leading, and you will not be able to tell from its reason string that it is wrong.**

## What good looks like

- **No question in your set names your solution category.** Not once, not in the closing question of a section, not as a friendly prompt.
- Behavioural questions ask for the last real occurrence and reconstruct it: what happened, what was tried, what it cost, what they did instead.
- **Your rewritten questions can be answered in a way that is bad for you.** If every answer to a question leaves you in the same position, the question is decoration.
- The disconfirmation question is genuinely uncomfortable. **At least one question in your set should be one you would rather not ask.**
- The data question asks where the data comes from, who may see it, whether it is correct, whether it is labelled, and whether anybody consented — and it asks these of somebody who would know.
- The who-loses question is asked of somebody other than the sponsor.

## How it goes wrong

- **The rewrite that still leads** — you removed the product name and kept the shape, so the question still tells the customer what you hope to hear. *Warning sign:* your question contains a benefit.
- **Specimens kept for the novice** — you retain the example answers because a new interviewer needs them, and use them in the room. *Warning sign:* you have a plan for when to read them out.
- **Numbers as norms** — a specimen answer contained a figure, you now expect that figure, and you will hear a customer's real number as high or low against a scale that came from an illustration. *Warning sign:* you can recall a number from the bank and not where it came from.
- **The safe dangerous question** — you wrote a disconfirmation question that no customer would ever answer in the disconfirming direction. *Warning sign:* you would be comfortable asking it in front of your own sponsor.
- **The compliance data block** — you added the data questions as a checklist item and will ask them of the person who invited you rather than the person who owns the data. *Warning sign:* your data questions have yes-or-no answers.
- **Premature zoom** — you go deep on a problem category before establishing that the category matters. People will helpfully rank details inside a category that is not among their priorities. *Warning sign:* your first questions are about a feature.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed here.** Three of them produce material assessed at [S020](/wiki/seminars/S020.md): the disconfirmation question and its answer, the who-loses question and its informant, and the data block.

**1 · The polite customer.** Pair up. Your partner answers your whole set as a maximally polite customer — encouraging, agreeable, helpful, and completely unwilling to say anything that would disappoint you. Count the questions they could answer flatteringly. Rewrite those. Run it again.

> **What to notice.** The count falls sharply after the first round and much less after the second. That is the point at which your remaining leading questions are leading by shape rather than by wording, and shape survives paraphrase. **A question whose every answer leaves you in the same position is decoration**, however neutrally it is phrased.

**2 · Classify twelve, rewrite the evaluative ones.** Take twelve questions from the supplied bank. Mark each behavioural, projective or evaluative. Rewrite every evaluative question so that it asks about something that has already happened.

> **Model answers.** *Would you use an agent that summarised these reports?* → *When did you last need a summary of one of these reports — walk me through what you did.* · *Is this process painful?* → *Tell me about the last time this process went wrong.* · *Do you think your team would adopt this?* → *What was the last tool this team was asked to adopt, and what happened?* **The test is whether the answer is a memory or a prediction.** A rewrite that still asks for a prediction has changed the tense and not the question.

**3 · Strip a specimen and name what it did to you.** Take three questions that ship with example answers. Delete the examples. For each, write down what the example would have made you expect to hear — and whether it contained a figure.

> **Criterion.** A passing answer names a specific expectation, not a general one. "It would have made me expect a number around a certain level" passes. "It would have biased me" does not, because it is a description of the mechanism rather than of the anchor you were actually carrying. **If a specimen contained a figure, you now hold a scale that nobody validated**, and the honest move is to write the figure down somewhere and stop using it rather than to claim you have forgotten it.

**4 · Build the leadingness evaluator and measure its disagreement with you.** Run the workflow through step 6. Report precision and recall separately.

> **What you should find.** Raw agreement will look reassuring and will be misleading, because leading questions are the minority class in a set you have already rewritten — **which is exactly the condition under which raw agreement is not recommended.** Expect the evaluator to be worse on your subtle rewrites than on the originals, which is the wrong way round for an instrument you were planning to trust. Note also what you are measuring: an agreement rate that the platform documentation publishes for none of its evaluators.

**5 · Go back to your own item 2, then break it.** Find what you wrote to opening item 2 — *who benefits from the answer?* — before anything was taught. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then rewrite the question the bank asks, so that the answer is not useful to you by construction.

> **Criterion.** A passing answer names the interviewer as a beneficiary and says what the answer would be used for. "The customer benefits, because it helps us build the right thing" fails on both counts: it identifies the wrong beneficiary and it treats the answer as an input rather than as an endorsement that will later be quoted back at the customer as their own idea.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is. **The source's evidence is from school, university and laboratory settings; there is no adult-professional study in its reference list.**

**6 · Name the silence.** Closed book: what does the platform documentation publish about how well its evaluators agree with human judgement, and what follows for the number you produced in exercise 4?

> **Answer.** Nothing. No agreement rates, no calibration data, and no account of where the default thresholds came from. What follows is that the documentation accurately describes what the tooling computes and is no evidence at all that what it computes is right — so your own measured disagreement is the only warrant your leadingness score has.
>
> **Then compare.** Look back at your answer to opening item 3, about what changes when specimen answers are read out. You wrote it about a customer. **Write the same sentence about the model you just used to grade your questions.**

## What this does not establish

**Nothing here shows that repaired questions produce better decisions.** No source behind this session measures it, and the three interviewing sources each state that they report no controlled comparison, no measured effect and no validated threshold.

**The diagnosis holds one craft tradition against another.** The bank is practitioner work with no citations; the books used to condemn it are practitioner work that reports no measurement. The asymmetry this session relies on is structural rather than empirical — the published sources describe how their own questions could fail, and the bank contains no question capable of failing — and a learner who finds that thin has read the argument correctly. It is examined properly at [WP-016](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-016.md) §8.

**The corpus behind the diagnosis cannot be checked by anybody outside the organisation that holds it.** It is access-controlled, this repository holds only a white-labelled paraphrase, and it publishes no outcome, evaluation or comparison of any kind.

**No evaluator's agreement with human judgement is published, and none is asserted anywhere in this session.** The platform documentation supplies an accurate description of what is computed and no evidence of correctness. Most agent evaluators are preview, one consulted page is a superseded classic variant, and the documentation was read through search excerpts rather than full page fetches, so page structure is reconstructed.

**The judge-bias literature establishes a taxonomy, not a rate for your case.** Its agreement result is for strong judges on one task family at one moment, and nothing in it tells you how a rubric evaluator will behave on interview questions. **The criteria-drift finding is qualitative**: the paper establishes that drift occurs and matters, not how often or how much, and its user study is small by construction.

**The instrument warnings are direction-only.** The jagged-frontier note rests on an abstract; no magnitude from it is usable and none is asserted.
