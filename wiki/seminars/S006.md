# S006 · Constraints You Cannot See Yet

**Module:** [M02](/wiki/modules/M02.md) · **Quarter:** [Q1](/wiki/quarters/Q1.md) · **Complexity class:** 2
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** Microsoft Purview DSPM for AI — discovering the data-governance constraints that no brief mentions and no customer volunteers
**Whitepaper:** [WP-006](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-006.md)
**Retrieves from:** S003, S005
**Entry state:** either

## The situation

Every engagement arrives with a list of things that cannot change. Almost none of them has been tested.

Some are genuinely immovable — a regulator's requirement, a contract already signed, a system nobody has the source for. Some were immovable three years ago. Some were never immovable at all and are simply the shape of an argument nobody wanted to have.

Then there is a fourth category, and it is the one that ends engagements. It is not on the list because nobody in the room knows it exists. The customer's own permissions estate, the classification state of the documents you intend to ground on, the audit record you will be asked for in nine months and cannot produce because nothing was configured to keep it — none of that is in a brief, and none of it is withheld deliberately. **It is absent because nobody has been made to look.**

Answer these in writing before anything is taught.

1. Name three things about this engagement that cannot change. For each, write the person who told you — a name, not a document.
2. Which of the three has anybody tested this year, and what did the test consist of?
3. Name one constraint you have been treating as immovable because moving it would be somebody else's argument to have.
4. The brief says nothing about who is allowed to see the data this system would read. What would you have to find out before that silence stopped being reassuring?
5. If a problem space were entirely frozen, what would be left?

**Item 4 is the one this session is really about.** Most people answer items 1 to 3 with something usable and treat item 4 as a compliance matter for later. It is not a matter for later; it is a constraint that already exists, that will not be volunteered, and that is discoverable this week with an instrument rather than a conversation.

**Item 5 also has an answer, and refusing to find it is the second failure this session names.** Constraint is not a licence to stop.

## What you must produce

A **frozen/fluid register**. Six parts:

1. **One row per constraint**, stated as a sentence somebody could disagree with.
2. **A classification** — frozen, fluid, or untested. Untested is a real answer and is the commonest correct one.
3. **The source of the belief**, as a named person and the date they said it.
4. **The test that would settle it**, with the person who could actually run it, and an explicit flag for whether it settles the question or merely enquires into it.
5. **A separate block for constraints nobody stated** — the governance and permissions constraints you found by looking rather than by asking, each carrying the resolution of the instrument that found it.
6. **For every genuinely frozen entry, the consequence for scope**, stated rather than lamented.

## The material

### Three kinds of constraint wear identical clothes

A constraint arrives as a flat declarative sentence, and that sentence conceals which of three things it is.

It may be a **fact**: a statute, a signed contract, a physical limit. It may be a **residue**: something that was true, is no longer, and has never been re-examined because re-examining it was nobody's job. Or it may be a **position**: something that was never a constraint at all, and is the compressed form of an argument that somebody did not want to have in public.

The negotiation literature gives the working move for telling them apart, and it is not "push back". When a policy is invoked, Fisher and Ury's instruction is to examine the policy itself — its principle, its precedent, its purpose, its exceptions and its reciprocal implications ([research/05-fde-craft/fisher-ury-getting-to-yes.md](/research/05-fde-craft/fisher-ury-getting-to-yes.md)). A rule with no traceable purpose and no exceptions on record is usually a position. A rule with a purpose, a precedent and a documented exception regime is usually a fact, and the exception regime tells you where the fluid part is.

**That source has real limits and they matter here.** It is a practitioner framework from the Harvard Negotiation Project, not a controlled evaluation; the note records that historical cases in it "can illustrate possibility and mechanism but do not isolate causes", and that no effectiveness claim for any teaching use of the framework is warranted by it. Read it for the move, not for evidence that the move works.

### A constraint's source is a person, and if it is not, nobody holds it

The register's source column is the cheapest diagnostic available in this stage, and it works because writing down who said something is not an accusation.

A constraint attributed to a document can be neither challenged nor confirmed: the document does not answer questions, and the person who wrote it has usually moved on. A constraint attributed to "everyone knows" is a description of a social fact rather than a technical one. **A constraint whose only source is a document nobody currently owns is not a constraint; it is a residue with good typography.**

The frozen/fluid classification itself comes from a customer-embedded engineering organisation within a large technology vendor, whose practice corpus is recorded here as an original paraphrase at [research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md). It is, in this repository's judgement, the most transferable original idea in that corpus. **It is also entirely unevidenced there**: no engagement in which it was applied, no comparison against working without it, no record of a constraint it correctly reclassified, and no outcome data of any kind. The idea is good and the warrant is absent, and both things are true at once.

### A guess that loses its provenance becomes a constraint

There is a mechanism by which invented constraints enter a system and become indistinguishable from real ones, and it is worth naming because you will build it accidentally.

The threat-modelling pipeline documented at [research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-agentic-threat-modelling.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-agentic-threat-modelling.md) — internal material, paraphrased here and never quoted — has an early stage that is instructed to infer missing security properties from best practice when the architecture documents do not state them, to resolve conflicts in the more secure direction, and to record those inferences in a separate justification section rather than marking them in the table. A later stage, assembling the delivered document, is instructed to exclude that justification section. The inference enters as a guess, travels as an assumption, and arrives as a stated property with its provenance removed by design and its bias pointing optimistic.

**The general form is the one to carry: a value that is inferred at one stage and consumed as given at the next is a manufactured constraint.** It will be repeated back to you by people who believe it, and every one of them will name the document.

That note is access-controlled internal material and this repository holds only a white-labelled paraphrase of it. It reports no observed result of any kind — no comparison against a human-authored threat model, no defect-escape rate, no coverage measurement — so it is usable as a specimen of a design pattern and never as evidence about outcomes.

### The constraints that arrive with the platform, and are in nobody's brief

Here the session stops being a conversation and becomes engineering work.

Microsoft's Purview documentation for data security posture management makes an argument that is more important than the product, and it is stated precisely: because of the power and speed with which AI can proactively surface content that might be "obsolete, over-permissioned, or lacking governance controls", generative AI "amplifies the problem of oversharing data" ([research/06-microsoft-platform/purview-dspm-for-ai-and-defender-ai-spm-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/purview-dspm-for-ai-and-defender-ai-spm-docs.md)).

Read that as a claim about constraint discovery rather than about security. Oversharing is not new. Organisations have held over-permissioned document estates for two decades, and the reason it caused little damage is that nobody read them. **Deploying a capable retrieval system converts every dormant permissions error in an organisation into a live one, on the day it is switched on.** That is a constraint which exists before you arrive, is invisible in every conversation you will have, and is not withheld from you — nobody in the room has looked, because until you proposed retrieval there was no reason to.

The same documentation family splits governance into two questions that need two instruments, and the split is itself the lesson. The data question — what is our information doing inside AI tools — is Purview's. The workload question — what AI systems do we even have — belongs to posture management on the infrastructure side, whose central artefact is an inventory described as "application components, data, and AI artifacts from code to cloud", and whose attack-path analysis exists to find what the documentation calls "toxic combinations": individually acceptable configurations that compose into a breach. That material names grounding and fine-tuning as the two moments a model touches proprietary data, and therefore as the two moments data can leak by construction rather than by attack.

**Two limits, and they are not decoration.** The Purview page this repository read is explicitly the *classic* version, states that it has been replaced, and states that improvements will not be added to it; the workload-posture material was read through search-returned excerpts rather than a full page fetch, so its structure is reconstructed. Agent-level discovery and posture are documented as moving to a separate licence on a stated future date. And most importantly for this session: **the documentation publishes no detection rate, no false-positive rate and no measurement of how much oversharing exists in a typical estate. None may be asserted here or anywhere in this wiki, and none is.**

### Every governance control has a resolution, and if you cannot state it you do not know what it covers

This is the durable idea and it survives every product in the sentence above.

The default data risk assessment scans a bounded sample of the estate — the most-used sites, not all of them — on a weekly cadence, with a multi-day delay before the first results display and a further wait before custom results appear. Item-level scanning carries a cap on how many sites may be covered, a cap on items per location, an accuracy caveat above a stated file count, and an outright exclusion of one major storage service. Custom assessment results expire.

**An engineer who cannot state their governance tooling's scan scope, its cadence and its detection latency does not know what their controls cover.** The tool gives you a posture estimate. It does not give you a real-time control, and reading it as one is how an untested constraint gets filed as a fact with a screenshot attached.

One further trap sits in the same territory. Without an explicit policy, certain workloads audit that an interaction happened and do not capture the prompts and responses themselves. Knowing that something occurred is not knowing what was said. If your engagement will one day be asked to demonstrate what a system told somebody, that capture is a constraint on the design, and it is off until it is turned on.

### The safest remediation is the most disruptive, and somebody has to own that

The documentation's own caution on removing a sharing link — that it is "an action to be used sparingly because it could prevent people from accessing the item legitimately" — is the whole governance dilemma in one sentence.

It is also the moment a constraint stops being a fact and becomes a decision with an owner. **Anywhere your register contains an entry that could be resolved by an action nobody is willing to authorise, you have not found a constraint. You have found an unassigned decision**, and it belongs in the frozen column with a name against it rather than in the fluid column with an intention.

### Constraint is not a licence to stop

The practice corpus's accompanying rule is that even in an almost entirely frozen space there is something you can change, and that finding it is the job. It is an exhortation and not a finding; there is no evidence behind it whatever.

It is here anyway, because the failure it addresses is real and observable: a register whose fluid column is empty is almost never a description of the world. It is a description of how much of the world the author was willing to test.

## The workflow

**Find one constraint that nobody in the engagement has stated, using an instrument rather than a conversation, and record what the instrument cannot see.**

You will use Microsoft Purview's data security posture management for AI. If your tenant does not carry the required licensing or administrative consent — which is common, and the documentation records substantial prerequisites — run steps 1, 3, 6, 7 and 8 against the published documentation alone. **The resolution of the instrument is readable without operating it, and that is most of the exercise.**

**Step 1 — decide which question you are asking.** Data posture and workload posture are different questions with different tools. Write down which one your brief needs before you open anything. A brief that proposes grounding on an existing document estate needs both, and most learners will name only the second.

**Step 2 — open the DSPM for AI area in the Purview portal and record which version you are in.** The page this programme read carries an explicit notice that it is the classic version and has been replaced. If your portal shows something different from what is described here, the difference is the finding and it belongs in your register.

**Step 3 — before reading a single result, write down the instrument's resolution.** Scan scope, cadence, latency, and every stated exclusion. Take them from the documentation rather than from a portal summary tile, because a tile reports what was scanned and not what was scannable.

**Step 4 — read the recommendations list as a map of the problem space, not as a to-do list.** Each entry names a distinct risk. Note in particular which capture behaviours are off by default: for some workloads, auditing records that an interaction happened and not what was said, unless a policy exists.

**Step 5 — inspect a data risk assessment and read its structure.** The detail view separates identification from remediation from ongoing observation. That separation is worth stealing regardless of tooling, because teams routinely collapse the three and then cannot say which one they have done.

**Step 6 — write the register in a fixed shape.** Create `constraints.yaml` in your engagement folder. Every entry carries a source and a test, and no entry is admitted without both.

```yaml
# constraints.yaml — one entry per constraint. No entry without a source and a test.
- id: C-014
  statement: "The assessment record's field structure is fixed by the regulator."
  classification: untested            # frozen | fluid | untested
  source:
    kind: person                      # person | document | consensus
    who: "Head of Compliance Operations"
    said_on: 2026-07-14
  test:
    what: "Obtain the instrument that mandates the field list, from the regulator's liaison."
    who_can_run_it: "Head of Compliance Operations"
    settles_it: true                  # false means this is an enquiry, not a test
  found_by: brief                     # brief | interview | governance-scan
  scope_consequence: "If frozen, a structured form cannot replace the free-text note."

- id: C-021
  statement: "Sites holding candidate grounding content are over-permissioned."
  classification: frozen
  source:
    kind: person
    who: "Site owner, after a data risk assessment surfaced the sharing links"
    said_on: 2026-07-16
  instrument:
    name: "Purview data risk assessment (default)"
    scope: "Most-used sites only; one storage service excluded from item-level scanning"
    cadence: weekly
    latency: "Multi-day before first results display"
    blind_to: "Sites outside the sampled set; anything below item-level scan caps"
  test:
    what: "Item-level scan of the two sites the retrieval index would read."
    who_can_run_it: "Tenant administrator"
    settles_it: false                 # the site cap means some sources cannot be checked this way
  found_by: governance-scan
  scope_consequence: >
    Retrieval cannot be enabled over these sites until remediation has a named owner.
    The remediation that would close it is disruptive to legitimate access.
```

**Step 7 — write the blind spots down as entries, not as caveats.** Anything outside the scan scope, below a cap, or excluded by service is not clean. It is unobserved. A register that records "no findings" for an unscanned region has converted an absence of evidence into a constraint classified as fluid, which is the exact failure this session exists to prevent.

**Step 8 — attack your own register.** Give the agent your `constraints.yaml` and ask it to argue that every `frozen` entry is misclassified and that every `settles_it: true` is false. Then require it to supply, for each, the specific observation that would show your classification wrong. Record what you kept, what you rejected, and one entry you changed.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** Three things. That a governance posture has a resolution, and that "we are compliant" is a sentence with a scan scope, a cadence and a latency hidden inside it. That a constraint can be found by an instrument rather than stated by a person — which is the only category in your register that nobody could have told you. And that the instrument's silence is not an all-clear, because you will have written down, in your own file, exactly where it was not looking.

## What good looks like

- Every entry names a person and a date as the source. **A constraint whose only source is a document is a constraint nobody currently holds.**
- At least one entry was found by an instrument rather than stated by anyone, and it is marked as such.
- Every instrument-derived entry carries that instrument's scope, cadence and latency, and a `blind_to` field that is not empty.
- At least one item is reclassified, in either direction, with the reasoning shown and the evidence named.
- At least one item is genuinely frozen, and its consequence for scope is stated rather than lamented.
- Untested entries are marked untested and carry a named next action and a person, not a hope.
- The register contains at least one entry whose resolution requires a decision nobody has been willing to take, filed as such rather than as a technical fact.

## How it goes wrong

Seven failure modes, each with the warning sign that precedes it.

- **Inherited freeze** — a constraint is immovable because it was immovable on the last engagement. *Warning sign:* the source of the belief is "everyone knows", or a person who has left.
- **Learned helplessness** — the space is declared entirely frozen and the work quietly stops. *Warning sign:* your fluid column is empty and your register reads as an explanation of why nothing happened.
- **The convenient freeze** — the items filed as immovable are precisely those whose testing would require an uncomfortable conversation. *Warning sign:* the frozen list and the list of people you have not contacted are the same list.
- **The untested thaw** — a constraint is declared fluid because that would be convenient. *Warning sign:* the test you wrote for it is "ask and see", and `settles_it` is true with nothing behind it.
- **Scan-scope blindness** — a governance tool reports no findings and the region it never scanned is recorded as clean. *Warning sign:* your `blind_to` field is empty, or you cannot say what the instrument's cadence is without looking it up again.
- **Laundered inference** — a value you or a tool inferred once is consumed as given afterwards, and its provenance disappears. *Warning sign:* you cannot say, for some entry, whether it came from a document, a person or an assumption you made on Tuesday.
- **The screenshot as fact** — a portal view is treated as the state of the world rather than as the output of a sampled, delayed instrument. *Warning sign:* your evidence for a classification is an image, and the image has no date and no scope attached.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The register is carried into [S008](/wiki/seminars/S008.md) and [S010](/wiki/seminars/S010.md), where a refusal resting on a constraint you marked untested will fail on those grounds.

**1 · Classify three, and name the kind.** Take these three statements. For each, say whether it is most likely a fact, a residue or a position, and write the single question that would tell you.

- *The record's field structure is set by the regulator.*
- *We cannot use that data because of GDPR.*
- *Security will never approve an external endpoint.*

> **Model answers.** The first is testable and probably a residue or a fact: the question is *which instrument mandates it, and may I see it?* The second is almost always a position — a regulation is not a prohibition, it is a set of conditions — and the question is *which lawful basis was considered, and who assessed it?* The third names no rule at all; it is a prediction about a person, and the question is *who in security has been asked, and what did they say?* **The test is whether your question has an answer other than the speaker's opinion.**

**2 · State the resolution.** Using the workflow above, find and write down the scan scope, the cadence, the detection latency and one stated exclusion for one governance instrument. Then write the sentence your register entry would carry if that instrument returned nothing.

> **What you should find.** A default assessment covering a sampled subset of the estate rather than all of it, running on a weekly cadence, with a multi-day delay before first results and a cap on item-level coverage — plus at least one service excluded from item-level scanning altogether. **The sentence you should write is not "no oversharing found". It is "not observed within this scope, at this cadence, as of this date."** If you wrote the first, that is the finding of this exercise.

**3 · Go back to your own item 4.** Find what you wrote to opening item 4, before you read anything, about the silence in the brief on who may see the data. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then rewrite it as a register entry with a source, a test and an instrument resolution.

> **Criterion.** A passing answer names something discoverable without the customer's help, says what instrument would discover it, and states what that instrument cannot see. "We would need to check the permissions" fails on all three. "The sites we intend to ground on can be assessed for oversharing by a scan that covers only the most-used sites on a weekly cadence, so anything outside that set stays unobserved" passes.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Launder an inference deliberately, then find it.** Take one entry in your register whose value you inferred rather than were told. Write it into the table as a flat statement with no marker, put your justification in a separate note, then hand the table alone to a peer and ask them which entries are inferred.

> **What to notice.** They will not be able to tell, and neither will you in three weeks. **This is the mechanism by which a design's own assumptions become the customer's constraints**, and it is structural rather than careless: the pipeline described in the threat-modelling material does exactly this by instruction, with the bias pointing towards the more secure reading. The repair is not more care. It is a field in the schema that survives the hand-off.

**5 · Two products, two questions.** From memory, closed book: name the two distinct governance questions that require separate tooling, and say which one a permissions fix addresses.

> **Answer.** What our data is doing inside AI systems, and what AI systems the organisation has at all. A permissions fix addresses the first and is silent on the second — an unregistered application built by a department is not made safe by correcting a document library.
>
> **Then compare.** Look back at your answer to opening item 1, where you named three things that cannot change. Which of those two questions did any of your three constraints come from? For most learners the answer is neither, and **that omission is more useful to you than the recall was.**

## What this does not establish

**The frozen/fluid classification has no evidence behind it.** The corpus that supplies it records no engagement in which it was applied, no comparison against working without it, and no instance of a constraint it correctly reclassified. It is a practitioner idea, recorded from a complete reading, and it is unwarranted.

**The accompanying rule — that even in an almost entirely frozen space there is something you can change — is an exhortation, not a finding.** It is included because the failure it addresses is real and observable, and because a practice with no answer to a hard constraint produces engineers who treat difficulty as a reason. It will also, predictably, be quoted at engineers who are correctly reporting that something cannot be done. Knowing that in advance is most of the defence available.

**The platform material is first-party documentation and its authority is definitional.** It tells you what the tooling does. It does not tell you that the tooling works, and it publishes **no detection rate, no false-positive rate and no measurement of oversharing prevalence** — so none appears here, and any such figure elsewhere in this wiki would be a defect. One of the two pages behind this session is explicitly superseded; the other was read through search excerpts rather than fetched in full.

**The oversharing amplification argument is a mechanism, not a measurement.** That capable retrieval makes latent permission errors consequential is a claim about how systems compose. Nobody has published how often it happens, how much is exposed, or what proportion of estates are affected.

**The inference-laundering pattern is a specimen, not a study.** It is a real design read in full, and it reports no observed result whatever — no comparison, no defect-escape rate, no coverage measurement. It shows that a thing is buildable and that it is buildable by competent people. It shows nothing about frequency.
