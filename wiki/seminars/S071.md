# S071 · What Actually Transfers

**Module:** [M15](/wiki/modules/M15.md) · **Quarter:** [Q8](/wiki/quarters/Q8.md) · **Complexity class:** 5
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** An agent configuration corpus read at a pinned commit, and the Microsoft Foundry control plane's portal-only Operate panes — what transfers is the versioned artefact, never the conversation that produced it, and never a governance surface that exists only as clicks
**Whitepaper:** [WP-071](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-071.md)
**Retrieves from:** S055, S058, S064, S070
**Entry state:** this-programme-only

## The situation

The system is live. The end date is fixed and near. Somebody has used the phrase *hand it over*, everybody in the room nodded, and nobody has said what the phrase refers to.

The meeting will happen. It will go well. There will be a deck, a walkthrough, a demonstration and a set of questions that get good answers. Somebody will take notes. At the end of it a senior person on the customer side will say *thank you, we've got it* — and they will mean it, and they will be wrong, and **the meeting is the mechanism by which they came to be wrong.**

Answer these in writing before the session opens. They are not rhetorical and they are not scaffolded.

1. Name the thing you are handing over. Not the system — the list of objects. How long is your list?
2. Which item on your list is currently held in a place that is not the repository? Name the place.
3. Which item, if you are honest, has no plausible receiver at this customer?
4. What happens to that item when you leave?
5. If somebody accepted your handover, what would they have done? Describe the act.

**Item 2 is the instrument.** Most learners answer it with one item, usually the cost model, and stop. The finding of this session is that the honest answer is long, that most of it is not a file at all, and that the parts which are not files are the parts the handover meeting most convincingly disguises.

Item 5 is the second finding, and it collapses differently: most learners can name a handover and cannot name an acceptance, which means what they have designed is a departure with paperwork.

## What you must produce

**A transfer manifest**, one line per object, with six fields on every line:

1. **The object**, named specifically enough that a stranger could go and find it.
2. **Its location**, as a path, a URL or an honest statement that it has none.
3. **Its current holder** — the person or system that has it now.
4. **The named receiving owner** — a role and a person, and the person must be one who could act.
5. **The acceptance condition** — something the recipient does, evaluable with you out of the room.
6. **The consequence if it is not accepted**, stated as what stops working.

The manifest must contain **at least one line recorded as unaccepted**, with its consequence stated. If your first draft has none, you have written a wish list.

It must also carry a **pin**: the commit, the package version, the host, the model and the date at which the manifest describes the system. A manifest of a moving target is a description of nothing.

## The material

### The thing everybody in the handover meeting believes, and why it is false

The belief is that knowledge is transferred by explanation. It is a good belief. It is how most of us learned most of what we know, and it works in the room — the recipient's questions get answered, their model of the system improves visibly, and everyone can feel it happening.

The change-management literature has a name for what has actually been produced, and it is not the thing the meeting was for. Hiatt's ADKAR model distinguishes **knowledge** — "the information, training and education necessary to know how to change" — from **ability**, which is "turning knowledge into action" and "is achieved when a person or group has the demonstrated capability to implement the change at the required performance levels" ([research/05-fde-craft/hiatt-adkar.md](/research/05-fde-craft/hiatt-adkar.md)). His statement of the gap is flat: "The presence of knowledge is often insufficient by itself. Someone who recently completed lessons with a golf pro does not walk onto the course and par every hole." He goes further than most practitioner writing dares: "Some employees, depending on the change, may never develop the required abilities."

**A handover meeting is a knowledge intervention being scored as an ability outcome.** That is the whole error, and naming it is most of the session.

**Two limits travel with that source and both matter.** ADKAR is a practitioner model published by the consultancy that owns its trademark and sells the training in it; the ordering of its five elements is argued from the meanings of the words rather than from any study, and the book offers no independent evaluation of the model anywhere. It also contains a retention-by-modality claim sourced to a commercial training handbook that this repository does not reproduce and you should not repeat. **Take the knowledge-versus-ability distinction, which is well argued and observable, and leave the numbers, of which there are none worth having.**

### What survives you is what is versioned

Here is the operational form of the same claim, and it is the durable content of this session.

**An object transfers if and only if it exists somewhere a stranger can find it without asking you.** Everything else — the reasoning in a chat thread, the constraint somebody mentioned in a stand-up, the reason the threshold is where it is, the thing you know about the third environment — is not held by the customer at the end of the engagement. It is held by you, and you are leaving.

The cleanest available demonstration is an openly published agent configuration corpus, read at a pinned commit and recorded at [research/06-microsoft-platform/hve-core-repository.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/hve-core-repository.md). Its organising claim is that a coding agent's behaviour is an engineering artefact, and that if it is an artefact it should be authored, versioned, reviewed, packaged and distributed like any other. What makes it a teaching object for *this* session is one specific design decision: the corpus makes files under a tracking directory **authoritative over session memory**, with separate locations for research, plans, implementation evidence, critique and review records, under stable identifiers, so that a decision made in one phase can be found by identifier in another.

Read that as a handover mechanism rather than as a workflow feature. The stated purpose is provenance and restartability — a run that is interrupted resumes from files. **The unstated consequence is that the run becomes transferable, because the state is on disk rather than in a conversation.** A workflow whose state lives in the session dies with the session, and a delivery whose reasoning lives in a chat history dies with the engagement.

The corpus also separates authoring source, packaging manifest and generated output, and declares the generated tree non-canonical. That distinction is the one your manifest will get wrong first. **A generated artefact is not a transferable object; the thing that generates it is.** Handing over a rendered diagram, an exported dashboard or a compiled bundle transfers an output whose source of truth remains with you.

**Its limits are extreme and travel with every use.** Perishability is total on paths, counts, plugin names and package versions, all of which moved while the note was being written. Host and model dependence is absolute — every artefact is inert without a host that loads it, and a result observed on one client and one model is not a result about the corpus. Its prose carries benefit vocabulary that nothing in the tree supports. And it holds evaluation machinery with no committed result corpus, which is [S068](/wiki/seminars/S068.md)'s subject rather than this one's.

### The governance surface that has no file, and is therefore the strongest instance of this session's thesis

Everything above is an argument. Here is the platform meeting it, and losing.

A cloud AI platform ships a governance console reached by a button labelled **Operate**, aggregating the agent estate, the compliance posture, the quota position, the cost trend and the project administration across every project in a subscription ([research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-control-plane-and-governance.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-control-plane-and-governance.md)). Its panes are Overview, Assets, Compliance, Quota and Admin. It is a good product and the documentation's justification for it is honest: without it you would be reading the same facts across scattered portal blades and per-project views, so what it adds is cross-project visibility rather than new capability.

Then the documentation says this, in one line, without emphasis: **"These features are currently available through the Foundry portal only."**

Read that against everything in this session. **There is no API, no command-line surface and no deployment template for the composed governance experience.** It cannot be put in a pipeline. It cannot be diffed. It cannot be reviewed by somebody who was not shown it. It cannot be re-created from a repository after a mistake, and it cannot be handed over as configuration — because it is not configuration, it is a sequence of clicks that somebody performed once and can describe only from memory.

The qualification matters and does not rescue it. Everything the panes *do* is ultimately enforced by the underlying policy engine, the role-assignment system, the monitoring service and the directory, **and every one of those is automatable.** So the durable governance decisions can be expressed as code. What cannot be expressed as code is the composed view — which policies were considered, which exceptions were granted and why, which projects were compared against which, what the person looking at the Compliance tab concluded. **The decisions survive as their mechanical residue and the reasoning does not**, which is the same failure this session opened with, arriving through a product rather than through a meeting.

One prerequisite compounds it. The documentation states that the advanced governance features assume a configured AI gateway in front of the platform. **A capability whose prerequisite lives in another team's product is a capability your manifest cannot claim without naming that team**, and it is the kind of dependency that is invisible in a demonstration because the demonstration was run in an environment where somebody had already done it.

**Its limits, and they are severe.** First-party product documentation, definitional throughout and evidential nowhere: it states what the console shows and commits to nothing about whether using it improves any outcome. It is extremely perishable, and perishable in a specific way it admits — the platform's own role names were renamed while the documentation was being written, so automation must key on identifiers rather than names. Preview status is not uniform and must be checked feature by feature. **Portal-only is a statement about one moment**, and if it changes the durable claim of this section does not: a surface that cannot be expressed as an artefact cannot be handed over, whichever surface currently qualifies.

### The inventory is permission-relative, so you and your recipient are looking at different estates

A second finding from the same source, and it undermines the first step of every handover.

The Assets pane discovers the platform's own agents, an operations agent from a sibling product, workflow-engine agent loops, and custom agents that somebody registered by hand. **It does not discover the platform's older agent generation or its assistant objects, and the documentation says so.** So the inventory has a published blind spot, which is the good case, because a published blind spot can be written into a manifest.

The harder finding is the next sentence: **different users may see different agents on that pane, depending on their access on each underlying resource.** The inventory is computed against the reader's permissions. It is not a list of what exists; it is a list of what you are allowed to know exists.

**Two consequences, and both belong on your manifest.** The enumeration you produce under your own credentials is not the enumeration your recipient will produce under theirs, so *the estate as I could see it* is the only honest title for your list — with the date and the identity it was taken under. And the difference between the two lists is not noise to be reconciled away. It is a finding about the recipient's access, discovered while there is still somebody around to fix it.

The repair for the published blind spot is available and unglamorous: **an agent the inventory cannot discover can be registered into it by hand**, at which point it becomes an object with a name that a stranger can find. That is the whole of this session's method applied to one pane.

### The language is part of the object, and it is the part you cannot upload

There is a second reason the conversation does not transfer, and it is subtler than the storage problem.

Evans's argument for a ubiquitous language is about the **cost of translation**, not about vocabulary hygiene. His diagnosis: "A few members of the team manage to become bilingual, but they become bottlenecks of information flow, and their translations are inexact," and the verdict, "Translation blunts communication and makes knowledge crunching anemic" ([research/05-fde-craft/evans-domain-driven-design.md](/research/05-fde-craft/evans-domain-driven-design.md)).

During delivery, you were the bilingual member. You translated between what the customer's people said and what the system does, continuously and invisibly, and the translation was inexact and it did not matter because you were there to correct it. **At handover the bottleneck does not narrow — it disappears, and everything that was passing through it stops.** An artefact whose names only make sense once somebody explains them is an artefact that requires a translator you have just withdrawn.

Evans supplies a live test for whether a document has landed that is worth stealing verbatim: "If the terms explained in a design document don't start showing up in conversations and code, the document is not fulfilling its purpose." That is a handover test disguised as a documentation test. He also warns "Always remember that the model is not the diagram," which is the same warning one register up.

**Its limits:** a pattern-language practitioner book making no quantitative claim of any kind, whose evidence is the author's own named projects — several of which he reports failing, which raises the quality of the evidence above ordinary consultant anecdote without turning it into a finding. Its strategic patterns assume iterative development and continuing access to domain experts, which he states as prerequisites rather than recommendations.

### What the delivery standard specifies, and the shape of what it omits

A customer-embedded engineering organisation within a large technology vendor maintains a delivery standard which this repository holds as an original paraphrase ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-fde-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-fde-delivery-practice.md)). One of its disciplines is directly load-bearing here: **once a technical specification is settled, a change to it is recorded as a decision record rather than as an edit.**

That is the cheapest transferable discipline in the whole standard, and the reason is exactly this session's. An edited specification loses the fact that it changed, and with it the reason. The reason is the part the recipient needs and the part that was never written down, because at the moment of the edit the reason was obvious to everyone present.

**The standard reports no outcome, no evaluation, no comparison and no measurement of anything.** Its phase durations are planning figures. No engagement run under it is described. It is a competent organisation writing down how it intends to work, which is a real thing to be and is not a finding. **It is also silent on transfer**, and that silence is what [Q8](/wiki/quarters/Q8.md) is built around.

### Acceptance is a unit of work, not a signature

The last piece comes from the assessment literature, and it is the frame the whole module uses.

An entrustable professional activity is a unit of professional practice that can be handed to somebody to perform unsupervised. Ten Cate's granularity test is behavioural: activities are "the units of professional practice that constitute what clinicians do as daily work", they are "activities with a beginning and an end", and they "are only entrusted to trained personnel" ([research/08-assessment-epas/ten-cate-epa-framework.md](/research/08-assessment-epas/ten-cate-epa-framework.md)). Could a supervisor delegate this whole thing and walk away, and would the recipient know when they had finished it?

**Your manifest lines should pass that test, and most first drafts contain lines that fail both halves.** "Own the evaluation harness" has no end. "Understand the architecture" is not an activity. "Run the weekly regression and act on a failure" is a unit with a beginning, an end and an observable completion.

Ten Cate also supplies the field this session's manifest borrows and nobody else's does: **an entrustment decision can expire.** If the recipient "has not practiced the EPA for a long time, the entrustment decision may expire." A handover that assumes permanence is claiming more than the model it borrows from.

**Its limits:** a clinical model from an advocate, reporting adoption rather than effectiveness. Medicine has patients, legally responsible supervisors and a licensure endpoint; an engagement constructs its stakes, and the construction is the hardest part of the transfer. The full evidence standard — multiple trained observers producing deliberated judgements — is expensive, and any version this programme runs is a reduced one.

## The workflow

**Pin a configuration corpus, then extract from it only the objects that would survive the disappearance of everyone who wrote them.**

**Step 1 — pin.** Record commit, package version, host, model, enabled configuration and date. Every count you take is a count at a revision. **Never audit a moving target**, and never write a manifest against one.

**Step 2 — separate source from output.** Walk the tree and mark every path as authoring source, packaging manifest or generated output. The corpus declares its generated tree non-canonical; find where that is stated rather than taking it from this page. **Anything you would have put on a manifest that turns out to be generated is a line you were about to get wrong.**

**Step 3 — find the state that outlives a session.** Locate the tracking directory and the identifier scheme. Read one record end to end and answer a single question: *if the person who produced this had left the day after, what would a stranger be able to reconstruct?* Write the answer down before you read the corpus's own account of what the record is for.

**Step 4 — find the state that does not.** Now do the reverse. Identify at least three things about the corpus that a reader can only learn by asking somebody — a convention that is followed but not declared, a threshold with no stated derivation, a check whose tier determines whether it is advisory or authoritative in a way you had to infer. **These are the handover's real content and none of them is a file.**

**Step 5 — write the manifest, with the pin in it.**

```yaml
# transfer-manifest.yaml
# SHAPE, NOT SCHEMA. Every path is a fact about ONE revision. Re-derive, never copy.
pin:
  commit: "<full sha>"
  package_version: "<version>"
  host: "<client+version>"     # artefacts are inert without a host that loads them
  model: "<model+version>"
  described_on: "<YYYY-MM-DD>"

objects:
  - object: "Nightly evaluation run and its threshold set"
    location: "repo path, or NONE"
    kind: "source | manifest | generated"   # generated is not transferable; its source is
    held_by: "<name, role>"
    receiving_owner: "<name, role>"         # a person who could act. Enthusiasm is not authority.
    acceptance_condition: >
      Recipient runs the nightly job unaided, reads one failure,
      and states what they would change. Observed, with me out of the room.
    expires: "<YYYY-MM-DD>"                 # entrustment lapses through disuse
    consequence_if_unaccepted: "Regression detection stops. Nobody is alerted that it has."

  - object: "Reason the retrieval threshold is set where it is"
    location: NONE                          # <- the honest answer, and the finding
    kind: "source"
    held_by: "me"
    receiving_owner: null
    acceptance_condition: null
    expires: null
    consequence_if_unaccepted: >
      First time the threshold is questioned, it is changed by guess or frozen by fear.

unaccepted_lines: 1                          # a manifest with zero has not been written honestly
```

**Step 6 — attack your own manifest with the agent, and do not let it approve.** One prompt, and it must be this shape: *here is my manifest and here is the receiving organisation. For each line, argue that the named owner could not in fact accept it. Then name the three objects I have omitted entirely, and say why each is easy to omit.*

Read it for the omissions, not the arguments. Keep what is better than your own reasoning and **record what you rejected and why** — the rejection record is the artefact, not the acceptance. An agent asked to *review* a manifest will approve it; an agent asked to *name what is missing* has been given a task with an answer. Record separately that the instrument shares a model family with the tooling under audit and is therefore not independent of it.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** The physical difference between the two lists produced at steps 3 and 4. One is long, precise, and takes an afternoon to compile; the other is short, embarrassing, and takes ten minutes — and every item on the second one is something you would have transferred by saying it out loud in a meeting and believing it had landed. **You cannot argue somebody into that observation. You can only make them write both lists.**

### Second pass — the objects that are neither source nor generated

The first pass assumed every object is a file, somewhere, in some state. **Run this pass to find the ones that are not.** It is short and it is the pass that finds the governance surface.

**Step 7 — enumerate the estate, and record the identity you enumerated it under.** Open the governance console's inventory. List every agent it shows you. Then write, on the same page, the account you were signed in as and the roles that account holds — because the pane computes its answer against them.

**Step 8 — find the two blind spots and tell them apart.** One is published: the documentation names the object classes the inventory does not discover, so read it rather than inferring it. The other is yours: things that exist and that your permissions hide. **The first is a property of the product and belongs in the manifest as a permanent note. The second is a property of you and disappears when you leave**, which makes it a different and more urgent kind of problem.

**Step 9 — attempt to export the governance surface, and record the failure.** Try to obtain the Operate panes' configuration as a file — an export, a template, a command that prints it. Record what you actually get. The answer available today is that the composed surface has no such representation, and the finding is not that you failed. **The finding is that you now know which of your manifest lines can never carry a location**, and you know it while there is still time to write down the reasoning by hand.

**Step 10 — add the third kind to the manifest.**

```yaml
# governance-surface.yaml — appended to transfer-manifest.yaml.
# A THIRD kind. Not source, not generated: state that exists only as a console view.
# Every path, pane and product name below is a fact about ONE date. Re-derive.
enumerated_by:
  identity: "<the account this list was produced under>"
  roles_held: ["<role>", "<role>"]      # the pane answers differently for a different reader
  on_date: "<YYYY-MM-DD>"
  # the recipient's list WILL differ. That difference is a finding, not a reconciliation task.

not_discoverable_by_the_console:
  published_blind_spot: ["<object classes the docs say are unsupported>"]
  registered_by_hand: ["<what you registered so it would appear at all>"]

objects:
  - object: "Guardrail policy set and its named exceptions"
    kind: "portal-state"                # <- neither source nor generated
    location: NONE                       # the composed view has no export
    mechanical_residue: "<the policy assignments it created, which ARE code>"
    reasoning_residue: NONE              # <- the honest answer, and the finding
    receiving_owner: "<role, person>"
    acceptance_condition: >
      Recipient opens the Compliance pane on their own credentials, names one
      exception, and states why it was granted. If they cannot, it was not transferred.
    consequence_if_unaccepted: >
      The exception is inherited as a fact with no reason. It will be either
      removed by somebody tidying up, or preserved forever by somebody afraid to.

  - object: "The gateway the advanced governance features depend on"
    kind: "prerequisite held by another team"
    location: "<their repository, or NONE>"
    receiving_owner: null                # <- if this is null, the pane degrades and nobody knows why
```

**Step 11 — attack the second list with the agent, on a different question from step 6.** One prompt: *here is a list of governance objects that exist only as a console view, and here is the receiving organisation. For each, describe the specific way a successor would misread it a year from now, having inherited the mechanical residue and none of the reasoning. Do not propose documentation.*

The forbidden move is the useful constraint. An agent allowed to propose documentation will propose documentation, which is the repair you already know about and the one that decays first. **An agent forced to describe a misreading has to model a successor**, and the misreadings it produces are testable against the people you are handing to. Record what you rejected. Record also that the instrument has never seen the console and is reasoning entirely from your description of it — which is exactly the position your successor will be in, and is the reason the exercise works at all.

**What the second pass makes observable.** That your first-pass manifest silently assumed a two-way classification — source or generated — and that the objects doing the most governance work fit neither. **A taxonomy is a claim about the world, and the fastest way to test one is to look for the thing it cannot name.**

## What good looks like

- The manifest contains objects the source standard never mentions transferring: the infrastructure definitions, the pipeline, the runbooks, the evaluation harness, the cost model and the monitoring. **Those six are the ones that go missing**, because none is the product and all are how the product keeps working.
- Every line carries a location, and **at least one location is honestly recorded as `NONE`**.
- Generated artefacts are marked as generated, and the manifest names their source instead.
- Each receiving owner is a role and a person, and the person could act.
- Each acceptance condition is something the recipient does, evaluable with you out of the room.
- Each line has an end a recipient would recognise, and an expiry.
- The unaccepted line is real, and its consequence is stated as what stops working rather than as risk.
- The manifest is pinned, and says that it describes one revision.

## How it goes wrong

- **Access mistaken for transfer** — permissions are granted, the manifest is marked complete, and nothing has moved. *Warning sign:* your acceptance condition is a thing you do rather than a thing they do.
- **The conversation counted as an artefact** — the reasoning was explained, thoroughly and well, and is recorded nowhere. *Warning sign:* you can answer a question about the system faster than you can find where the answer is written.
- **The generated object on the manifest** — a rendered output is transferred and its source stays with you. *Warning sign:* the recipient could read the object and could not reproduce it.
- **The everything manifest** — every artefact at equal weight, so no receiver can tell what matters. *Warning sign:* your manifest has no order, or its order is the order you built things in.
- **The orphan you did not look for** — the harness and the cost model have no owner because they are nobody's job on either side. *Warning sign:* every line has an owner and you found that easy.
- **The invisible location** — an object lives in a spreadsheet, a personal drive, a chat history or a memory, and appears as though it lived with the code. *Warning sign:* you cannot say the path.
- **The willing under-authorised owner** — the person who most wants the system is named against every line and holds no budget, no access and no mandate. *Warning sign:* one name appears against most of the difficult lines.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The manifest is carried to [S075](/wiki/seminars/S075.md), where it is one of the assessed artefacts.

**1 · Sort eight things into transferable, reconstructible and lost.** For each, say what a stranger would have to do to obtain it after you left.

- *The Terraform in the repository.* · *The reason the retry count is three.* · *The dashboard, exported as an image.* · *The dashboard's query definition, committed.* · *The name of the person in networking who unblocks things.* · *The decision record explaining why the second architecture was rejected.* · *Your sense of which alerts are noise.* · *The evaluation threshold, as a committed constant.*

> **Model answers.** Transferable: the Terraform, the committed query definition, the decision record, the committed constant — all findable without you. Reconstructible at cost: the exported image, since its source exists somewhere; and the retry count's reason, if a decision record was written at the time and not after. Lost: the name in networking, your sense of which alerts are noise, and the retry reason if it was never recorded. **The general rule: an object transfers if a stranger can find it without asking anyone.** Notice that two items differ only in whether a record was written at the moment the decision was made, which is why the decision-record discipline is a handover discipline and not a documentation one.

**2 · Run steps 3 and 4 on the pinned corpus and put the two lists side by side.** Time each.

> **What you should find.** The first list is long and takes an afternoon; the second is short and takes minutes, and every item on it is a thing a competent person would have transferred by explaining it. **The second list is the session.** If your second list is empty, you have not looked hard enough — try asking what you would need to tell a new joiner in their first week that is not written anywhere, and check whether the corpus's own documentation contains it.

**3 · Go back to your own item 2, then break it.** Find what you wrote for opening item 2 — which item on your list is held somewhere that is not the repository. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then write the honest version.

> **Criterion.** An answer passes if it names at least one object with **no file anywhere** — a reason, a convention, a relationship, a judgement — and states what a stranger would have to do to obtain it. An answer listing only misplaced files fails, because misplaced files are a tidying problem and this is not a tidying session. *"The cost model is in a spreadsheet"* is a start and not a pass. *"Why we route to the provisioned endpoint first is in nobody's file and three people's heads"* passes.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Rewrite three manifest lines as activities with an end.** Take three of your own lines and rewrite each so a recipient would know when they had finished.

> **Criterion.** Following ten Cate: bounded work with a beginning and an end, delegable whole. *"Own the harness"* fails on both counts. *"Understand the cost model"* is not an activity. *"Run the monthly cost review and produce the variance note"* passes. **Then add an expiry to each**, and notice how uncomfortable that is — the discomfort is the model being honest about something your handover was quietly assuming.

**5 · Find the bilingual bottleneck.** List every term your system uses that a new operator would have to have explained. For each, say whether the explanation exists in a file.

> **What to notice.** That the list is longer than you expected and that most of the explanations are in your head, where they have been doing useful work all engagement. Evans's test applies directly: if the terms in your design document are not showing up in the customer's conversations and code, the document is not fulfilling its purpose. **A glossary written at handover is written too late to be tested**, which is an argument for writing it in week two, not week twelve.

**6 · Pick the line you cannot defend, and give it to somebody else.** Hand your manifest to another learner with one instruction: choose the line they believe you cannot defend, and ask what the named owner would have to do to accept it.

> **Scoring criterion.** **You may not answer with a document.** If your answer is that you will write something for them, that line is unaccepted and moves to the unaccepted list with its consequence stated. A pass is a manifest that got shorter and more honest. **A manifest that survived this exercise unchanged was not examined.**

**7 · Sort six governance objects by whether they can be handed over as configuration.** For each, say what the recipient would actually receive, and what they would have to be told.

- *A role assignment granting an operator access to the agent endpoint.* · *The decision to grant that role rather than a broader one.* · *A policy assignment enforcing a minimum safety control across a subscription.* · *The list of deployments exempted from that policy, and why each was exempted.* · *The inventory of agents as it appeared on your screen last Thursday.* · *The published statement of which agent classes that inventory cannot discover.*

> **Model answers.** Handed over as configuration: the role assignment and the policy assignment, both of which have a mechanical representation somebody can read, diff and re-create. **The exemption list is the interesting case** — the exemptions themselves are configuration, and the reasons for them are not, so the object splits in half at exactly the seam this session is about. Not configuration at all: the decision behind the role grant, the reasons behind the exemptions, and the screenshot-shaped inventory, which is permission-relative and dated and will never reproduce. The published blind-spot statement is the odd one out and the most valuable: **it is not your configuration, it is the vendor's, and the correct handover act is to cite it with its date rather than to restate it** — because restating it makes you the source of a fact that will change without telling you.
>
> **The general rule this exercise installs.** Ask of every governance object: *what would a stranger receive, and what would they still have to be told?* An object where the second answer is empty is transferable. An object where the second answer is long is a conversation you are about to have for the last time, and it should be written down before it happens rather than after.

## What this does not establish

**That a manifest improves anything.** No source behind this programme reports whether any delivered system survived its delivery team, was maintained afterwards, or was quietly abandoned. The practice corpus records no sustainment evidence of any kind and no post-deployment record for any of its cases.

**The manifest's warrant is entirely negative.** It is built from an omission: the delivery standard specifies the removal of the delivery organisation's own assets in detail and says nothing about transferring the running system, its definitions, its pipelines, its runbooks, its harness, its cost model or its monitoring to anyone. Inferring a practice from an absence is weak, and this session is one of the places this programme does it.

**The versioning claim is a claim about availability, not about comprehension.** A committed artefact is findable. Nothing here establishes that a findable artefact is understood, and [S072](/wiki/seminars/S072.md) exists because it frequently is not.

**The knowledge-versus-ability distinction is a practitioner model with no independent evaluation.** ADKAR's ordering is argued from the meanings of its terms; its publisher sells the training in it; and its only empirical material is vendor-run practitioner surveys reporting what people say caused outcomes rather than measured outcomes.

**The pinned corpus's design decisions are one vendor's opinion about agent configuration and are not the field.** Everything observed through it is bound to one host, one model and one revision, and its own transparency statement says so.

**That a portal-only governance surface causes worse outcomes than an automatable one.** Nothing is published on that question by anybody. The platform documentation states the constraint and offers no rationale for it, no roadmap out of it, and no evidence about its consequences. **The argument that a click-only surface cannot be handed over is a deduction from this session's own definition of transfer, not a finding**, and a reader who rejects that definition is entitled to reject the conclusion with it.

**Nor does anything establish how large the permission-relative gap is.** The documentation states that two readers may see different inventories and publishes no measure of how different, in what circumstances, or how often the difference matters. The instruction to compare your list against your recipient's rests on the mechanism being real, which is documented, and not on the gap being large, which is unmeasured.

**The six object categories are this design's invention.** They are derived from what the source standard omits, not from anything it states, and no source establishes that these six are the ones that go missing. It is an argument from the shape of an absence.
