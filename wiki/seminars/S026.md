# S026 · What Cannot Move, and Who Decides

**Module:** [M06](/wiki/modules/M06.md) · **Quarter:** [Q3](/wiki/quarters/Q3.md) · **Complexity class:** 3
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** Microsoft Purview DSPM for AI, Microsoft Agent 365 enablement and the Microsoft Foundry Control Plane — the constraints that are not yours to negotiate, the consent-holder who is not in the room, and an access model written before the first developer arrives
**Whitepaper:** [WP-026](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-026.md)
**Retrieves from:** S006, S007, S021
**Entry state:** either

## The situation

You have a specification. Somebody has now handed you the constraints.

It is a list. None of the items on it has a name attached, several of them are wrong, and at least one is something you assumed in the second week and have been repeating back to people ever since.

**Answer these in writing before reading further.** This is the first session in the stage where nobody supplies the criterion.

1. List everything in this engagement you have been told cannot change.
2. For each item, name the person who could change it. **A role is acceptable. A department is not.**
3. Which items did you assume rather than hear?
4. Which single constraint, if it moved, would change the design most?
5. What does the person holding that constraint get out of holding it?

**Item 2 is the one this session is really about**, and item 3 is the one that will embarrass you. Most learners produce a long list for item 1 and stall on item 2 after three entries. That stall is the finding. Constraints propagate by repetition — an engineer says something is not possible, a second engineer hears it as a fact, a slide records it, and within a month it sits in the register with the authority of a policy and the provenance of a guess. **A constraint you cannot attribute is not a constraint. It is a rumour with good posture**, and a register full of rumours is what most engagements actually run on.

## What you must produce

A **constraint register**. Every element of the problem space that bears on your specification, classified on an axis running from frozen to fluid, and carrying five things for each entry:

1. **The authority** — the role that could move it, or an explicit statement that no such role is known.
2. **The provenance** — stated by somebody, or assumed by you.
3. **The evidence** — what you actually have that it is frozen, and the date you got it.
4. **The interest** — what the holder of the constraint gets from it, insofar as you can tell.
5. **The price** — what it would cost to move it, where it is movable at one.

**The axis has more than two values.** Something that is movable at a price is not frozen, and the register must name the price.

## The material

### Provenance is erased by repetition, and nothing in the artefact records that

A constraint list is a flat document. Nothing about the shape of a line distinguishes a contractual obligation from a regulator's requirement from something an engineer said in week two. All three are repeated in meetings, all three are eventually cited to explain a design decision, and by the time they are cited none of them has an author — because the process that turns an assumption into a constraint is repetition, and repetition erases origin.

The consequences run in both directions and only one of them is visible. **A team that treats an assumption as a constraint gives away design freedom it actually had**, quietly, and never finds out. A team that treats a real constraint as an assumption discovers it in a review, at the point where changing the design is most expensive. The first failure is the more common and the more expensive, because it never produces an incident.

The frozen-to-fluid axis this session uses comes from the practice corpus behind this programme, recorded at [research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md), where it is the most transferable original idea in the collection and **where it is never once used**. No problem space in that material is classified. No engagement is described in which the classification changed a decision. **The material contains no reference, footnote, study or citation of any kind, anywhere**, so there is nothing behind the axis except the assertion that it is useful. You are being taught it because the failure it names is real and because you can test it yourself in an afternoon. That is a reason to try a method. It is not a reason to believe one.

### Who decides is a different question from what the rule is, and the second answer does not contain the first

This is the session's central claim, and it is easy to nod at and hard to act on.

Most engineering conversations about constraint are conversations about content. *Is the rule really that data cannot leave the region? Does it apply to derived data? What about telemetry?* Those are good questions and they are the wrong first questions, because a rule you have understood perfectly and cannot attribute to anybody is still unmovable — not because it is right, but because there is nobody to take it to.

The change literature is direct about where authority actually sits, and it is not where an organisation chart says. Kotter's argument is that individual sponsorship is insufficient, and he is explicit that his claim exceeds the usual one: major change needs a coalition carrying position power, expertise, credibility and leadership, because **"Individuals alone, no matter how competent or charismatic, never have all the assets needed to overcome tradition and inertia except in very small organizations"** ([research/05-fde-craft/kotter-leading-change.md](/research/05-fde-craft/kotter-leading-change.md)). The inverse is the sentence your register needs: **"One well-placed blocker can stop an entire change effort."** A constraint with one identified holder is tractable. A constraint whose holder is not on your engagement, and whose interest nobody has asked about, is where your design dies.

**Two limits on that source and they are severe.** It carries no footnotes, and Kotter says so — he draws no example or major idea from any published source but his own writing, and cites no evidence to bolster his conclusions. Every case in it is pseudonymous and unverifiable. Read it for the structural claim that authority is distributed and that coalitions have named components. Read it for nothing else.

The adoption literature supplies the diagnostic half. Hiatt's model asks which element of a person's readiness is missing before choosing an intervention, and records that **resistance is most commonly cited at the middle-manager layer**, on the grounds that this is the layer whose span of control a change actually moves ([research/05-fde-craft/hiatt-adkar.md](/research/05-fde-craft/hiatt-adkar.md)). That is exactly the layer most constraint lists come from. **The limits are heavy: the model is proprietary, its supporting research is practitioner self-reported and commercially interested, no independent evaluation of it is cited anywhere in the book, and its diagnostic instrument is an anonymous self-rating with a threshold.** It licenses direction and mechanism. It licenses no numbers.

The domain-modelling literature arrives at the same place from a third direction. Evans observes that **"model divergences are as likely to come from political fragmentation and differing management priorities as from technical concerns"**, and more bluntly that **"In practice, political relationships between teams often determine how systems are integrated"** ([research/05-fde-craft/evans-domain-driven-design.md](/research/05-fde-craft/evans-domain-driven-design.md)). If boundaries follow ownership, so do constraints — and the authority field is not administrative overhead but the field that tells you which design conversations are possible at all. **That source makes no quantitative claim of any kind and declares two prerequisites, iterative development and close domain-expert access, without which it claims nothing.**

### A stated constraint is an answer, and the need behind it may be satisfiable another way

*It must run in their own tenant.* *It must use the existing identity provider.* *The data cannot leave the country.* These are solutions somebody chose, to a problem they had. The problem may be satisfiable another way, and finding out requires asking why the constraint exists and why the obvious alternative was not taken.

The negotiation literature names the distinction and supplies the move. A **position** is a stated answer; an **interest** is the need that makes the answer attractive, and interests are frequently satisfiable in more than one way while positions are not ([research/05-fde-craft/fisher-ury-getting-to-yes.md](/research/05-fde-craft/fisher-ury-getting-to-yes.md)). The same source supplies two devices your register needs. The **alternative to agreement** is what you will actually do if the conversation ends without one, and knowing it is what stops you accepting less than it. The **trip wire** is a threshold written in advance, whose purpose is not to bind you but to force a pause before drift carries you into a commitment nobody chose.

That source's own guidance names the discipline this session most needs: **treat a stated requirement as an answer to investigate, not as proof that the speaker's underlying interest is understood** — and, in the same breath, that this is not licence to override an authorised decision, only to bring better options to whoever owns it.

**Its limits.** A practitioner framework built from analytic distinction and worked cases, third edition. Its historical examples illustrate mechanism without isolating causes; **it reports no controlled comparison of anything**; and its own cautions state that in delivery settings not every constraint is negotiable, because a regulator or an accountable risk owner may hold legitimate decision authority that no amount of interest-mapping displaces.

### Some constraints are genuinely not yours, and the governance platform will show you one

Here the session stops being a conversation and becomes engineering work.

Microsoft Purview's data-security posture management for AI exists to answer a question that is not about your system at all: what is happening to the organisation's data inside AI tools, and who is over-permissioned. Its central argument is the clearest available statement of why deploying a retrieval system is a security event rather than a feature launch — **"Because of the power and speed AI can proactively surface content that might be obsolete, over-permissioned, or lacking governance controls, generative AI amplifies the problem of oversharing data"** ([research/06-microsoft-platform/purview-dspm-for-ai-and-defender-ai-spm-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/purview-dspm-for-ai-and-defender-ai-spm-docs.md)).

Read the generalisation, because it is the durable part: **deploying a capable retrieval system converts every dormant permissions error in an organisation into a live one.** Oversharing was survivable for two decades only because nobody was reading. The moment a grounded assistant reads all of it on behalf of anyone who asks, the latent misconfiguration becomes an active leak — and the constraint that lands on your engagement is not *do not overshare* but *the permission estate belongs to somebody who is not in your standup*.

Three properties of that tooling bear directly on your register.

**The instrument has a resolution, and its resolution bounds what you may claim.** The default data risk assessment samples a subset of sites on a weekly cadence, with a multi-day delay before results first display and an expiry on custom results; item-level scanning is capped at a small number of sites and excludes one major storage location entirely. A tool that samples part of an estate periodically, with a delay, gives you a posture estimate rather than a control. **An engineer who cannot state their scan scope, their cadence and their latency does not know what their controls cover** — and a register entry that cites a governance report without those three has cited an opinion.

**The safest remediation is the most disruptive, and the documentation says so.** Among the item-level actions, removing a sharing link is flagged as **"an action to be used sparingly because it could prevent people from accessing the item legitimately."** That caution is the governance dilemma entire: somebody has to decide, and that somebody is your authority field.

**The default tells you less than you assume.** Without an explicit policy, auditing records that an interaction happened and not what was said. Knowing that an event occurred is not knowing its content, and a constraint resting on "it is all logged" may rest on the former while meaning the latter.

**Now the limits, which are why this is worth citing at all.** The page read for this repository is explicitly labelled the **classic** version and states that it has been replaced. Agent-level discovery and posture move to a separate licence on a stated date, changing what is available under which plan. The companion posture-management material was read through search excerpts rather than fetched in full. And most importantly: **no detection rate, no false-positive rate and no coverage measurement of any kind is published for any of it.** None is asserted anywhere in this programme. You have an accurate description of what the tooling computes and no evidence at all about how well it computes it.

### The consent-holder is not in the room, and there are only ever one or two of them

The posture tooling tells you who configured a thing. This next class of constraint is harder, because **the person who owns it may never have configured anything at all** — they simply hold a right that nobody else in the tenant holds, and until they exercise it your design does not run.

The clearest instance available is a single permission. Using the Work IQ tool from a Foundry agent requires **Microsoft Entra Global Administrator consent for a named application permission** ([research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-iq-intelligence-layer.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-iq-intelligence-layer.md), read 2026-07-31), alongside two Foundry roles for the developer, a Microsoft 365 Copilot licence held by the developer *and by every user who calls the tool through the agent*, and — stated flatly — **no support for virtual-network integration at all.** Four constraints, and only one of them is yours. The Global Administrator is almost never a workshop participant. Neither is the person who decides who gets a Copilot licence.

The agent control plane has the same shape one level up ([research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-365-and-agent-identity.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-365-and-agent-identity.md), read 2026-07-31). Enablement needs a tenant licence, **and** a global administrator who has accepted the terms of service **and** selected the users or groups it applies to. The note attached to that is the kind of thing that eats an afternoon and belongs in your register rather than in your memory: none of it flows **"even if the Azure Resource Manager properties on a Foundry resource are set to enabled"**. A configuration flag reading true, with nothing behind it, is not a misconfiguration — it is two consents that have not been given, by two people who have not been asked.

Approving a tool for tenant-wide use is the same story again, with a role name attached: it requires tenant-wide consent, and only two roles can grant it. **If neither role-holder is engaged, that is a blocker with a name, and naming it in writing before design proceeds is the whole of this session's discipline arriving early.**

**And then the published role matrix, which is the single most consequential thing in this material and appears in a table nobody would think to read.** For an agent flagged as at risk, the link that says *review* leads into three different products — and **the AI Administrator, the role that can approve agents, assign owners and grant tenant-wide tool consent, cannot open the evidence behind the risks it is being asked to act on.** Nobody in the published matrix can investigate the data-governance risks at all; that needs separate investigator roles granted inside a different product entirely. The consequence is exact and it is a design constraint rather than an inconvenience: **a single-operator process for agent risk cannot be written against these roles.** Any real one needs at least two people, and [S072](/wiki/seminars/S072.md) is where that bill arrives.

One last item belongs in the register for a different reason. **The licensing statements for this surface contradict each other across pages** — one says access is independent of the assistant licence, another requires that licence in an emphasised box, a third requires it of every calling user. They may be reconcilable, since they describe different doors. **No page reconciles them.** Which of the three your account team believes is itself a constraint, held by somebody, and it has a different owner from all four above.

**Its limits.** Read 2026-07-31, when the agent control plane had been generally available for under three months. **Substantial parts of this surface are preview, and the preview tiers are not uniform** — several capabilities sit behind a more restricted programme again, and a learner shown a capability without its status will assume it is available. Three announced capabilities carry a stated delivery date now in the past and **were not re-verified**; treat their status as unknown rather than shipped. A dated migration of alert paths is already booked. The Work IQ material is preview throughout, its documentation is published twice in two documentation sets **with divergent content**, and the layer it belongs to is named differently on different pages. **No detection rate, false-positive rate, coverage measurement or incident-reduction figure appears anywhere in any of it.**

### The access model is written before the first developer arrives, and the surface that expresses it cannot be version-controlled

This is the constraint most likely to be discovered on day one, by somebody who thinks they have found a product defect.

The platform inherits the standing division between a **control plane** — creating and configuring the resource, assigning roles, setting up networking — and a **data plane** — running inference, talking to an agent, running an evaluation ([research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-control-plane-and-governance.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-control-plane-and-governance.md), read 2026-07-31). The rollout guidance states the consequence without hedging: **"Management roles such as Owner or Contributor are not sufficient for all development scenarios. For example, a user can manage resources but still need data plane roles to chat with an agent."** An administrator with total authority over the resource cannot talk to the thing running inside it. **That is not a bug. It is the design working, and a team that does not know it will spend its first day debugging a decision.**

Four consequences belong in a register rather than in a wiki page somebody reads later.

**The role that creates the estate is not the role that uses it.** Of the built-in roles, one has no data actions whatsoever, one covers only calling an agent endpoint, and exactly one spans both planes — described in the documentation as a highly privileged self-serve role, which is a polite way of saying it is what you grant when you have given up on separation of duties. Fine-tuning needs both planes, so it needs that role or a pair.

**The infrastructure-as-code path silently produces a resource its creator cannot use.** The developer role is auto-assigned when you deploy from the portal and **is not** auto-assigned when you deploy from the command line or an SDK. A team that automates its provisioning has automated its own exclusion.

**The role names are mid-rename and the identifiers are not.** The documentation carries the rename notices inline. **Automation must key on the role identifiers rather than the names**, which is the general lesson: in a renaming platform, the durable handle is the one nobody markets.

**And the governance boundary stops short of the data.** Connected storage, key management, search and database resources **"are independent Azure resources with their own governance boundaries"** — the platform references them and does not govern them. Their private endpoints are not created for you. A team that has hardened the agent platform and not hardened the store holding its conversation history has hardened nothing that matters, **and the authority for that store is a different name in your register.**

Now three constraints that are genuinely not movable by anybody on your engagement, and one property of the surface itself.

**Capacity can be refused, and the rule for refusing it works against you specifically.** Quota is assigned per subscription, per region, per model. Increases go through a form whose stated policy is that requests are processed in order of receipt and **priority goes to customers who actively use their existing allocation**, with requests failing that test possibly denied. There is a defensible logic in rationing finite capacity towards demonstrated consumption. Read as a delivery dependency it is a request with **no published response time, no published approval rate and no published appeal route**, whose prioritisation rule structurally disadvantages a new engagement that has not yet consumed anything. Partner and community models cannot be increased at all. Certain service limits are fixed and cannot be raised. **That is a register entry with an authority you cannot name and a price you cannot pay.**

**There is no hard spend cap, and the platform says so affirmatively rather than merely omitting it.** Budgets and alerts exist; a stop does not. Anything resembling a ceiling is something you build, which makes it an architecture decision rather than a setting — [S064](/wiki/seminars/S064.md) is where it is priced, and the gateway from [S023](/wiki/seminars/S023.md) is where it gets built.

**Residency is decided by a deployment-type choice, and the two halves of it are separate questions.** Several deployment types exist across global, data-zone and regional processing scopes, plus a developer type carrying **no residency guarantee, a short lifetime and no service-level agreement** ([research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-agent-build-and-runtime.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-agent-build-and-runtime.md)). Separate where data rests from where inference is processed, choose deliberately, and record what the choice cost in money or latency variance. There is a sharper version of this when the agent control plane is in play: **the two products follow different residency models entirely** — one the Azure region you chose, the other the storage location of the directory tenant — and activity data crossing from the first into the second **moves between residency models**, with processing that may happen across geographies. For a regulated customer that is a question answered before the first agent ships or not at all.

**And the surface that expresses all of this is portal-only.** The aggregated governance panes are stated to be available **"through the Foundry portal only"** — no API, no command line, no template. Everything beneath them is policy and role assignment, which are automatable; **the composed experience is not.** A control plane you cannot call from code cannot be put in a pipeline, cannot be diffed, cannot be reviewed and **cannot be handed over as configuration.** A team that builds a handover around it is building a handover around screenshots, which is the same failure [S027](/wiki/seminars/S027.md) names about an unconfirmed map that looks confirmed.

**Its limits.** Read 2026-07-31. Seven pages were fetched and read in full; roughly twenty more were read only through search-returned excerpts, so **their structure is reconstructed and their tables may be truncated**; several referenced pages were not read at all. **This is vendor documentation about vendor governance** — it argues that centralised oversight is desirable and never discusses what the oversight layer costs in latency, permissions overhead or organisational friction. **Definitional, not evidential: no adoption, failure-rate, performance or outcome data appears anywhere in it.** Preview features are load-bearing in the story, and a governance argument resting on a preview feature is a governance argument without a service-level agreement. **Every proper noun in it is provisional and every identifier is more durable than the name attached to it.** And the numbers throughout — notice periods, lifecycle windows, caps and propagation delays — are dated platform parameters rather than findings; **none is reproduced here, and two of them are interpretive thresholds asserted with no stated basis at all.**

## The workflow

**Build the register as a file, then use the governance tooling to find out who actually owns three of its entries.**

You will work in the Microsoft Purview portal's data-security posture management for AI area, and in your own repository. Steps 1, 3, 6 and 7 run with no tenant access; if you hold no Purview role, do those and record the absence as a finding rather than skipping the session.

**Step 1 — write the register as a structured file, not a table in a document.** Put it at `docs/constraints/register.yaml`. A table cannot distinguish a missing field from an empty one, and that distinction is the whole session.

```yaml
# docs/constraints/register.yaml
# axis: frozen | movable-at-a-price | fluid | unknown
- id: C-014
  statement: "Customer data may not be surfaced to any assistant outside the tenant."
  axis: frozen
  authority:
    role: "Data protection officer, customer side"
    status: named          # named | known-unnamed | none-known   <- never blank
  provenance: stated       # stated | assumed
  evidence:
    what: "DSPM for AI data risk assessment, Identify tab"
    scope: "weekly sample of most-used sites; item-level scan capped; one store excluded"
    latency: "multi-day before first results display"
    checked: 2026-07-31
  interest: "Accountable for a breach they would have to explain outside the company."
  price: null              # null only where genuinely immovable

- id: C-021
  statement: "The existing identity provider must be used."
  axis: movable-at-a-price
  authority: { role: "Platform engineering lead", status: named }
  provenance: assumed      # <- I said this in week two and it stuck
  evidence: { what: "none", checked: 2026-07-31 }
  interest: "Owns the on-call rota for every new authentication path."
  price: "One quarter of their roadmap, plus a support obligation not yet agreed."
```

**Step 2 — open the AI hub and record which version of the surface you are on.** The classic surface and its replacement differ. Write the version and the date into `evidence.checked` for every entry that rests on it. A governance finding without a date is not a finding.

**Step 3 — record the instrument's resolution before you record any of its results.** Scan scope, cadence, latency, and what falls outside all three. Put them in the register, not in a footnote. **Every constraint you derive from this tool inherits all three, and a reader who cannot see them will over-trust your entry.**

**Step 4 — run or inspect a data risk assessment and read the Identify tab only.** Do not remediate. You are not here to fix an estate you do not own; you are here to find out who does. For each of your three most load-bearing constraints, find the artefact behind it — a sensitivity label, a data-loss-prevention policy, a restricted-discovery exemption — and ask the portal who configured it.

**Step 5 — use the notification path as a discovery instrument.** Where an item-level action offers to notify a site owner, that owner is the authority you have failed to find by asking people. Send nothing. **Read the name and write it into the register.** This is the cheapest authority discovery available in most estates and almost nobody uses it for that.

**Step 6 — ask the two questions of your three most load-bearing constraints.** First: why does this constraint exist? That produces a rationale. Second, and this is the informative one: **why was the obvious alternative not taken?** Answering the second forces the holder to reconstruct their own choice, including consequences for people they must answer to, and it is where a constraint that looked arbitrary from outside stops looking arbitrary.

**Step 7 — attack your own register, in both directions.** Give the agent your register and ask it first to argue that each `frozen` entry is not frozen, naming the evidence that would settle it. Then ask the harder question: **to argue that each `assumed` entry is a real constraint you have been lucky to ignore.** Record what you kept and what you rejected. An agent asked only to loosen your constraints has been pointed at the answer you wanted.

**Step 8 — enumerate the consents your design needs before anybody commits to a date.** Not permissions your team will hold; **rights that only one or two named role-holders in the customer's tenant can exercise at all.** Tenant-wide tool consent. Acceptance of a product's terms of service. Selection of the users a licence applies to. For each, write the role, whether that role-holder is engaged, and what the design loses if the answer is no. **An unexercised consent is not a task. It is a constraint with an authority**, and it belongs in the register with the rest.

**Step 9 — write the access model before the first developer arrives, and check it against the plane split.** For each persona, name the scope and the role. Then ask the one question that catches the day-one failure: **can this person actually talk to the thing?** Management authority and usage authority are different rights and the platform enforces the difference. Record the identifiers alongside the names, because the names are being changed underneath you.

```yaml
# docs/constraints/register.yaml  (continued)
# Consents and access rights are constraints. They belong in the same file as
# the residency rule, not in a separate onboarding checklist nobody re-reads.

- id: C-031
  statement: "Tenant-wide admin consent is required before the agent may call the work-data tool."
  axis: frozen
  authority:
    role: "Directory global administrator, customer side"
    status: known-unnamed        # named | known-unnamed | none-known
    engaged: false               # <- the finding. Nobody has asked them yet.
  provenance: stated
  evidence:
    what: "vendor prerequisite list for the tool"
    scope: "consent is tenant-wide; there is no project-scoped form of it"
    latency: "unknown - no published turnaround for an internal consent request"
    checked: 2026-07-31
  interest: "Accountable to a security function for every tenant-wide grant ever made."
  price: "A security review we have not scheduled and cannot schedule for them."
  if_refused: "The tool is unavailable. Name the design that survives without it."

- id: C-032
  statement: "Managing the resource does not grant the right to use what runs inside it."
  axis: frozen                   # architectural, not negotiable by anybody
  authority: { role: "Platform team, via role assignment", status: named }
  provenance: stated
  evidence:
    what: "published control-plane and data-plane split"
    scope: "applies to every persona, including administrators"
    checked: 2026-07-31
  note: >
    Automated provisioning does not assign the developer role; portal
    provisioning does. Key automation on role IDENTIFIERS, not names: the names
    are mid-rename and the identifiers are not.
  price: null

- id: C-033
  statement: "Where inference is processed, and where data rests, are two decisions."
  axis: frozen
  authority: { role: "Data protection officer, customer side", status: named }
  provenance: stated
  evidence: { what: "deployment-type table", checked: 2026-07-31 }
  note: >
    A developer-grade deployment type carries no residency guarantee and no
    service level. Two products in this stack follow DIFFERENT residency
    models, and data crossing between them changes model. Answer before the
    first agent ships; it cannot be answered afterwards.
  price: "Cost, or latency variance, or both. Name which you paid."

- id: C-034
  statement: "There is no hard spend ceiling. Alerts notify; nothing stops."
  axis: movable-at-a-price
  authority: { role: "Our own architecture", status: named }
  provenance: stated
  price: "A gateway we build and operate. See S023 and S064."
```

**Step 10 — record what the governance surface cannot hand over.** Where a control is expressed only in a portal, with no interface a script can call, **write that in the register as a property of the control rather than as a footnote.** It is the difference between a governance decision the receiving team can inherit and one they must be shown. **A control you cannot express as code is a control you cannot transfer**, and [S075](/wiki/seminars/S075.md) is where that becomes somebody's problem.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That a governance claim has a resolution, that the resolution is discoverable in about ten minutes, and that it is almost never stated. You will watch a constraint everybody treats as a fact about the organisation turn out to be a fact about a periodic sample of part of the estate — and the gap between those two is where your design freedom lives. **Steps 8 to 10 add the other half of the lesson**, which is that some constraints have no resolution to discover at all: a consent is given or it is not, capacity is granted or it is not, and neither has a published turnaround you could put on a plan.

## What good looks like

- **Every frozen item names an authority, or is marked as having none.** The second condition is worse than the first and must not be allowed to look the same. A constraint nobody owns cannot be moved by anybody, cannot be defended when it becomes expensive, and cannot be checked.
- **Assumed constraints are separated from stated ones** and the separation is visible at a glance.
- **At least one item you were told was frozen has been reclassified** after you asked somebody. If nothing moved, either you asked nobody or you asked the wrong people, and the register should say which.
- **The register records the interest behind the position, not only the position.** A constraint as stated is an answer somebody gave. The need behind it is a different thing and can frequently be met another way.
- **The price is named where an item is movable at one.** "The platform team could take this dependency, at the cost of a quarter of their roadmap and a support obligation they have not agreed to" is a usable register entry. "Not possible" is not.
- **Every entry derived from a governance tool carries that tool's scan scope, cadence and latency**, and the date it was checked.
- **The register is written so that somebody else could use it**, because at [S030](/wiki/seminars/S030.md) somebody will.

## How it goes wrong

- **The anonymous constraint** — the item is on the list, everybody respects it, and no one person is behind it. *Warning sign:* when you ask who decided, three people each name a fourth.
- **The inherited freeze** — it is frozen because it always has been. The original reason has expired and nobody has noticed because nobody has asked. *Warning sign:* the constraint predates everyone currently on the engagement.
- **The position taken for a constraint** — what you were given is an answer, not a need. *Warning sign:* the constraint is stated as a solution — a named product, a specific arrangement — rather than as a property that must hold.
- **The constraint you invented** — you assumed something, said it out loud, and it entered the register with your own authority behind it. *Warning sign:* you cannot remember who told you, and you are confident anyway.
- **The all-frozen register** — everything is fixed, so nothing is attempted. This is the failure the module exists against and [S028](/wiki/seminars/S028.md) is where it is confronted. *Warning sign:* your register has no entry marked movable at a price.
- **The instrument mistaken for the estate** — a posture report is read as a description of what is true rather than of what was sampled. *Warning sign:* your register cites a scan and does not state its scope.
- **The register that is a complaint** — every entry is written as an obstacle placed in your way rather than as somebody's reasonable decision. *Warning sign:* nobody from the customer's side could be shown this document.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The register is checked at [S030](/wiki/seminars/S030.md), and only on two properties: that it is attached as the design's frozen inputs, and that each entry names its authority or is explicitly marked as having none.

**1 · Classify eight statements as position or interest.** For each, write the interest you would go looking for.

- *The data cannot leave the country.* · *We need a dashboard.* · *No production data in any environment we do not control.* · *It has to use our identity provider.* · *Nothing that talks to a model may reach the finance system.* · *We need this by the end of the quarter.* · *Human review on every adverse outcome.* · *No third-party AI tools.*

> **Model answers.** All eight are positions. The interests behind them are, plausibly: legal exposure under a residency regime; decision latency for a named person; fear of uncontrolled access rather than of the data itself; the cost of operating a second authentication path and who carries its pager; a segregation-of-duties obligation somebody signs for; a commitment already made to somebody else; accountability for a decision with legal consequences; and an inability to see what staff already paste into tools nobody bought. **The test is whether your stated interest could be satisfied by something other than the stated position.** If it could not, you have restated the position.

**2 · Find the resolution of one instrument.** Using the workflow, state the scan scope, the assessment cadence and the result latency of the governance tooling you can reach, and name one thing that falls outside all three.

> **What you should find.** A periodic sample of part of the estate, a delay before first results, and item-level detail for only a small number of locations. **If you cannot obtain all three, that absence is the finding** — record it as an open question and mark every entry resting on the tool as `unknown` rather than `frozen`. Note what you are doing: establishing the coverage of a control that the documentation describes and does not measure. **No detection rate or false-positive rate is published for any of it, and you must not infer one.**

**3 · Go back to your own item 2, then break it.** Find what you wrote for opening item 2 — the person who could change each constraint. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then rewrite your three weakest entries properly.

> **Criterion.** An authority entry passes if a reader could send that person an email this week. "Security" fails. "The information security review board" fails, because a board is a process and not a decider. "The data protection officer on the customer side, who signs the residency exception" passes. **Where no such person is known, the entry must read `status: none-known` rather than being left blank** — a blank field and a marked absence look identical in a table and completely different to a later reader.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Write one trip wire.** Take the constraint from opening item 4 — the one whose movement would change the design most — and write the observation that, if it occurs, forces you to stop and re-plan rather than continue.

> **Scoring criterion.** A trip wire passes if somebody other than you could notice it firing. "If it becomes clear this will not work" fails. "If we reach the end of sprint four with no named owner for the residency exception, the design is rewritten to the in-region option and the schedule consequence is put in writing" passes. **A trip wire is not a decision; it is a forced pause**, and its entire value is that it was written before the pressure arrived.

**5 · Name the silence.** Closed book: what does the governance documentation publish about how well its detections perform, and what follows for your register?

> **Answer.** Nothing. No detection rates, no false-positive rates, no coverage measurements. What follows is that a constraint sourced from a posture report is sourced from a description of what a tool computes, with no evidence about how well it computes it — so the entry must carry the instrument's scope, cadence and latency, and must not carry a confidence it has not earned.
>
> **Then compare.** Look back at your answer to opening item 5 — what the holder of your hardest constraint gets from holding it. Did you write an interest, or did you write a motive? **An interest is something they would agree with out loud. A motive is something you attributed to them.** If you wrote a motive, you have not begun the conversation this session exists to make possible.

**6 · Find the four authorities your design already depends on and has never met.** Working from the platform your engagement will actually use, list every right your design needs that **only a named tenant role can exercise**, and every capacity your design needs that **somebody else can refuse.** For each, write the role, whether they are engaged, the published turnaround, and what the design becomes if the answer is no.

> **Criterion, and what you should find.** A passing entry names a role a reader could send an email to this week, and states the turnaround **or records that none is published.** The second is the commoner answer and it is the more useful one. You should find at least a tenant-wide consent, a licence-assignment decision, an acceptance of somebody's terms, and a capacity allocation — **and that none of the four has a published response time, an approval rate or an appeal route.**
>
> **The one worth dwelling on is capacity**, because its published prioritisation rule gives precedence to customers already consuming their existing allocation. Read as engineering that is defensible rationing of a finite resource. **Read as a delivery dependency it is a rule that works against a new engagement specifically**, and a new engagement is exactly what you are. It is not movable, it has no named holder you can reach, and its entry is `axis: frozen` with `price: null` and an honest `if_refused` line. **A constraint that cannot be moved and cannot be attributed is not a rumour — it is the harder case, and the register must distinguish the two rather than letting `none-known` cover both.**
>
> **And check one thing about the surface itself.** For each control your register cites, ask whether it can be read or set by anything other than a person clicking. Where it cannot, mark the entry. **A governance control that exists only in a portal is a control the receiving team inherits as a screenshot**, which is a fact about your handover and not about their competence.

## What this does not establish

**Nothing here shows that producing a constraint register improves an engagement.** The frozen-to-fluid classification is entirely unevidenced. It appears in the practice material behind this module as a method and is never used there — no worked instance of a problem space classified, no engagement in which the classification changed a decision, no account of what it costs to produce, and no record of it being wrong. **That material contains no reference, footnote, study or citation of any kind anywhere in it**, so there is nothing behind the classification except the assertion that it is useful.

It is taught here because it names a failure mode nothing else in this programme names, and because it is cheap to test — an afternoon's work produces a register you can check. **That is a reason to try a method. It is not a reason to believe it.**

The negotiation devices in the workflow come from a case-based practitioner framework whose authors state plainly that it is not a controlled evaluation and that its examples establish possibility and mechanism rather than effect. **No claim is made here about how often a constraint conversation succeeds**, because the source licenses none.

**The change and adoption literature licenses direction and mechanism only.** Kotter states that he cites no evidence and draws no idea from any published source but his own, and every case in his book is pseudonymous and uncheckable. Hiatt's model is proprietary, commercially interested and independently unevaluated, and its diagnostic threshold is a subjective self-rating. **No number from either appears here.**

**The platform material is first-party documentation with no effectiveness data.** One consulted page is explicitly superseded; the companion material was read through search excerpts rather than fetched in full; agent-level capability moves to a different licence on a stated date. **No detection rate, no false-positive rate and no coverage measurement is published, and none is asserted anywhere in this programme.**

**The governance material is vendor documentation about vendor governance, and it argues for its own premise.** It holds that centralised oversight is desirable and nowhere discusses what the oversight layer costs in latency, permissions overhead or organisational friction. It is **definitional rather than evidential**: it establishes what the platform permits and commits to, and measures nothing. Its authority over what is *permitted* is as strong as authority gets — if the page says a right needs a directory administrator, it needs one. **Its authority over whether any of this produces a better outcome is nil, because no such claim is made anywhere in it.**

**Verification depth is uneven and the session's account of it is not exhaustive.** Seven pages of the control-plane material were fetched in full and roughly twenty more were read only through search excerpts, with structure reconstructed and tables possibly truncated; several referenced pages were not read at all. The agent-control-plane material was read under three months after that product reached general availability, **with preview status varying feature by feature and three announced capabilities carrying a stated date now past that were not re-verified.** The work-data material is preview throughout and its own pages contradict each other on licensing, on layer naming and on storage. **Every proper noun in this session's platform paragraphs is provisional**, the numbers behind them are dated platform parameters rather than findings and are not reproduced, and two figures in the source are interpretive thresholds asserted with no basis at all.

**Nothing here establishes that enumerating consents earlier produces a better engagement**, only that a consent nobody has requested is invisible in a plan and visible in a register. That is a claim about legibility. It is not a claim about outcomes, and no source behind this session offers one.

**A register makes a classification look settled.** A learner who has written *frozen* beside an item may be less likely to test it than one who wrote nothing down at all. This session has no answer to that, and [WP-026](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-026.md) concedes it rather than defending it.
