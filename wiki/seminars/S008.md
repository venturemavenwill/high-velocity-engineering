## The situation

The first question in a published responsible-AI assessment used by a customer-embedded engineering organisation is not about models, data or risk. It is: can this problem be solved without AI, and would simpler technology suffice?

It is the right first question and it is asked by the person with the least incentive to answer it honestly.

The question also has a second form that almost nobody asks, and it is the one this session's workflow is built on. Having established that something more than a form and a report is genuinely needed, **how much more?** There are several ways to build the thing, they differ in who owns the orchestration, where the compute lives and what you pay for when nobody is using it — and the differences are decided at the start and bind at the end.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught.

1. State the problem from [S003](/wiki/seminars/S003.md), then state the simplest thing that would resolve it.
2. What already runs in the customer's estate that does part of this?
3. If the honest answer is a structured form and a weekly report, who is worse off for your saying so?
4. What would the simple option fail at, specifically enough that somebody could observe the failure?
5. What does the sophisticated option cost after it is built, that the simple one does not?

**Item 4 is where the exercise is usually lost, and it is the one this session is really about.** A simple option dismissed with a generality was never a candidate. "It would not scale" is not a failure; it is a way of not naming one.

## What you must produce

A **sufficiency finding**. Five parts:

1. **The simplest option that would work**, described in enough detail that somebody could begin building it tomorrow.
2. **What already runs in the estate** that does part of the job, with a named source for the claim.
3. **The specific and observable thing the simple option fails at** — an event a person could witness, not a property claim.
4. **The named capability — not technology family — that justifies anything more**, and the axis on which it is needed: orchestration, model choice, initiation, distribution or scale.
5. **A build-path comparison for that capability**, across at least three options, on authoring model, hosting and what is paid for when nothing is happening — with the rejected options' rejection reasons written down.

## The material

### The question is right and the person answering it is wrong

The assessment that opens with this question is real, published, and mandatory for every machine-learning project in the organisation that publishes it ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md)). It is asked at governance review, with other people present.

Moving it into qualification, as this session does, has a cost that is worth stating up front: it is now asked privately, early, by a delivery team, and answering it there does not discharge the governance obligation. **It is asked here anyway because a question asked only at governance is asked after the architecture exists**, at which point the honest answer has become expensive.

The structural problem does not improve with seniority. The person best placed to describe the simple option is the one whose engagement, budget and professional interest depend on it losing. The session's response is not an appeal to honesty. It is procedural: describe the simple option well enough that it could be built, and name its failure specifically enough that somebody could watch it happen. **An option you have described properly is much harder to dismiss than one you have summarised**, and that asymmetry is the only lever available.

### Simplicity is not one axis, and the estate is the axis people forget

"Simpler" hides at least four different questions. Fewer moving parts. Less novel technology. Less operational surface. And — the one that matters most in a long-lived customer estate and appears in no comparison table — **already present.**

A capability the customer already licences, already operates and whose failure modes their own staff already recognise is a different proposition from an equivalent capability newly introduced, and the difference does not appear in a feature comparison. The practice corpus treats extending what the customer already runs as an operating principle rather than a preference ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md)) — and that corpus records no engagement it declined, no exclusion criterion, no cost side of any kind and no outcome data whatever, so the principle is a practitioner judgement and not a finding.

### The retrieval case: a strong baseline that almost nobody runs

The sharpest available instance of "simpler technology would do" is in retrieval, and it is unusually well evidenced for this stage.

BM25 is a lexical ranking function that predates the neural retrieval literature entirely. It is not folklore: the probabilistic relevance framework derives it, and each of its components answers a stated modelling question — inverse document frequency falls out of the probabilistic argument once you assume no relevance information, term-frequency saturation follows from treating an observed count as evidence about a latent property rather than as a linear measure of aboutness, and length normalisation exists because a long document may be long through verbosity or through scope and the two want opposite treatment ([research/02-technical-foundations/robertson-zaragoza-bm25-and-beyond.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/robertson-zaragoza-bm25-and-beyond.md)).

The empirical half comes from BEIR, which assembled retrieval datasets deliberately chosen to be dissimilar and evaluated systems spanning lexical, sparse, dense, late-interaction and re-ranking families under zero-shot conditions ([research/02-technical-foundations/thakur-beir.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/thakur-beir.md)). Its findings, stated as direction only: **BM25 is a robust baseline across the collection** — it does not win, and it does not collapse on any domain. Re-ranking and late-interaction models achieved the best average zero-shot performance and did so at high computational cost. Dense and sparse models were more efficient and often underperformed, which the authors characterise as room for improvement in generalisation.

Read that as an argument about sufficiency rather than about retrieval. **A method whose worst case is acceptable is operationally different from a method whose average is higher and whose worst case is unknown**, and the sufficiency finding is a claim about worst cases.

**Two limits.** The BM25 monograph is paywalled and this repository's note records that its full text was not retrieved, so its section structure and parameter treatment are reconstructed from the wider literature rather than read. BEIR's results are a snapshot of 2021 practice; the specific rankings have changed, the eighteen datasets are a sample rather than a map, and the benchmark has since become a target of optimisation, which weakens its own zero-shot premise over time. **Teach the methodology and the trade-off structure, not which system led.**

### Generalisation is the property you need, and a single score does not measure it

BEIR's methodological complaint transfers directly to sufficiency arguments. Improvements had been measured overwhelmingly on a small number of datasets and most often on the one the model was trained on — a design that cannot distinguish a model which has learned the task from one which has learned a corpus.

**Your demonstration has the same problem.** A well-chosen set of example queries against a familiar corpus will make almost any configuration look competent, which is precisely why the sophisticated option always wins a demo. If your case against the simple option rests on a demonstration, you have measured fit rather than capability.

### What the sophisticated option adds, and what it inherits

Adding a language model to a retrieval system adds capability and also adds a failure surface, and the second is systematically under-counted.

The seven-failure-point experience report is blunt that a retrieval-augmented system inherits the limitations of both of its components — vocabulary mismatch, ranking failures and incomplete recall from the retrieval half; hallucination, instruction-following failures and context sensitivity from the generation half ([research/02-technical-foundations/barnett-rag-failure-taxonomy.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/barnett-rag-failure-taxonomy.md)). Its two key takeaways are the ones that belong in a run-cost estimate: **validation is only feasible during operation**, and robustness evolves rather than being designed in at the start. Both mean that the sophisticated option's cost curve continues after the build.

There is a second inheritance, and it bounds what a compound system can be asked to do at all. Kambhampati's argument is that large language models do not plan in the sense the word usually implies ([research/02-technical-foundations/kambhampati-llms-cant-plan.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/kambhampati-llms-cant-plan.md)). If your justification for the sophisticated option is that it will work out the sequence of steps by itself, that is the claim most likely to be wrong, and it is the one demos conceal best.

And your own judgement about which is which is unreliable in a known direction. Capability in these systems is jagged: the boundary between competence and failure is uneven and does not track how hard a task feels to a person ([research/04-professional-formation/dellacqua-jagged-technological-frontier.md](/research/04-professional-formation/dellacqua-jagged-technological-frontier.md)). **That note rests on an abstract, so its shape is usable and no number in it is** — and none is asserted here.

### Three build paths, one requirement, three axes

If something more is genuinely needed, the next question is how much more, and the platform documentation supplies an unusually clean comparison ([research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md)).

The top-level split is by service model, with selection criteria stated as "your organization's technical capabilities, timeline, and control requirements". The three options this session compares sit at different points on that triple:

- **The serverless agents runtime** — agents authored as files, hosted on scale-to-zero serverless compute, so nothing is paid for when nothing is happening. The lightest authoring model of the three and the one with the least surrounding machinery.
- **Foundry hosted agents** — your own containerised code, managed by the platform on container compute. Distinct from Foundry's prompt agents, which are declarative and carry no agent compute at all; **the line between them is whether you are running code, and therefore whether you are paying for and operating compute.**
- **Copilot Studio** — low-code authoring with a large prebuilt connector library, hosted as software-as-a-service and consumed in credits, positioned by its own documentation as "designed for small-scale deployments, such as department-level solutions."

That last is a scale claim published by the vendor about its own product, and it is the most important line in the comparison. **A tool that is excellent at department scale is being used outside its design envelope when it is used at enterprise scale, and the symptoms appear as governance and lifecycle problems rather than as functional failures.**

The documentation is candid about the other trades too. Copilot Studio's stated disadvantages include limited ability to fine-tune API calls, actions and response formatting compared with a pro-code environment; source control and continuous delivery supported but less integrated than the project-based pro-code workflow; Adaptive Cards limited to an older schema revision; and slower feature availability. Orchestrator ownership differs by tool — Copilot Studio uses its own, while the pro-code paths are bring-your-own — and orchestrator ownership is the fundamental fork, because it determines model choice, extensibility and lifecycle tooling together.

Two further axes decide more than they appear to. **Initiation:** an agent that can act without being asked is architecturally different from one that cannot, in its failure modes and its governance requirements alike, and declarative agents in this family are documented as relying on user-initiated interaction. **Distribution:** only agents built on certain paths can be published to the commercial store with the toolkit, so a decision made in week one binds at the end of the project.

### The options are not mutually exclusive, which changes the question

The comparison documentation states plainly that these options "aren't mutually exclusive", and gives the example of a serverless agents runtime application exposing tools over a protocol that Foundry agents or Copilot Studio agents then consume.

**That single sentence reframes the whole exercise.** The choice is not one platform for a system. It is a platform per component, composed through protocols — which means the sufficiency question can be asked separately for each part, and the honest answer is frequently *simple here, sophisticated there.* A sufficiency finding that names one winner for the whole system has usually answered a question nobody asked.

**The limits on this documentation family are severe and must travel with it.** It was read through search-returned excerpts rather than full page fetches, so its structure is reconstructed and several comparison tables were truncated. It is the most perishable material in this repository's research set: product names are actively in flux, and one SDK is documented as formerly carrying a different name. It is vendor selection guidance, comparing Microsoft options against each other and not against alternatives. **It offers no adoption data, no failure-rate data, no cost comparisons and no evidence that its recommended mappings produce better outcomes.** Treat it as a well-constructed decision framework and never as validated findings.

### What an agent actually costs to keep, which is often the argument for not having one

The sufficiency question has a second half nobody prices, and the platform documentation now answers it in enough detail to be uncomfortable ([research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-agent-build-and-runtime.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-agent-build-and-runtime.md)).

**Compute first, because the shape is counter-intuitive.** A hosted agent is not a service with replicas. It is a fleet of single-session sandboxes: each session gets its own isolated compute and its own persisted filesystem, there is no replica count to configure and no warm pool to size, and sessions are deprovisioned after an idle period and deleted after a longer one. The consequence is stated outright and a costing exercise will otherwise miss it — **the processor and memory you configure describe one session, and billing runs across all active sessions**, so a generous default is multiplied by your concurrency rather than absorbed by it. The published right-sizing procedure is empirical rather than analytical: run a representative workload, read the telemetry, compare observed peaks against allocation. **Which means the number cannot be known before the thing exists.**

**Capacity second, because it is not an engineering problem.** Model capacity is allocated per subscription, per region and per model, and an increase is a request with no published response time, no published approval rate and no published appeal route, prioritised in favour of customers already consuming their existing allocation ([research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-control-plane-and-governance.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-control-plane-and-governance.md)). Certain model families cannot be increased at all. **The simple option has no such dependency**, and that difference belongs in the comparison rather than in a risk register at the back.

**Guardrail configuration third, because it is a standing obligation rather than a setting.** Agent guardrails are preview, are block-only — there is no annotate-only mode for an agent — and **fully override the underlying model's guardrail rather than adding to it.** The documentation's own worked example shows an agent whose guardrail names input and output and says nothing about tool calls, and the published expected behaviour is that the tool call and the tool's response are not scanned at all, whatever the model's stricter setting says. **A stage you did not name is a stage nobody scans**, and keeping that configuration correct is somebody's job for as long as the agent exists.

**Evaluation fourth, and this is where the cost stops being estimable.** Continuous evaluation scores a *sample* of production responses at a configurable rate, and runs are silently skipped once an hourly ceiling is reached. Conversation-level evaluation draws from telemetry within a bounded lookback window, using either uniform sampling or a service-managed heuristic the documentation describes as biased toward interesting conversations **and does not document further.** So a reported quality number has a denominator nobody can state. And the platform now ships an instrument for collecting human judgements about agent responses while **publishing no agreement, correlation or calibration between any built-in evaluator and those judgements** — an absence confirmed by a dedicated search rather than assumed. **None may be asserted here or anywhere in this wiki.**

**Retention and lifecycle last, because they are the costs that arrive when nobody is looking.** Tracing captures the agent's inputs, outputs, tool arguments, tool results, planning steps and token consumption — which is simultaneously where the reasoning becomes inspectable and where the sensitive data goes. The portal shows a bounded window, and **the platform owns no retention period of its own**: retention follows a monitoring resource somebody must configure, own and pay for. Meanwhile every deployed model carries a retirement date set at launch rather than announced later, notice of it goes to subscription owners rather than to the team that built the thing, and dedicated capacity is never auto-upgraded. **That is a scheduled outage nobody has written down**, and the simple option does not have one.

Two of those five are the fine-tuning argument in disguise, and it is worth saying because learners reach for fine-tuning as though it were free. The documentation's own rationale for it is substantially about reducing prompt overhead, and **its guidance on how much data is required is published in quantity while carrying no source, study or curve behind any of it** — including an asserted relationship between dataset size and quality that this programme forbids repeating. Read honestly, the vendor's own case for fine-tuning is frequently an argument against doing it.

**The limits, and they are the most severe in this session.** That source was read on 2026-07-31; four items were read in full and roughly thirty pages only through search-returned excerpts, so structures are reconstructed and no list from it is exhaustive. Preview status is pervasive and load-bearing — agent guardrails, workflows, human evaluation, conversation-level evaluation and several tracing paths are all preview, and **a cost argument resting on a preview feature is a cost argument with no service-level agreement.** **Four first-party surfaces disagreed about the agent definition file on the day it was read, one of them formally deprecated and all of them rendering**, which is why nothing here may be cited from a single page. And every number in that material is forbidden downstream: the durable content is the ordering and the mechanism.

### Run cost is not build cost

Every option in the comparison has a cost that continues after the build: operating it, evaluating it, correcting it, and eventually removing it. Two of the three axes in the platform comparison are about exactly that — hosting, and what you pay for when nothing is happening.

A sufficiency finding priced only at build has compared the wrong quantity. **The question is not what the sophisticated option costs to make. It is what it costs to keep**, and the retrieval literature above supplies the reason that number is not knowable in advance: validation is only feasible during operation, so the evaluation budget is open-ended by construction.

## The workflow

**Take one requirement and cost it against three build paths on the axes the documentation itself names, then argue against your own answer.**

**Step 1 — write the simple option first, and commit it.** Before any comparison, describe the simplest thing that would resolve the problem from [S003](/wiki/seminars/S003.md) in enough detail that somebody could start on Monday. Check it against your register from [S006](/wiki/seminars/S006.md): if the record's field structure turned out to be fluid, a structured form is on the table; if it is genuinely frozen, it is not.

**Step 2 — establish a real baseline, not a rhetorical one.** If the requirement involves finding things, stand up a lexical baseline before anything else and keep it as the number to beat. It costs an afternoon and it is the only figure in your finding that nobody can argue with.

```text
# The baseline that must be beaten, not the straw option.
# A lexical index over the same corpus, tuned on the same corpus,
# evaluated on the same queries as everything else you will propose.
#
#   index:      inverted index over the note text and its title field
#   ranking:    BM25 — saturating term frequency, length normalisation
#   parameters: k1 and b tuned per corpus, NOT left at the conventional defaults
#   queries:    the same set you will use for every later comparison, written
#               BEFORE you see any result, and including at least five that
#               contain an exact identifier, product code or error string
```

**Step 3 — name the capability, not the technology.** Write one sentence: *the simple option fails because it cannot do X, observably, in this situation.* If X is a technology family rather than a capability, you have not finished step 3.

**Step 4 — locate the capability on an axis.** Orchestration, model choice, initiation, distribution or scale. The documentation's axes are doing the work here; the products are instances.

**Step 5 — build the comparison.** Create `sufficiency.yaml` and fill it for the capability you named. Three options minimum, and the rejected ones carry their rejection reason.

```yaml
requirement: "Draft a rework note from an assessment record, for a reviewer to edit."
capability_needed: "Orchestration across two systems with a human edit step."
axis: orchestration        # orchestration | model-choice | initiation | distribution | scale

baseline:
  option: "Structured form plus a weekly lexical report"
  fails_at: >
    Reviewer must read every returned record to find the three that were
    reworked for the same reason. Observable: watch one reviewer do it.
  verdict: insufficient

options:
  - name: serverless-agents-runtime
    authoring_model: "Agent definition files in the repository"
    hosting: "Scale-to-zero serverless compute"
    idle_cost: "None — nothing runs when nothing is happening"
    orchestrator: "Yours"
    initiation: "Reactive; scheduled triggers available"
    distribution: "Check the published store-publishing constraint before committing"
    rejected_because: null

  - name: foundry-hosted-agent
    authoring_model: "Your containerised code"
    hosting: "Managed container compute"
    idle_cost: "Container compute is provisioned; it is not free at rest"
    orchestrator: "Yours — bring your own framework"
    initiation: "Reactive or proactive"
    distribution: "Publishable via the toolkit"
    rejected_because: "Compute cost at rest is not justified by this requirement."

  - name: copilot-studio
    authoring_model: "Low-code, large prebuilt connector library"
    hosting: "Software-as-a-service"
    idle_cost: "Consumption credits"
    orchestrator: "The product's own — not replaceable"
    initiation: "Reactive"
    distribution: "Not publishable to the commercial store via the toolkit"
    published_scale_envelope: "Documented for small-scale, department-level solutions"
    rejected_because: >
      The requirement is stated for the whole assessment function, which is
      outside the published envelope. Symptoms would appear as governance and
      lifecycle problems rather than as functional failure.

composition_check: >
  These options are documented as not mutually exclusive. State which components
  could be served by the simplest option and consumed by another over a protocol.
  A finding that names one winner for the whole system has answered a question
  nobody asked.

run_cost:
  evaluation: "Open-ended: validation of a retrieval-augmented system is feasible only during operation."
  correction: "Who fixes it, on what cadence, and out of whose budget?"
  removal: "What replaces it, and who decides?"
```

**Step 6 — attack your own finding, twice, in the right order.** Give the agent your committed simple option and ask it to strengthen the case for it. Then, in a fresh context, give it the same option and ask it to argue that it is insufficient. Both outputs will be fluent. **Record which of the two you found more persuasive, and why** — that record, not the outputs, is the artefact.

**Step 7 — check whether you were persuaded by specificity or by direction.** Re-read the argument you preferred and mark every claim that could be observed. If the persuasive argument had fewer observable claims than the other, you have learned something about yourself rather than about the options.

**A second pass: price the keeping, not the making.**

The table you have just filled compares options at build. This pass costs the thing after it exists, and it is the pass that most often changes the verdict. Every entry is answerable from published documentation; none of it requires a deployment.

**Step 8 — fill the keeping cost, and put `unknown` where it belongs.** An `unknown` here is a finding, not a gap, and several of these are unknown by construction rather than by omission.

```yaml
# keeping.yaml — what the sophisticated option costs once it exists.
# Attach this to sufficiency.yaml. The simple option gets the same file,
# and most of its rows are empty. That emptiness is the comparison.
compute:
  unit_of_scaling: session            # not replica. No warm pool, no replica count.
  sizing_describes: "one session"
  billed_across: "all active sessions"
  therefore: "sizing is multiplied by concurrency, not absorbed by it"
  right_sizing_method: empirical      # run a representative workload, read telemetry
  knowable_before_it_exists: false

capacity:
  allocated_per: ["subscription", "region", "model"]
  increase_request_sla: none
  increase_approval_rate: none
  appeal_route: none
  prioritisation: "favours existing consumption"
  some_model_families_ineligible: true
  simple_option_has_this_dependency: false

guardrails:
  status: preview
  modes_available_to_an_agent: ["block"]      # no annotate-only mode for an agent
  relationship_to_model_guardrail: "total override, not additive"
  stages_named_in_our_config: ["input", "output"]
  stages_therefore_unscanned: ["tool call", "tool response"]
  owner_after_handover: ""

evaluation:
  mode: sampled
  runs_skipped_at_hourly_ceiling: true
  conversation_sampling: "uniform, or an undocumented service-managed heuristic"
  denominator_of_reported_score: unknown       # this is the finding
  judge_agreement_with_humans: none_published  # confirmed by search, not assumed
  consequence: "a score may be reported and may not be interpreted"

retention:
  what_traces_contain: "inputs, outputs, tool arguments, tool results, planning steps"
  portal_window: bounded
  platform_owned_retention_period: none
  actually_owned_by: ""                        # a monitoring resource, and a person
  who_may_read_the_prompts: ""

lifecycle:
  model_retirement_date_set_at: launch
  notice_goes_to: "subscription owners"        # not the delivery team
  dedicated_capacity_auto_upgraded: false
  scheduled_outage_recorded_in_the_register: false
```

**Step 9 — attack the keeping cost.** Give the agent `keeping.yaml` and require it to argue that every `unknown` is knowable and that the simple option carries an equivalent obligation in each row. Then require, for each, the observation or the published page that would settle it. **Record the one row you changed** — and record any row where the agent produced a number, because that is the instrument doing exactly what this session warns about.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That "simpler" resolves into named axes as soon as you are forced to fill a table — and that a comparison you thought you had made in your head was a comparison on one axis with the others assumed. It also makes the composition point concrete: filling the table honestly usually produces a row where the simple option is sufficient for part of the system, which is invisible while the question is asked about the system as a whole. And the second pass makes one further thing physical: **the simple option's keeping file is mostly empty, and the sophisticated option's is mostly `unknown`** — which is a different comparison from the one the build table showed you, and usually a more decisive one.

## What good looks like

- The simple option is described in enough detail that somebody could begin building it tomorrow.
- Its failure is specific and observable. **"It would not scale" is not a failure, it is a way of not naming one.**
- A baseline exists that somebody else could reproduce, and it was tuned on the corpus rather than left at conventional defaults.
- The justification for anything more names a capability and an axis, not a technology family.
- At least three build paths are compared on authoring model, hosting and idle cost, and the rejected ones carry their reasons.
- The composition check is answered — which parts could stay simple — rather than left as a single verdict for the whole system.
- The ongoing cost appears: operating, evaluating, correcting and eventually retiring, with the evaluation line marked open-ended and the reason given.

## How it goes wrong

Seven failure modes, each with the warning sign that precedes it.

- **The foregone conclusion** — the simple option is written so that it loses. *Warning sign:* its stated failure is a generality and its description is a sentence long.
- **The incumbent blind spot** — nobody checked what the customer already runs. *Warning sign:* your estate column is empty, and you did not ask because the answer would be inconvenient.
- **Sophistication as identity** — the simple answer is rejected because it is not interesting to build. *Warning sign:* your reason for rejecting it describes the solution rather than the difficulty.
- **Cost stated once** — the sophisticated option is priced at build and never at run. *Warning sign:* nothing in your finding describes what happens to it in a year.
- **The tuned demo** — the comparison rests on a query set chosen after the options were seen. *Warning sign:* you cannot say when the queries were written, and the sophisticated option wins on all of them.
- **One winner for the whole system** — a single verdict is issued for a system with several components. *Warning sign:* your composition check is empty, and the documentation you cited says the options are not mutually exclusive.
- **Envelope blindness** — a tool is selected for a scope larger than its published positioning. *Warning sign:* nothing in your finding records what scale the vendor says the tool is for, and you did not look.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The finding is carried into [S010](/wiki/seminars/S010.md), where a refusal grounded in sufficiency must show the simple option described to a buildable standard.

**1 · Convert three generalities into observable failures.** Each of these is a way of not naming a failure. Rewrite each as something a person could witness.

- *A form would not scale.*
- *Search would not be intelligent enough.*
- *The simple option would not handle edge cases.*

> **Model answers.** *At the current arrival rate the reviewer queue is not cleared within the working day — watch the queue depth at close for one week.* · *A query for the exact instrument code returns the right record and a query describing the same situation in the assessor's own words returns nothing — here are five such pairs.* · *These three record types cannot be expressed in a fixed field set; here is one instance of each.* **The test is whether somebody could disagree with you by looking.**

**2 · Fill the table for one requirement.** Using the workflow, complete `sufficiency.yaml` for one capability across the three named build paths. Then answer the composition check in one sentence.

> **What you should find.** The axes separate the options far more cleanly than the product marketing does: authoring model, hosting, what you pay for at rest, who owns the orchestrator, whether the agent can act unprompted, and where it can be distributed. **If your three options come out differing on only one axis, you have compared three versions of the same option** and the genuinely simpler candidate is missing from the table.

**3 · Go back to your own item 4.** Find what you wrote to opening item 4 about what the simple option would fail at. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then rewrite it, and write the observation that would show you were wrong.

> **Criterion.** A passing answer names an event, a place to look, and a person who could see it. It also survives inversion: you must be able to state what you would observe if the simple option turned out to be sufficient after all. "It would not scale" fails; it cannot be observed and it cannot be wrong.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Run the baseline you were going to skip.** Stand up a lexical index over the corpus in your brief, write ten queries before you look at any result — five of them containing an exact identifier or error string — and record what it returns.

> **What to notice.** Exact-term matching remains essential in production for rare entities, identifiers, product codes and domain jargon that embeddings smooth over, and lexical and dense retrieval fail differently — which is the actual argument for hybrid retrieval rather than a preference for one ([research/02-technical-foundations/robertson-zaragoza-bm25-and-beyond.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/robertson-zaragoza-bm25-and-beyond.md)). **The default parameters are conventions from past collections and not universal optima**; if you left them alone, your baseline is weaker than it should be and every comparison against it is flattered.

**5 · Read the two agent arguments as instruments.** Take your two agent outputs from workflow step 6. Count the claims in each that could be checked by looking at something. Then say which output you preferred.

> **What to notice.** A model asked to improve something tends to add; asked to attack it, it must locate a weakness, and locating is a harder operation than adding. **The documentation publishes no measure of how consistently these systems argue opposite sides of the same question**, so the exercise demonstrates a property and measures nothing — which is why you record which argument persuaded you rather than which the agent produced.

**6 · Name the envelope.** Closed book: what does the vendor documentation say about the deployment scale one of these tools is designed for, and what does exceeding it look like?

> **Answer.** Copilot Studio is documented as designed for small-scale deployments, such as department-level solutions. Exceeding that envelope shows up as governance and lifecycle problems rather than as functional failure — the tool keeps working and the estate around it stops being manageable.
>
> **Then compare.** Look back at your answer to opening item 5, about what the sophisticated option costs after it is built. Did you name anything that was a governance or lifecycle cost rather than a bill? **For most learners the answer is no**, and that omission is more useful than the recall was.

**7 · Cost the keeping.** Fill `keeping.yaml` for the option you were about to recommend. Then answer two questions in one sentence each: what is the denominator of the quality number this system will report, and who owns the retention period for the record of its reasoning?

> **Answer key.** The denominator is **unknown, and unknown by construction rather than by omission**: continuous evaluation scores a sample at a configurable rate, silently skips runs once an hourly ceiling is reached, and the conversation-level alternative draws from a bounded lookback using either uniform sampling or an undocumented service-managed heuristic. A score computed that way may be reported and may not be interpreted, and **no agreement between any built-in evaluator and human judgement is published**, so nothing licenses treating the score as a measure of quality at all.
>
> The retention owner is **nobody, until somebody is named.** The portal shows a bounded window; the platform owns no retention period of its own; retention follows a monitoring resource that a person must configure and pay for. **A passing answer names a person or records that no person has been named.** "It is managed" fails — the traces contain the agent's tool arguments and planning steps, so the question of how long they are kept and who may read them is a design decision somebody inherits.
>
> **Scoring criterion for the whole exercise.** A passing `keeping.yaml` has `unknown` in at least three rows and an empty owner field in at least one, and the learner can say which of those the simple option also has. **If your sophisticated option's keeping file looks as clean as the simple option's, you have filled it in from optimism.**

## What this does not establish

**The question itself is real and published; nothing behind it is measured.** No source in this programme reports an engagement in which a simpler option was chosen, or one in which it was not and should have been. The corpus this session draws on records no case it declined and no outcome of any kind.

**The claim that engineers systematically over-select sophisticated solutions is asserted here, not shown.** It is consistent with a widely held professional intuition and with the incentive structure of a delivery organisation, and neither of those is evidence. The opposite failure — a customer left with an under-powered solution that has to be rebuilt — is at least as expensive and is not addressed anywhere in this session.

**The retrieval evidence is stronger than the rest and is still bounded.** BEIR's findings are a snapshot: the specific rankings have changed, its datasets are a sample rather than a map of retrieval tasks, and the benchmark has since become an optimisation target, which weakens its own zero-shot premise. Its results say nothing about what modest in-domain adaptation would recover. The BM25 monograph is paywalled and its full text was not retrieved by this repository, so its internal structure is reconstructed rather than read. **No score, ratio or magnitude from either appears here.**

**The build-path comparison is vendor selection guidance read through search excerpts.** It offers no adoption data, no failure-rate data, no cost comparison and no evidence that its recommended mappings produce better outcomes. Several of its comparison tables were truncated in the excerpts this repository read, so the feature lists are not exhaustive. It is the most perishable material in the research set, and a limitation recorded today may be closed tomorrow.

**The agent demonstration measures nothing.** Asked to strengthen a case it will strengthen it; asked to undermine the same case it will undermine it. That is a property of the instrument, demonstrated, and no source reports how consistently it holds.
