## The situation

The first question in a published responsible-AI assessment used by a customer-embedded engineering organisation is not about models, data or risk. It is: can this problem be solved without AI, and would simpler technology suffice?

It is the right first question and it is asked by the person with the least incentive to answer it honestly.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught.

1. State the problem from [S003](/wiki/seminars/S003.md), then state the simplest thing that would resolve it.
2. What already runs in the customer's estate that does part of this?
3. If the honest answer is a structured form and a weekly report, who is worse off for your saying so?
4. What would the simple option fail at, specifically enough that somebody could observe the failure?
5. What does the sophisticated option cost after it is built, that the simple one does not?

**Item 4 is where the exercise is usually lost.** A simple option dismissed with a generality was never a candidate.

## What you must produce

A **sufficiency finding**: the simplest option that would work, written seriously; the specific and observable thing it fails at; and the named capability — not category — that justifies anything more.

## What good looks like

- The simple option is described in enough detail that somebody could begin building it tomorrow.
- Its failure is specific and observable. **"It would not scale" is not a failure, it is a way of not naming one.**
- The justification for anything more names a capability, not a technology family.
- The ongoing cost of the sophisticated option appears — running, evaluating, correcting and eventually retiring it — and not only the cost of building it.

## How it goes wrong

Four failure modes, each with the warning sign that precedes it.

- **The foregone conclusion** — the simple option is written so that it loses. *Warning sign:* its stated failure is a generality and its description is a sentence long.
- **The incumbent blind spot** — nobody checked what the customer already runs. *Warning sign:* your estate column is empty, and you did not ask because the answer would be inconvenient.
- **Sophistication as identity** — the simple answer is rejected because it is not interesting to build. *Warning sign:* your reason for rejecting it describes the solution rather than the difficulty.
- **Cost stated once** — the sophisticated option is priced at build and never at run. *Warning sign:* nothing in your finding describes what happens to it in a year.

## Do it

Work from [Brief A](/wiki/quarters/Q1.md) and from your frozen/fluid register at [S006](/wiki/seminars/S006.md). If the record's field structure turned out to be fluid, a structured form is on the table; if it is genuinely frozen, it may not be.

Write the case for the simple option yourself and commit it. Then use an agent twice: once to strengthen the simple option, and once to argue that it is insufficient. Record both, and record which of the two arguments you found more persuasive and why.

Method is yours. **Nobody will tell you what establishes sufficiency**, and the choice of standard is the judgement being assessed downstream.

## Show it

Read out the specific failure of the simple option. Your reader has one job: to say whether they could recognise that failure if they saw it happen.

If they could not, you have not made a case against the simple option. You have expressed a preference for the other one.

## What this does not establish

The question itself is real and published; nothing behind it is measured. No source in this programme reports an engagement in which a simpler option was chosen, or one in which it was not and should have been, and the corpus this session draws on records no case it declined and no outcome of any kind.

**The claim that engineers systematically over-select sophisticated solutions is asserted here, not shown.** It is consistent with a widely held professional intuition and with the incentive structure of a delivery organisation, and neither of those is evidence.
