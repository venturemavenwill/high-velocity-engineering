# S022 · Making a Requirement Falsifiable

**Module:** [M05](/wiki/modules/M05.md) · **Quarter:** [Q3](/wiki/quarters/Q3.md) · **Complexity class:** 2
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** Agent Framework evaluators and test sets, and Fabric IQ ontology constraints — turning a stated requirement into a runnable check with a recorded pass condition, and into a typed constraint the platform itself refuses to accept data against
**Whitepaper:** [WP-022](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-022.md)
**Retrieves from:** S016, S020, S021
**Entry state:** either

## The situation

You have a requirement set from [S021](/wiki/seminars/S021.md). It is written in the customer's language, it names no technology, and everybody who has read it has agreed with it.

**That last fact is the problem.** Universal agreement is what a set of unfalsifiable sentences produces.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught. Item 5 is submitted and kept.

1. Take your first requirement. Write the sentence that would show it had **not** been met.
2. Who would run that check, on what, and when?
3. If nobody can run it, is it a requirement or a hope?
4. What would you accept as evidence that it had been met — and would the customer accept the same evidence?
5. Name a requirement in your set that you cannot verify at all. **Do not delete it.**

**Item 1 is the session.** [S016](/wiki/seminars/S016.md) established that a question inviting a flattering answer will receive one. This is the same claim about a sentence: a requirement that cannot be failed will not be.

## What you must produce

Every requirement in your set, rewritten into the capture form the open engineering playbook publishes — a name, a description, a priority, a measurement, a verification method, constraints, an owner, and dependencies. Page read 2026-07-30.

**Only two of those fields are assessed here, and the other six are administrative.** The measurement, and the verification method. Everything this session is for lives in those two.

Plus one more artefact, which is the session's second point: **the unverifiable list.** Requirements you could not put a measurement or a verification method against are moved there, kept, and carried forward. They are not deleted.

## The material

### Agreement is what an unfalsifiable sentence produces

A requirement set that everybody agrees with has almost certainly not been written yet.

"The system must be responsive" attracts no objection because there is nothing in it to object to — no quantity, no observer, no moment at which somebody could be shown to have missed it. **The sentence performs the social function of a requirement and carries none of the engineering content**, and it survives every review precisely because reviews are where objections are raised.

The difficulty is that the defect is invisible in the artefact. A requirement document composed entirely of such sentences looks like a requirement document. It is distinguishable from a real one only by attempting to fail it, which nobody does, because attempting to fail your own document is unpleasant and nobody has asked for it.

[S016](/wiki/seminars/S016.md) established that a question inviting a flattering answer will receive one. **This is the same claim about a sentence.**

### The capture form, and why only two of its eight fields are the requirement

An openly published engineering playbook from a customer-embedded engineering organisation supplies the form this session uses ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md)). Eight fields: requirement name, description, priority, measurement or metric, verification method, constraints, owner or responsible party, and dependencies. Page read 2026-07-30.

**Six of them are administrative. Two of them are the requirement.**

The reason for marking two of eight is not economy. It is that the other six can be filled correctly by somebody who has understood nothing. A name, a priority and a dependency list can be produced from the brief by a competent reader who has never met the customer. A measurement requires knowing what quantity would change and what could observe it. A verification method requires knowing who runs the check, on what data, and when. **Those two cannot be bluffed and the other six can.**

**The playbook's own limits, and they are not incidental.** Its pages carry individual footer dates spanning several years, so it has no single currency and no citation to it may carry one; pin every claim to a page and that page's own date. Roughly half of it is substantive practice and the rest is recipe, stub or link list, with nothing in the navigation distinguishing them. It offers no comparative evidence that any of its practices outperform alternatives and does not claim to. **And it reaches for a magnitude in several places and warrants it in none** — a cost claim about code review, a velocity claim about pairing, a defect-cost multiple attributed through a link that can no longer be followed, and a set of vendor efficiency figures with bands wide enough to be unfalsifiable. **None of the four is reproduced here or anywhere in this wiki.** They are worth knowing about as a specimen: the playbook writes a reasoned practice and a repeated number in exactly the same flat declarative register, and nothing on the page tells you which you are reading.

### A measurement names a quantity and an instrument that exists

Not a quality. "Timely" is a quality. A named quantity, observed at a named point, by something that is already running or that somebody has agreed to build, is a measurement.

The instrument that does not yet exist is legitimate — **but only if it becomes work with an owner.** A measurement is precise and nobody has asked who builds the thing that produces it is the commonest way a requirement set acquires the appearance of rigour without any of it.

The decomposition that makes this tractable is features and scenarios: break the system into what it can do, and then into the situations each capability must handle. Each scenario becomes an assertion ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/husain-your-ai-product-needs-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/husain-your-ai-product-needs-evals.md)). The same source breaks with testing convention in a way that matters here: **a complete pass rate is not required, and the pass rate is a product decision.** A requirement whose verification method implies a perfect score has usually imported a testing habit rather than made a decision.

**Its limits.** A practitioner essay resting on a single sustained case study, with no control condition, no measured before-and-after and no released dataset. Its tooling recommendations are dated and will drift. **Teach the pattern, not the product names.**

### A verification method names who runs the check, on what, and when

A verification method that is a meeting is a decision, not a verification. *Reviewed and approved by the steering group* establishes that somebody approved it.

The distinction is the one [S010](/wiki/seminars/S010.md) drew between a control and a check. **A control is an instruction somebody may follow. A check fails without anybody's cooperation.** The playbook's own weakest moment is instructive here: its page on evidence for code review proposes verifying the branch policy, which is evidence that review was *required* rather than that it was *done well*. That is a control presented as evidence, in a document that is otherwise careful.

The test of the pair is mechanical and it is the session's standard. **Hand the measurement and the verification method to somebody who was not in the room and ask them to produce a system that satisfies the words and would be rejected.** If they can, you are not finished.

### Thresholds have origins, and the origin most learners actually have is a catalogue

The playbook page that supplies the eight-field form also supplies a large catalogue of example thresholds — response times, availability figures, coverage targets, complexity limits. **That catalogue carries no citation of any kind.** The numbers are illustrations by the authors' own framing, and they sit on the page in the same register as the publication's best-sourced material.

An engineer who lifts a number from it has done nothing wrong. **An engineer who lifts it and strips the provenance has manufactured a specification, and the only difference between the two is a clause.** [S023](/wiki/seminars/S023.md) makes that clause a requirement of every threshold; this session establishes that *borrowed from a catalogue that carries no source* is an acceptable origin when it is written down as one.

### Two things that make a check honest, and both are usually skipped

**The criteria will move, and that is a property of evaluation rather than a symptom of disorganisation.** The finding is a genuine circularity: users need criteria to grade outputs, and grading outputs is how users define criteria ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/shankar-evalgen.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/shankar-evalgen.md)). Some criteria appear to be dependent on the specific outputs observed rather than definable in advance, which the authors say "raises serious questions for approaches that assume the independence of evaluation from observation of model outputs." For you that has a sharp consequence: **the verification method you write today is the one you could imagine, and the criterion that would actually have caught the failure may only become articulable after you have seen the system do something.** Write it anyway, date it, and expect to revise it. **The limits:** the evidence is qualitative — the paper establishes that drift occurs and matters, not how often or how much — and its user study is small by construction.

**A number without an interval is not a result.** The position to take is that an evaluation is an experiment: a reported score needs a standard error, a question count, and — where questions come in related groups — a clustered one ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md)). The part that bears on a requirement is power: **do the analysis before you run, so that "we tested and found nothing" is a statement about the system rather than about the test.** A verification method that could not have detected a violation of its own requirement has not verified anything. **The limits:** analytical rather than empirical; its illustrative magnitudes rest on stylised assumptions and no number from it appears here; and it addresses comparing scores rather than choosing what to score, so a tight interval around a meaningless metric is still meaningless.

### The platform: cheap checks constantly, judged checks periodically

The Agent Framework's evaluator design encodes the discipline the evaluation literature argues for in the abstract ([research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-framework-and-evaluations-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-framework-and-evaluations-docs.md)). A local evaluator "runs checks locally without API calls — ideal for inner-loop development, CI smoke tests, and fast iteration". A cloud evaluator connects to a hosted service for model-based judging. **They can be mixed in a single run**, which is the architecture carrying the lesson: a verification method has a cost, and cost decides cadence.

Three further facts belong in this session rather than the next.

**Repetitions exist "to detect non-deterministic behavior".** One sample from a stochastic system is not a measurement, so a verification method that runs once has not run.

**Output is a pass or a fail with a `reason`, and for scale-based evaluators a numeric score beneath it.** From which it follows that **the threshold is a policy choice separable from the measurement** — moving it changes the pass rate without changing the system — and that the `reason` string is what makes a judgement auditable rather than merely numeric.

**The one deterministic agent-behaviour evaluator takes an action sequence and an expected action sequence and requires no model parameter at all.** It is the only check in that group whose result does not depend on a judge, and it is therefore the natural home for a verification method you want to be able to defend without an argument about the judge.

**Its limits, and they bound every number this session produces.** First-party documentation read through search-returned excerpts rather than full page fetches; many evaluators are preview; one consulted page is a superseded classic variant; surfaces differ by language. **No agreement rate with human judgement is published for any evaluator, no calibration data, and no account of where any default threshold came from.** The documentation is an accurate description of what the tooling computes and no evidence at all that what it computes is right — so the disagreement rate you measure yourself is the only warrant your verification method has.

### A constraint the platform will enforce is a requirement that cannot be fudged

An evaluator is a check you wrote, and its authority is your own. There is a second kind of pass condition, and it is stronger in one specific way: **the platform refuses the data rather than reporting a score about it.**

Fabric IQ's **ontology** is a first-class modelling item made of entity types, entity instances, properties with declared types and semantic annotations, and typed directional relationships carrying attributes and cardinality rules ([research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-iq-intelligence-layer.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-iq-intelligence-layer.md), read 2026-07-31). Those definitions are attached to real data by **bindings**, which connect them to lakehouse tables, eventhouse streams and Power BI semantic models "without copying or moving the data", and which carry identity keys, column mappings, schema-evolution rules, **data-quality checks on nullability, ranges and uniqueness**, and provenance.

Read that list as a requirements engineer rather than as a data engineer, because it is a list of ways a sentence can be made to fail.

- *Every determination is attached to exactly one claim.* — a cardinality rule on a relationship.
- *A determination's review timestamp is never absent.* — a nullability check on a bound property.
- *No two determinations share an identifier.* — a uniqueness check on an identity key.
- *A risk score lies inside its declared band.* — a range check.

Written in prose all four are agreeable sentences of exactly the kind the first half of this session is about. Written as constraints on a binding, **all four fail visibly, against somebody's real data, without anybody's cooperation** — which is precisely the standard [S010](/wiki/seminars/S010.md) set for the difference between a control and a check, arriving here in a form you can execute rather than argue about.

The layer goes one step further. An ontology can carry **actionable rules**, of the documented form *if a stock level falls below a threshold, trigger replenishment*, which Operations Agents evaluate against live data and act on. A rule of that shape is a requirement with an executable body, and it has the property this session most wants: **you can show the customer a case the rule admits that they would reject.** That is a far faster route to the missing quantifier than another pass over the sentence.

**The framing argument underneath is sound and is not vendor-specific**, which is why it is worth teaching even as the product moves: organisations "work with data at the level of tables and schemas, which are structures built for machines, not meaning", while running on business concepts, so that "each question requires manual translation by a domain expert". The pragmatic route offered is better still — **ontologies can be generated from Power BI semantic models already in production**, so the vocabulary is bootstrapped from definitions the business already trusts rather than convened in a committee. Where your customer has a semantic model in production, the glossary from [S021](/wiki/seminars/S021.md) has a draft already written and somebody has already signed for it.

**Two cautions, and the second is the one that changes what you may claim.**

First, a constraint the platform enforces is still a constraint somebody chose. It is not more true for being executable. It has an origin, and the origin discipline of this session and of [S023](/wiki/seminars/S023.md) applies to a range check exactly as it applies to a latency figure.

Second, and stated twice on its own documentation: **the data is not live.** "Any updates in upstream data sources (like new rows) need to be **manually refreshed** before they're visible in the ontology item." A layer described in the vocabulary of operational intelligence over live data requires somebody to press refresh before new rows exist in it, and no qualification of that is offered anywhere. For you the consequence is exact: **a constraint check run against an ontology reports the state of the last refresh, not the state of the source**, so the refresh cadence belongs beside every result or the pass condition is undated.

**Its limits, and they are severe.** Read 2026-07-31, at which date **every Fabric IQ item that matters is in preview**. The overview and ontology overview pages were fetched and read in full; the ontology tutorials, how-to pages and the agent-integration page were not read at all, so the binding syntax and check vocabulary in this session are reconstructed rather than confirmed. The documentation family is internally inconsistent about its own membership — the number of layers under the umbrella name is stated differently on different pages — and **the naming is younger than most of the products underneath it.** Nothing here was deployed, measured or tested; every mechanism is as-described. **No evidence exists that a requirement expressed this way produces a better system than the same requirement expressed as an evaluator, and none is claimed.**

## The workflow

**Make one requirement runnable, then try to satisfy it in a way you would reject.**

Steps 1, 2, 3 and 6 run locally with no API calls and no project. Step 4 needs a deployment. **Step 6 is the one this session most wants you to have done.**

**Step 1 — fill the two fields for every requirement, and expect to be unable to.** Where you cannot, move the requirement to the unverifiable list. Do not delete it and do not soften it until it fits.

**Step 2 — write the test set before you write the check.** Conversation-shaped rows, in JSONL, including the tool calls, because trajectory evaluators need to see the trajectory and text evaluators do not. Write at least one row you expect to fail.

**Step 3 — write the local check first, deterministic, no model calls.** It runs in continuous integration on every change. This is the check that fails without anybody's cooperation.

```yaml
# eval/R03.yaml — illustrative. Evaluator names and surfaces differ by
# language and several are preview; verify against current documentation.

requirement: R03
measurement: >
  Count of ISSUED adverse determinations in the run that have no preceding
  recorded REVIEW DECISION. Observed by the local check below, over the
  test set at eval/R03.jsonl.
verification_method: >
  Runs in CI on every change to the agent or its instructions, by the
  delivery team, against eval/R03.jsonl. Owner: <named role>.

local_checks:
  - adverse_determination_has_prior_review   # deterministic; the check

judged_evaluators:
  - name: task_adherence
    input: "{{sample.output_items}}"   # trajectory evaluators need structured output

split_strategy: full        # DECISION, not a default. State it with every result.
repetitions: 5              # one sample from a stochastic system is not a measurement

threshold:
  task_adherence:
    cut: "<state it>"
    origin: "<policy | contract | observed baseline | regulator | borrowed, uncited>"
    why: "<a sentence, not an inheritance>"

# The threshold is policy. Moving it changes the pass rate without changing
# the system. No agreement-with-human data is published for any judged
# evaluator, so the disagreement rate measured at step 5 is the only
# evidence that this number means anything.
```

**Step 4 — add the judged evaluator, and treat the defaults as opinions.** Bind trajectory evaluators to the structured output and text evaluators to the plain text. **A mapping error here silently changes what is evaluated rather than producing an error.**

**Step 5 — hand-label a batch and report disagreement.** Compare your labels against the evaluator's. **Report precision and recall separately rather than raw agreement**, because raw agreement is misleading when the classes are imbalanced, and in a requirements dataset they always are.

**Step 6 — attack every requirement with the agent, once each, with one instruction: *satisfy this requirement in a way I would reject.*** This is the cheapest falsification test available, and it works because the instrument is literal. It will not infer the constraint you left unstated, it will not fill the gap out of professional courtesy, and it will not tell you the requirement was clear when it was not. If it produces something that satisfies your words and appals you, **the appalling part is the requirement you failed to write. Write it, and run the test again.**

Record every prompt, what the agent produced, and what you changed. **An agent that could not satisfy your requirement badly was given the requirement plus your intentions**, which is not the test.

**Step 7 — swap sets with one other person and have them do step 6 by hand, without an instrument.** Then compare the two lists: the requirements a person broke and the agent did not, and the ones the agent broke and the person did not. **The second list is the more interesting one.** It contains the places where your requirement relied on a reader who wanted it to work.

**Step 8 — take one requirement the evaluator could not settle and express it as a typed constraint instead.** Declare the entity types your customer's own language implies, give each property a type and a check, declare the relationship and its cardinality, then bind the whole thing to real data and record every binding that fails. **You are looking for failures.** A binding that succeeds on the first attempt usually means you declared the constraint the data already satisfies rather than the one the requirement asserted.

```yaml
# ontology/determination.yaml — illustrative only.
# Fabric IQ is preview at the date of writing; item names, binding syntax and
# the check vocabulary will move. Verify against current documentation, and
# record the date you verified it beside every result.

entity_types:
  - name: Determination
    properties:
      - { name: determination_id, type: string,    unique: true }      # R05
      - { name: reviewed_at,      type: timestamp, nullable: false }   # R03
      - { name: risk_score,       type: decimal,   range: [0, 1] }     # R11
        # origin of that band: the customer's own scoring policy, not a default.
        # A range check the platform enforces is still a number somebody chose.

relationships:
  - name: decides
    from: Determination
    to: Claim
    cardinality: many-to-one     # R05 stated as a rule rather than as a sentence

bindings:
  - entity_type: Determination
    source: "lakehouse.claims.determinations"
    identity_key: determination_id
    column_map: { reviewed_at: review_ts, risk_score: score }
    quality_checks: [nullability, range, uniqueness]

    # THE UNDATED PASS CONDITION. Upstream rows are not visible here until the
    # ontology is refreshed, and the refresh is manual. Every result below is a
    # statement about the last refresh and not about the source. State the
    # cadence, or you have reported a check whose observation window is unknown.
    refreshed_at: 2026-07-31
    refresh_cadence: "STATE IT — manual, and therefore somebody's job"
```

**Step 9 — run step 6 again, against the constraint rather than the sentence.** Same single instruction: *satisfy this requirement in a way I would reject.* The instrument now has something literal to work with, and what it produces is a **case the constraint admits** rather than a paraphrase you can argue about. Take that case to the customer. **The requirement you failed to write is whatever they object to**, and this is the cheapest way in the session to find out what it was.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That a requirement has a pass condition and the pass condition has a cost, a cadence, a unit of analysis, a threshold with an origin, and an error rate. Writing the two fields makes you assert those. Running them makes you find out which of your assertions were guesses — and the step that teaches most costs nothing but attention. **Steps 8 and 9 add the thing an evaluator cannot give you: a pass condition that belongs to the data rather than to your test set**, and, with it, the discovery that the check reports the state of a refresh somebody has to remember to run.

## What good looks like

- **The measurement names a quantity and an instrument that exists.** Not a quality. "Timely" is a quality. A named latency, observed at a named point, by something that is already running or that somebody has agreed to build, is a measurement.
- **The verification method names who runs the check, on what data, and when.** A verification method that is a meeting is a decision, not a verification.
- **A stranger could use the two fields to fail the requirement.** This is the test, and it is mechanical: hand the two fields to somebody who was not in the room and ask them to produce a system that satisfies the words and would be rejected. If they can, you are not finished.
- **Every threshold has a stated origin**, and *borrowed from an example bank that carries no source* is an acceptable origin when it is written down as one. [S023](/wiki/seminars/S023.md) is where that admission gets paid for.
- **The unverifiable list is present and is not empty**, or its emptiness is defended. A set in which everything is verifiable on the first pass has usually had its hard requirements quietly rewritten into easy ones.

## How it goes wrong

- **The agreeable requirement** — nobody objects, because there is nothing to object to. *Warning sign:* the review meeting was short and pleasant.
- **The borrowed threshold** — a number lifted from a catalogue of example values that carries no citation anywhere. It then becomes the number everybody defends. *Warning sign:* you cannot say where the figure came from, and neither can the person who signed it off.
- **The verification that is a meeting** — the method reads *reviewed and approved by the steering group*. That establishes that somebody approved it. *Warning sign:* your verification method contains a job title and no instrument.
- **The instrument that does not exist** — the measurement is fine and nothing can currently produce it. This is legitimate, but only if the instrument becomes work with an owner. *Warning sign:* the measurement is precise and nobody has asked who builds the thing that measures it.
- **The control wearing a check's clothes** — the safeguard is an instruction somebody may follow, recorded as if it were a test. *Warning sign:* your verification method cannot fail unless a person forgets to do something.
- **The single run** — the check ran once against a stochastic system and produced a number that was reported as a result. *Warning sign:* your pass rate has no interval and you did not notice it was missing.
- **The deleted unverifiable requirement** — you could not verify it, so you dropped it. It was a real constraint, and it is now invisible rather than absent. *Warning sign:* your set got shorter during this session and nothing was added anywhere else.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed here.** Two things from this session are checked at [S025](/wiki/seminars/S025.md): that every requirement names a measurement and a verification method both executable by somebody who is not the author, and that the unverifiable list is present and not empty or its emptiness defended.

**1 · Separate four controls from four checks.** For each, say whether it fails without anybody's cooperation.

- *Every adverse determination is reviewed by a person.*
- *The system prompt instructs the agent to route adverse determinations for review.*
- *A test asserts that any transcript containing an adverse determination also contains a call to the review queue.*
- *The branch policy requires two reviewers before merge.*

> **Model answers.** Check only in the third case. The first is a process that fails silently when somebody is busy. The second is an instruction a model may follow, partly follow, or silently not follow. The fourth establishes that review was *required* and says nothing about whether it was done well — and it is the example the published playbook itself proposes as evidence of code review, which is worth noticing in a document that is otherwise careful. **A configured control and a demonstrated check are different objects.**

**2 · Fail your own sentence.** Take one requirement and write, by hand, the description of a system that satisfies its words exactly and that you would refuse to accept.

> **What you should find.** It is easier than you expect, and the gap it exploits is usually a missing quantifier — *every*, *before*, *without*, *within* — rather than a missing concept. **The word you had to add to close the gap is the requirement you did not write.**

**3 · Give the threshold an origin.** Take every number in your set and write next to it where it came from. Where the honest answer is that you copied it from an example catalogue, write that.

> **Criterion.** Acceptable origins: the customer's existing policy; a contractual obligation; an observed baseline; a regulator's published requirement; and *borrowed from a catalogue that carries no source*. **That last one is acceptable in writing and unacceptable silently**, and it is the origin most learners actually have. A borrowed number with its provenance attached is a usable engineering artefact; the same number with the provenance stripped is a fabricated specification, and the only difference is a clause.

**4 · Go back to your own item 1, then break it.** Find what you wrote for opening item 1 — the sentence that would show your requirement had **not** been met. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then rewrite it as something that could run this week.

> **Criterion.** A passing answer names the quantity, the instrument that observes it, the data it runs against, who runs it, when it runs, and the threshold with its origin. "The response would be too slow" fails on all six. "The count of determinations issued without a prior recorded review, over the scenario set, run in CI on every change by the delivery team, must be zero — origin: the customer's own escalation policy" passes.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**5 · Run the local check, then break the split.** Using the workflow, run your deterministic check over the test set. Then score one trace that recovers from a bad first attempt under two different split strategies and reconcile the results in two sentences.

> **What you should find.** The trace scores well under the strategy that looks at the whole trajectory and badly under the one that scores each exchange independently. **Neither is wrong.** They answer different questions, and the question you meant to ask is a decision you made — or did not make — before you saw any number. Write the strategy next to every number you report from now on.

**6 · Defend the unverifiable list.** Take the requirement you named in opening item 5 and write the sentence that says why it is real and why nothing can currently check it.

> **Scoring criterion.** A passing sentence names the constraint, names what would have to exist for it to be checkable, and says who would own building that. **A list of things you gave up on is not the artefact; a list of instruments that do not exist yet is.** And note what you are not permitted to do: soften the requirement until it fits an available check. A set in which everything is verifiable on the first pass has usually had its hard requirements quietly rewritten into easy ones.
>
> **Then compare.** Look back at your answer to opening item 3 — whether an unverifiable requirement is a requirement or a hope. Having written the list, has your answer changed? If it has not, check whether your list is empty.

**7 · Turn three sentences into constraints, and predict which one the data breaks.** Take three requirements from your set. For each, write the constraint form — nullability, range, uniqueness or cardinality — and **write down, before you bind anything, which of the three you expect the customer's real data to violate and why.** Then bind them and compare.

> **Criterion, and what you should find.** A passing answer names the check kind for each requirement, states a prediction with a reason, and reports the outcome including the predictions that were wrong. **The interesting failures are the ones you did not predict**, and they divide into two kinds that must not be confused. A binding that fails because the data is dirty has found a data problem. **A binding that fails because the requirement was never true of this organisation has found a requirements problem**, and that one goes to the customer rather than to the data team — it is the same discovery as exercise 2's missing quantifier, arriving from the other direction and carrying evidence.
>
> **Two things a passing answer must also say.** That the range or cardinality you declared has an origin, in the sense [S023](/wiki/seminars/S023.md) will require of every threshold — a constraint the platform enforces is not thereby warranted. And that your result describes the last refresh rather than the source, because the refresh is manual and somebody has to do it. **A check whose observation window is unknown has reported a number and not a result.**
>
> **What this exercise cannot show you.** Whether expressing the requirement this way was worth doing. Nothing published compares it against an evaluator, against a test, or against a well-written sentence, and this session asserts no such comparison.

## What this does not establish

**Nothing here shows that requirements written in this form produce better systems.** The playbook that supplies the form offers no comparative evidence for any of its practices and does not claim to.

**Two limits of that source are carried forward rather than assumed away.** Its pages were written over several years and it has no single currency, so this session cites one page and the date it was read, never the document as a whole. And the same page that supplies the form also supplies a large catalogue of example thresholds **which carries no citation of any kind** — those numbers are illustrations, and this session forbids their use as benchmarks. The playbook also reaches for a magnitude in several other places and warrants it in none; **none of those figures is reproduced anywhere in this wiki.**

**The decision to mark only two of the eight fields is this design's, not the source's**, and it has not been tested. It is made because the other six can be filled correctly by somebody who has understood nothing, and these two cannot.

**The evaluation literature establishes patterns rather than outcomes.** The ladder rests on a single detailed case with no control condition and no measured improvement. The criteria-drift finding is qualitative: it establishes that the phenomenon occurs and matters, not how often or how much, and its user study is small by construction. The statistical material is analytical, and its worked magnitudes rest on stylised assumptions.

**No evaluator's agreement with human judgement is published, and none is asserted anywhere in this session.** The platform documentation supplies a description of what is computed and no evidence of correctness. Most agent evaluators are preview; one consulted page is a superseded classic variant; and surfaces differ across languages, so examples are not interchangeable.

**Nothing establishes that a requirement expressed as a platform-enforced constraint is better than the same requirement expressed as an evaluator, a test or a sentence.** No comparison of the three has been published by anybody, and none is offered here. The claim made is narrower and is still an argument rather than a finding: a check the platform runs fails without your cooperation, and a check you wrote does not. The ontology material was read on 2026-07-31 from two pages fetched in full while the tutorials, how-to pages and agent-integration page were not read at all, so **the binding syntax and the check vocabulary in this session are reconstructed and may be wrong in detail.** Every Fabric IQ item involved is in preview. Nothing was deployed, measured or tested. **And the documentation's own "operational" and "real-time" framing is contradicted, on its own pages and twice, by a manual refresh requirement** — which is the reason this session requires a refresh cadence beside every result rather than treating the check as live.

**And the adversarial step depends on an instrument nobody has characterised.** Its behaviour changes between releases, hosts and models. What it finds is real; what it fails to find establishes nothing.
