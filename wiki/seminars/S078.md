# S078 · The Dependency You Created

**Module:** [M16](/wiki/modules/M16.md) · **Quarter:** [Q8](/wiki/quarters/Q8.md) · **Complexity class:** 5
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** Foundry hosted agents and their operating burden — the container, the capacity commitment and the evaluation cadence somebody inherits
**Whitepaper:** [WP-078](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-078.md)
**Retrieves from:** S048, S072, S077
**Entry state:** this-programme-only

## The situation

Everything convenient you built is a dependency on you. Most of it was created without a decision, by somebody being helpful on a Tuesday.

And the largest one is not a habit, a credential or an undocumented script. It is the system. **The thing you are proudest of is the heaviest object you are leaving behind**, and on the day it ships it looks like an asset rather than an obligation, because on that day you are still there to operate it.

**Answer these in writing before reading further.** Item 3 is the diagnostic.

1. Name three things about this system that only you can currently do. Not *do best* — *do at all*.
2. For each, say what made it that way. Was it a design choice, an accident, or an act of helpfulness?
3. Which of the three is the one you are slightly pleased about?
4. Take any generated component nobody on either side has read closely. Who inherits it, and what do they inherit — the code, or the obligation to understand it?
5. If your organisation is paid to remain involved, what does that do to your answers?

Being needed is pleasant, and the pleasantness is what makes a dependency hard to see from the inside.

## What you must produce

**A dependency disposal record.** Every dependency the engagement created, with a disposal decision from four, and a reason:

- **Transfer** — somebody else will hold it, and they are named.
- **Simplify away** — the thing that needs holding is removed by making the system simpler.
- **Document and accept** — it stays, it is written down, and somebody has agreed to live with it.
- **Remove** — the capability it serves is deleted, along with the dependency.

**A record in which every line is *document and accept* has disposed of nothing.** Documenting a dependency changes who can read about it, not who can act.

The record must also carry, for the running system itself:

1. **The operating burden**, itemised: what runs, what it costs while idle, what has to be renewed, and on what cadence.
2. **The commitments with a term**, each with its expiry and the name of whoever must decide before it.
3. **The one-way doors** — the decisions that look reversible and are not.
4. **The comprehension debt**, stated as a separate object from the code.

## The material

### A hosted agent is a container, and a container is an operating burden

Start with the distinction the platform itself draws, because it is exactly the line between a system somebody can hold and one they cannot.

The agent-building documentation separates two Foundry agent types by hosting rather than by capability ([research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md)). **Prompt agents** use "declarative config in portal or code", are "managed by Foundry with no agent compute", and suit "agents that don't include custom code or require custom hosting". **Hosted agents** run "your code (containerized)", are "managed by Foundry (container compute)", and suit "agents with custom code running in a managed container".

**The line between them is whether you are running code, and therefore whether somebody is paying for and operating compute.** That sentence is the session in one line. A prompt agent is configuration a recipient can read. A hosted agent is a container image, a build that produced it, a registry it lives in, a base image that will need patching, a dependency set that will drift, and a compute allocation that bills.

The same documentation family sets out the setup decision that compounds it: the **basic setup** is "for rapid prototyping and individual development when speed and ease of access are priorities" and **"lacks network isolation"**, while the **standard setup** provides "fine-grained control over data, security, and networking". **Prototyping topology and production topology are different things, and the transition between them is a deliberate act** — an act somebody has to perform, and which is very often left undone at the point of handover because it is not a feature.

**The documentation's limits.** First-party product documentation with a selection-guidance purpose. Its authority is definitional: it tells you what the products are, not that choosing them this way works. **It offers no adoption data, no failure-rate data, no cost comparison and no evidence that its recommended mappings produce better outcomes.** It is highly perishable — product names and feature boundaries are actively changing — and it was read through search-returned excerpts rather than full page fetches.

### A capacity commitment is a bequest with a term

The heaviest single thing you can leave behind is a financial commitment somebody else must manage, and the platform documentation on dedicated capacity is unusually clear about how it behaves ([research/06-microsoft-platform/provisioned-throughput-for-foundry-models.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/provisioned-throughput-for-foundry-models.md)).

The defining property is stated in one clause. A provisioned deployment **"holds a fixed amount of processing capacity exclusively for your deployment's use, whether or not requests are being made."** Everything follows. Capacity held whether or not it is used must be paid for whether or not it is used, which is why the billing unit changes from tokens to time: the hourly meter runs **"regardless of the number of tokens consumed"**, starting at deployment creation and stopping at deletion.

**So the system you leave running has a cost that does not fall when nobody uses it**, and the person who inherits it will discover that on a monthly statement rather than in a runbook.

Three further properties are the ones that turn a commitment into a trap, and each is a dependency in its own right.

**Permission is not availability.** Quota is "a policy limit enforced by Azure, and it has no associated cost"; capacity is "the actual amount of PTUs per model version that's available to be deployed". The documentation states the consequence in its own call-out: **"Having PTU quota doesn't guarantee that capacity is available. If capacity in the region is insufficient for the requested PTU count, the deployment fails."** A recipient reading a quota allocation will believe they hold something they do not.

**Releasing looks reversible and is not.** Capacity availability "changes throughout the day based on customer demand across all regions and models", and deleting or scaling down a deployment releases its capacity back to the region pool with **"no guarantee the same capacity is available if you re-create or scale the deployment up later."** **That is a one-way door wearing the costume of a toggle**, and it belongs on the disposal record under its own heading.

**A financial commitment does not reserve anything.** Reservations are defined as "a financial discount applied to the PTU billing meter… not to individual deployments", and the documentation states the rule plainly: **"Reservations don't guarantee capacity. First create deployments to confirm that capacity is available, then purchase the reservation to lock in the discounted rate."** Deploy, verify, then commit — and the intuitive sequence is exactly wrong.

The documentation also advises directly against the manoeuvre a cost-conscious recipient will reach for first: **"don't plan to scale provisioned deployments up and down with traffic to stay on hourly billing"**, for two stated reasons — capacity may not be available when you need to scale back up, and sustained hourly billing at high utilisation typically exceeds reservation pricing.

**Its limits.** First-party documentation, definitional rather than empirical, single-vendor, with no comparison against alternatives and no evidence of observed reliability. **Perishable on every specific:** names, defaults, ratios, minimum deployment sizes and portal paths change, and the concrete per-model figures live on companion pages that were not fetched and are not reproduced here. Nothing in it may be assessed as fact; the durable content is the permission-versus-availability distinction and the deploy-before-you-commit ordering.

### An evaluation cadence is a dependency with a rota attached

The third inherited object is the one teams forget entirely, because it is not infrastructure and it does not bill.

An evaluation harness is not a thing that runs; it is a thing somebody must run, read and act on. The framework documentation makes the shape of that obligation explicit ([research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-framework-and-evaluations-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-framework-and-evaluations-docs.md)). Evaluation is tiered by cost: a **`LocalEvaluator`** "runs checks locally without API calls — ideal for inner-loop development, CI smoke tests, and fast iteration", while **`FoundryEvals`** connects to a cloud service "for cloud-based LLM-as-judge evaluation". Cloud evaluation requires a project with a model deployment and incurs cost, which is a second bill on the same inheritance.

Three decisions inside that harness are the ones a departing team makes casually and a recipient cannot revisit safely.

**The split strategy.** "Multi-turn conversations must be split into query and response halves for evaluation. **How you split determines *what you're evaluating*.**" Three strategies are supplied — last turn, full, per-turn — and an agent that recovers well from a bad first attempt scores well under one and badly under another. **Neither score is wrong; they answer different questions**, and a recipient who inherits a number without the strategy has inherited an answer to a question nobody told them was asked.

**The thresholds.** Output is pass or fail with a `reason`, plus a numeric `score` for evaluators using a scale before thresholding. **The threshold is a policy choice separable from the measurement**, and moving it changes the pass rate without changing the system. A threshold inherited without its justification is a decision the recipient is now defending and did not make.

**Repetitions.** The framework supplies them "to detect non-deterministic behavior" — which is the acknowledgement that one sample from a stochastic system is not a measurement. A recipient who runs the harness once and reads the result has not measured anything, and nothing in the output says so.

**And the silence that matters most: no agreement rate with human judgement is published for any evaluator, no calibration data, and no rationale for any default threshold.** Most of these evaluators are model-judged. **Assert no agreement figure for any of them, here or anywhere.** A recipient inheriting a dashboard of scores has inherited an apparatus whose correspondence to competent human judgement is undetermined — which is [S068](/wiki/seminars/S068.md)'s machinery-is-not-a-result, arriving as somebody else's operating burden.

**Its limits:** read through search excerpts rather than full page fetches, with structure reconstructed; most agent evaluators are marked preview; one consulted page is an explicitly superseded variant; and API surfaces differ across languages so examples are not interchangeable.

### Comprehension debt is a separate object from the code

The practice corpus records a phenomenon in the practitioners' own voice, and it is the one item on this list that no infrastructure register will ever show you: generated code accumulates comprehension debt — **it exists, it passes, and nobody understands it** ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md)).

Transferring the repository transfers the first object. It does not transfer the second, and the handover paperwork has no column for it.

The same corpus supplies the harder finding about its own withdrawal model. **The best idea in it is that the withdrawal trigger is a state change in an authoritative register rather than a person remembering** — environments are tagged to a project, the register is the source of truth for project status, and marking the project closed initiates decommissioning. Reusing an environment after project close is named as a policy violation rather than an inefficiency. That is genuinely good engineering.

**And there is no handover anywhere in it.** Nothing describes transferring the running system, its infrastructure definitions, its pipelines, its runbooks, its evaluation harness, its cost model or its monitoring to the customer. No named receiving owner, no exit criteria, no acceptance, no support taper, and no evidentiary requirement that the teardown was performed at all. **The organisation mechanised the teardown of its own assets to the level of a register-triggered state machine and left the transfer of capability as an unrecorded aspiration.**

**Its limits:** access-controlled internal material, paraphrased and never quoted, carrying no citation, footnote, study or dataset anywhere across roughly ninety documents. The comprehension-debt claim is a judgement offered by experienced people, with no measurement, no incidence and no consequence data. **Direction only.**

### Interdependence is what makes a system hard to leave

The change literature supplies the structural reason a dependency inventory is longer than anybody expects, and its image is the useful one ([research/05-fde-craft/kotter-leading-change.md](/research/05-fde-craft/kotter-leading-change.md)). It describes an office in which every piece of furniture is joined to every other by ropes, rubber bands and steel cables, and concludes that **"changing highly interdependent settings is extremely difficult because, ultimately, you have to change nearly everything."**

The recommendation that follows is the one this session builds on: **"the purging of unnecessary interconnections can ultimately make a transformation much easier."** Deleting a connection is worth more than documenting it, because a document about a connection is one more thing joined to everything else.

**Its warrant is practitioner counsel, stated as such by its author**: the book carries no footnotes or endnotes by design, draws no major idea from any published source except his own writing, and cites no evidence to bolster its conclusions. Every extended case is pseudonymous and untraceable. **Direction and mechanism only; none of its numbers appears anywhere in this wiki.**

### Reinforcement decays, and accountability is the part that does not

The change model this module borrows contributes one element that bears directly on an inherited cadence: **reinforcement** — "any action or event that strengthens and reinforces the change with an individual or an organization" ([research/05-fde-craft/hiatt-adkar.md](/research/05-fde-craft/hiatt-adkar.md)).

Its most useful claim is about what actually holds: accountability systems are described as **"one of the strongest forms of reinforcement"**, which is to say that a cadence with an owner and a consequence outlasts a cadence with a calendar entry. Its illustrative failure is a bank that deployed a quality programme well and then disbanded the implementation team; within a year the programme was gone. The diagnosis is the transferable part: **"The first mistake the implementation team made was to assume that managing change is essentially completing activities in a project plan."**

**Its warrant is thin.** A trademarked commercial model, published by the firm that sells the training and certification in it, whose quantitative claims come entirely from its own vendor's practitioner self-report surveys with no reproduced methodology and no independent replication. **No independent evaluation of the model appears anywhere in it.** Use the distinction; do not use the model as evidence. And note that the reinforcement list in the source does **not** include removing the old way as a still-available option — that item was invented by an earlier reading and withdrawn.

### Why a dependency nobody understands is worse than a dependency nobody wants

The efficacy literature names the failure mode that a heavy inheritance produces in the group that inherits it, and it is not reluctance ([research/04-professional-formation/bandura-self-efficacy.md](/research/04-professional-formation/bandura-self-efficacy.md)).

Among the catalogued underminers of a group's belief in its joint capability is **dependence on "complex physical technologies that most people neither understand nor believe they can do much to influence"**, alongside the diffusion of accountability through "mazy organizational mechanisms that diffuse and obscure responsibility", and long delays between action and noticeable result. The conclusion the module carries is the sharp one: **"The psychological barriers created by beliefs of collective powerlessness are more demoralizing and debilitating than are external impediments."**

**Read the underminers list as a description of what you are about to hand somebody.** A containerised agent on a capacity commitment with a model-judged evaluation cadence is, by construction, a complex technology most of the receiving team does not understand, whose responsibility is diffused across a platform team, a supplier and a business owner, and whose evaluation signal arrives long after the change that caused it. **That is three of the four underminers, installed by an engineering decision.** The disposal record is where you decide how many of them to leave in place.

**Its limits:** a conference collection edited by the field's founder, with no search strategy, no inclusion criteria and no disconfirming study in the editor's own chapter; settings in schools, families, clinics, migration and addiction treatment, with **no study of adult professionals learning a technical craft**; self-report measurement; and the foundational 1977 paper unread in this repository. **Direction and mechanism only.**

## The workflow

**Audit the operating burden of one running system, then dispose of it — including by deleting some of it.**

**Step 1 — pin what you are auditing.** Deployment type, region, model and version, the setup topology, the container image digest, the evaluation configuration, and the date. **Everything below is a fact about one configuration on one day**, and a recipient who cannot reproduce your pin cannot check your audit.

**Step 2 — separate what is configuration from what is compute.** For each agent in the system, record whether it is declarative with no agent compute or containerised code on managed compute. **The second class carries a build, a base image, a registry and a patching obligation that the first does not**, and it is the boundary along which the inventory divides.

**Step 3 — find every commitment with a term, and write its expiry.** Reserved capacity, support arrangements, licence terms, credential lifetimes, certificate expiries. For each, name the person who must decide before it, and the date they must decide by.

**Step 4 — find the one-way doors.** Ask, for each operation the recipient might reasonably perform: if they do this and then change their mind, can they get back? Scaling a provisioned deployment down is the specimen — the documentation states there is no guarantee the same capacity is available afterwards. **A reversible-looking irreversible operation with no warning on it is a defect you are leaving deliberately.**

**Step 5 — write the record where a machine can read it.** Not a table in a document. Something the recipient's tooling can validate, so that an unowned line fails a check rather than sitting unnoticed:

```yaml
# .handover/dependencies.yml   <- SHAPE, NOT SCHEMA. Every value is a fact about
#                                 ONE configuration on ONE date. Re-derive, never copy.
pin:
  read_on: "<YYYY-MM-DD>"
  region: "<region>"
  deployment_type: "<standard | provisioned | hosted-agent-container>"
  model: "<model+version>"
  image_digest: "<sha256:...>"        # null for declarative agents

dependencies:
  - id: DEP-001
    what: "Containerised agent; base image patched by us, never by them"
    class: technical
    disposal: transfer                 # transfer | simplify-away | document-accept | remove
    owner: "<named person, named team>"
    obligation: "rebuild and redeploy on base-image CVE; cadence: monthly"
    first_due: "<YYYY-MM-DD>"

  - id: DEP-002
    what: "Dedicated capacity commitment"
    class: commercial
    disposal: document-accept
    accepted_by: "<named person>"
    accepted_on: "<YYYY-MM-DD>"
    term_expires: "<YYYY-MM-DD>"
    bills_when_idle: true              # capacity is held whether or not requests are made
    one_way_door: "scaling down releases capacity; no guarantee it returns"

  - id: DEP-003
    what: "Evaluation cadence and its thresholds"
    class: operational
    disposal: transfer
    owner: "<named person>"
    split_strategy: "<last-turn | full | per-turn>"   # determines what the score means
    thresholds_justified_by: "<link, or 'inherited default - NOT justified'>"
    judge_agreement_with_humans: null  # no agreement rate is published for any evaluator
    repetitions: "<n>"                 # one sample from a stochastic system is not a measurement

comprehension_debt:                    # a SEPARATE object from the code
  - component: "<path>"
    read_closely_by: []                # empty is the finding
    inherits_obligation_to_understand: "<named person>"

removed:                               # the only disposal that cannot decay
  - what: "<capability deleted>"
    capability_given_up: "<what the system can no longer do>"
    decided_by: "<named person>"

our_own_interest:                      # goes on the record whether or not it is flattering
  paid_to_remain_involved: <true|false>
  dependencies_that_benefit_us: ["<DEP-id>", "..."]
  what_we_did_about_them: "<...>"
```

**Step 6 — delete something, and price the deletion.** Choose at least one line and take the *simplify away* or *remove* disposal rather than *transfer*. State what capability you gave up. **A system with fewer things that can be tuned is a system with fewer things to explain, and it is also a system that does less.** The trade is the interesting part, and a record with no deletions in it has disposed of nothing.

**Step 7 — attack your own record rather than extending it.** One prompt: *here is a dependency disposal record and the system it describes. Name three operating obligations this record does not contain that a team running this system would meet in its first quarter. Then find the line I have marked "transfer" that is really "document and accept", and say how you can tell.* Read it for the misclassification. Keep what is better than your own reasoning and record what you rejected. **Record separately that this instrument was not independent of the system it audited, and that an agent which endorsed your record was not used as an instrument.**

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** The length of the *transfer* column against the length of the *removed* column, on one screen. **Almost every real record is nearly all transfers**, and the reason is not carelessness: transferring is the disposal that requires no decision, no negotiation and no loss. **Deleting requires you to give something up, in front of somebody who asked for it.** The record makes the asymmetry countable, and nothing said in a meeting does.

## What good looks like

- The inventory includes **organisational dependencies, not only technical ones**: the escalation route that ends at your contact details, the credential issued in your name, the review that only you have ever performed, the meeting that only happens because you convene it.
- It includes **the operating burden of the system itself**: what runs, what it costs while idle, what has to be rebuilt when a base image is patched, and who does that.
- **Every commitment with a term has its expiry and a named person who must decide before it**, and the decision date is earlier than the expiry.
- **The one-way doors are marked**, including the ones that look like toggles. An operation the recipient cannot undo and was not warned about is a defect you left deliberately.
- It includes **comprehension debt**: components that exist, pass their tests, and are understood by nobody — and it states who inherits the obligation to understand them, which is a different transfer from inheriting the code.
- Any inherited evaluation cadence carries **its split strategy, its thresholds and the justification for them**, or records honestly that the thresholds are inherited defaults nobody has justified.
- At least one line is **simplify away** or **remove**, and the capability given up is stated. A dependency removed needs no runbook, no owner and no rehearsal, and it is the only disposal that cannot decay.
- Where the honest disposal is *document and accept*, the record names who accepted and when they were asked.
- **The commercial position is stated.** If remaining involved is rewarded, that appears on the record as a fact about the situation, not as an accusation and not as a silence.

## How it goes wrong

- **Dependency read as expertise** — the things only you can do are recorded as evidence of your value rather than as defects of the handover. *Warning sign:* your inventory is short and you are not troubled by it.
- **Documentation as disposal** — every line is documented, nothing is disposed. *Warning sign:* your record has one column of decisions and they are all the same.
- **The idle bill** — the system is transferred with a capacity commitment that meters whether or not anybody uses it, and nobody told the recipient. *Warning sign:* your cost discussion was about per-request pricing.
- **Quota mistaken for capacity** — the recipient is told they hold an allocation they do not, and finds out when a deployment fails. *Warning sign:* your record names a quota figure and no successful deployment against it.
- **The dependency you are** — the thing that cannot be transferred is your judgement about when something is wrong, and it does not appear on any inventory because it is not an artefact. *Warning sign:* somebody asks how the recipient will know something has gone wrong and your answer is that they will see it.
- **The deliberate dependency** — the simplification is available, cheap and not taken, and the reason is that it would end the relationship. *Warning sign:* the option was considered and no reason for rejecting it was recorded.
- **The generated inheritance** — a component nobody understands is transferred as though it were a component somebody understands. *Warning sign:* the handover treats the code and the comprehension as the same object.
- **The orphaned cadence** — the evaluation harness is transferred, nobody owns running it, and it is still green in six months because nothing has run. *Warning sign:* your record names the harness and not the rota.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The disposal record is carried to [S079](/wiki/seminars/S079.md), where an undisposed dependency shows up as an intervention, and to [S080](/wiki/seminars/S080.md), where the whole stage is judged as an entrustment decision.

**1 · Sort seven inherited objects by what they oblige.** For each, say who must do what, on what cadence, and what happens if nobody does:

- A containerised agent built from a base image with published vulnerabilities.
- A dedicated capacity commitment with a term.
- A quota allocation in a region.
- An evaluation harness with inherited thresholds.
- A runbook for an incident type that has not occurred.
- A generated data-transformation component that passes its tests.
- A prompt agent defined declaratively with no agent compute.

> **Model answers.** The first obliges a rebuild-and-redeploy cadence with a named owner; nobody doing it produces a silently ageing attack surface. The second obliges a renewal decision before a date and bills while idle; nobody doing it produces either an unexpected bill or an unexpected loss of capacity. The third obliges nothing and **guarantees nothing** — permission is not availability, and a recipient who reads it as an entitlement has been misled. The fourth obliges a rota and a justification; nobody doing it produces a dashboard that is green because nothing ran. The fifth obliges nothing until the incident, at which point it obliges accuracy nobody has checked. The sixth obliges comprehension and transfers none. **The seventh is the answer to the exercise: it obliges almost nothing, which is why the disposal that turns a hosted agent into a declarative one is worth more than any runbook you could write about the hosted one.**

**2 · Audit one commitment for its one-way doors.** Take the capacity arrangement in the supplied situation and list every operation a cost-conscious recipient might perform in their first quarter. For each, say whether they can get back.

> **What you should find.** That the obvious economy — scaling down when traffic is low — is the one the documentation explicitly advises against, for two stated reasons: capacity may not be available when they scale back up, and sustained hourly billing at high utilisation typically exceeds the committed rate. **The recipient will attempt it anyway**, because it is the reasonable move and the warning lives in a concepts page they have not read. A record that does not carry that warning has transferred a trap.

**3 · Go back to your own item 3, then break it.** Find what you wrote for opening item 3 — the dependency you are slightly pleased about. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then write its disposal properly.

> **Criterion.** A disposal passes if it states **what would have to be given up to remove the dependency**, and if that answer is neither "nothing" nor "I cannot say". *Nothing* means it should already be gone and you have been keeping it. *I cannot say* means you do not understand the dependency well enough to transfer it either. **A pass is available only by naming a real loss**, and the loss is usually smaller than the pleasure of being needed.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Convert one hosted agent to a declarative one on paper, and price it.** Take one containerised component and write what it would take to express it as configuration with no agent compute, then state exactly what capability that would cost.

> **What to notice.** That the answer is usually *some* capability rather than none, and that the capability lost is frequently one nobody asked for. **The exercise is not an argument that declarative is better.** It is that the boundary between configuration and compute is the boundary between something a recipient can read and something they must operate, and that most teams cross it without noticing they have made a handover decision.

**5 · Write the cadence rota, then check whether anybody agreed to it.** For every recurring obligation on your record — base-image rebuilds, evaluation runs, threshold reviews, capacity renewals — name the person, the frequency, and the date they were asked.

> **Scoring criterion.** A pass has a named person and an asking date for every recurring line. **A fail has frequencies and no names**, which is a calendar rather than an accountability system — and the change literature this module borrows from treats accountability as the strongest available form of reinforcement, with its illustrative failure being an implementation team disbanded after a successful deployment whose programme was gone within a year. **That source is a commercially interested practitioner model with no independent evaluation; take the distinction and not the model.**

**6 · Count your own interest.** Write the paragraph nobody writes: which dependencies your own organisation benefits from, and what you did about them.

> **What to notice.** That the list is rarely empty, and that the honest entries are usually not sinister — a support arrangement somebody negotiated in good faith, an escalation route that genuinely works because you are on it. **The record is not an accusation.** It exists because a disposal decision made by a party with an interest is a decision a reader should be able to weigh, and because the alternative to writing it down is that everybody involved knows and nobody says.

## What this does not establish

**That disposal improves survival.** Nothing behind this programme measures whether a system with fewer manufactured dependencies outlives one with more.

**The session's strongest claim is unwarranted and stated as such:** that removing a dependency is preferable to documenting one. That is an argument from decay — a document goes stale silently while a deleted capability cannot — and no source behind this programme tests it.

**The platform facts are definitional and perishable.** The capacity and hosting documentation states how the platform behaves. It offers no adoption data, no failure-rate data, no cost comparison and no evidence that its recommended mappings produce better outcomes, and its concrete figures live on companion pages that were not fetched and are not reproduced here. Names, defaults, minimums and portal paths change.

**The evaluation documentation publishes no agreement rate with human judgement for any evaluator, no calibration data and no threshold rationale.** Most of those evaluators are model-judged. **Assert no agreement figure for any of them.** It was read through search excerpts; most agent evaluators are marked preview and one consulted page is explicitly superseded.

**The comprehension-debt claim is a practitioner judgement**, offered by experienced people with no measurement, no incidence and no consequence data.

**The interdependence and reinforcement material is practitioner counsel.** One source is deliberately unfootnoted by its author's own statement, with untraceable cases; the other is a trademarked commercial model whose evidence is its own vendor's surveys and which cites no independent evaluation of itself anywhere.

**The collective-efficacy material is about schools, families, clinics and migration**, and contains no study of adult professionals. **Direction and mechanism only. No magnitude, no rate, no proportion.**
