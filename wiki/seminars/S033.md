## The situation

*Is this feasible?* is not a question. It has no answer format, no instrument and no failing outcome, and it will be answered — usually by whoever is most senior, and usually in a meeting.

Underneath it are four or five real questions that do have answers, and the work of this session is getting from the first to the second before anybody spends anything.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught.

1. Take your design's riskiest claim and write it as a question a test could answer. Now write the answer format: a number, a yes, a transcript, a count, a demonstration.
2. Which of your uncertainties can be settled by talking to somebody, and which cannot? Sort them.
3. Somebody demonstrates the hard part working, once, on an example they chose. What have you learned?
4. A model produces a fluent, confident, well-structured answer to something you cannot check. What is your position?
5. What is the cheapest thing that could stop this engagement, and have you done it yet?

**Item 3 is the trap.** A successful demonstration on a chosen example establishes that the thing is possible on that example, which is a much smaller claim than the one it will be reported as.

## What you must produce

A **feasibility question set** — one row per uncertainty, and a row is not admissible unless every column is filled.

- **The uncertainty**, in one sentence.
- **The question**, with an answer format stated.
- **The class** — is this about whether it can be built and will keep working, or about whether anybody wants it, will fund it and is permitted to have it? The two need different instruments and merging them is the commonest failure in the set.
- **The instrument** — a spike, representative data, an evaluation over a set you did not choose, an observation, a constraint check, a conversation with a named role.
- **The cost of the instrument**, stated before it is run.
- **The decision that turns on the answer**, and the direction each answer sends it.

## The material

### What *is this feasible?* decomposes into

The question has no answer format, so it will be answered by whoever is most senior. Underneath it are four or five questions that do have answers, and getting from the first to the second is the whole session.

A usable feasibility question has three properties and each of them is checkable by a reader.

**It names an answer format.** A number, a yes, a count, a transcript, a demonstration on inputs somebody else chose. **If the answer could be a paragraph, the question is still a goal.**

**It names an instrument that could produce that format.** Not a person's confidence. Not a meeting. A documented statement, a run over a stated set, an observation, a constraint check, a conversation with a named role about something that already happened.

**It names the decision waiting on it, and where each answer sends that decision.** A question whose every answer leaves the design unchanged has already been settled, and asking it is a way of looking busy while the schedule runs.

The fourth property is the one learners resist. **A feasibility set should be ordered by cheapness of disqualification rather than by interest.** The question most likely to stop the work, answerable most cheaply, goes first — whatever it is, however dull. This is design reasoning with no external warrant, and it is held because the alternative ordering has an obvious failure mode: you spike the part you wanted to build and discover in week three that a regional availability constraint settled the whole thing on day one.

### Two classes of question, and why merging them is the commonest failure in the set

Whether the organisation wants the outcome, will fund it, is permitted to have it and will adopt it, is one family of question. Whether the system can be built, whether the data supports it, whether the model behaves and whether the integration holds, is another.

**Conversations reduce uncertainty in the first family and cannot resolve the second.** That is not a slogan; it is a routing rule, and it fails most often in one specific direction. A confident engineer's assurance is the cheapest instrument available and it settles nothing, so the temptation to route a technical question to a conversation is strongest exactly where the conversation is least capable. The corresponding failure in the other direction is rarer and more expensive: building something to answer a question about whether anybody wants it.

The practical test is to write the instrument next to the question and read the pair aloud. *Will the model do this — instrument: interview the sponsor* fails immediately when you say it. It does not fail when you write it in a table.

### Some feasibility questions already have a documented answer, and one of them is no

This is the part of the session that saves the most time and is used least.

There is a standing position in the planning literature that auto-regressive language models **cannot, by themselves, do planning — and cannot do self-verification either**, with the parenthetical that closes the usual escape route: self-verification "is after all a form of reasoning" ([research/02-technical-foundations/kambhampati-llms-cant-plan.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/kambhampati-llms-cant-plan.md)). The constructive half is that models should be treated as **"universal approximate knowledge sources"** — broad coverage, no correctness guarantee — and paired with an **external verifier** in a two-way critique loop, with correctness coming from the verifier and flexibility from the model.

**Read that as a feasibility answer and it settles a whole class of design.** A design whose reliability rests on the model checking its own plan has a documented negative answer available before anybody spends a week finding it out. A design that generates candidates and checks them against something outside the model does not. **You do not need to spike the difference; you need to read.**

**The limits matter and must travel with the claim.** It is a **position paper, not an experiment.** Its central claims are arguments and have been contested in the subsequent literature. It targets auto-regressive models as of early 2024, and models explicitly trained for reasoning postdate the argument, so **the empirical picture should be re-checked rather than assumed static.** It also does not tell you how to build a verifier for an open-ended domain, and its verifier-construction step still depends on human review. **What it licenses is a question you should ask and an instrument you should not use** — not a prohibition on a design.

### Generalisation is a feasibility question, and the published answer is that it frequently does not hold

The cleanest available demonstration of a feasibility question with a measured, uncomfortable answer comes from retrieval, and it transfers well beyond retrieval.

The complaint the work was built on is methodological. Neural retrieval models had been reporting improvements measured overwhelmingly on a small number of datasets, and most often on the dataset the model was trained on — a design that **cannot distinguish a model that has learned retrieval from one that has learned a particular corpus, a particular query style and a particular notion of relevance.** Since almost every practical deployment involves a domain with no in-domain training data, **the quantity practitioners actually need is generalisation, and the literature was not measuring it** ([research/02-technical-foundations/thakur-beir.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/thakur-beir.md)).

The benchmark assembled dissimilar retrieval tasks deliberately — varying query length, document length and the shape of the retrieval relationship, since finding a paper that supports a claim, finding a duplicate question and finding a passage that answers a question are not the same operation — and evaluated systems spanning lexical, sparse, dense, late-interaction and re-ranking architectures under zero-shot conditions.

Three findings are directly useful to a feasibility set, and none of them requires a number.

**A long-standing lexical method proved a robust baseline across the collection.** It was not the best, and it did not collapse on any domain. **A method whose worst case is acceptable is operationally different from one whose average is higher and whose worst case is unknown**, and that distinction belongs in your criteria rather than in a footnote.

**The best average zero-shot performance came from the most computationally expensive families**, and the cheaper ones — dense and sparse retrieval — often underperformed, which the authors characterise as considerable room for improvement in their generalisation. **Efficiency and generalisation were in tension**, which is the shape of the trade you will meet whenever a demonstration is cheap and a deployment is not.

**And the transferable conclusion is the one to write into your question set: a score on the dataset a model was trained on is not evidence about a new corpus.** Retrieval quality is a property of the model and the domain jointly.

**The limits are recorded and are not small.** The results are a snapshot from a particular moment; **the specific rankings have since changed and the work should be used for its methodology and trade structure rather than for which system led.** Eighteen datasets are a sample of retrieval tasks and not a map of them, so strong performance is evidence of breadth and not a guarantee for any new domain. Zero-shot was the chosen condition, so the results say nothing about what modest in-domain adaptation would recover. **And the benchmark has itself become a target of optimisation, which weakens its own zero-shot premise over time.**

### A demonstration on a chosen example, and what a small sample can support

A successful demonstration on an example the demonstrator chose establishes that the thing is possible on that example. It will be reported as something much larger, and the gap between those two statements is where most feasibility findings go wrong.

Two repairs, and the second is the one this programme insists on.

**Do not choose the inputs.** Run over a set somebody else assembled, or over an unfiltered sample of the real thing, and **state the sampling frame before you look at the outcome.** Any frame stated afterwards was selected in the presence of the result.

**State the uncertainty, or state that you have not.** An investigation is an experiment; a reported result needs to carry how many cases it rests on, and where the cases come in related groups — several questions about the same document, the same question in several forms — **a naive treatment of them will make the result look more precise than it is** ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md)). The part that bears hardest on a feasibility set is the discipline of asking, before the run, **what difference this could have detected.** Without it, *we found no problem* and *we could not have found this problem* are the same sentence and the finished document does not distinguish them.

**A finding from a small sample that states no uncertainty overclaims**, and it overclaims in the direction that flatters the person who ran it. **That source is analytical rather than empirical**, its illustrative magnitudes rest on stylised assumptions, and it addresses comparing scores rather than choosing what to score — so a tight result around a badly chosen question is still meaningless. **No number from it appears here.**

### The instrument this session actually uses, and the boundary of what documentation can settle

A surprising share of technical feasibility questions are settled by reading, not by running — and they are settled in minutes rather than weeks. Whether a model is available in the region the customer's data must stay in. Whether a deployment type exists at the throughput shape the workload needs. Whether a modality is supported at all. Whether the tool the brief names is designed for the scale the brief assumes.

That last one is worth stating as a specimen, because vendors rarely publish scale envelopes and this one did: **Copilot Studio is "designed for small-scale deployments, such as department-level solutions."** The same documentation family states its selection criteria as **"your organization's technical capabilities, timeline, and control requirements"**, splits build paths by service model, and records that the basic Foundry setup **"lacks network isolation"** while the standard setup provides fine-grained control over data, security and networking ([research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md)). **A brief that assumes enterprise-wide rollout on a department-scale tool has a documented feasibility answer, and nobody has to build anything to find it.**

**Three limits, and the third is the one learners forget.** The documentation is first-party with a selection-guidance purpose, so **its authority is definitional**: it tells you what the products are, not that choosing them this way works. It publishes **no adoption data, no failure-rate data, no cost comparison and no evidence that its recommended mappings produce better outcomes**. It was read here through search-returned excerpts rather than full page fetches, with several comparison tables truncated. And it is **highly perishable** — product names and capability boundaries are actively changing, so **a documented answer carries the date you read it or it carries nothing.**

**The fourth limit is about the corpus rather than the page: documentation settles what is stated and is silent on what is not.** An absence is a finding and must be recorded as one. **Inferring a limit from a silence is the error this session exists to prevent** — a tool that publishes no scale envelope has not published a large one.

### Model, region and rate are one question, not three

The commonest feasibility question on this platform is written as four separate rows and is really one conjunction: **can we get that model, in that region, at that throughput, under that deployment type.** A design fails if any single clause answers no, and a set that scatters the four across four rows will settle three of them and discover the fourth in delivery.

The reason they are one question is that the platform's own decomposition puts them on the same object. A deployment type is chosen along two axes at once — **how you pay**, standard by the token or provisioned as reserved capacity, and **where inference is processed**, globally, within a named data zone, or in the deployment region — with batch variants alongside and a **Developer** type intended for evaluating fine-tuned models that carries **no service-level agreement and no data-residency guarantee** ([research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-agent-build-and-runtime.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-agent-build-and-runtime.md)).

**One published rule settles more designs than anything else in the catalogue, and it is the rule teams get backwards.** Data stored at rest remains in the designated geography **for every deployment type**. What the type actually selects is **where inference is processed**: global types may be processed in any region where the model is deployed, data-zone types stay within the specified zone, and regional types are processed in the deployment region. **All types perform identical inference operations. Billing, scale and performance are what differ.** A residency requirement therefore does not eliminate a model — it eliminates deployment types, which is a smaller and more checkable claim, and one a documented answer can settle in minutes.

A second published detail is worth the whole session on its own, because it corrects a near-universal misreading. **Provisioned capacity is offered for low latency *variance*, not for low latency.** Read the selection guidance carefully and it recommends bursty traffic to standard, consistent high volume to provisioned, large non-time-sensitive work to batch. **That is a claim about the shape of a distribution and not about its centre**, and a requirement written as *the response must be fast* cannot be settled against it, while one written as *the slowest responses must not exceed what a handler will wait for* can.

The rate clause has its own instrument and its own trap. Capacity is allocated **per subscription, per region and per model, in tokens per minute**, and two published interfaces answer the two different questions — one reporting consumption against limits, the other reporting available capacity by model and region ([research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-control-plane-and-governance.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-control-plane-and-governance.md)). **The trap is that the capacity interfaces return information for models that are retired and no longer available**, so a capacity answer taken alone will happily describe something you cannot deploy. **Pair every capacity answer with a lifecycle answer, in the same row, on the same date.**

And the escape route most teams assume is open should be recorded as what it is. *We will ask for more* is not an instrument. Increase requests are **processed in order of receipt with priority given to customers already consuming their allocation**, and requests failing that condition **might be denied**; **no response time, approval rate or appeal route is published**; and **partner and community models do not support increases at all.** A row whose plan depends on an increase has a dependency with no service level attached, which belongs on the [S032](/wiki/seminars/S032.md) record as an acceptance rather than in this set as an answer.

**So the answer format for this row is not yes or no.** It is **four clauses and a date** — model, region, rate, deployment type — and a row missing any of them is inadmissible in exactly the way [S033](/wiki/seminars/S033.md)'s standard already requires. There is also a published path where some models are callable by name with **no deployment created at all**, which changes the shape of the question rather than the answer, and which a row should record if it applies.

**The limits are the usual ones and they bind hard here.** Both notes are first-party documentation whose authority is **definitional**: they state what the platform offers and commit to nothing about outcomes. Neither publishes adoption data, failure-rate data, cost comparison or evidence that any recommended mapping produces a better result. Both were read on one day, with substantial portions taken from **search-returned excerpts rather than full page fetches** and several tables truncated. **Every number in both notes is forbidden downstream and none appears above.** And the material is **extremely perishable**: the agent-runtime note records that **four first-party surfaces disagreed about one configuration file on the day it was read**, one formally deprecated and all rendering, which is the sharpest argument in this repository for pinning an answer to a page, a version and a date.

### Prompt agent or hosted agent is a feasibility fork, not a preference

Teams argue about this one and the argument is usually a preference dressed as an assessment. **It has a determinate answer once a constraint is stated**, which makes it a feasibility question rather than a design taste, and it is one of the few rows in a set where the wrong answer's bill arrives months later.

The two shapes are genuinely different objects. A **prompt agent** is declaratively defined, fully managed, and **consumes no agent compute.** A **hosted agent** is your containerised code, pushed as an image or shipped as source and built for you, which the service pulls and runs on managed compute — **assigning it a dedicated identity and a dedicated endpoint** as it does so ([research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-agent-build-and-runtime.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-agent-build-and-runtime.md)).

**The published reasons to choose the second are a checklist, and that is what makes the question answerable.** Bring your own code in any framework. Use custom protocols and accept payloads the managed shape does not. Control processor and memory allocation. Run stateful work that persists files across turns. **State your constraint first, then read the four.** If none of them fires, hosted is a preference and the row is not a feasibility question at all — which is a legitimate and underused outcome for a row. If one fires, the answer is determinate, cheap and available before anything is built.

**The cost of getting it wrong has an unusual shape and is the part a feasibility set must carry.** Hosted agents **scale per session rather than per replica**: there is no replica count to configure and no warm pool to size. The processor and memory you set describe **a single session**, and billing runs across all active sessions — so **sizing is multiplied by concurrency**, and a generous default is the most expensive mistake available on the surface. The published right-sizing procedure is **empirical rather than analytical**: run a representative workload, read the performance telemetry, and compare observed peaks against allocation. **A row that answers *hosted* and states no concurrency assumption has answered half the question.**

One further fork hides inside the first and produces bugs that look like model failures. **The protocol is a separate decision**: one protocol includes conversation-history management, the other has none at all and your own container manages state. And the vocabulary is treacherous — **a session is sandbox compute plus a persisted filesystem, a conversation is the message history, and they are different objects with different lifetimes.** Under the history-managing protocol, **reusing the same session identifier does not, on its own, replay prior messages to the model.** A design that assumed it does has a defect that will be diagnosed as forgetfulness.

**The limits are the same as the section above and one more.** Several of the pages behind this material are **superseded or deprecated variants that still render**, including the schema reference whose own first paragraph calls the model it documents deprecated. **Preview status is pervasive and load-bearing.** The material was read largely through search-returned excerpts, so **no list here is exhaustive**, and **every engineering constant in it — timeouts, tiers, ceilings — is forbidden downstream and appears nowhere in this wiki.** What transfers is the shape of the fork and the fact that it has an answer.

### The buried finding, read as a worked example

The internal practice corpus contains one passage that is a feasibility finding in everything but name, and it is in speaker notes.

On a mature production codebase the assistant could not be asked to add a feature end to end: the generated code reused outdated patterns and ignored architectural constraints, and the workaround was to make failing tests the specification and spend most of the human effort on the tests ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md)).

**Read it as a row in a question set and it is complete.** It has a question — can the assistant add a feature end to end on this codebase. It has an instrument — attempting it. It has a negative answer with a stated cause. It has a next step that changed the design of the work. **It also contradicts its own collection's headline claims about speed, and it is filed where nobody will read it.**

That is the session's second lesson and it is about routing rather than about questions. **A finding's value is bounded by where it lands.** Write down where in your own practice a finding of that shape would end up, and who would see it. **The corpus is access-controlled, contains no cost side of any kind, records no failed engagement, and carries not one external citation in roughly ninety documents** — which is why this passage is offered as a specimen of the form and never as evidence of the claim.

## The workflow

**Take your feasibility set and settle every row that a documented answer can settle, before anybody runs anything.**

You will use GitHub Copilot in agent mode with the Microsoft Learn MCP Server attached, querying the Foundry model catalogue and its surrounding documentation. **One warrant note before you start: this repository holds no research note for the Learn MCP Server.** Its behaviour — an unauthenticated remote endpoint exposing documentation search, documentation fetch and code-sample search — is **carried forward from [S001](/wiki/seminars/S001.md) and is unverified here.** Treat the server as a tool this programme has used and not as a source this programme has read.

**Step 1 — split the classes before anything else.** Sort every uncertainty into *wanted, funded, permitted, adopted* or into *buildable, supported, behaves, integrates*. Then, within the second family only, split again: settled by reading, settled by running, settled by neither.

**Step 2 — write the set as a file, with the answer format in it.** One row per uncertainty. A row is inadmissible until every column is filled, and the `answer_format` column is the one that does the work.

```yaml
# docs/feasibility/questions.yaml   — written before any instrument was run
- id: F1
  uncertainty: "The model we need may not be deployable in the region the data must stay in."
  question: "Is the named model available for the deployment type we need, in that region?"
  answer_format: yes-no                 # settled by reading, not by running
  class: technical
  instrument: documentation             # Learn MCP Server + model catalogue
  cost: minutes
  cheapest_disqualifier: true           # ordered first because it can stop the work
  decision: "Region choice, and whether the engagement proceeds at all."
  on_no: "Stop. Re-open the region constraint with the sponsor."
  answer: ""                            # page + date, or NOT STATED IN THE DOCS
  read_on: ""
- id: F2
  uncertainty: "Retrieval may work on our examples and not on the customer's corpus."
  question: "On fifty cases chosen by the operations team, does retrieval return the passage a case handler needs?"
  answer_format: count-per-case
  class: technical
  instrument: run-over-a-set-we-did-not-choose
  sampling_frame: "Stated here BEFORE the run: all cases closed last month, unfiltered."
  could_have_detected: "A failure on a whole case type. NOT a small difference between configurations."
  cost: two days
  decision: "Whether the retrieval layer is in the design at all."
  on_no: "The design loses its grounding claim; escalate to S030."
- id: F3
  uncertainty: "The reviewers may not accept an automated determination."
  question: "What did the review team do the last three times an automated output was proposed?"
  answer_format: transcript
  class: organisational                 # NOT merged with F1 or F2
  instrument: conversation-with-named-role
  cost: one hour
  decision: "Whether a human-in-the-loop step is a requirement or an option."
  on_no: "The design's routing step becomes mandatory rather than optional."
```

**Step 3 — attach the documentation server and confirm the agent will use it.** Attaching is not invoking, and the failure is silent: declining the trust prompt leaves requests running **without** the tools and without an error. **A connected server is an available tool, not a used one.**

**Step 4 — force the absence into view before you ask anything.** Add an instructions file requiring that every claim about a platform capability carry a citation to a fetched page in the same sentence, that an unsupported claim be answered with *not found in the documentation* rather than from general knowledge, and that the fetch date be recorded. **Send one prompt and confirm the instruction text appears in the request payload** before you trust a single answer.

**Step 5 — ask the catalogue questions, one row at a time, in the form you wrote them.** Availability by region and deployment type. Supported modality. Stated scale envelope. Whether the setup topology the brief assumes carries network isolation. **Fill the `answer` and `read_on` columns with a page and a date, or with `NOT STATED IN THE DOCS`.**

**Step 6 — treat the absences as findings and do not convert them into inferences.** A tool that publishes no scale envelope has not published a large one. Move that row to the *settled by neither* pile with the absence recorded, and decide whether it is worth an instrument or an acceptance.

**Step 7 — for the rows documentation cannot settle, write the run before you run it.** Name the input set, name who assembled it, state the sampling frame, and **write the `could_have_detected` line before the first case is scored.**

**Step 8 — attack your own answers, at a named target.** Do not ask the agent whether your set is good. Give it your filled rows and ask it, for each documented answer, **to argue that the page you cited does not settle the question you asked** — to name the gap between what the documentation states and what your design needs. Then ask which rows would have a different answer if the sampling frame were the one you would have chosen. Keep what is better than your own reasoning; record what you rejected and why. **An agent that confirmed your row was not used as an instrument.**

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That a large share of what a team calls feasibility is settled by reading, in minutes, and that the reason nobody reads is that the question was never stated precisely enough for a page to answer it. **You will also watch the grounded instrument go silent** on a question the corpus does not cover — the in-sentence citation requirement is what makes that silence visible, and **grounding makes an answer accurate within a corpus and silent outside it, with nothing in an ungrounded output telling you which side of that line you are on.**

**The row that is four rows, and the fork that has an answer.**

Two more rows, run after the set above, because both are common, both are usually deferred into delivery, and both are settled by reading.

**Step A — collapse your model, region, rate and deployment-type rows into one.** If your set has three of the four and not the fourth, that is the finding. **Write the row so that a single missing clause makes it inadmissible**, because a design fails if any one of them answers no and a set that scores three out of four has scored zero.

```yaml
# docs/feasibility/questions.yaml  (continued)
- id: F4
  uncertainty: "We may not be able to run the model we need where the data must stay, at the rate the workload implies."
  question: "Model, region, rate and deployment type — all four, or which one is no?"
  answer_format: four-clauses-and-a-date          # NOT yes-no. One missing clause = inadmissible.
  class: technical
  instrument: documentation + capacity interfaces
  cost: minutes
  cheapest_disqualifier: true
  clauses:
    model:            ""                          # page + date, or NOT STATED IN THE DOCS
    region:           ""
    rate:             ""                          # consumption against limit, AND available capacity
    deployment_type:  ""                          # what it selects is WHERE INFERENCE IS PROCESSED;
                                                  # data at rest stays in the geography for every type
  lifecycle_cross_check: ""                       # REQUIRED. The capacity interfaces report on
                                                  # retired models, so a capacity answer alone
                                                  # can describe something undeployable.
  latency_requirement_form: ""                    # variance or centre? Provisioned is offered for
                                                  # low latency VARIANCE. A requirement written as
                                                  # 'must be fast' cannot be settled against that.
  decision: "Region, deployment type, and whether the engagement proceeds."
  on_no: "Escalate to S026. Do NOT route to 'we will request an increase' — see below."
  increase_is_not_an_instrument: >
    Requests are processed in order of receipt with priority to customers already
    consuming their allocation, and requests failing that condition might be denied.
    No response time, approval rate or appeal route is published. Partner and
    community models admit no increase at all. If the plan depends on an increase,
    that is an ACCEPTANCE on the S032 record with an owner, not an answer here.

- id: F5
  uncertainty: "We may be choosing a build shape by preference rather than by constraint."
  question: "Does any of the four published triggers for the heavier shape actually fire for us?"
  answer_format: yes-no-per-trigger
  class: technical
  instrument: documentation
  cost: minutes
  triggers:
    own_framework:     ""   # bring your own code, any framework
    custom_protocol:   ""   # payloads the managed shape does not accept
    controlled_sizing: ""   # processor and memory under your control
    stateful_turns:    ""   # files persisting across turns
  concurrency_assumption: ""  # REQUIRED IF ANY TRIGGER FIRES. Sizing describes ONE session
                              # and billing runs across all active sessions, so the bill is
                              # sizing multiplied by concurrency. A row without this has
                              # answered half the question.
  protocol_choice: ""         # one protocol manages conversation history; the other does not.
                              # Session is compute-plus-filesystem; conversation is message
                              # history. Reusing a session id does not replay prior messages.
  decision: "Build shape, and who owns the runtime cost line."
  on_no_trigger: "The lighter shape. This row was a preference, and recording that is the result."
```

**Step B — fill the four clauses one at a time, with a page and a date, or with `NOT STATED IN THE DOCS`.** Do not summarise four answers into one sentence. **The row exists so that a reader can see which clause carried the decision**, and a merged answer conceals exactly that.

**Step C — run the lifecycle cross-check even when the capacity answer is good.** This is the step everybody skips and it is the one that produces a wrong row rather than a missing one. **A capacity report that describes a retired model is not an error and does not look like one.**

**Step D — attack the four-clause row at a named target.** Give the agent your filled clauses and ask it to argue that **the pages you cited settle a narrower question than the one your design asks** — naming the gap in each clause separately. Then ask which clause would change if the requirement were written about the shape of the latency distribution rather than about its centre. **Keep what beats your reasoning, record what you rejected, and note which clause the instrument found weakest**, because that is usually the one nobody re-reads.

**What this second half makes observable.** That a feasibility question can be *precise and still wrong*, because it was precise about three of four clauses. **And that a build-shape argument the team has been having for a fortnight has a published checklist behind it** — four triggers, each a yes or no, none of them a matter of taste. If none fires, the honest row records that the question was a preference. **That outcome is a pass, and it is the outcome the set most often refuses to write down.**

## What good looks like

- **Every question has an answer that would stop the work**, or the row says explicitly that it does not and why it is still worth asking.
- **The two classes are separated and not merged.** Whether the system can do the thing, and whether the organisation wants and may have the thing, are not settled by the same activity.
- **No technical uncertainty is routed to a conversation.** [S020](/wiki/seminars/S020.md) established the routing; this session applies it where the temptation is strongest, because a confident engineer's assurance is the cheapest instrument available and settles nothing.
- **Every row that could be settled by reading was settled by reading**, with a page and the date it was read — or with the absence recorded in the documentation's own words.
- **The instrument is cheaper than building the thing**, and the row says by what.
- **At least one question is ordered first because it is the cheapest disqualifier**, not because it is the most interesting.
- **The set names what would make a positive answer untrustworthy** — a chosen example, an unrepresentative sample, a single run.

## How it goes wrong

- **The unanswerable question** — *can we do this* has no answer format, so the answer will be somebody's confidence. *Warning sign:* your question could be answered by nodding.
- **The demonstration mistaken for feasibility** — it worked once, on an example the demonstrator chose. *Warning sign:* nobody selected the input.
- **The merged classes** — a market question and a technical question share a row and a test. *Warning sign:* your instrument for *will the model do this* is a stakeholder interview.
- **The persuasive wrong answer** — the output was fluent, structured and confident, and it was accepted. *Warning sign:* your check on the answer is that it looked right.
- **The inferred limit** — the documentation says nothing, and you conclude something. *Warning sign:* a row whose answer cites no page and is not marked as an absence.
- **The interesting first** — you spiked the part you wanted to build. *Warning sign:* your cheapest disqualifier is still unrun in week three.
- **The deferred question** — feasibility is scheduled into the build, where it becomes a defect rather than a decision. *Warning sign:* a row whose instrument is *we will find out in delivery*.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The question set is submitted with the finding at [S035](/wiki/seminars/S035.md), where its outcomes are read against the charter.

**1 · Give four questions an answer format.** Rewrite each so that the answer is a number, a yes, a count or a transcript, and name the instrument.

- *Can the model handle our documents?* · *Is the integration going to be difficult?* · *Will this scale?* · *Is the data good enough?*

> **Model answers.** *On fifty documents chosen by the records team, how many exceed the deployment's supported input length — a count?* · *Does the case system expose the three operations the design needs, and is each documented — yes or no per operation, with the page and the date?* · *Is the tool the brief names published as suitable for the deployment scale the brief assumes — a documented statement, or a recorded absence?* · *Of one hundred rows drawn without filtering from last month, how many have the field the design depends on populated with something other than the default — a count?* **The test is that a person who was not there could tell from the answer alone whether the row passed.**

**2 · Settle one row by reading, and record one absence.** Run workflow steps 3 to 6 on your own set. Fill one row with a page and a date. Then find a question the documentation does not settle and record the absence in the words the instructions file requires.

> **What you should find.** That at least one row you had scheduled for an investigation is settled in minutes — usually a region, a deployment type or a modality. And that **the absence is harder to record than the answer**, because a fluent paragraph is available for every question and only the in-sentence citation requirement makes its ungroundedness visible. **If you could not produce an absence at all, check whether your instructions file reached the payload.**

**3 · Go back to your own item 3, then break it.** Find what you wrote for opening item 3 — what you learned from a successful demonstration on an example the demonstrator chose. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference.

> **Criterion.** An answer passes if it states **what the demonstration establishes and what it does not, in the same sentence, and names who chose the input.** "It shows the approach works" fails. **"It shows the approach produced an acceptable output on one case selected by the person demonstrating it; it establishes nothing about cases they did not select, and nobody has stated the sampling frame" passes.** The retrieval literature makes the general form of this available as evidence rather than as caution: **a score on the dataset a model was trained on is not evidence about a new corpus**, because quality is a property of the model and the domain jointly ([research/02-technical-foundations/thakur-beir.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/thakur-beir.md), **a snapshot whose specific rankings have since changed and whose eighteen datasets are a sample rather than a map**).
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Find the row with a documented negative answer.** Look through your set for any question whose design depends on a model checking its own output, planning without an external check, or verifying its own reasoning.

> **What you should find.** That a published position exists holding auto-regressive models cannot by themselves plan or self-verify, since self-verification is itself a reasoning task — and that this **settles a design question without a spike** ([research/02-technical-foundations/kambhampati-llms-cant-plan.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/kambhampati-llms-cant-plan.md)). **Then state the limits in the row itself**, because a documented answer taken without them is the same defect as a demonstration taken without its sampling frame: it is a **position paper rather than an experiment**, its claims are contested, and it addresses auto-regressive models as of early 2024 so the picture should be re-checked rather than assumed static.

**5 · State what your run could not have detected, before you run it.** For your one *settled by running* row, write the `could_have_detected` line and the difference the run would miss.

> **Scoring criterion.** The answer passes if it distinguishes *the run found no problem* from *the run could not have found this problem*, and if it names the case type the sample cannot cover. **A finding from a small sample that states no uncertainty overclaims**, in the direction that flatters whoever ran it ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md), **analytical rather than empirical; no number from it is used here**). If you cannot answer, write the sentence into the row. **An admitted limit is a finding; an unadmitted one is a defect.**

**6 · Route the buried finding.** Take the corpus's speaker-note passage described in the material and write it out as a row in your own question set. Then write where, in your own practice, a finding of that shape would end up and who would read it.

> **What to notice.** The row completes easily — question, instrument, negative answer, next step — which is what makes it a specimen worth having. **What does not complete easily is the routing line.** A finding that contradicts the headline claim of the document it sits inside will be filed somewhere it is technically available and practically invisible, and the mechanism is not dishonesty. **If your own answer to the routing line is "the finding document", check who reads that document and when**, because the whole of [S035](/wiki/seminars/S035.md) rests on the answer being somebody rather than somewhere.

**7 · Break one question into its clauses and find the one nobody asked.** Take the row in your set that reads most like *can we use this model*. Split it into model, region, rate and deployment type, and fill each clause separately with a page and a date or with a recorded absence.

> **What you should find.** Three answers in minutes and one that nobody in the team had thought to ask — most often the deployment type, because it looks like a configuration detail and is actually the clause that carries the residency requirement. **The published rule is the one to hold**: data at rest remains in the designated geography for every deployment type, so what the type selects is **where inference is processed**, and all types perform identical inference while billing, scale and performance differ ([research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-agent-build-and-runtime.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-agent-build-and-runtime.md), **first-party and definitional, read 2026-07-31, largely through search excerpts; several consulted pages are superseded variants that still render; every number in it is forbidden downstream**).
>
> **Two checks on your own answer.** First, if your rate clause cites only available capacity, add the lifecycle cross-check — **the capacity interfaces report on retired models**, so a good-looking rate answer can describe something you may not deploy ([research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-control-plane-and-governance.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-control-plane-and-governance.md), **definitional, mostly excerpt-read, with no published service level for a capacity request of any kind**). Second, if your `on_no` for the rate clause is *request an increase*, that is not an answer and not an instrument: **the prioritisation rule favours customers already consuming, no response time or appeal route is published, and some model families admit no increase at all.** Move it to the [S032](/wiki/seminars/S032.md) record as an acceptance with an owner. **A feasibility row whose fallback has no service level has deferred the question rather than settled it.**

## What this does not establish

Nothing here shows that structured feasibility questioning produces better decisions.

The distinction between the two classes of risk comes from a practitioner handbook that **establishes no effect and no threshold and says so.** Its warrant is diagnostic: it names plausible ways evidence becomes misleading and proposes observable behaviours in response. That is what this session teaches.

**The documented negative answer in the material is a position paper's answer, not an experiment's.** Its claims are arguments, they have been contested in the subsequent literature, it does not specify how to build a verifier for an open-ended domain, its verifier-construction step still depends on human review, and **it addresses auto-regressive models as of early 2024** — models explicitly trained for reasoning postdate it, so the picture must be re-checked rather than assumed static. **It licenses a question to ask and an instrument not to rely on, and not a prohibition on any design.**

**The generalisation evidence is a dated snapshot and is used for its shape.** Its specific rankings have since changed. Its eighteen datasets are a sample of retrieval tasks and not a map of them, so breadth is evidence of breadth and not a guarantee for any new domain. Its constituent datasets differ in the density and construction of their relevance judgements, so their per-dataset scores are not strictly comparable. Zero-shot was the chosen condition, so it says nothing about what modest in-domain adaptation would recover. **And it has itself become a target of optimisation, which weakens its own zero-shot premise over time. No figure from it appears here.**

**The measurement literature is analytical rather than empirical.** Its illustrative magnitudes rest on stylised assumptions, and it addresses comparing scores rather than choosing what to score — so a tight result around a badly chosen question is still meaningless. **No number from it appears here.**

The internal corpus supplies no support for feasibility practice at all. It contains **no cost side anywhere** — every claim in it is a gross benefit, and the cost of the investigation itself is never counted — so it cannot say whether any instrument was worth its price. It records **no failed engagement**, and it carries **not one external citation in roughly ninety documents.** Its speaker-note passage is offered as a specimen of the form and never as evidence of the claim, and it is access-controlled, so no reader of this page can check it.

**The platform documentation is first-party with a selection-guidance purpose.** Its authority is definitional — it states what the products are, not that choosing them this way works. It publishes **no adoption data, no failure-rate data, no cost comparison and no evidence that its recommended mappings produce better outcomes.** It was read through search-returned excerpts rather than full page fetches, with several comparison tables truncated, and it is **highly perishable**: a documented answer carries the date you read it or it carries nothing.

**And the instrument this session runs on has no research note in this repository at all.** The Learn MCP Server's behaviour — unauthenticated, remote, exposing documentation search and fetch — is **carried forward from [S001](/wiki/seminars/S001.md) and is unverified here.** That is a gap in this repository rather than a caveat about the tool, and it is recorded as one.

**The ordering rule in this session — cheapest disqualifier first — is design reasoning with no external warrant.**

**And the catalogue rows settle availability, never suitability.** The deployment-type material, the residency rule, the capacity interfaces and the build-shape triggers are all **definitional statements by the party that operates the platform**: they establish what exists and what is permitted, and measure nothing. **No adoption data, no failure-rate data, no benchmark and no evidence that any recommended mapping produces a better outcome appears anywhere in either note.** Both were read on one day, largely through search-returned excerpts with tables truncated, so **no list above is exhaustive.** **Every engineering constant in both — tiers, ceilings, timeouts, windows — is forbidden downstream and appears nowhere in this wiki.**

**Three silences bear directly on rows this session tells you to write.** There is **no published service level for a capacity increase** — no response time, no approval rate, no rejection-reason distribution and no appeal route — so a row whose fallback is a request has a dependency with no commitment behind it. There is **no published measure of how quickly regional availability, deployment types or capability boundaries change**, so the `read_on` date is required without any guidance on how long an answer stays good. And **nothing published states how much of an agent's behaviour a documented answer predicts**; the catalogue settles whether a thing can be deployed and says nothing about whether it will do the job. **A row that treats the first as evidence about the second has confused availability with suitability, which is the same error as treating a demonstration as a finding.**

**One more, recorded because it is the strongest teaching material in the new sources and not a caveat.** On the day these notes were read, **four first-party surfaces disagreed about one configuration file**: one formally deprecated the model it documented, one denied that the file exists in the current project shape, one still described it as the deployed artefact, and a locally installed vendor artefact still offered to author it. **All four rendered. None was wrong at the time it was written.** No amount of care in reading a single page would have caught it — **only pinning and cross-checking would**, which is why every row in this set carries a page and a date, and why an answer reused next term without re-reading is not an answer.
