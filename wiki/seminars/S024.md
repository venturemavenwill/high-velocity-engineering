# S024 · The Decision Record

**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** none

## The situation

Some months from now, an engineer who was not here will look at something in this system and ask why it was done that way.

The people who know will have moved on. What is left will be the code, which says what was done and never says what was rejected.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught.

1. Name a decision already taken in this engagement that nobody wrote down.
2. Who took it? Not which team — **who.**
3. What would have to change for it to be reversed?
4. What did you decide against, and what did that cost you?
5. Where would a new engineer look for the answer, and would they find it there?

**Item 1 is easy and item 2 is not**, and the gap between them is the session. Decisions in delivery are mostly taken by accretion: somebody tried something, it worked well enough, nobody objected, and by the time it was a decision it had no author.

## What you must produce

A **decision record** for each architecturally significant decision your requirement set has already forced, using the published field list: an ascending number and title, a date, a status from a fixed vocabulary, a context, a decision, and consequences. Stored with the code. Page read 2026-07-30.

Plus a **decision log** that indexes them: a one-sentence summary, the date, what else was considered, a short rationale, a link to the record, who decided, and the work item it relates to.

**One record in your set must be superseded by another**, and the superseding record must name what it replaced.

## What good looks like

- **The context is value-neutral.** A reader given the context alone cannot tell which way the decision went. This is the session's central requirement and it is mechanically checkable: hand the section to somebody and ask them to guess.
- **The decision sentence is a commitment**, phrased as something the team will do or has agreed to do. Not a preference, not an assessment, not a recommendation. A record whose decision section says the team *prefers* an approach has recorded an opinion.
- **The consequences include something that got worse.** Every real decision costs something. A consequences section that is a list of benefits is an advertisement, and the reader six months from now will not be able to use it.
- **The record entered review as a proposal**, before the thing was built, so that reviewing it could still change the outcome. A record written after the code is a minute, not a decision.
- **One record is superseded, and the superseding record names it.** The status vocabulary exists to be exercised. If nothing in your set ever reaches that state, you have adopted a field with no content.
- **Each record is tied to the work it came from**, so that parallel authorship does not collide and so that a reader can get from the code to the reasoning without knowing the record exists.

## How it goes wrong

- **The advocacy context** — the context section is written to make the decision look inevitable. It usually is inevitable, by then. *Warning sign:* the context contains a comparative adjective.
- **The fait accompli** — the record documents something already built, entering review with nothing at stake. *Warning sign:* the record's date is later than the first commit that depends on it.
- **The consequence-free record** — everything about the decision is good. *Warning sign:* your consequences section has no sentence beginning with a concession.
- **The unexercised status** — nothing is ever deprecated or superseded, so the vocabulary is decoration. **The published examples this specification comes from have the same defect: neither of them ever reaches the superseded state.** *Warning sign:* every record in your set is accepted.
- **The orphan decision** — the record exists, is well written, and is attached to no work item and no owner. Nobody will find it. *Warning sign:* the record is in a folder nobody has reason to open.
- **The record nobody could have disagreed with** — it documents a choice that was never genuinely open. That is not a decision record; it is a note. *Warning sign:* the alternatives section lists options nobody proposed.

## Do it

Write two records.

**One for a decision already taken.** Notice what it is like to write a value-neutral context for something you already believe. This is the harder of the two and it is the one that exposes the habit.

**One for a decision not yet taken.** Write it as a proposal, put it in review, and let somebody argue with it. This is what the specification is actually for, and it is the use most teams never make of it.

Then supersede one of them. Write the replacement record, name what it supersedes, and change the status of the original rather than editing it. **The superseded record stays.** A record you can no longer read is a record you cannot learn from, and the whole point of the apparatus is that the reasoning outlives the reasoner.

## Show it

Give the context section, alone and unlabelled, to one other person. Ask them one question: **which way did this decision go?**

If they can tell, the context is advocacy and you rewrite it. If they cannot tell but they can name the forces the decision had to balance, it is a context section and it will still be useful when nobody remembers the meeting.

## What this does not establish

Nothing shows that teams keeping decision records make better decisions, or that engineers arriving later actually read them.

**The field list is a specification from a published page read on 2026-07-30, and a specification is not a finding.** The publication offers no comparative evidence for any of its practices and does not claim to. Its pages were written across several years and it has no single currency, which is why this session cites a page and a date rather than the document.

**The specification is also not followed in its own examples**, which is taught here rather than hidden: neither published record exercises the full status vocabulary, and neither refers to a documented comparison although the parent page instructs that a data-driven decision should refer to one. **A standard that its own author does not follow is the normal condition of standards, and recognising it is the transferable skill.**

The claim that a value-neutral context is achievable is **contestable and is not established anywhere.** Writing an account of forces without revealing your conclusion may be a skill, or may be an appearance produced by careful omission, and this session's check — whether a reader can guess — cannot distinguish the two.
