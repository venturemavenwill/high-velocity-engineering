# S021 · A Requirement Is a Business Statement

**Module:** [M05](/wiki/modules/M05.md) · **Quarter:** [Q3](/wiki/quarters/Q3.md) · **Complexity class:** 2
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** Microsoft Agent Framework evaluations — a requirement that cannot be turned into an evaluator is not yet a requirement
**Whitepaper:** [WP-021](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-021.md)
**Retrieves from:** S003, S018, S020
**Entry state:** either

---

## The situation

The discovery packet from [S020](/wiki/seminars/S020.md) is finished and somebody has asked you for the requirements. They expect a numbered list by Friday.

The list you would write today would be the solution the customer described in the first meeting, in a different font.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught. You will be held to your answers at [S025](/wiki/seminars/S025.md).

1. Take the central finding of your discovery packet and write it as a single requirement sentence. **Does your sentence contain a technology?**
2. Who in the customer's organisation would recognise their own words in that sentence?
3. If the requirement were met exactly and nothing else changed, what would be different for somebody who does not work in technology?
4. Name one word that you and the customer both use and mean differently.
5. Where does this requirement live — a document, a slide deck, or the repository?

**Most people cannot answer item 4 at all**, and the ones who can usually name a technical term rather than a business one. The words that cause damage are the ordinary ones: *order*, *customer*, *complete*, *approved*, *site*, *shift*. Everybody is confident they know what those mean, which is exactly why nobody checks.

## What you must produce

Three artefacts. The first two are written together because neither is correct without the other, and the third is the test of both.

1. A **requirement set** — small — in which every requirement is stated as something the customer's business would experience, in the customer's own vocabulary sharpened, and each names the person or role who would notice if it were quietly withdrawn.
2. A **glossary** of the terms your requirements depend on, recording at least one word where you and the customer meant different things, which meaning was adopted, and who agreed to the adoption.
3. A **rubric draft** for one requirement: the criterion an evaluator would apply to decide whether a system met it, written in the vocabulary of the glossary and no other. Every word in the rubric that is not ordinary English must be a glossary term. Words you had to invent are findings, not vocabulary.

All three live as text in the repository, in the same place as the code that will implement them. **Not in a document repository, not in a deck.**

## The material

### The step from discovery to requirement is where most of the discovery is lost

A discovery tells you about a situation. A requirement commits somebody to something. The loss between them is silent, because a badly written requirement looks exactly like a well written one — same length, same register, same numbered list.

Two failures do most of the damage and they have different causes.

**The requirement restates the solution.** The customer arrived with an answer, the discovery examined the problem behind it, and the requirement document quietly returns to the answer, now phrased as an obligation. This is not laziness. It is gravity: the answer is the only thing in the room everybody can already picture, and the shape of the request — a numbered list, one line each, by Friday — cannot hold a contradiction between informants, an observation nobody reported, or the sentence *we do not yet know*. Everything the previous stage worked to preserve is inexpressible in the format the next stage asks for, so it is dropped. Not decided against. Dropped.

**The requirement is written in the wrong language.** Not the wrong jargon — the wrong *ownership* of language. A requirement written in the engineer's vocabulary can only be checked by the engineer. A requirement written in the customer's unaltered vocabulary inherits every ambiguity that vocabulary carries.

### Translation is the cost, and the cost is structural rather than lexical

The domain-modelling literature frames this as an economic problem and not as vocabulary hygiene, which is the framing that makes it actionable ([research/05-fde-craft/evans-domain-driven-design.md](/research/05-fde-craft/evans-domain-driven-design.md)). Where two languages coexist, "a few members of the team manage to become bilingual, but they become bottlenecks of information flow, and their translations are inexact." The verdict is blunt: "Translation blunts communication and makes knowledge crunching anemic."

**That applies to you with unusual force, because in a forward-deployed engagement you are the bilingual bottleneck by default.** You are the person who has sat in both rooms. The role's advantage and its characteristic failure are the same fact.

The remedy is not a bigger dictionary. It is a shared language, and the source is precise about what "shared" means. The language is **not** the customer's jargon adopted whole: specialist jargons "can't be used unadulterated because they contain ambiguities and contradictions", and the working model "will typically derive from the domain experts' own jargon but will have been 'cleaned up,' to have sharper, narrower definitions." Then the counterweight, in the next breath: "Of course, the domain experts should object if these definitions diverge from the meanings accepted in the field."

**Both halves are load-bearing, and dropping either produces a different and worse practice.** Drop the sharpening and you inherit their ambiguity. Drop the veto and you get a model they will not use — which the same source names as the thing you must never adopt, "no matter how elegant it seems." A learner who takes away only *use the customer's words* has taken the wrong half.

There is a worked failure in that source and it is the one to memorise. A syndicated-loan design was built around an object called "loan investment", which "was not a banking term. In fact, the business experts had told us a number of times that they didn't understand it. They had deferred to our software knowledge and assumed it was useful to the technical design." The experts noticed, said so, and were overruled by deference. **Deference from the customer is the failure signal, and it is granted to forward-deployed engineers by default.**

**The limits on that source.** It is a book of patterns from named, followed-up personal projects. It makes **no quantitative claim of any kind** — no productivity figure, no defect comparison, no trial — and none is attributed to it here. The local copy this repository read carries no publisher, date or edition in its metadata, so it cannot be cited by edition. And it declares two prerequisites flatly: development is iterative, and developers have a close continuing relationship with domain experts. **Without those it claims nothing**, and many engagements satisfy neither. [WP-021](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-021.md) §8 concedes that this session teaches the discipline outside the conditions its author sets for it.

### The false cognate, and why the dangerous words are ordinary

The failure inside a single shared vocabulary has a name in that literature: a **false cognate**, "when two people who are using the same term (or implemented object) think they are talking about the same thing, but really are not."

The technical terms are safe because they are obviously unfamiliar; somebody asks. The ordinary ones are dangerous precisely because they are not obviously anything. *Complete* means the form was submitted, or the work was done, or the record was reconciled at month end, and three people can use it in one meeting with three meanings and no friction at all. **A conversation about a familiar term that takes longer than it should and ends without anybody being corrected is the signature.**

The diagnostic that transfers exactly: when domain experts use vocabulary that appears nowhere in your design, that is a warning. When both you and they use a term that appears nowhere in the design, it is a doubly strong one.

### The move that converts a symptom into a requirement

The practice corpus behind this programme — the internal material of a customer-embedded engineering organisation within a large technology vendor ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md)) — teaches one move here and it is the best-argued thing in the collection. After a user states a complaint, ask why that is a problem for them, and keep asking.

It is taught as a worked dialogue rather than as a principle, and that is the right way to teach it, because the value arrives on the second and third repetition rather than the first. Its own example turns a request about the lighting into a requirement about what happens when an alert fires. **Stop when the answers stop changing, and write the requirement at that level.**

The same corpus supplies the artefact rule this session adopts: move engineering artefacts out of documents and slides into text in the repository, because that is what makes them actionable. **It evidences that nowhere.** The mechanism is legible — text can be diffed, reviewed and referenced from a decision record, and a slide cannot — and the mechanism is all there is. The corpus contains no reference, footnote, study or citation anywhere in it, and it is access-controlled, so no public reader can check the paraphrase you are reading.

### Evaluation is where a requirement stops being a wish

Here is the sentence the module turns on, and it belongs to the measurement literature rather than to this design.

The practitioner argument is that most failed products built on foundation models share one root cause — no robust evaluation system — and that the consequence is not poor quality but arrested iteration: teams can change behaviour and cannot tell whether the change helped, "which prevents them from improving their LLM products beyond a demo" ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/husain-your-ai-product-needs-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/husain-your-ai-product-needs-evals.md)). The decomposition that makes a check writable is **features** — what the system can do — and **scenarios** — the situations each feature must handle. Each scenario becomes an assertion.

The book-length treatment states the same position and names it. "Before investing time, money, and resources into building an application, it's important to understand how this application will be evaluated. I call this approach *evaluation-driven development*" ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/huyen-ai-engineering.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/huyen-ai-engineering.md)). And it concedes the objection in the author's own voice, which is why the concession is worth carrying: "focusing only on applications whose outcomes can be measured is similar to looking for the lost key under the lamppost (at night). It's easier to do, but it doesn't mean we'll find the key."

**Hold both.** A requirement you cannot write a check for is a wish. And a practice that only admits requirements it can check will build only the things that are easy to check. [S022](/wiki/seminars/S022.md) makes the first operational, and this module's unverifiable list — carried forward, never deleted — is the standing answer to the second.

The same source supplies the sharpest statement of why the rubric belongs in this session rather than the next: "Creating a clear evaluation guideline is the most important step of the evaluation pipeline. An ambiguous guideline leads to ambiguous scores that can be misleading. If you don't know what bad responses look like, you won't be able to catch them." It reports a practitioner finding alongside it that is a language problem wearing a measurement costume: **a correct response is not always a good response.** "You are a terrible fit" is correct and useless. Whether that distinction is expressible at all depends on whether the vocabulary you agreed with the customer can carry it.

**Its limits.** The practitioner essay rests on a single sustained case with no control condition, no measured before-and-after and no released dataset; its tooling recommendations are dated. The book is a practitioner text and reports most of its figures second-hand from other people's studies; it marks its own strongest claim about evaluation as a belief rather than a finding. **No magnitude from either appears here.**

### The platform makes the language test physical

The Agent Framework's evaluation model is three types: an item wrapping a whole conversation and deriving a query and a response from it, an evaluator that scores items, and a results object aggregating pass and fail counts with per-item detail ([research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-framework-and-evaluations-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-framework-and-evaluations-docs.md)). **Evaluation is a function from an interaction to a judgement, aggregated over a set.** Everything else is a detail of who does the judging.

Two things in that surface matter to a session about language.

The judged evaluators score against a rubric expressed in words, and the words are yours. A rubric is a sentence a stranger applies to your customer's business. **If you cannot write it without inventing a term, the term you invented is a hole in the glossary** — and today is much cheaper than the month when the hole is discovered by an evaluator disagreeing with a domain expert.

And the item's split strategy decides what is being scored at all, because "how you split determines *what you're evaluating*." Last turn scores the final response; full scores the whole trajectory; per-turn scores each exchange. Your requirement named a person who would notice its absence. **The split strategy is the same question asked of the machine: at what moment would the absence show?** A requirement that cannot answer it has not decided what it is about.

**Its limits, and they bound everything above.** This is first-party product documentation, read through search-returned excerpts rather than full page fetches. Many evaluators are preview; one consulted page is a superseded classic variant; surfaces differ by language. And the silence that matters most: **no agreement rate with human judgement is published for any evaluator, no calibration data, and no account of where any default threshold came from.** None is asserted anywhere in this programme.

## The workflow

**Turn one requirement into an evaluator, and find out whether your glossary can carry it.**

You will use the Agent Framework's evaluation surface. Steps 1 to 4 and steps 6 and 7 run locally with no API calls and no project; only step 5 needs a deployment, and step 5 is the least important of them.

**Step 1 — pick the requirement you are least willing to have misread.** Not the easiest one. The one whose misreading would embarrass you.

**Step 2 — write the scenario rows before you write the rubric.** Conversation-shaped, in JSONL, each row a situation the requirement must survive. Write at least one row you expect to fail. A dataset in which everything passes is a dataset written after the answer.

**Step 3 — write the rubric in glossary vocabulary only.** Then mark every term in it that is not ordinary English, and check each against the glossary. Each unmatched term is either a missing glossary entry or a word you invented for the machine's convenience.

```yaml
# eval/requirement-R03.yaml — illustrative. Names and surfaces differ by
# language and several evaluators are preview; verify against current docs.

requirement_id: R03
statement: >
  A claim reaching an adverse determination is not issued until a
  case handler has recorded a review decision against it.
noticed_by: "Claims team leader — the role that receives the escalation"

split_strategy: full        # DECISION. Last turn scores the final answer;
                            # full scores the trajectory. R03 is about the
                            # trajectory, so the default would be wrong.

rubric: >
  Fail the response if a DETERMINATION with outcome ADVERSE has been
  ISSUED without a preceding REVIEW DECISION recorded by a CASE HANDLER.

glossary_terms_used: [DETERMINATION, ADVERSE, ISSUED, REVIEW DECISION, CASE HANDLER]
glossary_terms_missing: [ ]     # anything here is today's finding
invented_for_the_machine: [ ]   # anything here is a defect, not a term
```

**Step 4 — run the rubric past the person whose words it claims to use.** Not for approval. Ask them to say it back in their own words, and write down what they said. **The difference between their sentence and yours is the ambiguity, and it belongs in the glossary with a date and a name.**

**Step 5 — if you have a deployment, run the judged evaluator over the rows and read the `reason` strings, not the scores.** The reason string is the only place you can see which meaning the judge took. A judge that passes a row for a reason you disagree with has told you more than a judge that fails one.

**Step 6 — attack your own requirement with the agent.** Give it the requirement sentence and the glossary and one instruction: *write a system that satisfies this sentence exactly and that the author would refuse to accept.* Do not give it your intentions. It will not infer the constraint you left unstated and it will not fill the gap out of professional courtesy. Record the prompt, what it produced, and what you changed. **If it could not satisfy your requirement badly, you gave it your intentions as well as your words, which is not the test.**

**Step 7 — write the two sentences that close the session.** First: which word in my requirement did the attack exploit? Second: is that word in the glossary, and was the customer asked?

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That a vocabulary disagreement has a physical consequence. Reading a requirement aloud produces agreement; handing it to an instrument that has no social reason to agree produces a specific, dated, reproducible divergence, and the divergence lands on a word. **You cannot argue with a rubric you were unable to write.**

## What good looks like

- **No requirement names a technology**, unless the technology is a constraint imposed on you rather than a choice — and where it is, it is recorded as a constraint at [S026](/wiki/seminars/S026.md) rather than smuggled in here as a requirement.
- The vocabulary is the customer's, **sharpened rather than adopted whole.** Specialist jargon carries ambiguities and contradictions a requirement cannot afford; the requirement's terms are narrower and more definite than the customer's ordinary usage.
- **The customer kept a veto over the sharpening.** Where your narrower definition diverges from what the term means in their field, they objected and you changed it or recorded the objection.
- Each requirement names a person or a role that would notice its absence. **A requirement nobody would miss is not a requirement.**
- The glossary contains a genuine disagreement, resolved, dated and attributed. A glossary of terms nobody disputed is a dictionary.
- **The rubric is written entirely in glossary vocabulary**, and every term it needed that the glossary lacked has been added or recorded as an open question.
- Every artefact is text in the repository, where it can be changed by a pull request and reviewed alongside the thing it constrains.

## How it goes wrong

- **The restated solution** — the requirement is the answer the customer arrived with, expressed as an obligation. *Warning sign:* your requirement could be satisfied by exactly one product, and you can name it.
- **The bilingual translator** — one person on the team understands both vocabularies and everything routes through them. They become a bottleneck and their translations are inexact. *Warning sign:* somebody keeps being asked what the customer meant.
- **The false cognate** — you and the customer use the same word, believe you are talking about the same thing, and are not. *Warning sign:* a conversation about a familiar term takes longer than it should and ends without anybody being corrected.
- **The imposed vocabulary** — the requirement uses a term you invented because it was tidier than theirs. The experts defer to your technical authority, do not use the word, and the model quietly stops matching the business. *Warning sign:* the domain experts have told you more than once that they do not understand a term, and you kept it because it was useful to the design.
- **The unwritable rubric** — the requirement reads well and no criterion can be stated for it. *Warning sign:* your rubric needed a word that is in neither the glossary nor ordinary English, and you added it without asking anybody.
- **The slide-resident requirement** — it exists in a deck, so it cannot be diffed, reviewed, or referenced from a decision record. *Warning sign:* somebody asks which version is current.
- **The unchosen moment** — the requirement does not say when its absence would show, so the unit of analysis is inherited rather than decided. *Warning sign:* you accepted the default split and could not say what question it answers.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed here.** Two things from this session are checked at [S025](/wiki/seminars/S025.md): that no requirement names a technology unless the technology is an owned constraint, and that the glossary records a word the two sides meant differently.

**1 · De-solutionise three requirements.** Rewrite each as something the business would experience:

- *The system must use a vector database for claim history.*
- *The assistant must have a human-in-the-loop step.*
- *We need a dashboard for the operations team.*

> **Model answers.** *A handler answering a query about a claim can see what has already been decided on it without leaving the queue.* · *A determination that goes against the claimant is not issued until a named person has recorded a decision on it.* · *The team leader can tell, at the start of a shift, which claims will breach their deadline today.* The test is mechanical: **can the sentence be satisfied by more than one architecture?** If not, you have written a design and called it a requirement.

**2 · Run the repeated why on your own sentence.** Take one requirement and ask why that is a problem, three times, writing each answer down. Then write the requirement at the level where the answers stopped changing.

> **What you should find.** The first answer restates the request. The second names a cost. The third names a constraint on the work that the first two were symptoms of. **If your third answer is the same as your first, you were asking a rhetorical question rather than a real one** — you already had the answer you wanted, and the repetitions were decoration.

**3 · Read it aloud, then break it.** Read each requirement to one person who did not write it. Speech is the cheapest modelling instrument available and awkwardness is audible before it is visible. **A requirement you cannot say without inserting an explanatory clause has a missing definition, and the clause you inserted is that definition.**

> **What to do with the finding.** Put the clause in the glossary as a candidate term, say the sentence again without it, and check whether the sentence still means what you intended. If it does not, the clause was not a definition — it was a second requirement hiding inside the first, and it needs its own line.

**4 · Go back to your own item 4, then break it.** Find what you wrote for opening item 4 — the word you and the customer mean differently. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then find a real one, from your discovery notes, and write the glossary entry properly.

> **Criterion.** A passing entry names an ordinary business word, gives both meanings in the words each side used, says which meaning was adopted for the requirement set, names the person who agreed, and carries a date. "By *customer* we mean the end user" fails on all five: one meaning, no disagreement, no adopter, no date, and no evidence anybody was asked.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**5 · Write the rubric and count the holes.** Using the workflow, write the criterion for one requirement using only glossary vocabulary, and list every term you needed that the glossary did not have.

> **What you should find.** Most learners need at least one term they had never written down, and it is usually a word the customer uses constantly. **The count is not the finding; the identity of the words is.** A term you needed for the rubric and never recorded is a term you have been resolving silently, in your own head, every time it appeared.

**6 · Choose the moment.** For one requirement, say which split strategy an evaluation of it would need, and justify the choice in one sentence without naming the tool.

> **Answer form.** *This requirement is about whether the task completed, so the unit is the whole exchange rather than the last answer* — or *this requirement is about the answer a person reads, so the last turn is the unit.* **The point is not which you chose.** It is that you chose, and that the choice is the same question as opening item 3: at what moment, and to whom, would the absence show?
>
> **Then compare.** Look back at your answer to opening item 2 — who would recognise their own words in your sentence. Is that the same person as the one who would notice its absence? If it is not, you have written a requirement in one person's language about another person's experience, which is the commonest way a set passes review and fails use.

## What this does not establish

**Nothing here shows that requirements written this way get built better, faster, or at all.** No source behind this session measures it.

The domain-modelling source this session takes its language discipline from **makes no quantitative claim of any kind** — no productivity figure, no defect comparison, no trial. Its evidence is named, followed-up personal experience across identified projects, including ones that failed. It also declares two prerequisites without which it claims nothing: that development is iterative, and that developers have continuing close access to the people who understand the domain. **Where either is missing, this session's discipline is being applied outside the conditions its source claims for it**, and you should say so rather than assume it transfers. [WP-021](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-021.md) §8 concedes the point rather than arguing around it.

**The repository rule is asserted and unevidenced.** The corpus that supplies it contains no reference, footnote, study or citation anywhere in it, and it is access-controlled, so nothing in it can be checked by a public reader. The mechanism is legible — text in a repository can be reviewed, diffed and referenced, and a slide cannot — and the mechanism is the whole of the argument.

**The evaluation material establishes a pattern rather than a result.** The practitioner essay rests on a single sustained case with no control condition and no measured improvement. The book-length treatment reports most of its evaluation figures second-hand from other people's studies and marks its own strongest claim about evaluation as a belief rather than a finding.

**The platform documentation is definitional, and its most important property is a silence.** No agreement rate with human judgement is published for any evaluator on this platform, no calibration data, and no threshold rationale. **A rubric that passes tells you a judge agreed with a sentence you wrote. It does not tell you the sentence was right.**
