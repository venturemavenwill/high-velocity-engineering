# S017 · Watching Work Instead of Asking About It

## The situation

An engineer has just told you how their deployment works. It took ninety seconds, it was clear, and it made sense.

Now ask them to do one.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught.

1. Describe how you personally get code into production at your own organisation. Time yourself. Now list everything you left out.
2. Why did you leave those things out? Choose: you forgot, you thought they were obvious, or you did not know you did them.
3. Somebody says their pipeline takes forty minutes. What would you have to watch to find out whether that is the problem?
4. You ask to observe a deployment. On the screen there is a customer's data, a credential and a colleague's message. What is your obligation?
5. Somebody proposes that instead of interviewing users, you have a model simulate them. What is your answer, and why?

**Item 1 is the demonstration.** Everybody's account is shorter than their practice, and the gap is not carelessness — it is what expertise does to the memory of a routine.

## What you must produce

A **contextual observation record** of one real piece of work, watched rather than described. It has four separated parts and the separation is the artefact:

- **Observed** — what happened, in sequence, including the parts nobody mentioned.
- **Reported** — what the person said, in their words, including their account of why.
- **Inferred** — what you concluded, marked as yours.
- **Consent and handling** — what you were permitted to see, what you recorded, what you did not record, and what happens to it afterwards.

## What good looks like

- The observed section contains **something the reported section does not**, and you noticed it during rather than afterwards.
- You checked an interpretation with the person while the context was still in front of you, and their correction is in the record.
- **The person's own words are preserved**, including terms you would have translated. A native term is evidence; a translated one is your paraphrase.
- The record separates the three things so completely that a reader can disagree with your inference without disturbing your observation.
- Consent was obtained before substantive observation and before any recording, and the record says what was excluded and why.
- **You have at least one divergence between what was told, what was taught and what was done**, and you have not resolved it by picking the account you preferred.

## How it goes wrong

- **The demonstration that becomes a tour** — the person performs a polished version for your benefit and you record the polished version. *Warning sign:* nothing went wrong during the demonstration.
- **The unchecked interpretation** — you observed something, formed a view, and wrote it down without saying it aloud to the person who could have corrected it. *Warning sign:* your record's inferences all survived.
- **The credential on the screen** — you captured, screenshotted or recorded something you were not entitled to, because it was in shot. *Warning sign:* your consent covers the person and not the screen.
- **The averaged divergence** — told, taught and done disagreed, and you resolved it by choosing one. *Warning sign:* your record contains a single account.
- **The simulated informant** — you replace the person with a model's simulation of the person. *Warning sign:* nobody involved has ever done the work you are studying.
- **Observation as ceremony** — a direct test would have answered the question faster and you ran a field study anyway. *Warning sign:* you already knew what you would see.

## Do it

Three passes over one activity, and the order matters.

**Told.** Ask the person to describe the activity. Do not interrupt. Record it.

**Taught.** Ask them to teach it to you as though you had to do it tomorrow. Teaching surfaces things describing does not, because a teacher has to include the steps a describer assumes.

**Done.** Watch them do it, with their own data, on their own machine, on a real case — ideally the most recent real case rather than a chosen one. Take the apprentice's position: they are the practitioner, you are learning. Ask about what you see rather than about what they think. **When you form a view, say it aloud and let them correct it.** The correction is worth more than the observation.

Then handle the thing the corpus does not handle. **Its discovery instrument contains no observation of any kind** — nobody is asked to demonstrate a deployment, share a screen during a pipeline run, or set up an environment while being watched. And it has **no data questions at all**: nothing about provenance, access, correctness, labelling or consent, in material that describes recording sessions and collecting telemetry.

So write the data block into your record yourself. Where did this come from, who may see it, is it correct, is it labelled, did anybody agree to this. Ask it of the person who would know, which is rarely the person who invited you.

Finally, the substitution. The same corpus proposes **using a model's simulation of a user in place of a user**, and offers no discussion of whether the simulation is valid — in a collection that opens by warning against building for an imagined user. Write down the two sentences you would say to a colleague proposing it, and the one circumstance in which you would accept it.

**This programme's position is that a simulated informant is not an informant, and that position is design reasoning with no external warrant.** Argue with it if you can.

## Show it

Hand your record to a peer with the three sections' labels removed.

**Their job is to put the labels back.** If they cannot tell your observation from your inference, the separation exists in your head and not in the artefact — and the artefact is the only part that survives you.

## What this does not establish

Nothing here shows that observation produces better requirements than interviews.

The contextual design method this session draws on was **read in full for this repository** and states plainly that its effectiveness claims rest on practitioner experience rather than comparative evidence, and that its sampling guidance is heuristic. Its mechanism-level argument is what this session uses: familiar action becomes tacit, retrospective accounts omit detail, general questions get general answers, and requests for features are shaped by the tools people already have. **Direction and mechanism only. No magnitude is asserted.**

The field-interviewing manual behind the told–taught–done sequence is a first edition; **its later edition has not been read here.** Its guidance on consent, recording and releases is a practitioner account from one jurisdiction at one time and **is not legal advice** — the observation you run will be governed by rules this programme does not know.

The refusal of the simulated informant is **an argument, not a finding.** Nobody has tested it.
