## The situation

An engineer has just told you how their deployment works. It took ninety seconds, it was clear, and it made sense.

Now ask them to do one.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught.

1. Describe how you personally get code into production at your own organisation. Time yourself. Now list everything you left out.
2. Why did you leave those things out? Choose: you forgot, you thought they were obvious, or you did not know you did them.
3. Somebody says their pipeline takes forty minutes. What would you have to watch to find out whether that is the problem?
4. You ask to observe a deployment. On the screen there is a customer's data, a credential and a colleague's message. What is your obligation?
5. Somebody proposes that instead of interviewing users, you have a model simulate them. What is your answer, and why?

**Item 1 is the demonstration.** Everybody's account is shorter than their practice, and the gap is not carelessness — it is what expertise does to the memory of a routine.

## What you must produce

A **contextual observation record** of one real piece of work, watched rather than described. It has four separated parts and the separation is the artefact:

- **Observed** — what happened, in sequence, including the parts nobody mentioned.
- **Reported** — what the person said, in their words, including their account of why.
- **Inferred** — what you concluded, marked as yours.
- **Consent and handling** — what you were permitted to see, what you recorded, what you did not record, and what happens to it afterwards.

## The material

### Expertise hides the routine from the person performing it

The reason an account is shorter than a practice has nothing to do with candour. People know their own activities intimately but do not ordinarily articulate their structure: familiar action becomes tacit, retrospective accounts omit detail, general questions invite general answers, and requests for features are shaped by the tools people already know ([research/05-fde-craft/beyer-holtzblatt-contextual-design.md](/research/05-fde-craft/beyer-holtzblatt-contextual-design.md)).

Read that list again as a list of things you cannot fix by asking better. **The steps a practitioner always takes have stopped being available to introspection**, the exceptions handled without noticing are handled without noticing, and the workaround old enough to have become the process is now the process. None of it is withheld. It is absent from the account because it is absent from the account-maker's access to their own practice.

The second consequence bites in engineering discovery specifically. A customer asking for a faster pipeline is describing their pipeline. A customer observed working reveals where the time actually goes, which is frequently not where the request pointed — and the difference is not a lie, it is a limit on what a person can see about work they do every day.

**The source's limits, and they are recorded plainly in it.** *Contextual Design* is a method text grounded in the authors' professional practice and accumulated project examples. **It offers procedural rationale and cases, not a controlled comparison demonstrating that the method outperforms alternatives**; its timings, sample sizes and sequencing advice are practitioner heuristics; and it states directly that observation is not neutral and that the method manages subjectivity rather than eliminating it. This session takes its mechanism-level argument and nothing more.

### Four principles, and each one is a prohibition

Contextual Inquiry adopts a master and apprentice stance: the person is the practitioner of their own work, the interviewer is learning. Four principles discipline it, and it is worth reading each as a thing you must not do.

**Context** — stay close to ongoing action, artefacts and specific past events rather than abstractions. Do not accept a general account when a particular one is available. **Partnership** — investigate with the person rather than interrogating them or receiving a polished tour. Do not let yourself be presented to. **Interpretation** — state emerging interpretations aloud and let the person confirm, reject or refine them. Do not record a conclusion you did not say out loud. **Focus** — use the project's concern to decide what to pursue, while treating surprises as possible corrections to that concern. Do not let your focus survive evidence against it.

The third is the one learners skip and the one that carries the method. An interpretation formed silently is your view of somebody else's work, and it hardens. **Saying it aloud costs a sentence and buys you either a confirmation or the more valuable case — a correction that reveals the distinction you were missing.**

### Told, taught, done

The three-pass sequence comes from the field-interviewing literature, which treats the interview as a platform for observation and elicitation rather than as a question-and-answer exchange ([research/05-fde-craft/portigal-interviewing-users.md](/research/05-fde-craft/portigal-interviewing-users.md)).

Asking somebody to summarise a process reveals their memory and their interpretation. Asking them to **demonstrate** it reveals steps, artefacts, interruptions and emotion. Asking them to **teach** it makes tacit detail more explicit, because a teacher has to include the steps a describer assumes their listener already has.

The order is fixed and it is not arbitrary. Told comes first because a description given after a demonstration is contaminated by the demonstration. Taught comes second because teaching surfaces assumed steps while still being cheap to arrange. Done comes last because it is the most expensive to obtain and because the first two supply the questions worth asking while you watch.

**Its limits.** This is a 2013 first edition; the later edition has not been read here. Its guidance on consent, releases, incentives and recording is a practitioner account from one jurisdiction at one time and **is not legal advice**. Its staffing, timing and scheduling figures are the author's own heuristics and are not thresholds.

### Divergence is the finding, and averaging destroys it

When telling, teaching and doing disagree, the instruction in both sources is the same: **explore the divergence rather than averaging it away.**

The reason is that the disagreement is information about which parts of the practice are visible to the practitioner. A step present in *done* and absent from *told* is a step that has gone tacit. A step present in *taught* and absent from *done* is a step the person believes is required and has stopped performing. A step present in *told* and absent from both is aspiration, or policy, or something that used to be true. Those are three different findings and choosing one account discards all three.

**This is the same discipline [S018](/wiki/seminars/S018.md) applies across people rather than within one**, and it is the module's spine. Contradiction is a missing distinction before it is an error.

### Consent is not one thing, and it is not only the person's to give

Contextual access exposes personal data, credentials, confidential records, bystanders and third-party customer information. **Consent from the scheduled participant may not authorise capture of everyone and everything in view** — that sentence is the field manual's, and it is the one most often discovered too late.

So the record's consent section has to answer four things separately: what you were permitted to see, what you recorded, what you deliberately did not record, and what happens to the record afterwards. Sketching can document a sensitive screen without reproducing private data. Notes can be constrained. Sometimes the warranted choice is no recording at all.

**This session does not know the rules that govern your observation.** They are the customer's, the jurisdiction's and your employer's, and none of the sources behind this programme is a legal authority. What the session requires is that the exclusions be recorded, so that a later reader can see the shape of what is missing.

### The artefact is evidence the account omits

Here is the bridge to the platform work, and it is the session's most transferable idea.

Every activity you observe leaves artefacts: the pipeline definition, the pull-request template, the runbook, the ticket, the rota, the exception log, the chat thread where the deployment was actually coordinated. **Those artefacts were produced by the work rather than by the account of the work**, which makes them a second, independent source with a completely different bias profile.

The contextual design method collects them for exactly this reason — artefacts are part of the evidence, alongside relations, terminology and interruptions. What is new is that you can now put them all in one searchable place in an afternoon, and doing so makes a specific class of divergence visible that no amount of watching would surface: **the step that appears in the artefacts and in nobody's account of the process.**

### The artefacts are already indexed, and only a person can open them

That afternoon got shorter again, because the vendor has now put an access surface over the material a person's work produces.

Work IQ exposes Microsoft 365 signal — email, meetings and calendar, documents in the file and collaboration stores, chat messages, people and organisational context, plans, and enterprise search results — over an agent-to-agent endpoint, a remote tool server, a request interface, and a local tool server driven from a command line ([research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-iq-intelligence-layer.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-iq-intelligence-layer.md)). Read against this session's subject, that is an index of produced artefacts with a query interface in front of it — precisely the independent second source the previous section argues for, at a fraction of the collection cost.

**And the property that makes it usable here is a restriction rather than a capability.** Authentication is Entra delegated only. The on-behalf-of flow is supported and **application-only authentication is not** — there is no way to call it except as somebody. For a security discussion that is a constraint. For an observation session **it is the feature**: consent stops being procedural and becomes structural, because a retrieval you cannot perform without a participant's identity is a retrieval that cannot happen without their participation. You may not quietly index a person's mail. The platform will not let you.

That does not discharge the consent obligation, and reading it as though it did is the failure this session most wants to prevent. **A consenting participant's identity opens material that other people wrote to them**, and their consent is not those people's consent. The record's consent-and-handling section still has four parts; delegated authentication has answered only the first.

**Two honest deflations, and both belong in the record.** First, the layer's context component is assembled from machinery that predates its name — the organisation's semantic index, the assistant's memory of explicit user actions and implicit activity patterns, and a structured-data intelligence surface. **It is a new access surface over an old index, not a new understanding of work**, and a discovery finding that treats it as the latter has attributed to the tool what belongs to the estate. Second, **the documentation contradicts itself on material points**: on whether use requires a particular licence, on the names of its own internal layers — one page is published twice, in two documentation sets, with divergent bullets immediately beneath an identical sentence — and on whether anything is stored, where one page asserts no data storage while another describes persistent working storage. **Read the tables rather than the prose, and read more than one page.** That is the same discipline this session applies to informants.

**The silences are the part to carry into the record.** **Nothing states what proportion of a tenant's mail, chat, meetings or files is actually reachable through it, what is excluded, or how a practitioner would find out** — a set relation is published and a coverage figure is not. **No indexing latency or freshness bound is published**, so an artefact that exists may not yet be retrievable and nothing tells you which. **No retrieval-quality measure exists for it at all**, and its own tool-server documentation asserts continuous evaluation measuring accuracy, latency and reliability while publishing no value for any of the three. And **no latency bound is published for a call that sits synchronously inside an agent's turn.** Every one of those is a `blind_to` entry in the sense [S006](/wiki/seminars/S006.md) taught, and an observation record reporting "no artefact found" without them has repeated that session's exact error.

**The source's limits.** Read 2026-07-31 and recorded as the newest-named material in this repository's research set — the only entry whose *names* are more perishable than its capabilities. Thirteen documentation pages and one engineering blog were fetched and read in full; several sub-pages, both linked code repositories and the terms of use were not read at all. Almost the whole surface is preview and **no service-level agreement covers any of it.** It is single-vendor throughout, with no comparison to any non-Microsoft work-graph product, and **nothing in it was deployed, measured or tested — every mechanism is as described.**

### An index is a copy, and copies do not inherit their originals' access rules

The moment you index somebody else's artefacts you have created a second copy of their content with its own permission model, and the permissions do not come with it ([research/06-microsoft-platform/azure-ai-search-and-acl-patterns.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/azure-ai-search-and-acl-patterns.md)).

The documentation is unusually clear about why this matters more for a system that reads documents into an answer than for one that lists them: a conventional search interface returning a forbidden document at least shows a link the user cannot open, whereas a retrieval-augmented system reads the contents into the generated text. **The leak is the substance, not the listing.**

Two of its cautions belong in an observation session rather than in a security lecture, because both produce a system that appears to work.

**Chunking silently discards permissions unless they are projected.** When a skillset chunks documents, the permission metadata moves from indexer field mappings to index projections, and without that projection the chunk-level references are not filtered. An unprojected chunk is an unprotected chunk, and chunking is standard practice.

**Enforcement always lags the source system.** Query-time checks evaluate the caller's claims against permission metadata already stored in the index, so a revocation in the source takes effect only after the index catches up. The documentation states this repeatedly and **publishes no latency figures**, which means the size of your revocation window is unknown and must be measured per deployment.

**Its limits.** Most of that page describes a preview API surface that changes without notice; the documentation explicitly places compliance, geographic-boundary and testing responsibility on the implementer; and it compares its four approaches on fit rather than on security strength.

### What a retrieval failure is, and why it is usually not the model

One more idea, because you will meet it the moment your index returns nothing useful. The failure taxonomy for retrieval-augmented systems locates defects by stage — corpus, retrieval, context assembly, generation — and the first of them, **missing content**, is a fact about what you indexed rather than about anything intelligent ([research/02-technical-foundations/barnett-rag-failure-taxonomy.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/barnett-rag-failure-taxonomy.md)). **Diagnose the stage before you change anything.**

The same source makes a claim this session should hold uncomfortably: that validation of such a system is only feasible during operation, because the space of real queries cannot be adequately anticipated. **It is an experience report from three deployments with no frequency data and no controlled comparison**, and the names of its seven failure points are reconstructed rather than quoted in this repository's note. Treat the taxonomy as a diagnostic vocabulary, not a measurement.

And a caution about recall from the index literature, which generalises directly to observation: approximate nearest-neighbour recall is measured against a ground truth computed by exact search over a fixed dataset, and **it says nothing about whether the nearest neighbours are the right results for a user's actual information need** ([research/02-technical-foundations/malkov-yashunin-hnsw.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/malkov-yashunin-hnsw.md)). Your index can be working perfectly and answering the wrong question. So can your observation.

## The workflow

**Index the artefacts the work produced, then use them to interrogate the account of the work.**

You will build a small Azure AI Search index over material from a single observed activity. Steps 1, 2 and 6 need no cloud resource at all, and step 6 is the one this session most wants you to have done.

**Step 1 — collect only what you were permitted to collect.** From the *done* pass, list the artefacts the activity actually touched: the pipeline definition, the pull-request template, the last three deployment tickets, the runbook, the rota, the exception log. **Write the consent line before you copy anything**, naming what you were permitted to take and what you excluded.

**Step 2 — give every document a permission field before you give it any content.** Design the index so that the access decision is a field rather than an afterthought. The generally available approach is a security filter: a string field carrying the identities entitled to see the document, applied as a query filter at search time. **The documentation calls this a workaround** relative to Entra-recognised permission filters, and it is right to: a string filter is only as trustworthy as the code that supplies the string, so the trust boundary sits in your application rather than in the service.

```jsonc
{
  "name": "observation-s017",
  "fields": [
    { "name": "id",        "type": "Edm.String", "key": true },
    { "name": "artefact",  "type": "Edm.String", "searchable": true },
    { "name": "kind",      "type": "Edm.String", "filterable": true, "facetable": true },
    { "name": "produced_by_step", "type": "Edm.String", "filterable": true },
    { "name": "observed",  "type": "Edm.Boolean", "filterable": true },
    { "name": "reported",  "type": "Edm.Boolean", "filterable": true },

    // Security trimming. Populated from the source system's ACLs at ingestion.
    // This is a STRING COMPARISON. Nothing in the service validates that the
    // caller is who this filter says they are.
    { "name": "allowed_groups", "type": "Collection(Edm.String)", "filterable": true },

    // Consent and handling travel WITH the document, not in a separate note.
    { "name": "consent_basis",  "type": "Edm.String" },
    { "name": "excluded_note",  "type": "Edm.String" },
    { "name": "captured_on",    "type": "Edm.DateTimeOffset", "filterable": true, "sortable": true }
  ]
}
```

**Step 3 — chunk something, and then check the chunks.** Split one long runbook, index the chunks, and query as an unentitled identity. **If you did not project `allowed_groups` onto every chunk, the chunks come back.** This is the single most instructive failure in the topic and it is quick to produce deliberately. Fix it, then write down how long the defect would have survived in a system nobody queried adversarially.

**Step 4 — query the index with the told account.** Take each step from the person's spoken description and search for the artefact it should have produced. Two results matter and neither is the answer you expected. A step with **no artefact** is either invisible work or a step that no longer happens. An artefact with **no step** is a step nobody mentioned — which is the tacit routine you came for.

**Step 5 — revoke something and watch the lag.** Remove an identity from the source system's group and re-run the query. **The document stays retrievable until the index catches up**, and the documentation publishes no figure for how long that is. Time it yourself. That number is a property of your deployment and of nobody else's.

**Step 6 — attack your own record.** Give the agent your observation record with the *inferred* section removed, and ask it to reconstruct what you probably concluded. Then ask it which of your observations could support a different conclusion, and which of your inferences the artefacts do not support. Record what you kept and what you struck out. **Do not ask it to improve the record** — an instrument that agreed with you was not used as one.

**A second pass: retrieve the artefact trail under the participant's own identity.**

**A warning about warrant before you start**, of the same kind [S018](/wiki/seminars/S018.md) carries. This repository's note on the work-signal layer records that its own documentation contradicts itself on licensing, on internal layer naming and on whether anything is stored. **Every endpoint, header, method name and parameter below must be verified against current documentation before you rely on any of it.** What is durable is the shape — one identity, one consent, one comparison — and not the syntax.

**Step 7 — obtain the identity before you obtain the data.** The interface is delegated-only: there is no application-only path, so the call is made as the participant or it is not made at all. Record in the consent section which of your four consent questions this answers and which three it does not. **A person consenting to your retrieval has not consented on behalf of everyone who wrote to them**, and the mail thread is full of those people.

**Step 8 — retrieve the trail around one described step.** Take a single step from the *told* pass and ask for the surrounding artefacts — the meetings, the threads, the documents — in the window in which the person says it happens.

```http
POST /a2a/
Authorization: Bearer <the PARTICIPANT's delegated token, not the application's>
A2A-Version: <the protocol version you verified today>
Content-Type: application/json

{
  "method": "<the verb you verified today, in the body rather than the path>",
  "params": {
    "query": "the release approval for the payments service, last three occurrences",
    "contextId": "s017-observation-01",
    "location": "<time-sensitive queries are documented as requiring this>"
  }
}
```

**Step 9 — write the coverage line before you write the finding.** An empty result has two readings and they are not the same object: the artefact does not exist, or the artefact is not reachable. **No coverage figure, freshness bound or indexing latency is published for this layer**, so you cannot distinguish them from the result, and the honest record says so. The sentence is *not retrieved under this identity, through this interface, on this date* — the same form [S006](/wiki/seminars/S006.md) required of a governance scan, applied to a retrieval.

**Step 10 — put the two accounts side by side and let the disagreement stand.** For each step in the *told* pass, mark whether the trail supports it, contradicts it, or is silent. For each artefact the trail returned that no pass mentioned, write the question you would ask about it. **Do not reconcile the two.** A step described and untraced, and an artefact traced and undescribed, are two different findings, and the second is the one you came for.

**Step 11 — attack the comparison.** Give the agent the two accounts and ask it to argue that every silence in the trail is a coverage gap rather than an absent step, then to argue the reverse. Require the observation that would settle each. **Record which argument you found more persuasive and why** — that record is the artefact, not the outputs.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That the artefacts and the account disagree, in named places, and that the disagreement is discoverable by query rather than by memory. It also makes two silent security failures physical: **an unprojected chunk is an unprotected chunk, and a revocation is not immediate.** Both produce a system that appears to work, which is why reading about them is not enough. And the second pass makes a third thing physical: **a retrieval that cannot be performed except as somebody puts the consent question inside the call path** — while leaving three quarters of the consent question exactly where it was.

## What good looks like

- The observed section contains **something the reported section does not**, and you noticed it during rather than afterwards.
- You checked an interpretation with the person while the context was still in front of you, and their correction is in the record.
- **The person's own words are preserved**, including terms you would have translated. A native term is evidence; a translated one is your paraphrase.
- The record separates the three things so completely that a reader can disagree with your inference without disturbing your observation.
- Consent was obtained before substantive observation and before any recording, and the record says what was excluded and why.
- **You have at least one divergence between what was told, what was taught and what was done**, and you have not resolved it by picking the account you preferred.

## How it goes wrong

- **The demonstration that becomes a tour** — the person performs a polished version for your benefit and you record the polished version. *Warning sign:* nothing went wrong during the demonstration.
- **The unchecked interpretation** — you observed something, formed a view, and wrote it down without saying it aloud to the person who could have corrected it. *Warning sign:* your record's inferences all survived.
- **The credential on the screen** — you captured, screenshotted or recorded something you were not entitled to, because it was in shot. *Warning sign:* your consent covers the person and not the screen.
- **The averaged divergence** — told, taught and done disagreed, and you resolved it by choosing one. *Warning sign:* your record contains a single account.
- **The simulated informant** — you replace the person with a model's simulation of the person. *Warning sign:* nobody involved has ever done the work you are studying.
- **Observation as ceremony** — a direct test would have answered the question faster and you ran a field study anyway. *Warning sign:* you already knew what you would see.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed here.** Two products of this session are assessed at [S020](/wiki/seminars/S020.md): that observation, report and inference remain separated throughout the packet, and that the data block exists and names its informant.

**1 · Three passes over one activity.** Told, taught, done, in that order, on one real piece of work. Then list every step that appears in one pass and not another.

> **What you should find.** Your *done* pass contains at least one step absent from your *told* pass, and the person will be surprised when you name it. **Do not resolve the divergence.** A step present in *done* and absent from *told* has gone tacit; present in *taught* and absent from *done* is believed-but-abandoned; present in *told* and absent from both is aspiration or policy. Three different findings, and choosing one account discards all three.

**2 · Label restoration.** Hand your record to a peer with the observed, reported and inferred labels removed. Their job is to put them back.

> **Scoring criterion.** Count the misclassifications and note their direction. **Inference read as observation is the failure that matters**, and it is the common one. If your peer cannot reconstruct the separation, it exists in your head rather than in the artefact — and the artefact is the only part that survives you.

**3 · Break the chunk permissions on purpose.** Using the workflow, index a document without projecting its permission field onto the chunks, then query as an identity that should not see it.

> **What you should find.** The chunks come back. **An unprojected chunk is an unprotected chunk**, and nothing in the result tells you that anything is wrong. Then fix it and write one sentence on how a team would have discovered this defect if nobody had gone looking. If your sentence is "a user would have reported it", you have described a system whose access control is enforced by its users.

**4 · Time a revocation.** Remove an entitlement in the source system and measure how long the document stays retrievable.

> **What to notice.** There is a window, the documentation publishes no figure for it, and it is a property of your deployment. **This is the difference between an access rule and an access rule that has taken effect**, and the gap is where a discovery record containing somebody's production data lives after the engagement ends.

**5 · Go back to your own item 1, then break it.** Find the description you wrote of how you personally get code into production, and the list of things you left out. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference. Then say which of the three reasons in item 2 was actually true for each omission.

> **Criterion.** A passing account names at least one step you did not know you performed until you tried to write the list — not a step you knew and thought was obvious. The distinction is the whole session: forgetting is a memory failure and can be repaired by trying harder, whereas **not knowing you do it cannot be repaired by any question**, which is why observation exists.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). **School, university and laboratory evidence; no adult-professional study in its reference list.**

**6 · Argue against the refusal.** This programme holds that a simulated informant is not an informant. Write the strongest case that a model's simulation of a user is an acceptable substitute when no real user is available, then write the one sentence of the programme's position that your case does not answer.

> **Scoring criterion.** A passing case is one an assessor cannot immediately dismiss, and the strongest version runs on cost: the practical alternative is frequently not a real informant but no informant at all. The programme's answer is narrow and you should test it. **The whole argument for observation is that practitioners cannot articulate their own tacit steps — and a model trained on articulations has access to exactly the articulated layer.** If you can defeat that, the position deserves to fall. It has no external warrant and nobody has tested it.

**7 · Retrieve the trail, then write the sentence you are entitled to.** Using the workflow's second pass, retrieve the artefacts around one step the participant described. Then write two sentences: what the trail shows, and what an empty result would have meant.

> **Answer key.** The first sentence is easy and the second is the exercise. An empty result has **two indistinguishable readings** — the artefact does not exist, or the artefact is not reachable under this identity, through this interface, on this date — and you cannot tell them apart from the result, because **no coverage statement, no freshness bound and no indexing latency is published for this layer at all.** A passing second sentence takes the form *not retrieved under this identity, through this interface, on this date*, and names at least one of those three absences as the reason.
>
> **What fails.** "There is no record of this step" fails, and it fails in exactly the way [S006](/wiki/seminars/S006.md)'s "no oversharing found" fails: an absence of observation recorded as a finding about the world. **"The tool did not find it" also fails**, because it attributes to the tool a completeness the tool does not claim.
>
> **A second criterion, on consent.** The retrieval was delegated-only, so it could not have happened without the participant. State in one clause whose consent that establishes and whose it does not. **A passing answer names the other people in the thread.**

## What this does not establish

**Nothing here shows that observation produces better requirements than interviews.**

The contextual design method this session draws on was **read in full for this repository** and states plainly that its effectiveness claims rest on practitioner experience rather than comparative evidence, and that its sampling guidance is heuristic. Its mechanism-level argument is what this session uses: familiar action becomes tacit, retrospective accounts omit detail, general questions get general answers, and requests for features are shaped by the tools people already have. **Direction and mechanism only. No magnitude is asserted.**

The field-interviewing manual behind the told–taught–done sequence is a first edition; **its later edition has not been read here.** Its guidance on consent, recording and releases is a practitioner account from one jurisdiction at one time and **is not legal advice** — the observation you run will be governed by rules this programme does not know.

**The platform material is definitional and largely preview.** The access-control documentation describes what the system does and contains no measurement of synchronisation latency, no failure-rate data and no security comparison between its four approaches. Most of the page describes a preview API surface that changes without notice, and the documentation places compliance, geographic-boundary and testing responsibility explicitly on the implementer. **Nothing in this session is compliance guidance.**

**The retrieval failure taxonomy is an experience report.** It says what can fail across three deployments; it supplies no frequency data, no severity ranking and no controlled comparison, and this repository's note records that the specific names of its failure points are reconstructed rather than quoted. Its claim that validation is only feasible during operation is a strong position to be examined rather than an axiom.

**A working index is not a correct one.** Recall in an approximate index is measured against a ground truth computed by exact search on a fixed dataset, and says nothing about whether the retrieved items answer the question that mattered. The same is true of a well-run observation.

**The refusal of the simulated informant is an argument, not a finding.** Nobody has tested it in either direction.

## Perishable content in this session

|---|---|
| A practitioner's account of a routine is shorter than the routine | The particular activity you observe, and the toolchain it runs on |

| An interpretation not checked in context is your view of their work | Present recording and screen-sharing conventions |
