# S017 · Processes, Scheduling and the Illusion of Simultaneity

**Module:** [M04](wiki/modules/M04.md) · **Quarter:** [Q2](wiki/quarters/Q2.md) · **Complexity class:** 2
**Strands:** SY · CF · PE
**Whitepaper:** [WP-017](wiki/whitepapers/WP-017.md)
**Retrieves from:** S002, S007, S016
**Platform anchor:** Foundry deployment types as scheduling classes; Global Batch's separate enqueued token quota · **MCP servers:** Azure MCP Server (read) · Microsoft Learn MCP Server

---

## What this day is for

Students believe their program runs. It does not; it runs sometimes, in intervals decided by a policy they did not choose, interleaved with work they cannot see, on a machine whose state they share.

The day's target is that **scheduling is an allocation policy, every policy optimises something at another thing's expense, and there is no neutral choice.** A scheduler that minimises average waiting time starves long jobs. One that guarantees fairness sacrifices throughput. One that prioritises interactive work degrades batch work, which is usually correct and is never free.

The professional consequence, which is the reason a systems day sits in a programme about deployed AI systems: **the person whose work is deprioritised by your policy is a person**, and the policy is a decision made about them.

The platform makes the decision unavoidable and puts a price on it. A Foundry subscription holds a quota pool; deployments draw from it; and the vendor sells a deliberately deprioritised class — Global Batch, with a twenty-four-hour target turnaround, a **separate enqueued token quota so that online workloads are not disrupted**, and a rate half that of the interactive class. **The scheduler is a product with a price list, and choosing the cheap class for someone else's work is the clearest instance of the day's argument the programme has.**

## Phase 1 · Pretest and prediction (25 min)

Five items. Items 1 to 4 concern a supplied workload of eight jobs with stated arrival times and durations.

1. Schedule them to minimise average completion time. What is it?
2. Now minimise the maximum completion time. Same question.
3. Which job is worst off under each, and by how much?
4. Predict: is there a schedule that is best on both?
5. **Copilot prediction, sealed.** You will ask the agent to build a client that runs a bulk embedding job and an interactive request path against the same Foundry quota. Write down what it will produce, and **name the one thing it will get wrong**. Opened at phase 8.

Item 3 is the day's move — every schedule has a victim — and item 4's answer is no, which most students expect to be yes.

**Warrant.** Pretesting with a construction demand. The two objectives are simple enough to optimise by hand and the conflict between them is immediate and undeniable. Item 5 is sealed before instruction, per instrumentation rule 8.

## Phase 3 · Contrasting cases and generation (45 min)

**Executed, not read.** Groups have one Foundry quota allocation, a bulk job and an interactive request stream, and they run the same mixed workload under four arrangements, measuring completion time for each class and the number of throttled requests.

| Case | Arrangement |
|---|---|
| A | One standard deployment, both classes, no separation — first-come |
| B | Two standard deployments splitting the same subscription quota, interactive given the larger share |
| C | Bulk work moved to a **Global Batch** deployment, which draws on a separate enqueued token quota |
| D | **Priority processing** — demonstrated, not operated |

They predict, for each arrangement, the completion time of each class and which class suffers most — and commit.

Then the runs happen. **Case D is not run.** Priority processing is in preview and available by invitation, and the programme does not hold one; its documentation is read against the other three and it is not assessed. Students are told this in those words, because a student who cannot distinguish what they ran from what they were shown will report both as experience.

Then **the workload changes**: the same three arrangements are run against a mix with a different shape — bulk-heavy rather than interactive-heavy — and the ranking reverses.

Task after: **state what property of the workload determined the ranking.**

**Warrant.** Contrasting cases with two adjudicators, run against a metered service. The second workload is what prevents the phase from teaching "batch everything," which is the conclusion a single workload supports and which is wrong. Case D's exclusion is itself instruction: the capability that would have won is one the room cannot have, which is a condition students will meet constantly and which no textbook scheduler chapter contains.

## Phase 4 · Consolidating instruction (50 min)

1. **The illusion.** One core runs one thing. Everything else is interleaving, and interleaving has a cost — S016's, since a context switch discards locality. On a shared model deployment the same illusion operates at a larger scale: your request is not being processed continuously, and the interleaving is with work belonging to people you will never meet.
2. **What a scheduler is choosing between:** average waiting time, maximum waiting time, throughput, fairness, responsiveness. These conflict pairwise and no policy optimises all.
3. **The four arrangements from phase 3,** with what each optimises and what each starves. Class isolation — case C's separate enqueued quota — is the strongest of the three and is bought with latency that somebody accepted. **And the taxonomy stops at the class boundary.** You choose which class work goes into; you do not choose the policy inside it, it is not published, and it cannot be inferred from outside. A student who describes a managed class as a first-come queue has made a commitment the vendor did not make. Nor is price an ordering: the priority path is documented as drawing on the same quota as the standard path and costing more, so price, quota and latency are three axes rather than one.
4. **Ageing and priority inversion,** as the standard repairs and their own costs.
5. **The workload determines the answer,** from phase 3's reversal. A policy recommendation without a workload characterisation is not a recommendation.
6. **Preemption's cost,** in S016's terms. The switch is not free and its cost is a cache cost — which on the platform is a prompt-cache cost, and is why interleaving two prefixes through one deployment is worse than either alone.
7. **The default the platform chose for you.** You allocate tokens per minute. **You do not allocate requests per minute** — assigning TPM sets the RPM limit too, at a ratio that varies by model, and quota is issued in units of capacity that carry both numbers together. A workload of many small requests and a workload of few large ones are scheduled by the same pair of limits, and you chose one of them. Every allocation you make is therefore two allocations, one of which you did not think about.
8. **Who the victim is.** Every policy deprioritises something. In a deployed system that something is somebody's work, and choosing a policy is deciding whose work waits. On this platform the decision has a receipt: the deprioritised class costs half as much, and the saving is the price of somebody else's delay.

**Warrant.** Consolidation indexed to phase 3's reversal. Point 8 is the day's professional content and is stated last so that it lands on an established technical base rather than as preamble. Point 7 is the day's perishable platform-specific point and its named default; the ratio table it rests on is expected to change and the structure — one control, two consequences — is not.

## Phase 5 · Whole-task learning task (90 min)

**Class 2. The policy is not given. This phase produces a build.**

Students use Copilot in agent mode, through the Azure MCP Server in read configuration, to ship a working two-class client against the substrate's real workload — actual jobs, actual arrival distribution, actual durations. They:

1. **Characterise the workload.** Shape, not just averages.
2. **State the objective.** Which of point 2's quantities matters here, and why.
3. **Choose a policy.** Any, including a hybrid: quota split, class separation, admission control, or a scheme not covered.
4. **Name the victim.** Which class of work is worst off, by how much, and who submits it. **Two are required: one inside your subscription and one outside it.** The inside victim you can name, measure and apologise to. The outside victim is whoever else is drawing on the shared pool when your burst raises demand across it, and you cannot name them, cannot measure their loss, and will never hear from them. Say so, in those terms, and say what you would have to be told to know you had harmed them.
5. **Predict** the three quantities from items 1 to 3 of the pretest, with numbers.
6. **Build and measure,** with the client running alone and again while the room is running theirs.
7. **Reconcile,** and state the workload change that would invalidate your choice.

**The artefact carries three further elements**, per the [Copilot Practice Standard](wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md).

- **Prompt record.** The instructions you gave, in order, including the ones you abandoned.
- **Intervention record.** Every correction you made to the agent's work. **An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that the design could have detected an intervention-worthy error — a concurrency bound that the client enforces and logs is the obvious example and is accepted.
- **Frontier statement.** One thing the agent did faster than you could have, and one thing it did badly that you had to catch.

**Fading:** step 1's characterisation is worked with the room; step 5's template is supplied.

**Warrant.** Whole-task at class 2, now shipping rather than describing. Step 4's *who submits it* clause is the day's professional demand and converts an abstract trade-off into a decision about people. Step 6's dual condition is the module's standing practice and here the loaded condition is the room, which means the contention is real and attributable.

## Phase 6 · Error clinic (40 min)

Instructor first: a substrate scheduling or prioritisation decision that was correct on aggregate metrics and made one group of users' experience materially worse — with attention to how long it took to notice and why the metrics did not show it.

Then the agent case: a Copilot-generated bulk client that was **confidently wrong and passed review**. Asked to make the job fast, it parallelised with unbounded concurrency — which is the correct answer to the question asked, is clean, is tested, and starves every interactive request sharing the allocation. Attention goes to why review did not catch it: the reviewer was reading for correctness and the code was correct. **Nothing in the diff said whose work would wait**, because the victim is not a property of the code, it is a property of the code and the allocation together.

Then students: a time you optimised an average, or a place today where the agent optimised the objective you actually stated rather than the one you meant.

**Warrant.** Error management. The instructor disclosure connects to S013's tail material and to point 8, and its detection-delay dimension seeds S068 and S079. The agent case is chosen because the failure is invisible in review of the artefact alone, which is the specific reason this class of error survives.

---

## Phase 7 · Peer articulation (30 min)

Pairs. Each student states their step 4 victim, and the partner's task is to **write that victim's complaint** — one paragraph, in the victim's voice, describing what they experience.

Then: does the student's step 2 objective still look right?

**Warrant.** Interactive-tier articulation with a perspective demand. Writing the complaint in the affected person's voice is a stronger move than naming them, and is the earliest form of the stakeholder work at S082. **The complaint is written for the outside victim**, the one neither student can identify, which makes the exercise harder and makes its difficulty the content: a complaint you cannot check against its author is the ordinary condition of a shared platform, and the discomfort of writing one is the accurate response to it.

## Phase 8 · Calibration and commitment (20 min)

- Items 3 and 4 resolved.
- Step 5's numeric predictions against measured.
- **Item 5 opened.** Sealed Copilot predictions read against what the agent actually produced, and the cohort's hit rate recorded and added to the running frontier map.
- The room's step 4 victims collected and shown as a list, unattributed. Overlaps are discussed.
- Each student names one place in their own work where they chose a priority without naming who it deprioritised.

## Assessment

Specification item in SY102. Checked elements: the workload characterisation; the stated objective; the policy choice; the named victim with magnitude and submitter; **the outside victim, with what you would have to be told to know you had harmed them**; numeric predictions; dual measurements; the reconciliation; the invalidating change; the prompt record; the intervention record; the frontier statement.

**A named victim without a magnitude and a submitter fails the specification.** **An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that the design could have detected an intervention-worthy error.

Priority processing is demonstrated rather than operated and is not assessed.

Nothing else is assessed.

## Perishable content in this day

| Durable claim | Platform instance, perishable |
|---|---|
| Scheduling objectives conflict pairwise | Interactive, batch and priority classes as sold |
| Every policy has a victim | The twenty-four-hour target turnaround and its price |
| Class isolation is bought, not free | Global Batch's separate enqueued token quota |
| The workload determines the answer | Which arrangement wins on which mix |
| One control can carry two allocations | Assigning TPM also sets RPM, at a model-dependent ratio |

The substrate's current workload shape, the deployment types on offer, their prices, their turnaround targets and the capacity-unit ratios are **perishable**. The conflicting-objectives structure, the workload-determines-answer principle, preemption's locality cost, and the victim analysis are durable and are what the specification assesses.
