# S034 · Data You Were Promised and Data You Have

**Module:** [M07](/wiki/modules/M07.md) · **Quarter:** [Q4](/wiki/quarters/Q4.md) · **Complexity class:** 3
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** none
**Whitepaper:** [WP-034](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-034.md)
**Retrieves from:** S017, S020, S033
**Entry state:** this-programme-only

## The situation

At discovery you were told the data exists. Somebody said the field is populated, the history goes back years, and an extract can be arranged.

Every clause in that sentence is a claim, none of them was checked, and the one that fails is usually the one nobody thought to state.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught.

1. Name every data dependency in your design. For each, write who told you it existed.
2. For each, write what you have personally looked at. Be precise: a schema, a sample, a document describing it, or nothing.
3. Somebody offers to send you an extract next week. What have you learned in the meantime?
4. The field you need is present in every row. Under what circumstances is that bad news?
5. The data was collected for one purpose and you intend to use it for another. Whose question is that, and have you asked them?

**Item 2 is the finding.** Most learners discover that their entire data position rests on descriptions, and that nobody on the team has seen a row.

## What you must produce

A **data reality record**, one row per dependency, with the promise and the reality in separate columns so that the gap is visible rather than reconciled.

- **Promised** — what you were told, by whom, by role, and when.
- **Held** — who actually holds it, in which system, under whose authority.
- **Inspected** — what you looked at yourself, and what you did not. Blank is a legitimate and important entry.
- **Correctness** — how you would know whether it is right, and whether anybody has ever checked.
- **Labelling** — whether the thing you need to predict, extract or verify is recorded anywhere, and by whom.
- **Permission and purpose** — who may see it, what it was collected for, and whether that purpose covers this use.
- **Consequence** — what happens to the design if this row is wrong, on the [S032](/wiki/seminars/S032.md) routing.

## What good looks like

- **At least one row is filled from something you inspected yourself**, and the record says what you were shown and what you were not.
- **Promised and inspected are never merged.** [S017](/wiki/seminars/S017.md) separated observed, reported and inferred; this record is the same separation applied to a system rather than to a person.
- **Provenance is named by role.** *The data team* is not a provenance.
- **A blocking absence is recorded as blocking**, not converted into a risk with a mitigation. If the labels do not exist, the design that needs them does not have a data problem, it has a different design.
- **The purpose question was asked of somebody who could answer it**, and their answer is quoted rather than summarised.
- **The record names what you would need to see to be confident**, in a form somebody could actually supply.

## How it goes wrong

- **The promised extract** — the whole position rests on something arriving later, and the schedule assumes it arrives correct. *Warning sign:* your data column is a date.
- **The schema mistaken for the data** — you have a field list, and you believe you have a dataset. *Warning sign:* you can name every column and no value.
- **The sample that is not a sample** — you were sent good rows. *Warning sign:* somebody else chose which rows you saw.
- **The populated field** — the column is never null because a default is written into it. *Warning sign:* completeness with no variance.
- **Consent assumed from access** — you can read it, so you conclude you may use it. *Warning sign:* your permission column contains a system name rather than a person's answer.
- **The wrong informant** — you asked the sponsor, who invited you, rather than the person who maintains the thing. *Warning sign:* your provenance and your invitation come from the same role.

## Do it

**Start from the design and work backwards.** Every claim your system makes rests on something it reads. List them before you go looking, so that the record's blanks are visible rather than absent.

**Then get something in front of you.** Not a description, not a schema, not a dashboard. The told–taught–done sequence from [S017](/wiki/seminars/S017.md) transfers directly and the third pass is the one that matters: ask the person who maintains it to show you a real case, recent rather than chosen, on their own screen. **You are looking for the rows that do not look like the description**, and there are always some.

**Then ask the correctness question of the person who would know it is wrong.** Not who owns the system — who gets the complaint. That person can usually tell you, immediately and without notice, the three ways the data lies.

**Then handle the absence the corpus leaves you.** Its discovery instrument has **no data block of any kind** — nothing about where data comes from, who may see it, whether it is correct or labelled, or whether anybody consented — in a body of work that describes recording workshops and collecting telemetry. So the block is yours to write, and the questions are yours to place. **Ask them of the maintainer, the person who receives the complaints, and whoever is accountable for the purpose the data was collected under.** Those are three people and they are rarely the same one.

**Then respect the boundary the open playbook draws and this programme does not soften.** Its privacy guidance states, for engagements of exactly this kind, that production data is not to be accessed directly. **That is a constraint on how you satisfy this session**, not an exception to it: you establish reality through a maintainer's demonstration, a permitted extract, a synthetic construction checked against described distributions, or a documented refusal — and the record says which. **A learner who cannot obtain any of the four has produced the session's most useful finding**, and it belongs in the [S035](/wiki/seminars/S035.md) submission as a fact rather than as an apology.

Finally, use an agent to attack the record. Give it your promised column and ask what would have to be true for each promise to hold, and what commonly is not. **Keep the checks you would not have thought of; discard the generic warnings**, and record the split.

## Show it

Give your record to a peer with the promised and inspected columns swapped at random in three rows.

**Their job is to find the swaps.** If they cannot, your promises and your inspections are written in the same voice, and a reader six weeks from now will not be able to tell them apart either. **That reader is the one who will build on this.**

## What this does not establish

Nothing here shows that early data inspection improves delivery.

**No source behind this session measures it.** The practice corpus's omission is verified — its instrument contains no data questions at all — but an absence in a document establishes only that the document is silent. Inferring a practice from a silence is weak, and this session is largely built on one.

The observation technique is borrowed from a contextual design method that **states plainly that its effectiveness rests on practitioner experience rather than comparative evidence**, and from a field-interviewing manual whose consent material is a practitioner account from one jurisdiction at one time. **Neither is legal advice and neither is a threshold.**

**The rules that will actually govern your access are the customer's, their regulator's and their jurisdiction's, and this programme does not know any of them.** The playbook's prohibition on direct production access is one organisation's standard, published openly, on a page this repository read in full — **and it is that organisation's rule, not a general one.** Everything in this session about permission and purpose is a prompt to ask, never an answer.

## Perishable content in this session

This stage has no platform anchor, so the second column records organisational and tooling instances rather than a version-bound service.

| Durable claim | Perishable instance |
|---|---|
| A description of data is not data | The particular systems your customer runs and what they expose |
| The person who receives the complaints knows how the data lies | Current ownership of the systems in question, which moves |
| Access is not permission and permission is not purpose | Present data-protection law and policy, none of which this session states |
| A blocking absence is a design finding, not a risk to manage | Current tolerance for synthetic substitutes in early evidence |
