# S034 · Data You Were Promised and Data You Have

**Whitepaper:** [WP-034](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-034.md)
**Retrieves from:** S017, S020, S033
**Entry state:** this-programme-only

## The situation

At discovery you were told the data exists. Somebody said the field is populated, the history goes back years, and an extract can be arranged.

Every clause in that sentence is a claim, none of them was checked, and the one that fails is usually the one nobody thought to state.

Answer these in writing before anything is taught.

1. Name every data dependency in your design. For each, write who told you it existed.
2. For each, write what you have personally looked at. Be precise: a schema, a sample, a document describing it, or nothing.
3. Somebody offers to send you an extract next week. What have you learned in the meantime?
4. The field you need is present in every row. Under what circumstances is that bad news?
5. The data was collected for one purpose and you intend to use it for another. Whose question is that, and have you asked them?

**Item 2 is the finding.** Most learners discover that their entire data position rests on descriptions, and that nobody on the team has seen a row.

## What you must produce

A **data reality record**, one row per dependency, with the promise and the reality in separate columns so that the gap is visible rather than reconciled.

- **Promised** — what you were told, by whom, by role, and when.
- **Held** — who actually holds it, in which system, under whose authority.
- **Inspected** — what you looked at yourself, and what you did not. Blank is a legitimate and important entry.
- **Correctness** — how you would know whether it is right, and whether anybody has ever checked.
- **Labelling** — whether the thing you need to predict, extract or verify is recorded anywhere, and by whom.
- **Permission and purpose** — who may see it, what it was collected for, and whether that purpose covers this use.
- **Consequence** — what happens to the design if this row is wrong, on the [S032](/wiki/seminars/S032.md) routing.

## The material

### Every clause in *the data exists* is a separate claim

"The field is populated, the history goes back years, and we can arrange an extract." Three sentences, and at least nine claims.

That the field exists. That it exists in the system you will read from rather than in a different one. That it is populated for the rows you care about rather than for the rows somebody remembers. That what populates it is what you think it is. That the history is in the same shape as the present. That the extract can be produced. That it can be produced within your bound. That somebody is permitted to give it to you. That the purpose it was collected under covers what you intend to do with it.

**Every one of those is checkable and none of them was checked.** The one that fails is usually the one nobody thought to state, and this session exists because it is nearly always a data claim rather than a model claim. **The distance between the data you were described and the data that exists is the single most common cause of a feasibility finding being wrong**, and it is a large claim made here as design reasoning with no external warrant.

The record's shape is the whole intervention. **Promised and inspected go in separate columns and are never reconciled**, because a record that merges them cannot be read six weeks later by anybody who was not there. This is [S017](/wiki/seminars/S017.md)'s separation of observed, reported and inferred, applied to a system instead of to a person.

### The published failure taxonomy for grounded systems is mostly a catalogue of data-reality gaps

When a retrieval-augmented system gives a bad answer, the instinct is to change the model or the prompt. That is the least likely place the defect lives.

The most-cited experience report on this identifies seven failure points across an indexing pipeline and a query pipeline, distilled from building three such systems in the research, education and biomedical domains ([research/02-technical-foundations/barnett-rag-failure-taxonomy.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/barnett-rag-failure-taxonomy.md)). Its diagnostic value is that it converts *the answer was wrong* into *which stage failed*, and **the first failure point is missing content — a property of the corpus and not of the model.**

The report also names why the failure surface is so large. A grounded system **inherits the limitations of both of its components**: the failure modes of information retrieval, including vocabulary mismatch, ranking failures and incomplete recall, and the failure modes of generation, including hallucination and sensitivity to how context is assembled. **The compound system has a larger failure surface than either component alone**, which is precisely why an undifferentiated complaint is useless.

Two of its stated takeaways bear directly on a feasibility session, and they are uncomfortable ones.

**"Validation of a retrieval-augmented generation system is only feasible during operation."** The space of queries, documents and interactions cannot be adequately anticipated before deployment, so pre-release testing systematically under-represents the failure distribution. **Read against this module: your data reality record is not a substitute for operating the thing, and cannot be.** What it can do is tell you whether the corpus can support the claim at all — which is a smaller question and the only one available before anything is built.

**Robustness evolves rather than being designed in at the start.** It is accumulated by observing real failures and responding, not specified into an initial architecture.

**The limits are real and the note records them.** This is an **experience report, not a study**: no controlled comparison, no frequency data, no severity data. It says what can fail, not what usually fails or what matters most. Its three case studies are specific systems in specific domains and are not a representative sample. It **predates widespread agentic retrieval and multi-hop patterns**, which introduce failure modes it does not cover. **And the specific names of the seven failure points are reconstructed rather than quoted in this repository's reading** — so this session uses the taxonomy's shape and the two takeaways, and does not enumerate the seven.

### Drift and skew are different comparisons, and confusing them wastes a week

The production-systems literature supplies the vocabulary that turns a data complaint into a locatable defect, and it is worth taking exactly rather than approximately ([research/02-technical-foundations/crowe-machine-learning-production-systems.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/crowe-machine-learning-production-systems.md)).

**Drift compares successive iterations of one source over time.** It splits into **data drift**, a change in the statistical properties of the inputs, and **concept drift**, a change in the relationship between inputs and labels, which **invalidates the mapping a system learned rather than merely moving the inputs.**

**Skew compares two different sources**, characteristically the data you developed against and the data the system will meet. It splits into **schema skew** — incompatible types — **feature skew** — different values for the same identified case — and **distribution skew**.

**The one that concerns this session is schema skew, and it is the promised-versus-actual gap by another name.** You were described a schema. Somebody sends an extract. The extract's types do not match the description, and nothing errors: a field described as a date arrives as a string in three formats, a field described as an identifier arrives with leading zeros stripped, a field described as a code arrives with a display label in it.

The same source names the defect class that this session's opening item 4 is about. **Encoding ambiguity is a data defect with the same status as a missing value**, and its example is exact: an elevation reading of zero that may mean sea level or may mean that no measurement arrived. **A field that is never null is not necessarily a field that is always populated.** It may be a field with a default written into it, and the two are indistinguishable from a schema.

One further claim from that book belongs in every data reality record. **Where one system's output is another system's input, errors propagate over time**, so a dependency on a derived field is a dependency on whatever produced it and on whatever monitors that.

**The limits.** It is **practitioner guidance from a commercial publisher, not research**: no study design, no comparison group, no sampling frame for the experiences reported, and no measurement of whether teams adopting these practices produce better outcomes. **Three of its four authors work at the vendor whose toolchain it defaults to**, so it is strong evidence about how one large organisation operates production systems and no evidence at all about comparative practice. **Every quantity in it is a property of a particular system after a particular run and does not transfer; none is reproduced here.**

### What people say their data is, and what it is

The reason your record's *promised* column is unreliable is not that anybody lied.

The field-research literature is unambiguous on the mechanism: **people know their own activities intimately and do not ordinarily articulate their structure.** Familiar action becomes tacit, retrospective accounts omit detail, general questions invite general answers, and requests stated as solutions are shaped by the tools people already have ([research/05-fde-craft/beyer-holtzblatt-contextual-design.md](/research/05-fde-craft/beyer-holtzblatt-contextual-design.md)). **A description of a dataset is a retrospective account of a familiar activity**, which is exactly the category the literature says is least reliable.

The repair is the master-and-apprentice stance and its four principles, and all four transfer to data without modification. **Context**: stay close to ongoing action, artefacts and specific past events rather than abstractions — ask to be shown a real recent case rather than a description of typical cases. **Partnership**: investigate *with* the person rather than receiving a polished tour — a curated extract is a tour. **Interpretation**: state your emerging reading and let them correct it while the context is present — *so a zero here means the reading failed?* is worth more than any schema document. **Focus**: use the project's concern to decide what to pursue, while treating surprises as possible corrections to that focus.

**The limits are stated by the source itself.** It is a **method book from a commercial publisher**, and its account of effectiveness rests on practitioner experience rather than comparative evidence. It is a second edition of a workplace method broadened to always-connected products, and nothing in it is a threshold or a legal position.

### The indexer is where the described schema meets the documents

An index is a copy, and **copies do not inherit their originals' properties unless somebody makes them.** That is true of access rules, and it is equally true of structure, completeness and meaning.

An indexer is therefore the cheapest available instrument for the gap this session is about, and it is cheap because it is honest by construction: **it will tell you how many documents it processed, how many it failed on, and why.** The failures are the finding. A described corpus of a hundred thousand documents that indexes as a much smaller number of documents plus a list of rejection reasons has just answered a feasibility question that nobody had stated.

The surrounding documentation is unusually candid about the places where properties silently fail to travel, and three of them are worth carrying ([research/06-microsoft-platform/azure-ai-search-and-acl-patterns.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/azure-ai-search-and-acl-patterns.md)).

**Chunking breaks inheritance unless it is handled deliberately.** When a skillset chunks documents, permission metadata **"move from indexer field mappings to index projections"**, and without that projection **"chunk-level references aren't filtered."** An unprojected chunk is an unprotected chunk, and chunking is standard practice. **The general form is the lesson: a transformation carries what you told it to carry and silently drops the rest.**

**Enforcement is against indexed metadata, not against the live source.** Permission changes in the source system **"are only reflected in search results after that metadata is synchronized to the index"**, and the preview terms say it more bluntly: **"a timing lag occurs before the 2026-05-01-preview recognizes changes to those access or permission restrictions."** A revocation is not immediate. **No latency figure is published, so the size of the window is unknown and must be measured per deployment.**

**Synchronisation is asymmetric in a way that traps people.** For one source, changes on items with unique permissions **"are detected and refreshed on each successful indexer run"**, while changes inherited from a parent scope **"require an explicit refresh"** through a resynchronisation or a document reset. **The inherited case is both the more common one and the one that does not self-heal.**

One more sentence from that page is worth reading closely for what it says about instrument quality. The simplest access approach — passing an identity string into a query filter — is described by the documentation itself as a **"workaround"** relative to token-recognised permission filters, because it is **"simple string comparison"** and the trust boundary therefore sits in your application rather than in the service. **A vendor naming its own generally available option a workaround is the kind of candour that should raise your estimate of everything else on the page.**

**The limits are heavy.** Most of that page describes a **preview API**; preview surfaces change without notice and its terms place responsibility for compliance and geographic boundaries on the implementer. It publishes **no latency figures, no failure-rate data and no security comparison of its four approaches against each other.** It is single-vendor, so the general principles transfer and the mechanisms do not.

### Declare the model you were promised, bind it to the data, and let the platform do the checking

An indexer tells you which **documents** failed. There is a second instrument that tells you which **promises** failed, and it is the closer match to this session's question, because a promise is what you were given at discovery and a document is only where it lands.

A **Fabric IQ ontology** is a first-class modelling item holding entity types, entity instances, properties with declared types and semantic annotations, and **typed directional relationships with attributes and cardinality rules** ([research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-iq-intelligence-layer.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-iq-intelligence-layer.md)). Underneath it sit **data bindings**, which connect those definitions to lakehouse tables, event streams and semantic models **"without copying or moving the data"**, and which carry identity keys, column mappings, schema-evolution rules, **data-quality checks on nullability, ranges and uniqueness**, and provenance.

**Read that list of what a binding carries and the exercise writes itself.** Declare the entity types and relationships the customer's own language implies — *a customer places an order*, *a shipment breaches a cold chain*, *a case has exactly one handler* — bind them to what actually exists, and **record every binding that fails on nullability, on range, on uniqueness or on a missing key.** That failure list **is** the promised-versus-actual gap, produced mechanically, with provenance attached, by a mechanism that has no stake in the answer.

The framing argument behind it is sound, and it is not vendor-specific, which is why it belongs in the material rather than in the workflow: **organisations work with data at the level of tables and schemas, which are structures built for machines rather than for meaning, while they run on business concepts** — customers, shipments, assets — **so without a semantic layer every question requires manual translation by a domain expert.** That is the case for a semantic layer stated in one paragraph, and it would be stated the same way by anybody selling one.

One piece of pragmatic advice in the same material is worth more than the feature. **An ontology can be generated from a semantic model already in production**, which means an organisation bootstraps its vocabulary from definitions it already trusts rather than convening a modelling committee that will never finish. **That is testable advice and it is cheap to test**, which is more than can be said for most of what a platform tells you about your own data.

**And then the caveat the same documentation states twice, which undercuts the vocabulary around it.** Updates in upstream sources — new rows — **"need to be manually refreshed"** before they are visible in the ontology item. **A layer described as delivering operational intelligence over live data requires a manual refresh before new rows appear in it, and no qualification is offered anywhere.** So the instrument gives you two gaps rather than one: the distance between the promised schema and the actual data, which is what you came for, and the distance between the data and the model of the data, which is a second and separate thing your design may depend on without anybody having said so.

**The limits on that note are the heaviest in this session and every one of them binds.** It is **first-party documentation read on one day**, never exercised — nothing in it was deployed, measured or tested, and every mechanism is as-described. The ontology item and most of what surrounds it are **preview**, and one whole documentation set carries a prerelease notice. **The material is the most perishable in this repository and the only entry whose names decay faster than its capabilities.** Its own pages contradict one another on how many layers exist, on licensing, on layer names, on storage and on whether actions are supported at all — **the safe conclusion is that no single page can be trusted alone.** **No scale limits are published for an ontology**: no maximum entity-type count, instance count, relationship depth or graph size. **No indexing latency or freshness bound is published for any layer.** **No coverage statement exists anywhere** — nothing states what proportion of an organisation's data is actually reachable through any of this, what is excluded, or how a practitioner would find out. And **nobody has published evidence that any of these layers outperforms a competently built plain retrieval implementation**; the one benchmark that exists deliberately does not make that comparison, and its headline figure compares the product against itself at a lower setting. **No figure from it appears anywhere in this wiki.**

### Data that is unreachable is a different failure from data that is absent

Your record asks who holds the data and in which system. **There is a third question it does not ask and should: from where the thing that reads it will be running, can it be reached at all?**

The two failures look identical at discovery and behave completely differently afterwards. **Absent data invalidates the design.** **Unreachable data invalidates the topology** — and the topology is usually somebody else's decision, made before you arrived, defended by people who are not in the room, and covered by [S026](/wiki/seminars/S026.md) and [S028](/wiki/seminars/S028.md) rather than by anything you can fix in the record.

Four published mechanisms make the distinction concrete, and they are worth knowing by shape rather than by name ([research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-agent-build-and-runtime.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-agent-build-and-runtime.md), [research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-iq-intelligence-layer.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-iq-intelligence-layer.md)).

**Agent compute has a network position, and it is not yours.** Containerised agents run in a delegated subnet with their own interface and their own outbound address, and tool traffic routes through a per-project proxy. **Private endpoints to the connected data resources are not created for you**, which is stated plainly and is the single most common way a promised system turns out to be unreachable rather than absent.

**Authentication mode can be foreclosed by the network choice.** On a private virtual network the search connection **must** use a managed identity, because **key-based authentication is not supported**. A design that assumed a key is not slightly wrong; it does not run.

**Some data surfaces do not support network isolation at all.** The work-data tool is documented as **not supporting virtual-network integration**, and additionally requires tenant-level administrator consent for its permission plus a per-user licence **for every user whose request reaches it.** **Three of those are owned by somebody who is not in the room**, and none of them is a data question in the sense your record currently means.

**And one is reported rather than verified, and is recorded that way.** A first-party artefact states that the platform's own tooling **cannot reach a network-isolated project even from inside the network** — so the thing that provisions the isolation may not be able to operate what it provisioned. **That claim comes from a locally installed vendor artefact rather than from published documentation and was not confirmed against any page.** Treat it as a hypothesis a practitioner can test in an afternoon, not as a fact.

There is a fifth item and it is the one most likely to bite, because the record has a column for it already. **The same family's pages disagree with each other about licensing**: one states that access is independent of a per-user licence and billed by usage, and two others state that the licence is required. **A constraint the vendor's own documentation contradicts itself about is a constraint you confirm with a person, not with a page** — and the person is not the one who invited you.

**Finally, reachable is not permitted.** Where the retrieval layer is a managed one, document-level access controls are documented as **not automatically honoured** unless the knowledge source explicitly supports them **and** somebody configured the synchronisation. **A row that records reachability and stops has recorded two of the three things it needs**, and the third is the one workflow step 8 measures.

**The limits are as above and one more.** These are **definitional statements about network and identity behaviour, read on one day, largely through search-returned excerpts**, in a family where preview status is pervasive and several consulted pages are superseded variants that still render. **No latency figure, no failure-rate figure and no measure of permission-trimming completeness is published anywhere in either note**, so every mechanism above is a thing to check rather than a thing to quote.

### The absence the corpus leaves you, and the boundary the playbook draws

The internal practice corpus contains a customer discovery instrument, and **it has no data block of any kind** — nothing about where data comes from, who may see it, whether it is correct or labelled, or whether anybody consented — in a body of work that describes recording workshops and collecting telemetry ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md)). It also names, as an antipattern, a workshop whose labs run on sample data rather than the customer's own scenarios — **which is the right instinct with no instrument attached to it.**

**So the block is yours to write and the questions are yours to place.** Ask them of the maintainer, of the person who receives the complaints, and of whoever is accountable for the purpose the data was collected under. **Those are three people and they are rarely the same one.**

And respect the boundary the open playbook draws, which this programme does not soften. Its privacy guidance states, for engagements of exactly this kind, that **production data is not to be accessed directly.** That is a constraint on how you satisfy this session rather than an exception to it: you establish reality through a maintainer's demonstration, a permitted extract, a synthetic construction checked against described distributions, or a documented refusal — **and the record says which.** A learner who can obtain none of the four has produced the session's most useful finding.

**Both sources carry their limits into every use.** The corpus is access-controlled, paraphrased and never quoted, records no failed engagement, contains no cost side of any kind, and carries not one external citation in roughly ninety documents. The playbook is published openly, offers **no comparative evidence for any of its practices**, contradicts itself in at least a dozen places without noticing, and asserts four unsupported magnitude claims which this repository records as specimens and reproduces nowhere.

## The workflow

**Point an indexer at whatever you are permitted to hold, and read its failures. The gap between the described schema and the documents that actually index is the session's finding.**

**Step 1 — list the dependencies before you go looking.** Every claim your design makes rests on something it reads. Write them all down first, so that the record's blanks are visible rather than absent.

**Step 2 — get something real in front of you, from the person who maintains it.** Not a description, not a schema, not a dashboard. Ask the maintainer to show you a recent case on their own screen. **You are looking for the rows that do not look like the description, and there are always some.** [S017](/wiki/seminars/S017.md)'s told–taught–done sequence transfers exactly and the third pass is the one that matters.

**Step 3 — ask the correctness question of the person who gets the complaint.** Not who owns the system. That person can usually tell you, immediately and without notice, the three ways the data lies.

**Step 4 — establish what you are permitted to index, and record which of the four routes you took.** A maintainer's demonstration, a permitted extract, a synthetic construction checked against described distributions, or a documented refusal. **The playbook's rule is that production data is not accessed directly, and a documented refusal is a pass.**

**Step 5 — define the index against the schema you were *described*, deliberately.** Do not correct it first. The point is to find where the description and the documents disagree, and you cannot find that if you have already fixed it.

```json
{
  "name": "reality-check-indexer",
  "dataSourceName": "permitted-extract",
  "targetIndexName": "reality-check",
  "fieldMappings": [
    { "sourceFieldName": "case_id",      "targetFieldName": "id" },
    { "sourceFieldName": "closed_date",  "targetFieldName": "closedDate" },
    { "sourceFieldName": "outcome_code", "targetFieldName": "outcome" },
    { "sourceFieldName": "handler",      "targetFieldName": "handler" }
  ],
  "parameters": {
    "maxFailedItems": -1,
    "maxFailedItemsPerBatch": -1,
    "configuration": { "failOnUnsupportedContentType": false }
  }
}
```

**The two `-1` values are the whole instrument.** They tell the indexer not to stop on failures, so that instead of a run that aborts on the first bad document you get a complete inventory of everything that could not be processed and why. **Set them to zero and you will learn that your data has a problem. Set them to minus one and you will learn what the problem is, how much of the corpus it affects, and whether it is one defect or several.**

**Step 6 — run it and read the execution status as the finding.** Documents processed. Documents failed. The error per failed document. **Put those three numbers and the reason list into the *inspected* column of the record.** Then compare against the description: is `closed_date` a date in every row, is `outcome_code` ever a display label, does `handler` ever hold a team rather than a person, and is `case_id` unique.

**Step 7 — chunk something and watch what does not travel.** Add a skillset that splits documents, and observe that document-level fields do not reach the chunks unless they are projected onto them. **The permission case is the documented one and the general case is the lesson**: a transformation carries what you told it to carry and drops the rest silently.

**Step 8 — change something at the source and time how long the index disagrees with it.** Remove or alter an entitlement, or edit a document, and re-query. **No latency figure is published, so the window is unknown until you measure it.** Put your measurement in the record with the date. **This is the row that will be wrong first when the design goes to operations.**

**Step 9 — attack your own record with the instrument, at a named target.** Do not ask the agent to review the record. Give it your *promised* column and ask **what would have to be true for each promise to hold, and what commonly is not** — then give it your indexer's failure reasons and ask **which of your promised rows those failures already contradict.** Keep the checks you would not have thought of; discard the generic warnings; record the split.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That a described corpus and an indexed corpus are different sizes, and that the difference has reasons you can read. **The failure list is the fastest data-reality instrument available to a forward-deployed engineer**, it costs an afternoon, and almost nobody runs it before committing to a design. You will also watch a property fail to survive a transformation, which is the general shape of every silent data defect in this session.

**The promise, bound to the data; and the data you cannot reach from where you are standing.**

The run above compares a described **schema** against actual **documents**. The two below compare a described **model** against actual **data**, and then ask a question the record does not currently have a column for.

**Step A — write the customer's own sentences down as entity types and relationships, before you look at anything.** Use their nouns and their verbs, not the system's. *A case has exactly one handler. A shipment belongs to one consignment. A closed case has an outcome.* **Each of those is a promise**, and each is about to be checked by something with no stake in the answer.

**Step B — bind them, and let the data-quality checks be the finding.** A binding carries identity keys, column mappings, schema-evolution rules and checks on nullability, ranges and uniqueness, without copying the data. **Configure the checks to match what you were told, not what you suspect** — the same discipline as defining the index against the described schema in step 5.

```yaml
# docs/data-reality/ontology-bindings.yaml
# Written from the CUSTOMER'S sentences, before anything was inspected.
# Every constraint below is a PROMISE somebody made at discovery.

entity_types:
  - name: Case
    properties:
      - { name: case_id,     type: string,   unique: true,  nullable: false }   # "case ids are unique"
      - { name: closed_date, type: datetime,                nullable: true  }   # "populated once closed"
      - { name: outcome,     type: string,    nullable: false, allowed_from: outcome_codes }
  - name: Handler
    properties:
      - { name: handler_id,  type: string,   unique: true,  nullable: false }

relationships:
  - { from: Case, verb: handled_by, to: Handler, cardinality: exactly_one }      # "one handler per case"

bindings:
  - entity: Case
    source: "<lakehouse table>"
    identity_key: case_id
    column_mappings: { case_id: "…", closed_date: "…", outcome: "…" }
    provenance: "who told you this, by role, and when"

# THE FINDING IS THE FAILURE LIST, NOT THE MODEL.
# Record every binding that fails on nullability, range, uniqueness or a missing
# key, and put it in the record's *inspected* column against the *promised* one.
failed_checks: []

# SECOND, SEPARATE GAP — do not merge it with the first.
# Upstream changes need a MANUAL REFRESH before they are visible here. So there
# is the distance between promise and data, and the distance between data and
# the model of the data. Record which one your design depends on.
refresh:
  last_refreshed: ""
  design_depends_on_freshness: ""     # yes/no, and what breaks if it is stale
```

**Step C — add a reachability column to the record and fill it from the agent's position, not from yours.** For every dependency ask: **from where the thing that reads this will run, can it be reached at all?** Record the network position, whether private endpoints to the data resources exist — **they are not created for you** — whether the authentication mode you assumed is available on that topology, and whether any surface in the design refuses network isolation outright. **Mark each answer's owner by role.** Most of them will not be the data owner, and that is the finding.

**Step D — confirm one constraint with a person because the pages disagree.** Pick the licensing or consent constraint on whichever work-data surface your design touches, find the disagreement between the vendor's own pages, and take it to somebody who can answer. **Record the answer, the role and the date in the record's *permission and purpose* column.** A constraint the documentation contradicts itself about is not a documented answer, and treating it as one is the [S033](/wiki/seminars/S033.md) error arriving in a data record.

**Step E — attack the reachability column, not the record.** Give the agent your bindings, your failure list and your reachability column, and ask it in order for **the promise you wrote as a constraint that the data cannot express**, then **the dependency whose reachability answer assumes a network position nobody has confirmed**, then **which of your rows would change if the design's compute moved.** Keep what beats your reasoning; discard the generic; record the split.

**What this second half makes observable.** That **absent and unreachable are two different findings that a discovery conversation cannot tell apart**, and that only one of them is yours to fix. And that a promise expressed as a constraint either survives contact with the data or produces a named, dated, provenanced failure — **which is the same instrument as the indexer's failure list, pointed at the sentence rather than at the document.**

## What good looks like

- **At least one row is filled from something you inspected yourself**, and the record says what you were shown and what you were not.
- **Promised and inspected are never merged.** [S017](/wiki/seminars/S017.md) separated observed, reported and inferred; this record is the same separation applied to a system rather than to a person.
- **Provenance is named by role.** *The data team* is not a provenance.
- **The record says which of the four routes established each row** — a maintainer's demonstration, a permitted extract, a synthetic construction, or a documented refusal.
- **A blocking absence is recorded as blocking**, not converted into a risk with a mitigation. If the labels do not exist, the design that needs them does not have a data problem, it has a different design.
- **The purpose question was asked of somebody who could answer it**, and their answer is quoted rather than summarised.
- **The record names what you would need to see to be confident**, in a form somebody could actually supply.

## How it goes wrong

- **The promised extract** — the whole position rests on something arriving later, and the schedule assumes it arrives correct. *Warning sign:* your data column is a date.
- **The schema mistaken for the data** — you have a field list, and you believe you have a dataset. *Warning sign:* you can name every column and no value.
- **The sample that is not a sample** — you were sent good rows. *Warning sign:* somebody else chose which rows you saw.
- **The populated field** — the column is never null because a default is written into it. *Warning sign:* completeness with no variance.
- **The run that reported one defect** — the instrument stopped at the first failure, so you know there is a problem and not what it is. *Warning sign:* your failure list has one entry.
- **Consent assumed from access** — you can read it, so you conclude you may use it. *Warning sign:* your permission column contains a system name rather than a person's answer.
- **The wrong informant** — you asked the sponsor, who invited you, rather than the person who maintains the thing. *Warning sign:* your provenance and your invitation come from the same role.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The data reality record is submitted with the finding at [S035](/wiki/seminars/S035.md), where its blocking absences must survive unsmoothed.

**1 · Decompose one sentence into its claims.** Take *"the field is populated, the history goes back years, and we can arrange an extract"* and write out every separate claim in it. Then mark each one *inspected*, *reported* or *inferred*.

> **Model answer.** At least nine claims: the field exists; it exists in the system you will read; it is populated for the rows in scope; what populates it is what you think; the history is in the same shape as the present; an extract can be produced; it can be produced within your bound; somebody is permitted to release it; the collection purpose covers your use. **Most learners find every one marked *reported*.** That is the finding, and it is the same finding as opening item 2.

**2 · Read the failure list.** Run workflow steps 5 and 6. Record the count of documents that failed and the distinct reasons.

> **What you should find.** That the reasons cluster, and that at least one cluster contradicts something in your *promised* column — a type that is not what it was described as, an encoding that varies by era, a document class nobody mentioned. **If the run reported no failures at all, check `maxFailedItems`**: an indexer configured to stop on the first failure reports one defect and hides the rest, which is the difference between knowing there is a problem and knowing what it is.

**3 · Go back to your own item 2, then break it.** Find what you wrote for opening item 2 — what you have personally looked at for each dependency. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference.

> **Criterion.** An entry passes only if it names **an artefact, a date and who chose what you saw.** "I have seen the schema" fails — a schema is a description, and this session is about the distance between descriptions and documents. **"On 4 September the records maintainer showed me eleven closed cases from the previous week on her own screen, chosen by scrolling rather than by search; four of them had a display label in the outcome field" passes**, because a reader can tell what was observed, who selected it, and what the observation cost in trust. **Blank is a legitimate and important entry**; a vague entry is not, because it reads like an inspection six weeks later.
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Classify six incidents.** For each, say whether it is data drift, concept drift, schema skew, feature skew, distribution skew, or none of these — and name the instrument that would detect it.

- *The extract's date field arrives as a string in three formats.* · *Cases from before a system migration use a different outcome vocabulary.* · *The team's own sample has twice the proportion of complex cases as the live queue.* · *A field is never null because a default is written into it.* · *What counts as a satisfactory outcome changed when the policy changed.* · *A derived field is wrong because the system that produces it is wrong.*

> **Answers.** Schema skew · feature skew, unless the vocabulary change also changed what the label means, in which case concept drift · distribution skew · **none of these — it is an encoding-ambiguity defect, and it is the one this list exists to catch**, because it produces completeness with no variance and every skew test passes · concept drift, which invalidates a learned mapping rather than moving the inputs · none of these — it is upstream propagation, and the instrument is monitoring the producer rather than the consumer ([research/02-technical-foundations/crowe-machine-learning-production-systems.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/crowe-machine-learning-production-systems.md), **practitioner guidance rather than research; no comparison group; every quantity in it is a property of a particular system and none transfers**).

**5 · Watch a property fail to survive a transformation.** Run workflow step 7. Report what reached the chunks and what did not.

> **What to notice.** That the documentation states this directly for permission metadata — the fields **"move from indexer field mappings to index projections"**, and without the projection **"chunk-level references aren't filtered"** — and that **nothing in the output announces the loss.** The general form is the one to carry: a transformation carries what it was told to carry. **The reason this belongs in a data-reality session rather than a security one is that the same silence applies to provenance, to freshness and to anything else you were relying on travelling.**

**6 · Measure the window nobody publishes.** Run workflow step 8 and record how long the index disagreed with the source.

> **Scoring criterion.** The answer passes if it states a measurement, a date and a configuration — not a reassurance. **The documentation states that a timing lag occurs and publishes no figure for it**, so the size of the window is unknown until somebody measures it, and the person who measures it is you ([research/06-microsoft-platform/azure-ai-search-and-acl-patterns.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/azure-ai-search-and-acl-patterns.md), **most of which describes a preview API whose surface changes without notice**). **A row in your record that says "revocation is immediate" is a promise you have adopted from nobody.**

**7 · Write the data block the corpus does not have.** Draft the questions you would add to a discovery instrument that currently has none, and name which of three people you would ask each one.

> **What to notice.** That provenance, correctness, labelling, permission and purpose are answered by **different people**, and that the person who invited you is rarely any of them. **If your provenance and your invitation come from the same role, you have one source and a schema.** The corpus's own antipattern list warns against labs running on sample data rather than the customer's own scenarios — **the right instinct with no instrument attached**, and this exercise is the instrument.

**8 · Sort ten dependencies into absent, unreachable and unpermitted.** Take your record and mark each row with which of the three it would fail on if the design shipped tomorrow. Where you cannot tell, mark it *unknown* and name the single question that would decide it.

> **What you should find.** That the three failures are indistinguishable in your *promised* column and completely different in consequence. **Absent invalidates the design** — the labels do not exist, so a different design is needed. **Unreachable invalidates the topology**, which is somebody else's decision made before you arrived and belongs at [S026](/wiki/seminars/S026.md) or [S028](/wiki/seminars/S028.md) rather than in a mitigation. **Unpermitted invalidates nothing visible at all**, which is why it is the dangerous one: a document that is reachable and not permitted returns an answer, and the system looks like it works ([research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-iq-intelligence-layer.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-iq-intelligence-layer.md), which records that document-level access controls are **not automatically honoured** unless the source states support and somebody configured synchronisation — **first-party, read 2026-07-31, preview throughout, with its own pages contradicting one another on licensing, layer names, storage and topology, and no measure of permission-trimming completeness published by anyone**).
>
> **Two things to check in your own answer.** First, a row marked *unreachable* must name **whose decision the topology was**, by role — without that it is a complaint rather than a finding. Second, a row marked *unknown* must name **one question**, not a workstream. **"We need to understand the network" fails. "Does a private endpoint to the case store exist from the subnet the agent will run in, and who would create it if not" passes**, because private endpoints to connected data resources are documented as **not created for you** and somebody specific has to make one ([research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-agent-build-and-runtime.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-agent-build-and-runtime.md), **read 2026-07-31, largely through search excerpts, with preview status pervasive and several consulted pages superseded**).

## What this does not establish

Nothing here shows that early data inspection improves delivery.

**No source behind this session measures it.** The practice corpus's omission is verified — its discovery instrument contains no data questions at all — but an absence in a document establishes only that the document is silent. **Inferring a practice from a silence is weak, and this session is largely built on one.** The corpus is access-controlled, contains no cost side of any kind, records no failed engagement, and carries not one external citation in roughly ninety documents.

**The failure taxonomy is an experience report, not a study.** It offers no controlled comparison, no frequency data and no severity data, so it says what can fail and not what usually fails or what matters most. Its three case studies are specific systems in specific domains and are not a representative sample. It predates widespread agentic retrieval and multi-hop patterns. **And the names of its seven failure points are reconstructed rather than quoted in this repository's reading**, which is why this session uses its shape and its two takeaways and does not enumerate them. Its strong claim that validation is only feasible during operation should be examined rather than adopted, because it has real consequences for release governance in regulated settings.

**The production-systems vocabulary comes from practitioner guidance from a commercial publisher, not from research.** No study design, no comparison group, no sampling frame, and no measurement of whether teams adopting these practices do better. Three of its four authors work at the vendor whose toolchain it defaults to, so it warrants what its authors do and not that it beats what they do not discuss. **Every quantity in it is a property of a particular system after a particular run; none transfers and none is reproduced here.**

The observation technique is borrowed from a contextual design method that **rests its account of effectiveness on practitioner experience rather than comparative evidence**, and from a field-interviewing manual whose consent material is a practitioner account from one jurisdiction at one time. **Neither is legal advice and neither is a threshold.**

**The platform page is mostly preview API surface.** Preview surfaces change without notice, and its own terms place responsibility for compliance, geographic boundaries, testing and mitigations on the implementer. It publishes **no latency figure for permission synchronisation**, so the revocation window is unknown and must be measured per deployment. It publishes **no failure-rate data** and **no security comparison of its four approaches against each other**. It is single-vendor: the principles transfer and the mechanisms do not.

**The rules that will actually govern your access are the customer's, their regulator's and their jurisdiction's, and this programme does not know any of them.** The playbook's prohibition on direct production access is one organisation's standard, published openly, on a page this repository read in full — **and it is that organisation's rule, not a general one.** That publication also offers **no comparative evidence for any of its practices**, contradicts itself in at least a dozen places without noticing, and asserts four unsupported magnitude claims which this repository records as specimens and reproduces nowhere. **Everything in this session about permission and purpose is a prompt to ask, never an answer.**

**And the largest claim in the session is the least warranted.** That the promised-versus-actual gap is the most common cause of a wrong feasibility finding is **design reasoning with no external warrant.** It is held because the failure taxonomy's first failure point is a corpus property, because the production-systems literature treats silent data defects as the characteristic failure of data systems, and because the corpus that describes this work asks no data questions at all — three converging absences, which is an argument and not a measurement.

**The ontology instrument establishes that a promise failed and never that the data is fit.** A binding check tests nullability, range, uniqueness and key presence. **It cannot test whether the value means what somebody said it means**, which is the defect class this session spends most of its material on — an outcome code with a display label in it satisfies every constraint you can declare. **A clean binding run is an absence of a finding, not a positive one**, on the same rule as a clean indexer run.

**The material behind it is preview throughout, unexercised, and contradicts itself.** It was **read as documentation and never run**: nothing in it was deployed, measured or tested, and every mechanism is as-described. One documentation set carries a prerelease notice; the ontology item is preview; **the naming is younger than the products underneath it**, and the vendor's own pages disagree about how many layers exist, about licensing, about layer names, about storage and about whether actions are supported. **The safe conclusion is that no single page can be trusted alone, not that the product is broken.** **No scale limits are published for an ontology** — no entity-type ceiling, instance ceiling, relationship depth or graph size. **No indexing latency or freshness bound is published for any layer**, and the ontology's own page states that upstream changes need a manual refresh, which is a stated behaviour rather than a bound. **No coverage statement exists anywhere**: nothing says what proportion of an organisation's data is reachable, what is excluded, or how a practitioner would find out. **And no evidence has been published by anyone that any of these layers outperforms a competently built plain retrieval implementation** — the single benchmark that exists deliberately does not make that comparison, and its headline figure compares the product against itself at a lower setting. **No figure from it appears anywhere in this wiki.**

**The reachability material is mechanism-only and one of its claims is unverified.** The network position of agent compute, the non-creation of private endpoints to connected resources, the unavailability of key-based authentication on a private network, and the absence of network-isolation support on one work-data surface are all **first-party statements read on one day, largely through search-returned excerpts**, in a family where preview status is pervasive. **The claim that the platform's own tooling cannot reach a network-isolated project even from inside the network comes from a locally installed vendor artefact rather than from any published page, and was not confirmed.** It is recorded as reported. **No latency figure, no failure-rate figure and no measure of permission-trimming completeness is published anywhere in this material**, so every mechanism above is something to check rather than something to quote.
