## The situation

The internal corpus behind this stage contains one sentence about generated code that is sharper than anything in its own promotional material, and this session is built on it.

**Generated code accumulates comprehension debt: it exists, it passes, and nobody understands it.**

That is the whole hazard, and every clause is doing work. *It exists* — so it cannot be ignored. *It passes* — so nothing will flag it. *Nobody understands it* — so the first person who needs to change it starts from zero, and that person is usually somebody with an incident open.

The debt is not contracted at the moment of generation. **It is contracted at the moment of acceptance**, and acceptance in current tooling is a small, frequent, dismissible act. The vendor's own documentation for the agent mode this programme anchors on records that the agent determines its own steps and iterates rather than stopping after one response, and that it requests confirmation before running a terminal command or a tool that is not built in — with that confirmation being **a dismissible default rather than a boundary**, auto-confirmable per session, per solution, or permanently. This repository's reading of that documentation was made on 2026-07-30 and **has not been re-read for this session**; treat every detail of it as due for verification before the session runs.

**Permanently is the word to sit with.** It is a supported, reasonable, well-designed option, and it is also the mechanism by which a team stops reading, one dismissal at a time.

Answer these before instruction.

1. Point at a line in your increment and say why it is that line and not a different one.
2. Which parts of your increment would you be unable to change safely?
3. What did you auto-confirm this week, and when did you stop reading the confirmations?
4. If this broke while you were unreachable, who could fix it?
5. Who pays for the parts nobody can explain, and when do they pay?

**Item 1 is the session and it is harder than it looks**, because the honest answer for most working code is *because that is what came out and the tests went green.*

## What you must produce

A **comprehension audit** of your own increment, region by region. Nothing is exempt, including the parts you wrote yourself.

Each region is classified as one of three things, and the classification is the artefact.

- **Explained** — you can say what it does and why it is written this way rather than another way.
- **Operational** — you can say what it does, and you cannot say why it takes the form it takes.
- **Opaque** — you cannot say what it does without reading it again from scratch, and possibly not then.

For every region that is not *explained*: what it would cost to move it up a class, who would have to do it, and what you are doing instead in the meantime.

The audit closes with a **confirmation record**: what your executor asked permission for during this increment, what you granted, and what is now standing permission.

## What good looks like

- **The audit has entries in all three classes.** An increment that is entirely explained has been audited by its author's optimism.
- **The opaque regions are located precisely**, by file and function, not by feeling.
- **The remediation cost is stated per region**, and at least one is declared not worth paying with the reason.
- **The confirmation record names standing permissions**, including any granted before this session and forgotten.
- **A test passing over a region is not recorded as comprehension.** The audit distinguishes *this is verified* from *this is understood*.
- **A comment generated alongside the code is not recorded as an explanation.** An account written by the thing being accounted for is another output, requiring the same scrutiny.

## How it goes wrong

- **The passing unknown** — the region is green, therefore it is fine, therefore it is not audited. *Warning sign:* your audit classes correlate with your test coverage.
- **The permanent confirmation** — permission was granted once, months ago, for a class of action nobody now remembers. *Warning sign:* you cannot reconstruct what you have already agreed to.
- **The explained-by-comment** — the code is annotated, by the same process that produced it, and the annotation is treated as understanding. *Warning sign:* your explanation uses the comment's own vocabulary.
- **The debt declared paid by a test** — a check exists, so the region is reclassified as explained. *Warning sign:* you could not change the region without running the check to find out what you broke.
- **The audit that finds nothing** — everything is explained because the standard for *explained* was set after the audit. *Warning sign:* item 1 took you no time.
- **The debt attributed to the machine** — the opaque regions are all generated, none is yours, and the audit becomes an argument about tooling. *Warning sign:* your own code is exempt.

## Do it

**First, do item 1 in front of somebody.** Pick a region at random — genuinely at random, not the region you are proud of — and explain it aloud. **The discomfort is the measurement.**

**Second, reconstruct the confirmation record before you can remember it accurately.** Look at what standing permissions exist rather than at what you remember granting. This is the part of the audit that is verifiable, and it is the part learners assume they already know.

**Third, price one region properly.** Take an opaque region and establish what it would actually take to move it to *explained*: reading time, the person who would have to do it, what else they would not do that day. **The point is not to pay the debt. The point is to find out that it has a price**, because a debt with no stated price is one that never appears in a plan.

**Fourth, notice what the vendor does not publish.** The documentation for the agent mode above **publishes no measure of how often the self-correction loop converges, or how often the agent reports completion on work that is wrong.** That absence is not an accusation; it is the condition under which you are working, and it is why the audit is yours to run rather than something a status line can tell you.

**Fifth, refuse the easy exemption.** Audit your own hand-written regions with the same classes. **Comprehension debt predates generated code** — it is what a rushed engineer leaves behind under any tooling — and a learner who classifies it as a machine problem has misdiagnosed the mechanism and will not recognise it in their own work.

## Show it

Give one *operational* region to a colleague and ask them to change its behaviour in a small, specified way, without asking you anything.

Watch what they do. **What they have to read in order to make the change is the region's real size**, which is almost always larger than the region's boundary on screen, and that difference is the interest on the debt.

## What this does not establish

Nothing here shows how much comprehension debt costs, how fast it accrues, or whether generated code accrues it faster than written code.

**The claim is a practitioner judgement from a collection with no measurement of it and no measurement of anything else that matters** — no defect data, no incident data, no maintainability data, no post-deployment record, and no failed engagement anywhere in approximately ninety documents. The phrase is excellent and the evidence behind it is one person's experience, stated once.

The vendor documentation establishes the **mechanism** — that confirmation is dismissible and can be made standing — and establishes nothing about consequence. It publishes no convergence measure and no false-completion measure, so the frequency of the failure this session is designed around is unknown, including to the vendor, or at least unpublished by it.

**The three-class audit is this design's invention.** No source proposes it, the boundary between *operational* and *opaque* is a judgement rather than a criterion, and two auditors will classify the same region differently. That is stated in the session rather than hidden, and [WP-048](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-048.md) §8 concedes it properly.
