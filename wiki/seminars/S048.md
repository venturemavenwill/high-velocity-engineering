# S048 · The Judge Is Biased: Measuring the Measurer

**Module:** [M10](wiki/modules/M10.md) · **Quarter:** [Q5](wiki/quarters/Q5.md) · **Complexity class:** 4
**Strands:** ME · IS · SG
**Whitepaper:** [WP-048](wiki/whitepapers/WP-048.md)
**Depends on:** S027, S046
**Re-tests:** S047
**Platform anchor:** Foundry LLM-as-judge evaluators and the judge-model choice · GitHub Copilot reviewing its own output · **MCP servers:** Foundry MCP Server (preview) · Microsoft Learn MCP Server

---

## What this day is for

A criterion says what good means. Somebody or something then decides, case by case, whether a given output is good — and that judge has an error structure of its own.

Human judges drift within a session, anchor on the first case they see, and disagree with themselves on re-presentation. Model judges prefer longer outputs, prefer outputs resembling their own generation style, and are sensitive to position and formatting in ways unrelated to quality. Rubric-guided judges of both kinds trade one bias for another.

The target: **the judge is an instrument with an error structure, S027 applies to it, and the systematic component must be identified and its direction stated** — because a systematic judge error does not average out over more cases, and more cases are exactly what a scaled evaluation supplies.

**This is also the day the programme turns the argument on its own primary tool.** Every build in this programme is produced by an agent and reviewed by a human who is frequently helped by the same agent. **A judge that scored its own work would be dismissed in any other setting**, and it is the ordinary condition of agent-assisted engineering. The [Copilot Practice Standard](wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md) names this day as the place that commitment is discharged, and it is discharged by measurement rather than by warning: the student characterises the agent as a judge of its own output, on their own artefact, and reports the systematic component they find.

## Phase 1 · Pretest and prediction (25 min)

Five items. Twenty outputs and their scores from a judge are supplied.

1. Which of these scores do you disagree with?
2. Here are the same twenty outputs, reordered, scored again by the same judge. Which scores moved?
3. What would explain the movement?
4. Predict: would a different judge have the same problem?
5. **Copilot prediction, sealed.** You will shortly ask the agent to *review the evaluator you built at S046 and the check you built at S047, and tell you what is wrong with them.* Write down what it will flag, **what it will not flag, and which of those two lists is longer**. Sealed with items 1 to 4 and opened at phase 8.

Item 2's movement is substantial and is not random with respect to position.

Item 5 is the day's contribution to the running frontier map and the sharpest one the programme takes. The agent wrote most of both artefacts. The design's expectation is that it will produce a fluent, correct, useful review of the things it can see — schema, coverage, edge cases — and will not raise the defect it introduced, because the reasoning that produced the defect is the reasoning being asked to find it.

**Warrant.** Pretesting with a re-presentation reveal. Same judge, same outputs, different order, different scores — which is unarguable and requires no framework to see. Item 5 is administered here and may not migrate into phase 5, per instrumentation rule 8, and its two-list form is deliberate: predicting that a self-review misses things is easy, and predicting the ratio is the measure.

## Phase 2 · Cumulative retrieval set (20 min)

Free recall, no notes.

- The S027 four error sources and their remedies.
- The S047 three drift mechanisms.
- The S046 traceability requirement.
- Which S027 source does a judge's preference for longer outputs belong to, and what does more judging do to it?

The answer is systematic offset, and more judging does nothing.

**Warrant.** Expanding-interval retrieval importing S027's taxonomy onto the judge, which is the day's central move.

## Phase 3 · Contrasting cases and generation (45 min)

**Executed, not read.** Groups take **the same fifty outputs** and have them scored by **four judges**, three of which they run themselves.

- **Judge A** — a human, unguided. Supplied.
- **Judge B** — a human, following the S046 criterion as a rubric. Supplied.
- **Judge C** — a model, unguided: a built-in Foundry quality evaluator with a judge model the group names in `deployment_name`.
- **Judge D** — **the model that produced the outputs, judging them.** Same family, same deployment, now scoring its own generation against the group's S046 rubric.

The four disagree, and their disagreements are structured: each pair disagrees on a different subset, in a consistent direction.

Groups characterise each judge's systematic component and commit.

Then **the outputs' known properties are supplied** — length, format, position in the batch, and whether each output was produced by the judge-D model or by a different one — and the correlations are shown.

Task after: **state which judge you would use, for what, and what you would correct for.**

**Judge D is the payload and the room's expectation about it is wrong in a specific way.** Students predict that a model marking its own work will be generous. What the correlations show is narrower: the effect is concentrated on the outputs the judge's own family produced, and it survives the rubric — Judge B and Judge D are following the same written criterion and disagree on a subset that is identifiable from provenance and not from quality. Microsoft's own AI playbook names the mechanism among the known reliability problems with model judges, alongside positional and verbosity bias, and states plainly that **evaluation by language model has not yet been systematically studied.** The playbook also records, citing external work, that reference-free metrics carry a bias toward their underlying models' outputs **and a bias against higher-quality text** — which is the ranking-inversion case phase 7 asks students to construct, named in the documentation before anyone in the room has built one.

**No magnitude is asserted for any of these effects, here or anywhere in this day.** The direction is what the documentation supports; the magnitude is what the group measures on its own fifty outputs, and it is theirs alone.

**Warrant.** Contrasting cases across judges with a fixed output set and a property-correlation adjudicator, now run rather than supplied. The four-way structured disagreement is the phase's payload: no judge is neutral and the biases are not the same bias. Judge D discharges the [Copilot Practice Standard](wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md)'s commitment that this day treat the agent as a reviewer of its own output, and it is placed in phase 3 rather than phase 4 so that the effect is measured by the room before it is named by anyone.

## Phase 4 · Consolidating instruction (50 min)

1. **A judge is an instrument.** S026's construct-instrument distinction and S027's error taxonomy both apply, unchanged.
2. **Systematic judge error does not average out,** and a scaled evaluation supplies more cases rather than more judges — which increases confidence in a biased estimate. S027 point 3, at the point where it costs most.
3. **Known human judge effects:** anchoring on early cases, within-session drift, self-disagreement on re-presentation, and sensitivity to order. Directions stated where the repository supports them.
4. **Known model judge effects:** length preference, self-style preference, position sensitivity, and sensitivity to formatting. **This list is not exhaustive and the field is moving; it omits effects specific to particular model families and effects that appear only above certain scales. Safe as a checklist of properties to correlate against when characterising a judge you have; unsafe as a description of any specific judge you have not measured — the day's whole position is that you measure your own.**
5. **Rubrics trade biases,** they do not remove them. A rubric constrains attention, which reduces some effects and introduces rubric-interpretation variance.
6. **Characterising a judge:** score the same set twice reordered, correlate scores against output properties, and compare against a held-back reference.
7. **What to do:** correct, restrict the judge's use, or use a different judge for that subset — each with a cost.
8. **Agreement is not accuracy.** Two judges agreeing is evidence they share a bias as readily as evidence they are right. And **agreement with a human is not published for any evaluator in this stack.** A neighbouring product on the same documentation estate names the statistic it tracks — Cohen's Kappa — and publishes no value for it. Foundry names neither the statistic nor a value. **Every judged score any of you will produce this quarter is therefore uncalibrated, and the sentence reporting one must say so.** This is not a complaint. It is the condition under which the work is done, and stating it is the whole of the remedy available.
9. **Defaults are opinions, and one of them decides who the judge is.** Four were chosen for you today. A judge model is a parameter you must supply, and the documentation **ranks candidate models by judge quality** — recommended, acceptable, not recommended — warning that one produces *noticeably less reliable* scores, with **no reliability figure attached to any row and two ranks missing from the published table.** A rubric dimension is scored only when the judge decides it applies, unless someone marked it `always_applicable`, so the judge has a degree of freedom nobody's report mentions. Continuous evaluation **redacts the judge's stated reason by default**, and you get it back only by setting a redaction flag off — so the platform's default posture is that a score travels and its reasoning does not. And a reasoning-model judge requires a flag of its own to be treated as one. **An engineer who cannot state what was chosen for them does not understand their own system.**
10. **What is perishable here and what is not.** Every model name in that ranking, every measured effect, the redaction default and the preview status of half this tooling will move within the year, and the model-judge effects at point 4 are the fastest-moving content in the programme. **The durable claims are that a judge is an instrument, that a systematic component does not average out, that a rubric trades biases rather than removing them, that shift is tolerable and inversion is not, and that a score whose agreement with human judgement is unpublished is uncalibrated whoever publishes it.** If agreement data is published next year, the last claim's instance changes and the claim does not.

**Warrant.** Consolidation indexed to phase 3. Point 4 carries the strong-form scope statement, which is unusually important because the field moves faster than the material. Point 8 is the day's most-needed correction and is now reachable from phase 3's judge-D correlations rather than asserted, per instrumentation rule 7. Points 9 and 10 are the [Copilot Practice Standard](wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md)'s required platform content — a default the platform chose, and a perishable point marked as perishable. **The published judge ranking is the day's cleanest documentation silence: a total order over judges with no measurement behind it is a preference presented as a finding, which is the exact object this module exists to detect.**

## Phase 5 · Whole-task learning task (90 min)

**Class 4. The judge and its characterisation are the student's, and this phase produces a build.**

Students use Copilot in agent mode, through the Foundry MCP Server, to **ship a working judge characterisation** for their own S046 criterion. They:

1. **Choose a judge** for it, and state why — naming the model deployment if it is a model, and stating what the documentation's ranking did and did not tell you.
2. **Design the characterisation:** what you will score twice, what properties you will correlate against, what reference you will hold back. **At least one correlated property must not appear on point 4's list and must be drawn from your own outputs.**
3. **Predict the systematic component** and its direction, before running it.
4. **Run it.**
5. **State the systematic component found,** with its direction and rough magnitude.
6. **Decide:** correct, restrict, or replace — and state the cost.
7. **State what your characterisation could not have detected.**
8. **Characterise the agent as a judge of its own work.** Ask Copilot to review the artefact it helped you build, record what it flagged and what it did not, and state the systematic component of *that* judge in the same terms as steps 5 and 7.

**The artefact carries three further elements**, per the [Copilot Practice Standard](wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md).

- **Prompt record.** The instructions you gave the agent, in order, including the ones you abandoned.
- **Intervention record.** Every point at which you corrected it, and what the correction was. **An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that the design could have detected an intervention-worthy error — a held-back reference chosen so that a judge agreeing with the student would still be caught is the obvious example and is accepted.
- **Frontier statement.** One thing the agent did faster than you could have, and one thing it did badly that you had to catch. Both specific, both from this build. **Step 8's finding may not be reused as the frontier statement**; they are different measurements and collapsing them loses the one the programme is short of.

**Fading:** step 2's design is worked with the room; step 8's protocol is supplied. **Step 3 is unsupported.**

**Warrant.** Whole-task at class 4, now shipping rather than describing. Step 2's off-list property is [WP-048](wiki/whitepapers/WP-048.md) §8's objection-1 repair: a student correlating against exactly the four properties on the list has satisfied the step while assuming the list is complete. Step 3's pre-commitment is the module's standing discipline, and step 5's requirement that a systematic component be *found* — not merely looked for — is defended in the whitepaper. **Step 8 is the day's contribution to the programme's central claim about its own tool**, and it is a step rather than a reflection because a self-review characterised in the same vocabulary as any other judge is evidence, and one described in prose is an impression.

## Phase 6 · Error clinic (40 min)

Instructor first: a substrate evaluation where two judges agreed closely and were both wrong in the same direction, and how long it took to discover — with attention to **the fact that the agreement was reported as a reliability figure**.

Then the agent case, and it is the one from phase 1 item 5. A student asked Copilot to review a prompt-based evaluator it had written the day before. The review was substantial and useful: it caught an unhandled empty-context case, tightened two dimension descriptions, and flagged a field-mapping risk. **It did not mention that the evaluator's scoring instruction rewarded a response for restating the question, which the agent had written and which had already inflated the pass rate by a visible margin on the substrate's own data.** Attention goes to why review did not catch it: the human reviewer read a review that had found real problems, concluded the artefact had been reviewed, and stopped — **a review that finds something is more effective at ending scrutiny than one that finds nothing.**

Then students: a time you trusted a judgement because two people made it, or a place today where you accepted agent output you had not checked.

**Warrant.** Error management targeting point 8. High inter-rater agreement reported as evidence of quality is the day's most consequential misreading and appears in published work routinely. The agent case is the [Copilot Practice Standard](wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md)'s required addition and is chosen because the review was genuinely good, which is the condition under which self-review is actually dangerous.

## Phase 7 · Peer articulation (30 min)

Pairs. Each student presents **their step 5 systematic component**, and the partner's task is to construct an output set on which that component would produce a *wrong ranking* between two genuinely different-quality outputs.

Then: would the student's step 6 decision handle it?

Then, briefly: **the partner reads the agent's step 8 review against the artefact it reviewed, and adds one item to the missed list.** The student may not remove it.

Per rule 3, the outcome is recorded in the artefact. Per rule 5, this reuses the [S029](wiki/seminars/S029.md) dual-defeat structure.

**Warrant.** Interactive-tier articulation with a ranking-inversion demand. A bias that shifts all scores equally is harmless; one that reorders is not, and the distinction is what makes step 6's decision necessary. **The third demand is [WP-048](wiki/whitepapers/WP-048.md) §8's objection-3 fix**: step 8's missed list was otherwise a self-report about a self-review, made by the person with the most reason to shape it, with an external observer sitting in the next phase — which is instrumentation rule 1 violated in a day about judges.

## Phase 8 · Calibration and commitment (20 min)

- Items 1 to 4 resolved: the re-presentation movement and its correlation with position.
- The room's step 3 predictions against step 5 findings: how many predicted the right component, and how many predicted the right direction.
- **Item 5 opened.** The sealed predictions about the agent's self-review are read against step 8's record: what it flagged, what it did not, and whether the student's predicted ratio held. **The cohort's aggregate is the programme's running measure of whether students can predict their own tool's blind spot**, and it is carried forward through the [Durable and Perishable Register](wiki/program/09-Durable-and-Perishable-Register.md).
- The cohort's chosen judges shown; whether any student chose a human is discussed.
- Each student names one judgement in their own life whose judge they have never characterised.
- Retrieval schedule: next morning, day 3, week 2, week 6.

## Assessment

Specification item in ME201 and IS201 jointly. Per rule 1, the checked elements are: a chosen judge with a reason **and its model deployment named where it has one**; a characterisation design with all three components **and one off-list correlated property**; a pre-committed prediction; the characterisation run; a systematic component with direction and magnitude; a correct-restrict-replace decision with its cost; a statement of what could not be detected; **the agent-as-self-reviewer characterisation from step 8**; the phase 7 outcome; **the prompt record**; **the intervention record**; **the frontier statement**. **Nothing else is assessed.**

**A characterisation reporting no systematic component fails the specification** unless the artefact demonstrates that the design could have detected one — the same structure as [S041](wiki/seminars/S041.md)'s leak audit, and stated the same way.

**A step 8 record whose two lists are identical fails**, on the same terms: either the agent flagged everything it missed, which is checkable, or the review was not characterised. **The partner's phase 7 addition to the missed list is a checked element and may not be removed.**

**An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that the design could have detected an intervention-worthy error.

## Perishable content in this day

| Durable claim | Platform instance, perishable |
|---|---|
| A judge is an instrument with an error structure | The judge model named in `deployment_name` |
| A systematic component does not average out | Scaled evaluation adds cases, not judges |
| A rubric trades biases rather than removing them | Judge B and Judge D following one rubric and disagreeing by provenance |
| A ranking with no measurement behind it is a preference | The published recommended / acceptable / not-recommended judge table |
| A score whose agreement with human judgement is unpublished is uncalibrated | No Foundry evaluator publishes it; a neighbouring product names the statistic and not the value |
| A score and its reasoning are different artefacts | The judge's `reason` redacted by default in continuous evaluation |

The specific model judges, their measured effects, the published ranking, all magnitudes and every preview marking are **perishable** and are marked at point 10 explicitly. Judge-as-instrument, the characterisation method, rubrics-trade-biases, shift-versus-inversion, agreement-is-not-accuracy and the uncalibrated-score rule are durable and are what the specification assesses.
