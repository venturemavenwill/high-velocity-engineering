## The situation

The internal corpus behind this stage contains one sentence about generated code that is sharper than anything in its own promotional material, and this session is built on it.

**Generated code accumulates comprehension debt: it exists, it passes, and nobody understands it.**

That is the whole hazard, and every clause is doing work. *It exists* — so it cannot be ignored. *It passes* — so nothing will flag it. *Nobody understands it* — so the first person who needs to change it starts from zero, and that person is usually somebody with an incident open.

The debt is not contracted at the moment of generation. **It is contracted at the moment of acceptance**, and acceptance in current tooling is a small, frequent, dismissible act.

The hard part is that the debt is silent by construction. It does not appear in a build, a test run, a review comment or a dashboard. **It appears the first time somebody has to change the thing or defend it**, which is precisely the moment at which discovering it is most expensive. Everything in this session is an attempt to move that discovery earlier, and the instrument that does the moving is not a code reading. It is an evaluation.

**Answer these in writing before reading further.**

1. Point at a line in your increment and say why it is that line and not a different one.
2. Which parts of your increment would you be unable to change safely?
3. What did you auto-confirm this week, and when did you stop reading the confirmations?
4. If this broke while you were unreachable, who could fix it?
5. Take one region of your increment and write, in one sentence, the check that would fail if its behaviour were wrong. If you cannot write it, say what stops you.

**Item 5 is the session.** Item 1 is harder than it looks — the honest answer for most working code is *because that is what came out and the tests went green* — but item 5 is the one that converts that discomfort into an artefact, because **the sentence you cannot write is a measurement of what you do not understand**, and it is available today rather than at the incident.

## What you must produce

A **comprehension audit** of your own increment, region by region. Nothing is exempt, including the parts you wrote yourself.

Each region is classified as one of three things, and the classification is the artefact.

- **Explained** — you can say what it does and why it is written this way rather than another way.
- **Operational** — you can say what it does, and you cannot say why it takes the form it takes.
- **Opaque** — you cannot say what it does without reading it again from scratch, and possibly not then.

For every region that is not *explained*: what it would cost to move it up a class, who would have to do it, and what you are doing instead in the meantime.

**And, for every region you have classified *explained*, one evaluator** — a check that would fail if the region's behaviour were wrong. Not a test that the code runs. A statement of the behaviour you claim to understand, in a form something else can score.

The audit closes with a **confirmation record**: what your executor asked permission for during this increment, what you granted, and what is now standing permission.

## The material

### Why the debt is invisible until it is expensive

The sentence this session rests on is a practitioner judgement from a collection with **no measurement of it and no measurement of anything else that matters** — no defect data, no incident data, no maintainability data, no post-deployment record, and no failed engagement anywhere in approximately ninety documents ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md)). **The phrase is excellent and the evidence behind it is one person's experience, stated once.** It is access-controlled, paraphrased here and never quoted, and no figure from it is reproduced anywhere in this repository.

What the collection describes is nonetheless recognisable, and the mechanism is not mysterious. Every ordinary signal an engineering organisation has — a green build, an approved review, a closed ticket, a shipped release — reports on the *artefact*. None of them reports on anybody's *understanding of* the artefact. **The two were correlated for as long as writing the code was how you came to understand it, and that correlation is what has changed.**

The internal platform synthesis behind this strand states the industry version of the same problem in one sentence: demonstrating the technology is relatively easy, and deploying into production while extracting sustained return is the difficulty ([research/06-microsoft-platform/03-accelerate-agentic-ai.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/03-accelerate-agentic-ai.md)). **That document is internal, not publicly accessible, not citable externally, the most perishable artefact in this repository, and contains a precise unverifiable performance figure, a maturity model with no causal evidence and analyst projections presented as fact. None of its quantitative claims is repeated anywhere in this wiki.**

### Debt is contracted at acceptance, and acceptance has been made cheap

The vendor's documentation for the agent mode this programme anchors on records that the agent determines its own steps and iterates rather than stopping after a single response, and that it requests confirmation before running a terminal command or a tool that is not built in — **with that confirmation being a dismissible default rather than a boundary**, auto-confirmable per session, per solution, or permanently. This repository's reading of that documentation was made on 2026-07-30 and **has not been re-read for this session**; treat every detail of it as due for verification before the session runs.

**Permanently is the word to sit with.** It is a supported, reasonable, well-designed option, and it is also the mechanism by which a team stops reading, one dismissal at a time. Note too that the vendor's own quickstart material instructs the reader to select the autopilot option, which removes the approvals entirely — so the default that looks like a control is one the documentation itself recommends switching off.

**And the documentation publishes no measure of how often the self-correction loop converges, and none of how often the agent reports completion on work that is wrong.** That absence is not an accusation; it is the condition under which you are working, and it is why the audit is yours to run rather than something a status line can tell you.

### The scaffold: debt contracted in seconds, with no diff to review

Everything above describes debt accumulating one accepted suggestion at a time. **There is a faster route and almost everybody takes it first: a generator writes an entire project in one command.**

A scaffold from the vendor's agent toolchain produces five directories — editor debugging configuration, the manifest templates, the environment files, **infrastructure templates for provisioning cloud resources**, and the source ([research/06-microsoft-platform/agents-toolkit-and-sdk-family.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/agents-toolkit-and-sdk-family.md)). **Two of those five contain things the developer did not write and will be asked to own**: the infrastructure templates, and the identity configuration the provisioning stage performs on their behalf.

**The debt here is contracted at scaffold time rather than at edit time, and that changes its shape in two ways that matter.**

First, **there is no diff.** Accepting a suggestion inside an existing file at least shows you what changed; a scaffold arrives with nothing to compare it against, so the ordinary review reflex has nothing to grip. The whole project is *operational* on arrival, in this session's middle class, and stays there indefinitely because nothing ever prompts a reading.

Second, **the debt concentrates precisely where a developer is least likely to look and most likely to be paged.** Infrastructure and identity are the two areas the source directory's reader habitually skips, and they are the two whose failures arrive as opaque platform error codes at an unhelpful hour.

Three diagnostics make that concrete, and they are worth reading as a specimen rather than memorising as facts. An identity-creation action requires a specific flag; **omit it and provisioning fails with an authentication error code that names nothing about the missing flag.** A missing tenant identifier in a local configuration file causes the software development kit to use **the wrong token authority**, producing an authorisation failure from the connector that looks like a permissions problem and is not. And a persistent authorisation failure surviving an authentication fix may mean **the development tunnel address has been blocked** and a fresh one is needed. **Every one of those is diagnosable only by somebody who read the generated configuration**, which is this session's thesis stated as three ways of losing an afternoon.

**Where that knowledge lives is itself a finding about comprehension debt, and it is the sharper half.** None of those three diagnostics appears on any published documentation page. **They ship inside an editor extension, versioned separately from the documentation, undiscoverable from it, with no public address and no currency guarantee.** So the material most likely to let you understand your own generated project is the material least likely to be found by looking for it. **Treat all three as hypotheses a practitioner can test in ten minutes rather than as facts** — they are field-derived and unverifiable, they are recorded in this repository as reported, and they are cited by extension version because that is the only pin available.

One more thing the command list admits without meaning to. **An upgrade command exists to bring a generated project into line with current tooling**, which concedes that a project can fall out of step with its generator. **And nothing is published about the depreciation schedule of that debt:** no compatibility matrix, no support window for a tooling version, and **no statement of how long a generated project remains buildable at all.** A liability with no maturity date is not thereby a smaller liability.

**The limits.** Six pages of that family were fetched and read in full and roughly thirty more reached only through search excerpts, **so no list here is exhaustive.** **Every principal name in the area has changed at least once and one package twice.** The ergonomics vocabulary throughout — streamlines, dramatically simplifies, rapid — is asserted with no measurement anywhere, and **there is no measure of how much any of it reduces time to a first working agent: no baseline, no comparison, no study.** Nothing in it supports any claim about work arriving in one duration rather than another, and none is made here.

### The counter-design: a scaffold instructed to arrive smaller than itself

Everything above describes a generator that gives you as much as it can. **There is a public artefact from the same vendor built on the opposite premise, and reading the two together is the fastest way to see what the debt is actually made of** ([research/06-microsoft-platform/caira-reference-architectures.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/caira-reference-architectures.md)).

It is a reference library rather than an application generator, and it says so about itself. Its entrypoint is a skill installed into your project which instructs an agent to inspect the library and adapt only what fits. **Almost every rule in that skill exists to make the agent take less.** Prefer a small component over the full stack. Copy only the files the stack needs. Delete the sample text, the model names, the environment variables and the infrastructure variables that do not apply. **The premise is that the value is the pattern and the liability is the volume**, which is the reverse of the usual pitch for reusable starting points and is the more defensible half of the argument.

One rule matters more than the rest for this session, and it is the one to carry out of it. **The agent is required to state which reference paths it used and what it deliberately left out.**

Read that against the problem the previous section names. A scaffold's debt is contracted in seconds and **there is no diff**, so the review reflex has nothing to grip. A provenance-and-exclusion statement **is the missing artefact** — not a comparison against a previous state, which does not exist, but a declaration of where the code came from and what was consciously not taken. **It manufactures something reviewable at the only moment when the information still exists**, which is while the agent is choosing.

It also inverts the debt's direction. An ordinary scaffold leaves you owning everything it wrote and knowing what none of it is for. A library that obliges its agent to name the exclusions leaves a record of the decisions, and **a decision you can read is a decision you can reverse.**

**Two further rules in the same skill are discovery moved into configuration**, which is the same move [S046](/wiki/seminars/S046.md) makes with instructions an executor actually receives. Before generating anything, the agent must establish what the user is trying to build, what they already have, what boundaries apply, and which components are genuinely needed now. **One of those is qualification in a sentence: determine what the user already has before proposing new infrastructure.** The identical guidance in a playbook does not execute. Here it does.

**Now the part that belongs in this session rather than in a design lecture, because it is the sharper lesson.** That repository runs a scheduled test of its own skill in which **one agent generates a project and a second agent grades it.** The generator is run unattended and told explicitly not to deploy or run anything. The verifier is pointed at the workspace, forbidden to change it, and required to end with a pass or fail token.

**All five of its criteria are presence checks** — that real files exist rather than prose, that a backend and a frontend and setup documentation are present, that placeholders stand in for secrets, that an infrastructure layer exists using the expected module, and that an API layer exists that builds an agent and reaches the frontend. **Nothing is built. Nothing is run. Nothing is deployed.**

**So the weekly green tick establishes that an agent produces artefacts of the expected shape, and nothing at all about whether they work.** That is a defensible trade — a scheduled real deployment costs money and blast radius — and it is **not** a defensible thing to read as a working-system check. The generator and the verifier are also the same model family through the same provider, with no human label anywhere and no disagreement rate published. **A green check on a shape criterion is a fact about form**, and this is the same distinction the pinned configuration corpus states about its own graders, arriving here in a second artefact that looks considerably more like judgement.

**Its limits.** A public repository read as a downloaded snapshot on 2026-08-06, and **no commit identifier is recoverable from it**, so unlike the pinned corpus this cannot be cited to an exact revision and a later reader cannot reconstruct what was read. Its declared skill version is a package version, not a revision. **It reports no outcome of any kind** — no measurement that its references produce faster delivery, fewer defects or lower cost, no comparison against building from scratch, and no adoption data. **Its transparency note is three sentences and names no limitation, no failure mode and no human-oversight requirement**, which is a real gap and is visible only because a sibling repository from the same vendor does state those things. And every path, component and module name in it is perishable at the same rate as everything else in this stage.

### You cannot write an evaluator for behaviour you cannot explain

This is the session's mechanism and it is why the anchor is an evaluation framework rather than a code-reading exercise.

The framework's programming model is deliberately small — three types. An **`EvalItem`** is "a single item to evaluate — wraps the full conversation and derives query/response via a split strategy". An **`Evaluator`** is "a provider that scores items". **`EvalResults`** is the aggregate ([research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-framework-and-evaluations-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-framework-and-evaluations-docs.md)). Three types is a good sign: it says that evaluation is a function from an interaction to a judgement, aggregated over a set, and that everything else is a detail of who does the judging.

Two tiers implement the cost gradient the evaluation literature argues for in the abstract. A **local evaluator** "runs checks locally without API calls — ideal for inner-loop development, CI smoke tests, and fast iteration". A cloud tier connects to a judged service. **They can be mixed in one run**, which means the architecture itself carries the discipline: cheap deterministic checks constantly, expensive judged checks periodically.

**The idea that decides everything else is the conversation split strategy, and the documentation flags it correctly:** "Multi-turn conversations must be split into query and response halves for evaluation. How you split determines *what you're evaluating*." Three are supplied — **last turn** (the default; response quality at a point), **full** (task completion and overall trajectory), and **per-turn** (fine-grained, with cumulative context) — plus a custom splitter. **An agent that recovers well from a bad first attempt scores well under one and badly under another. Neither score is wrong; they answer different questions**, and a team that never chose deliberately is reporting an answer to a question it did not ask.

The tool-usage catalogue is the part that gives you a vocabulary for the debt itself. It decomposes into **selection** (the wrong tool), **input accuracy** (the right tool, wrong arguments), **call success** (it failed technically), **output utilisation** (a successful result ignored or misread), and an overall accuracy covering selection, parameter correctness and efficiency. **Two of those — wrong arguments and ignored output — produce a system that reports success and is wrong**, which is exactly the failure class hardest to detect in production and exactly the class an opaque region hides.

Three further details are worth carrying. **Repetitions exist "to detect non-deterministic behavior"** — the documentation's own acknowledgement that one sample from a stochastic system is not a measurement. **Pre-existing responses can be evaluated "from logs or previous runs… without re-running the agent"**, which is what makes evaluating what actually happened possible rather than only what you thought to ask. And **the threshold that converts a score into pass or fail is a policy choice separable from the measurement** — moving it changes the pass rate without changing the system, so a threshold must be justified rather than inherited.

**The silence in this source is the most important thing about it, and it is total on the question a reader most needs answered. No agreement rate with human judgement is published for any evaluator, no calibration data, and no rationale for where the default thresholds came from.** Most of these evaluators are model-judged. **Assert no agreement figure for any of them anywhere**, and validate the validator yourself. Further limits: the pages were read through search-returned excerpts rather than full fetches, so structure was reconstructed; most agent evaluators are marked **preview**; one consulted page is a **classic** variant explicitly superseded; and the surfaces differ across languages, so examples are not interchangeable.

### Criteria drift, and why your three classes will move

There is a circularity in evaluation design that this session will run straight into, and it is better met named than discovered.

The finding is **criteria drift**: "users need criteria to grade outputs, but grading outputs helps users define criteria" ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/shankar-evalgen.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/shankar-evalgen.md)). It is a genuine circularity rather than a workflow inefficiency — you cannot fully specify what counts as good before seeing outputs, because seeing outputs is how you discover which dimensions of quality vary and matter. The sharper version is that some criteria appear to be **dependent on the specific outputs observed**, which "raises serious questions for approaches that assume the independence of evaluation from observation of model outputs".

The same work names the trap this session is designed around from the other side: **using a model to grade a model does not solve the trust problem, it relocates it.** "LLM-generated evaluators simply inherit all the problems of the LLMs they evaluate, requiring further human validation." **Its evidence is qualitative and from a small interface study; it establishes that the phenomenon is real and consequential and does not quantify its prevalence**, and it does not resolve the held-out problem it creates — if criteria are revised after seeing outputs, those outputs cannot cleanly serve as a held-out set.

The practitioner literature supplies the ladder and the discipline. Teams need three capabilities — evaluate quality, debug by inspecting data, and change behaviour — and **most teams have only the third**, which is why they can change the system and cannot tell whether the change helped ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/husain-your-ai-product-needs-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/husain-your-ai-product-needs-evals.md)). Its decomposition into **features and scenarios** is the move that makes a check-writing exercise tractable, and its most useful line for today breaks with software convention: **"unlike traditional unit tests, you don't necessarily need a 100% pass rate. Your pass rate is a product decision."** It also warns that **"raw agreement is generally not recommended and can be misleading when classes are imbalanced"**, and that precision and recall should be reported separately. **It is a practitioner essay resting on one company's case study, with no control condition and no measured improvement; its tooling references are dated and should be read as pattern rather than product.**

And if you reach for a model as your judge, the four documented biases arrive with it: **position, verbosity, self-enhancement and limited reasoning** ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/zheng-mt-bench-chatbot-arena.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/zheng-mt-bench-chatbot-arena.md)). Two bear directly on this session. **Self-enhancement** means a judge from the same family as the system under test is not an independent instrument — and your executor and your judge are, by default, the same family. **Limited reasoning** means a judge cannot reliably grade a problem it cannot itself solve, which is the exact situation you are in when the region is opaque. **That work predates current models and its bias taxonomy has held up better than its specific figures; none is reproduced here, and agreement with human preference is not correctness in any case.**

### Why you cannot feel this, and why a harness is the only structure available

Two findings explain why self-report is not an instrument here.

Capability is **jagged** — the boundary between what these systems handle well and badly is uneven and does not follow how hard a task feels to a person ([research/04-professional-formation/dellacqua-jagged-technological-frontier.md](/research/04-professional-formation/dellacqua-jagged-technological-frontier.md)). **Outside that boundary you do not get an error. You get a persuasive wrong answer in the same register as the correct ones.** That note was written from an abstract; **the shape of the finding is usable and no number in it is.**

And in a study of experienced maintainers working in codebases they knew well, participants were **slowed while believing they had been sped up** — and the belief did not correct after they had done the work ([research/04-professional-formation/metr-ai-assisted-productivity-study.md](/research/04-professional-formation/metr-ai-assisted-productivity-study.md)). **Its limits are severe and must travel with it:** a very small sample; a narrow and adversarial population; self-reported time; the authors themselves now describe the findings as out of date and have redesigned the study because participants will no longer reliably work without the tooling. **Its follow-up results point the other way and its authors judge them unreliable; neither set of figures is reproduced here.** What survives is the direction: **perceived and actual effect diverged, and direct experience did not correct the divergence.**

The last piece is the one that explains why an evaluation harness rather than more diligence.

The meta-analysis of deliberate practice found that how much practice volume explains about performance differs systematically **by domain**, and the ordering is the interesting part: it explains most in fields with stable rules, immediate unambiguous feedback and clear success criteria — games, music, athletics — and **least in education and professional work** ([research/04-professional-formation/macnamara-deliberate-practice-limits.md](/research/04-professional-formation/macnamara-deliberate-practice-limits.md)). Professional performance is the domain it explains least, and it is the domain you are in.

**The useful reading is not that practice does not work. It is that the structure of the domain decides whether practice converts into performance** — and the structural reading of the ordering, which is an interpretation rather than the authors' claim, is feedback latency, feedback ambiguity, rule stability and criterion clarity. Software work with a generative executor has none of those in your favour. **So the job is to manufacture the missing structure, and an evaluation harness is exactly that manufacture:** it converts a delayed, ambiguous, contested signal about whether you understood something into a fast, unambiguous one you can run today.

**The note's own limits are heavy.** Its figures are variance explained from correlational studies, often using retrospective self-reported practice hours; **range restriction is the strongest technical objection and is not fully resolvable within the design**; the professions estimate rests on the domain with the least standardised performance measurement and is the least robust of its results; the literature is actively contested; and **no figure from it appears here.** The structural explanation of the ordering is explicitly interpretive.

## The workflow

**Write the evaluator for the region you claim to understand, and watch which regions you cannot write one for.**

**Step 1 — do item 1 in front of somebody.** Pick a region at random — genuinely at random, not the one you are proud of — and explain it aloud. **The discomfort is the measurement.** Classify it, provisionally, into one of the three classes.

**Step 2 — reconstruct the confirmation record before you can remember it accurately.** Look at what standing permissions exist rather than at what you recall granting, and include any granted before this session. This is the part of the audit that is verifiable, and it is the part learners assume they already know.

**Step 3 — write one evaluator per *explained* region, and declare the split strategy in the file.** The shape below is a specification, not a schema — **the source for this surface was read through search excerpts rather than full page fetches, several evaluators are in preview and one consulted page is a superseded variant, so verify every key against the current documentation and record the version you checked.**

```yaml
# evals/increment-01.eval.yaml   <- SHAPE, NOT SCHEMA. Verify keys; record version.
split_strategy: per_turn        # last_turn | full | per_turn
                                # WHY: this region is judged on each exchange, not on
                                # eventual recovery. Choosing `full` would score a
                                # different question and would flatter the region.
repetitions: 5                  # one sample from a stochastic system is not a measurement

regions:
  - id: R3
    class: explained            # I claim to be able to say WHY it is written this way
    claim: >
      When the ledger call returns a partial result, the region retries once
      and then routes to a person. It never returns a partial total to the caller.
    local_checks:               # deterministic, no API call, runs in CI on every change
      - name: no_partial_total_returned
        assert: "response.total is null OR response.completeness == 'full'"
      - name: escalation_emitted_on_partial
        assert: "count(events['routed_to_person']) == 1"
    judged_checks:              # expensive, periodic, and NOT independent - see below
      - evaluator: task_adherence
        threshold: 3            # POLICY, not measurement. Justified below or delete it.
        threshold_rationale: >
          UNJUSTIFIED - inherited default. TODO before this leaves the branch.
        judge_family_same_as_executor: true   # self-enhancement bias: NOT independent
    data_mapping:
      trajectory: "{{sample.output_items}}"   # tool calls + results
      text: "{{sample.output_text}}"          # words only

  - id: R7
    class: opaque
    claim: null
    why_no_evaluator: >
      I cannot state the behaviour, so I cannot state a check that would fail
      if the behaviour were wrong. THIS ENTRY IS THE FINDING.
```

**Step 4 — notice what step 3 did to your classes.** Every region where you could not write the check is a region you had misclassified. **Move it down and leave the record of the move visible**, because the movement is the audit's most useful output and a clean audit has concealed it.

**Step 5 — run the checks under two split strategies and reconcile the difference.** Take one region, score it under the default and under one other, and write the sentence that reconciles them. **If you cannot say why the two numbers differ, you do not yet know what either one measures.**

**Step 6 — price one region properly.** Take an opaque region and establish what it would actually take to move it to *explained*: reading time, the person who would have to do it, what else they would not do that day. **The point is not to pay the debt. The point is to find out that it has a price**, because a debt with no stated price never appears in a plan.

**Step 7 — refuse the easy exemption.** Audit your own hand-written regions with the same classes and the same evaluator requirement. **Comprehension debt predates generated code** — it is what a rushed engineer leaves behind under any tooling — and a learner who classifies it as a machine problem has misdiagnosed the mechanism and will not recognise it in their own work.

**Step 8 — use the agent to attack your own audit, not to extend it.** One prompt: *for each region I classified as explained, produce an input for which my stated claim is false.* Then read what came back. Every region where it succeeds was *operational* wearing the wrong label. **An agent that agreed your audit was thorough was not used as an instrument**, which is [S001](/wiki/seminars/S001.md)'s claim arriving again. Record what you kept and what you rejected — and record, separately, that this instrument shares a model family with your executor and is therefore not independent of it.

**Step 9 — audit the scaffold, which is the part of your codebase with no diff and no author.** Every region so far was something you accepted. **Take the project the generator produced and classify it under the same three classes, file by file, including the two directories you have never opened.**

```yaml
# audit/scaffold.yaml   <- SHAPE, NOT SCHEMA. Directory names are this term's.
# Record the tooling version. There is no published compatibility matrix, no support
# window for a tooling version, and no statement of how long this project stays buildable.
tooling_version_checked: "<fill this in>"

regions:
  - path: "src/"
    class: operational
    note: "I have edited it, so I know what it does. I did not choose its shape."

  - path: "infra/"                       # infrastructure templates I did not write
    class: opaque                        # be honest. Almost nobody has read these.
    claim: null
    why_no_evaluator: >
      I cannot state what this provisions, so I cannot state a check that would fail
      if it provisioned the wrong thing. THIS ENTRY IS THE FINDING.
    remediation_price:
      who: "whoever holds the subscription, not me"
      cost: "<hours>, and one conversation I have been avoiding"
      paying: false
      why_not: "<state it, or move the region up a class>"

  - path: "<the identity action in the provisioning stage>"
    class: opaque
    prediction_before_reading: "<what breaks if the flag that creates the principal is absent?>"
    observed_after_breaking_it: "<the error code, verbatim>"
    note: >
      The published documentation does not carry this diagnosis. It ships inside an
      editor extension, versioned separately, undiscoverable from the docs, and is
      field-derived and unverified. Cited by extension version or not at all.
```

**The prediction line is the exercise and the fix is not.** Write what you think breaks, then break it, then compare. **A prediction that was wrong is worth more than a repair that worked**, because it locates the boundary of what you actually understood about a project you have been treating as yours.

**What this makes observable that prose cannot.** That *I understand this* and *I can state a check that would fail if it were wrong* are different claims, and that the gap between them is large, specific and locatable this afternoon rather than at the next incident. **The evaluator does not measure the code. It measures your account of the code**, and that is the thing the debt is denominated in.


## What good looks like

- **The audit has entries in all three classes.** An increment that is entirely explained has been audited by its author's optimism.
- **Every *explained* region carries an evaluator**, and the evaluator states a behaviour rather than restating the implementation.
- **At least one region moved down a class when its evaluator could not be written**, and the movement is visible in the record rather than tidied away.
- **The split strategy is declared and justified**, and the justification says what a different strategy would have measured instead.
- **The opaque regions are located precisely**, by file and function, not by feeling, with a remediation cost per region and at least one declared not worth paying with the reason.
- **The confirmation record names standing permissions**, including any granted before this session and forgotten.
- **A test passing over a region is not recorded as comprehension**, and a comment generated alongside the code is not recorded as an explanation. The audit distinguishes *this is verified* from *this is understood*, and an account written by the thing being accounted for is another output requiring the same scrutiny.

## How it goes wrong

- **The passing unknown** — the region is green, therefore it is fine, therefore it is not audited. *Warning sign:* your audit classes correlate with your test coverage.
- **The restated implementation** — the evaluator describes what the code does line by line, so it cannot fail unless the code is deleted. *Warning sign:* your check would still pass if the behaviour were wrong and the structure unchanged.
- **The inherited threshold** — a pass mark arrived as a default and nobody asked where it came from. *Warning sign:* your threshold rationale is blank, and moving it would change your result without changing your system.
- **The permanent confirmation** — permission was granted once, months ago, for a class of action nobody now remembers. *Warning sign:* you cannot reconstruct what you have already agreed to.
- **The explained-by-comment** — the code is annotated by the same process that produced it, and the annotation is treated as understanding. *Warning sign:* your explanation uses the comment's own vocabulary.
- **The audit that finds nothing** — everything is explained because the standard for *explained* was set after the audit. *Warning sign:* item 1 took you no time.
- **The debt attributed to the machine** — the opaque regions are all generated, none is yours, and the audit becomes an argument about tooling. *Warning sign:* your own code is exempt.
- **The undeclared exclusion** — you took a reference component and deleted the parts that did not apply, and no record of what you removed survives. *Warning sign:* you cannot say what the original contained that you decided you did not need.
- **The shape check read as a working system** — a scheduled job goes green every week, nothing in it builds or runs anything, and the team reports the tick as evidence of health. *Warning sign:* you cannot name a single thing the test would have to execute in order to fail for a functional reason.

## Exercises

**None of these is assessed.** The audit is carried into [S050](/wiki/seminars/S050.md), where its opaque class must still be populated and the assessor chooses the region you defend.

**1 · Write the check for a region you are sure of.** Pick the region you understand best. Write the sentence that would fail if its behaviour were wrong, then write the check.

> **Model answer shape.** A pass states a behaviour under a condition: *when the upstream call returns a partial result, the caller receives no total and one escalation is emitted.* **A fail restates the implementation** — *the function calls `retry` once then calls `escalate`* — which cannot discriminate between a correct implementation and a wrong one, because it is the implementation. The discipline generalises: decompose the system into features and the scenarios each feature must handle, and let each scenario become one assertion ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/husain-your-ai-product-needs-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/husain-your-ai-product-needs-evals.md)). **That source is one practitioner's case study with no control condition and no measured improvement; take the pattern, not the tooling.**

**2 · Score one region under two split strategies.** Run the same region under the default and under one other. Report both, then write the sentence that reconciles them.

> **What you should find.** That the region which recovers well from a bad first attempt scores differently depending on where the conversation was cut, and that neither score is wrong. The documentation states it plainly: **how you split determines what you are evaluating.** **A team that never chose deliberately is reporting an answer to a question it did not ask** — so the deliverable here is the declared choice, not the number. Record the version of the documentation you checked; the note behind this was read through search excerpts, several evaluators are in preview, and one consulted page is a superseded variant.

**3 · Go back to your own item 5, then break it.** Find what you wrote for opening item 5 — the check that would fail if the region were wrong, or the reason you could not write it. **Do not rewrite it yet.** Put it beside the criterion below and write one sentence naming the difference.

> **Criterion.** An answer passes if the check could fail while the code still runs. It fails if it is (a) a restatement of the implementation, (b) a claim about the code's structure rather than its behaviour, or (c) an appeal to a test that already exists — because *the tests pass* is the premise of the whole problem, not a response to it. **And an honest "I could not write one" passes, provided it names what stopped you**: not knowing the intended behaviour, not knowing what would count as wrong, or not being able to observe the thing that would go wrong. **Those three are different debts and they are settled by different people.**
>
> **Why the comparison step is mandatory rather than encouraged.** The research this session's structure rests on tested exactly this: showing learners a correct answer next to their own wrong one produced no benefit, and only the group *instructed to compare them* learned more ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). Reading the criterion is not the exercise. Naming the difference between it and what you wrote is.

**4 · Justify one threshold or delete it.** Take a judged check with a pass mark. Write why that mark and not one either side of it.

> **Scoring criterion.** A pass names a consequence: what happens to a user, an operator or a downstream system at each side of the line. **A fail says the default was reasonable.** The documentation is explicit that the threshold is separable from the measurement, so moving it changes the pass rate without changing the system — which means an inherited threshold is a policy somebody else set for a situation that was not yours. **If you cannot justify it, delete the check rather than keeping a number nobody can defend**, and record that you deleted it.

**5 · Establish whether your judge is independent.** For any model-judged check in your specification, say whether the judge shares a model family with your executor, and what follows.

> **What you should find.** That by default it does, and that this is the documented condition called self-enhancement bias — a judge tends to prefer output from its own family ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/zheng-mt-bench-chatbot-arena.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/zheng-mt-bench-chatbot-arena.md)). The related and worse condition is **limited reasoning**: a judge cannot reliably grade a problem it cannot itself solve, which is exactly your situation on an opaque region. **The framework's own documentation publishes no agreement rate with human judgement for any evaluator, no calibration data and no threshold rationale, so nothing licenses you to assume the judge is right** — and this session asserts no agreement figure for any of them. **That bias work predates current models; its taxonomy has held up better than its figures, none of which is reproduced here.**

**6 · Have the agent falsify your explained class.** Run workflow step 8 and count how many *explained* regions survived.

> **What to notice.** The survivors are usually the regions with the narrowest claims, and the casualties are the ones where you wrote a claim broad enough to feel like understanding. **Expect your classes to move, and expect to be annoyed by it** — that movement is the criteria-drift phenomenon, in which grading outputs is what allows the criteria to be defined ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/shankar-evalgen.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/shankar-evalgen.md)). **Its evidence is qualitative and from a small interface study; it establishes the phenomenon and does not quantify it.** Note also that this instrument is not independent of your executor, and record that beside the result.


**7 · Predict a scaffold failure in writing, then cause it.** Run workflow step 9. Remove the flag that creates the identity principal, write down what you expect, provision, and compare.

> **What you should find.** That the failure arrives as **an opaque platform error code that names nothing about the missing flag**, and that nothing in your prediction resembled it unless you had read the generated configuration. **The exercise is the prediction, not the fix.** Two further observations belong in your audit beside it: that the diagnosis for this failure **is not on any published documentation page** but ships inside an editor extension versioned separately from the documentation and undiscoverable from it; and that it is therefore **field-derived, unverified and citable only by extension version** — so you have just relied on a hypothesis rather than a fact, and should say so. **The general lesson survives the product entirely: the material that explains a generated project is frequently not where the generated project's documentation is.**

**8 · Date the debt you cannot date.** For the scaffolded regions, write when they stop being maintainable and how you know.

> **Scoring criterion.** A pass states that **you cannot know**, and says why: no compatibility matrix is published for the tooling, no support window is stated for a version, and **nothing anywhere states how long a generated project remains buildable.** The existence of an upgrade command is the only published acknowledgement that a project can fall out of step with its generator, and an upgrade path is not a maturity date. **A fail invents a date, or says the question does not apply because the project is new** — which is the same error as treating a green build as comprehension, moved onto the time axis. **A liability with no stated maturity is not a smaller liability; it is one nobody will schedule.**

## What this does not establish

Nothing here shows how much comprehension debt costs, how fast it accrues, or whether generated code accrues it faster than written code.

**The claim is a practitioner judgement from a collection with no measurement of it and no measurement of anything else that matters** — no defect data, no incident data, no maintainability data, no post-deployment record, and no failed engagement anywhere in approximately ninety documents. The phrase is excellent and the evidence behind it is one person's experience, stated once. It is access-controlled, paraphrased and never quoted here.

**Nothing here shows that writing an evaluator produces understanding either.** The session's claim is narrower: that being unable to write one is evidence of not understanding. The converse — that being able to write one is evidence of understanding — is not established and is probably false, because a narrow check can be written by somebody who has grasped one behaviour and nothing around it. **The audit records a lower bound on ignorance and not an upper bound on comprehension**, and a learner who leaves believing otherwise has acquired a false confidence.

**The evaluation framework's documentation is definitional and is silent where it matters most.** It accurately describes what the tooling computes. **No agreement rate with human judgement is published for any evaluator, no calibration data, and no rationale for any default threshold** — so it is no evidence at all that what it computes is right. It was read through search-returned excerpts rather than full page fetches, many evaluators are marked preview, one consulted page is an explicitly superseded variant, and the surfaces differ across languages.

The vendor documentation for the agent mode establishes the **mechanism** — that confirmation is dismissible and can be made standing, and that the quickstart itself recommends the option that removes approvals — and establishes nothing about consequence. **It publishes no convergence measure and no false-completion measure**, so the frequency of the failure this session is designed around is unknown, including to the vendor, or at least unpublished by it.

**The evaluation literature is qualitative, practitioner or dated.** The criteria-drift work establishes that the phenomenon occurs and matters and does not quantify its prevalence; the practitioner essay rests on one case study with no control and no measured improvement; the judge-bias work predates current models and its specific figures are not reproduced here. **The professional-formation sources are cited for direction only:** one was read from an abstract, one has a very small sample in a narrow population and is described by its own authors as out of date, and one reports variance explained from correlational studies subject to a range-restriction objection its design cannot resolve. **No magnitude from any of them appears in this session.**

**The three-class audit is this design's invention.** No source proposes it, the boundary between *operational* and *opaque* is a judgement rather than a criterion, and two auditors will classify the same region differently. That is stated here rather than hidden, and [WP-048](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-048.md) §8 concedes it properly.
