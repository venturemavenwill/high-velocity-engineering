# WP-019 — Contention, Queueing and Why Latency Is Not Throughput

**Seminar day:** [S019](wiki/seminars/S019.md) · **Module:** [M04](wiki/modules/M04.md) · **Quarter:** [Q2](wiki/quarters/Q2.md)
**Strands:** SY · MS · CF · **Complexity class:** 2
**Platform anchor:** Foundry tokens-per-minute allocation as the operating point; rate-limit enforcement windows; the shared standard pool · **MCP servers:** Azure MCP Server (read) · Microsoft Learn MCP Server

## Abstract

This paper defends teaching queueing behaviour through measured curves and a single arithmetic relation rather than through analytic queueing theory, and defends identifying the paging cliff and the queueing knee as two members of one class of non-linear saturation. The first argument is that the competence required is the ability to reason about where a system sits on a curve and what moves it, which measured curves supply directly and which analytic derivation supplies only after machinery that would consume the day; and that Little's law is the one relation that connects the three quantities and can be applied without that machinery. The second argument is that two mechanistically unrelated phenomena sharing a curve shape is the strongest available evidence that saturation is a structural property rather than a memory-specific accident, and that meeting them on consecutive days is the only opportunity the timetable offers to make that generalisation. The paper records that this day implements the two-signal comparison WP-018 identified as unmade, and that it now also implements the arrival-side variability requirement this paper's own earlier version identified as unmade. It concedes that the day teaches variability's importance without any apparatus for reasoning about it, that its curves were long measured on an uncharacterised arrival process, and that the platform's curves are a composite of queueing and admission control which the day explains as though it were only the first.

## 1. The instructional problem this day must solve

Human intuition about queues is reliably and severely wrong. Asked what happens to response time as a system goes from half loaded to ninety per cent loaded, almost everyone predicts something like a doubling. The true answer is a large multiple, and at ninety-five per cent it is a much larger one. This is not a subtle error; it is an error of kind, because the underlying relationship is not linear and no amount of care with a linear model recovers it.

The error is expensive precisely where it matters. Capacity decisions are made by people extrapolating from observed behaviour at moderate load, and the extrapolation is wrong in the dangerous direction — it underestimates how bad things get. Systems are consequently run closer to saturation than their operators believe is safe, because their operators' mental model says the margin is larger than it is.

The day's second problem is that the mean conceals the mechanism. Two services with identical mean service times behave very differently if one has variable service times and the other does not, and the difference is dramatic near saturation. Nothing in an average exposes this. Students who have spent M03 learning that averages conceal distributions now meet the most consequential instance of it, and phase 3's second condition is constructed so that the only difference between the two curves is a quantity that does not appear in any mean.

The third problem is a generalisation the module has been set up to make. S018 produced a performance cliff from a positive feedback loop in memory pressure. S019 produces a curve of the same shape from an entirely unrelated mechanism — the compounding probability of arriving to a busy server. Two unrelated mechanisms producing one shape is evidence that the shape is structural, and point 5 makes non-linear saturation a named class. Students who hold that class recognise it later at S062's batching curves, at S064's quota behaviour and at S077's exhaustion attacks, none of which are memory or queueing phenomena.

The fourth problem is that S013's caller C has been outstanding for two weeks. Phase 2 reopens it and phase 8 closes it explicitly.

## 3. Theoretical basis for the design

### 3.1 Load profile and element interactivity

**The module's joint peak with S016, and the harder of the two for most students.** The compounding argument at point 2 requires holding two effects that reinforce each other — higher probability of finding the server busy, and longer queues when you do — and neither is intuitive alone. The day then adds a second dimension, variability, whose effect interacts with utilisation rather than adding to it.

Management:

- **No analytic derivation.** The curves are measured. Deriving them would require a probability apparatus students do not have and would consume the entire day.
- **One relation only.** Little's law, stated and used. Chosen because it connects the three quantities, is applicable arithmetically, and requires no distributional assumptions.
- **Phase 3 supplies full distributions,** not summaries, so that the mean-versus-tail distinction is visible rather than argued.
- **Seven utilisation levels with the knee between two of them,** the same coarse-sampling decision as S018 and for the same reason.
- **The same substrate service across the module,** for the fourth consecutive day.
- **Step 7's first signal may be reused from S018,** which removes a construction task and focuses the step on the comparison.

### 3.2 Desirable difficulties selected, and those deliberately not

**Selected.**

- *Quantitative extrapolation at items 1 and 2,* where the error is large and immediate.
- *Sketching both the mean and the tail curve before seeing data,* phase 3, which forces a commitment about the relationship between them.
- *The variability condition,* which defeats any model built on means.
- *Predict-then-load to locate the knee,* step 2.
- *The two-signal comparison,* step 7 — WP-018's identified improvement, implemented here.
- *Defending an operating point to someone with an opposing financial interest,* phase 7.

**Deliberately not selected.**

- *Analytic queueing theory.* Refused entirely. Kendall notation, arrival process taxonomies and closed-form results are all omitted.
- *Deriving Little's law.* Stated and used.
- *Distributional analysis of the variability condition.* Measured, observed, deferred to S028.
- *Withholding the compounding explanation.* Point 2 works it fully.
- *Assessment stakes on the same day,* though S020 is the next day and the module artefact is due.

**The platform instance, scoped in the strong form.** The day teaches a rate-limited deployment as the site of the utilisation-latency relationship, and the simplification must carry its safe and unsafe conditions.

*Safe.* Choosing where to sit relative to an allocation; reasoning about why a mean rate that fits inside a limit is nonetheless refused; predicting that variability in either request size or arrival timing degrades behaviour near the limit; and defending an operating point to somebody who wants it moved. Each of these holds regardless of what is happening inside the service.

*Unsafe.* Reasoning about **what the observed latency is made of**. The curve a student measures on this platform is a composite: some of it is service time, some is the retry delay their own client introduced after a refusal, and some is a shared pool they cannot observe. A student who reads it as a queueing curve will attribute all of it to compounding and will propose remedies — more capacity, smaller requests — for a delay their own backoff created. It is equally unsafe to carry the **denominator** across: on a standard deployment the effective limit can be reduced by the service without the configured quota changing, so "we are at eighty per cent of capacity" is a statement about a number that moves.

The boundary is met in the artefact rather than only in this paper: **step 1 requires the latency distribution to be reported separately for first-attempt and retried requests**, which makes the composite visible in the student's own data, and step 5's currency clause is where a student reasoning from an unstable denominator would fail.

### 3.3 Engagement and motivational mechanism

The day runs on **an extrapolation error of a magnitude students find hard to believe**.

Items 1 and 2 give a known response time at fifty per cent utilisation and ask for it at ninety and ninety-five. The answers students give are low by a large multiple, and — importantly — students are confident. The subsequent measurement is not a correction of a hesitant guess but a refutation of a firmly held model, which is the more memorable event.

Phase 3's variability condition supplies the second surprise, and it is the more professionally consequential one. Two services, same mean service time, and one is far worse. Students who have absorbed M03's lesson about averages recognise the shape immediately; those who have not meet it in its starkest form.

Phase 7 supplies a different engagement: it is the first adversarial defence with money on the other side. The partner's single question — why not run closer to saturation and save the money — is the question a real finance-side stakeholder asks, it is entirely reasonable, and the only adequate answer is the curve. Students find this phase demanding and report it as the most realistic thing they have done.

Competence is protected by the fact that point 2's explanation, once given, is genuinely satisfying and fully accounts for the measured curves. The day has an unusual property for M04: it resolves completely.

## 4. Phase-by-phase design and its warrant

**Phase 1 (25 min).** Two extrapolations at increasing utilisation, then a question about doubling server speed whose answer is not "it halves" — because halving service time at fixed arrival rate changes utilisation, which moves the operating point non-linearly. Item 4's accuracy band is the calibration. **Item 5 is the sealed Copilot prediction**, whose expected answer — that the agent will report a sustainable rate as a per-minute average — is the day's own payload arriving as a frontier datum.

**Phase 2 (20 min).** The final item reopens caller C at a two-week gap, immediately before resolution. This is the module's third kept deferral and the design's expectation is that by now students notice the pattern, which is itself the intended lesson about how the programme treats promises.

**Phase 3 (45 min).** Seven demand levels, full distributions, dual sketches, then four stream configurations, **executed against a metered deployment**. Sketching both mean and tail before the data forces students to commit to a relationship between them, and most draw them as roughly parallel, which is wrong in the way that matters. **Case C is the payload and it is the arrival-side condition this paper's earlier version identified as missing.** Its per-minute rate is identical to case A's and its requests are identical, and it is refused where A is comfortable, because enforcement operates over windows of a second or ten seconds. Nothing in any per-minute figure predicts it, which is the strongest available statement of the day's thesis. The closing task — which curve would you show to a person deciding about capacity — is the day's professional question and anticipates phase 7.

**Phase 4 (50 min).** Nine points, the module's longest. Point 2 is the central explanation and is worked completely. Point 5 is the generalisation and is the module's most important single claim. Point 7 supplies the only arithmetic. **Point 8 is the perishable platform-specific point and is also a documentation silence stated as one:** the enforcement window is given as typically one or ten seconds, which one applies is not published, and the finest published time grain for the platform metrics is one minute — so the window in which a client is judged is shorter than the finest window in which it can observe itself. **Point 9 makes the operating point a bet with a named currency**, which is S007's framing on its sixth application and is the form [S058](wiki/seminars/S058.md), [S062](wiki/seminars/S062.md) and [S064](wiki/seminars/S064.md) inherit.

**Phase 5 (90 min).** Seven steps at class 2, **shipping a client that holds a stated operating point** under real traffic. Step 1's first-attempt-versus-retry split is the §8 objection-3 fix. Step 3's *which caller said so* clause continues the module's insistence that objectives have origins. Step 5's currency clause is what stops the operating point being a number with no owner. Step 6 makes variability a measured quantity on both sides. Step 7 implements WP-018's improvement.

**Phase 6 (40 min).** A capacity decision correct on mean utilisation that failed under a burst, then an agent failure. The instructor case is chosen because arrival variability is the part of the day that transfers least readily — students accept service-time variability from phase 3 and rarely extend it to arrivals. **The agent case is the same blind spot relocated into the instrument**: a capacity probe that ramps smoothly, reports the last rate at which nothing failed, and is correct about a workload nobody asked for. It is chosen because the workload a generated benchmark produces is the part of an agent's output that reviewers reliably do not read.

**Phase 7 (30 min).** The paying stakeholder. One question, repeated, answerable only with the curve. This is the S020 rehearsal placed one day early and deliberately unassessed. **The partner is briefed to press the uncomfortable first answer** — that on a standard deployment the headroom is not costing money at all, because quota is documented as a policy limit with no associated cost — which forces the student off the price and onto the real currency. Without that briefing the phase can be won by reciting a figure, which is the failure mode the platform introduced and the briefing removes.

**Phase 8 (20 min).** Prediction ratios at both utilisations, sketches against the curves, sealed predictions opened, and the explicit closure of caller C with the interval named.

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**Students with more relevant prior knowledge.** A student who has taken a queueing or performance-modelling course will know the shape and may predict items 1 and 2 correctly. Predicted failure: they answer from a memorised model rather than from the compounding mechanism, and are then unable to handle the variability condition, which their memorised model — usually assuming exponential service — does not distinguish from the base case. Countermeasure: phase 3's second condition is where their advantage disappears, and they are asked at phase 4 to state which of their model's assumptions the condition violates.

**Students with less relevant prior knowledge.** The compounding argument is the module's hardest explanation and it arrives on the module's fourth consecutive high-load day. Countermeasures: no derivation, measured curves, one arithmetic relation, a supplied loading procedure, and a reusable first signal. Residual risk: step 6's attribution of the knee's position to variability may be beyond them, and the fallback is to report the measured variance without attributing its effect, which is honest and passes.

**Phase 3 is too easy.** Not a live risk. The mean curve is sketched wrongly by most, the tail curve by nearly all, and the variability condition by essentially everyone. Fallback if a group sketches all three correctly: ask them to predict the effect of *reducing* variability while raising utilisation to keep mean response time constant, which is a genuine trade and is hard.

**Affective failure.** The characteristic risk is **the fourth consecutive difficult day**, which is a module property rather than a day property. Students arrive at S019 having been wrong about layouts, wrong about schedules, wrong about the memory curve, and are about to be wrong about queues — and S020's assessment is the next day. The cumulative effect is a plausible loss of confidence immediately before an assessment occasion.

Countermeasures are structural. The day resolves completely, which none of the prior three did — point 2 fully explains the measured curves and nothing is deferred. Phase 7 gives students a defensible position to hold rather than an error to absorb. And S020 is a defence of their own argument rather than a further correction. The design's position is that the module's shape — four days of being wrong followed by a day of defending a conclusion — is correct, and that the risk is concentrated at exactly this point and is managed by S019 resolving rather than by lowering the module's demands.

## 7. Assessment and evidence generated

No entrustment evidence.

One specification item in SY102 requiring the full chain from measurement to two compared signals, **plus the prompt record, the intervention record and the frontier statement**.

**An operating point defended without reference to the measured curve fails.** This is the day's central discipline and is the same clause, in a new domain, as S016's punished pattern and S015's rejected alternatives: a class-2 decision must be shown to have been made against evidence.

**An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that the design could have detected an intervention-worthy error. A load generator that reports its own arrival-window distribution rather than only its mean rate is the accepted demonstration, and it is deliberately the artefact that would have prevented the phase 6 failure.

Programme instrumentation: items 1 and 2's prediction ratios are the cohort's non-linear extrapolation baseline, re-probed at S062; phase 3's dual sketches are the mean-versus-tail relationship measure; step 7's signal comparison quality is the detectability baseline and supersedes S018's defeat rate as the carried measure to S068; the phase 7 defence quality is the informal predictor for S020; **item 5's sealed prediction accuracy is the module's fourth frontier datum and the last before the assessed one**. The frontier statements sample one cohort on one substrate with one model family, per instrumentation rule 3.

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: teaching queueing without any distributional apparatus leaves students unable to reason about the thing the day says matters most.**

At its strongest: point 4 establishes that variability dominates behaviour near saturation, and the day supplies no means of reasoning about variability at all. Students can measure a variance with supplied tooling and cannot say what distribution produced it, cannot distinguish a heavy tail from a wide but bounded spread, and cannot predict how variability will change under any modification. The day therefore installs a belief — variability matters enormously — with no capacity to act on it, which is a worse position than not knowing, because the student now has a factor they know is decisive and cannot estimate.

The rebuttal is that the alternative orderings are both worse. Teaching the distributional apparatus first would require S027 and S028 to precede S019, which the course structure does not permit; teaching queueing without mentioning variability would leave students with a model that is wrong in the direction that causes incidents. Given that neither is available, introducing variability as a *measured and observed* effect, with the analysis deferred to a named later day, is the least-bad ordering.

The concession is that this is now the fifth instance of the same structural problem in Q2, and the fourth whitepaper to concede it. WP-011 conceded measurement ahead of measurement theory, WP-012 conceded it again, WP-016 named it a curriculum-sequencing problem, WP-018 named it a fourth instance, and this is a fifth. At five instances the correct characterisation is no longer "forced local compromise." It is that **Q2's systems content structurally requires Q3's measurement content**, and the programme has sequenced them the wrong way round. The available fix is not local: it is to move ME101 into Q2 and displace one of the existing courses, which is a curriculum-level change this design has not made and has not seriously costed. Recording it a fifth time without acting is itself a finding.

**Objection 2: the measured curves depend on an arrival process the design has never characterised.**

At its strongest: the entire day rests on curves measured from the substrate under real traffic. The shape of those curves depends on the arrival process — whether requests arrive independently, in bursts, with daily periodicity, or correlated with each other — and the design has not characterised it anywhere. Phase 3's second condition varies *service* time variability while holding the arrival process fixed at whatever the substrate happens to produce, and phase 6's disclosure is about arrival variability specifically, which means the design knows arrivals matter and has not measured them. Students are therefore shown curves whose most important input is unexamined, and told to reason from them.

This objection the design does not fully answer. Characterising the arrival process would require distributional tools the day does not have, which is objection 1 again in a different place. But the objection identifies something objection 1 does not: the design has made the *service*-side variability a taught and measured quantity at step 6 while leaving the *arrival*-side variability as an anecdote at phase 6, and there is no principled reason for the asymmetry. Both are measurable with the same tooling; only one is required.

The fix is small and **is now made**. Step 6 requires the arrival-side variability alongside the service-side, each with its contribution to the knee's position, and phase 3's case C makes arrival timing a measured condition rather than an instructor's story. The platform is what made it cheap: request rate is enforced over windows of a second or ten seconds, so a burst is not merely theoretically worse but observably refused, and a load generator that can vary its own arrival pattern is ten lines.

**The concession is retained rather than deleted, because the finding is about how the design works and not about this day's content.** The asymmetry existed for one reason: service-time variability was easy to produce on the original apparatus and arrival-time variability was not, and the design wrote its curriculum around what its tooling made convenient and then defended the result on pedagogical grounds. That is the failure worth remembering. The repair arrived because the substrate changed, not because the argument improved, and a design that only fixes what its instruments make easy will keep producing this shape.

**Objection 3: the curves are not queueing curves, and the day explains a mechanism the students did not measure.**

This is the objection that bites on the practical turn, and it bites on the day's central explanation rather than on its periphery.

At its strongest: point 2 accounts for the non-linearity by compounding — as utilisation rises, the chance of arriving to a busy server rises and the queue joined is longer. That is a correct account of a queue. It is not what happens on a rate-limited deployment. A request over the limit is **not queued; it is refused**, and the latency the student records is largely the delay their own client chose before trying again. The service did not make them wait; it made them come back. The day therefore presents a compounding explanation, indexed to curves produced substantially by admission control and client backoff, and **the fit is close enough that neither staff nor students will notice the substitution.** The design's own instrumentation rule 3 requires a day to state how its evidence was sampled; the strongest reading of that rule here requires stating what produced the curve, and the day did not.

The rebuttal is genuine but narrower than the objection. There is real queueing behind a model endpoint, and the platform documents an effect of it: time to first response is documented as rising with prompt size and falling cache-hit size, which is service-time behaviour and not admission control. Case C's burst effect is an enforcement-window phenomenon and is neither. So what students measure is a **composite of at least three mechanisms**, one of which point 2 explains correctly.

The concession is that the day never decomposes it and, with the tooling available, largely cannot. What it can do is stop presenting the composite as a single quantity.

**The cheap fix is one clause and it is made.** Step 1 now requires the latency distribution to be reported separately for requests that succeeded on the first attempt and requests that were retried. Every client already knows which is which, the split costs nothing, and it puts the composite in front of the student in their own data: the first-attempt distribution is service behaviour, the retried distribution is a decision the student made, and the difference between them is the part of the curve that point 2 does not explain. A student who sees those as two distributions will not confuse a backoff schedule with a queue.

What remains conceded and unfixed is the third component. The shared pool's contribution is unobservable from the client, the documentation gives no way to distinguish a pool-driven reduction from a self-inflicted one except by reading a header after the fact, and no available instrument separates them. The day says so and stops there.

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** Item 2 predictions will be low by a larger factor than item 1 predictions, and the ratio of errors will exceed the ratio of true values. *Instrument:* prediction ratios at both utilisation levels. **This is the claim that the error is one of kind rather than of degree.**

**Prediction 2.** A large majority of phase 3 sketches will draw the mean and tail curves as roughly parallel. *Instrument:* sketches coded for the divergence relationship between the two curves.

**Prediction 3.** Students with prior queueing coursework will predict items 1 and 2 substantially better and will handle the variability condition no better than students without. *Instrument:* item accuracy and phase 3 second-condition performance, split by the admission background survey.

**Prediction 4.** Step 7's two-signal comparisons will be rated higher in usefulness than S018's single-signal artefacts, blind-rated against a common rubric. *Instrument:* blind comparative rating of S018 and S019 signal artefacts. **This tests whether WP-018's identified improvement was in fact an improvement.**

**Prediction 5.** Before step 1's split is required, a majority of students asked what their measured latency is made of will attribute it to the service; after the split, a majority will identify their own retry schedule as a component. *Instrument:* a free-response probe at the start and end of phase 5, coded for attribution. **This is the §8 objection-3 estimate. If the post-split rate is not higher, the split is being computed and not read, and the fix has produced a column rather than an understanding.**

## Evidence status

**Verified in this repository.** The utilisation-latency relationship, variability's effect near saturation, tail-latency reporting and the capacity-planning failure in phase 6 are grounded in [research/02-technical-foundations/](research/02-technical-foundations/collected-materials.md) and [research/05-fde-craft/](research/05-fde-craft/collected-materials.md). The detectability and signal-comparison position follows [research/03-measurement-evaluation/](research/03-measurement-evaluation/collected-materials.md).

**Grounded in vendor documentation, with its version and its silence recorded.** The enforcement and allocation behaviour at phase 3 and points 4, 6, 8 and 9 is taken from Microsoft Learn's quota how-to for Azure OpenAI in Microsoft Foundry Models, the Azure OpenAI monitoring data reference, and the provisioned throughput concept and billing pages, all read 2026-07-26. Specifically: request-rate limiting **evaluates the rate of incoming requests over a small period, typically one or ten seconds**, so a burst inside that window is refused even where the per-minute total is within the limit; standard deployments **share a resource pool across customers** and the service may **temporarily reduce a deployment's effective rate limit**, visible in `x-ratelimit-limit-tokens` and not in the configured quota; the platform metrics for this service are published with a **one-minute time grain**; time to response is documented as **increasing as prompt size increases and cache hit size reduces**, and as being an approximation; PTU quota is **a policy limit enforced by Azure with no associated cost**; and provisioned deployments are billed **hourly on the units deployed regardless of the number of tokens consumed**.

**Three silences are recorded and one is taught.** The documentation does not say **which** enforcement window applies to a given deployment, only that it is typically one or ten seconds, which is the silence point 8 names to students and which is load-bearing because it is the difference between a burst that is refused and one that is not. The temporary effective-limit reduction is documented as resolving "typically within a few hours" with **no bound and no notification**, so the day's most important contention effect has no published duration. And one metric in the reference is annotated "Do not use for Azure OpenAI service" **without stating what it does measure**, which is the sharpest single instance in this module of vendor documentation warning without explaining. Consistent with the folder-wide asymmetry in [research/06-microsoft-platform/](research/06-microsoft-platform/collected-materials.md), all of these sources state windows, grains and prices and **none states an accuracy or a frequency**, and none is asserted here.

**Cited from general knowledge, not verified here.** Contrasting cases across two dimensions, prediction before adjudication and quantitative extrapolation error, sketching as externalisation, cognitive load and compounding-mechanism explanations, retrieval practice at expanding intervals, and error management training are cited for **direction and mechanism only**. No effect size is asserted for any of them.

**Design reasoning with no external warrant.** The refusal of analytic queueing theory in favour of measured curves plus one relation; the five-item phase 1 and the doubling-speed item; the seven-level coarse sampling and the dual-sketch demand; the four stream configurations and the elevation of the arrival-timing case to the payload; the nine points of phase 4 and the elevation of non-linear saturation to a named class; the two-currency form of the operating point and the claim that it is what survives into S058, S062 and S064; the seven-step phase 5 with the first-attempt-versus-retry split, two-sided variability attribution and two-signal comparison; phase 7's single-question adversarial defence with the briefed follow-up; the explicit closure of caller C with the interval named; and all five §9 predictions are this design's own reasoning, untested. The §8 objection-1 concession records the fifth instance of Q2 requiring practice ahead of its justification, states that the correct characterisation is now a curriculum-sequencing error rather than a series of local compromises, and names an uncosted structural fix. The objection-2 concession is retained with its fix recorded as made, together with the finding that the repair arrived because the substrate changed rather than because the argument improved. The objection-3 concession states that the day's central explanation does not account for most of what its students measure, records the first-attempt-versus-retry split as the fix and as made, and records that the shared pool's contribution remains unobservable and unseparated.
