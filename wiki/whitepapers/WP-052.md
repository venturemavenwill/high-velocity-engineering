## In plain terms

This session teaches a rule that is both the best discipline in the module and the source of its largest blind spot, and it teaches both halves at once because they are the same rule.

**A threat survives only if it can be bound to a named component and a numbered flow.** Everything else is discarded with a logged reason.

The rule is genuinely good. It is what stops a threat model becoming a list of things true of software in general. It makes every entry actionable, because an entry that names a component tells an engineer where to go, and it makes the whole registry joinable by the next stage.

**And it removes, systematically and quietly, everything about the operator, the supply chain, human process and out-of-band administration.** Each of those is discarded with an entirely plausible justification, individually written, collectively catastrophic, and read by nobody.

The session's response is not to weaken the rule. It is to add an artefact the source material does not have: **the discards grouped into categories, so that the shape of what the method cannot see is written down once rather than scattered across a log.** That register is the assessed artefact, and it is assessed because **a method that never states its own scope exclusions invites the reader to assume it has none.**

One more thing is installed here and it is easy to skip. **Your candidate threats came from somewhere, and that somewhere has blind spots which are now yours.** Nothing in the pipeline behind this module records the currency of its knowledge source in any output, and a stale source produces a confident, well-formatted, out-of-date model with no signal that this happened.

## Abstract

This paper defends the binding rule as the constitutive discipline of a system-specific threat model, and defends the addition of a grouped exclusion register that the source material lacks.

**First**, the case for binding. A threat model is a claim about a particular system, and a claim that would survive being pasted into a different system's model unchanged is not a claim about this one. Binding is the cheapest available test of that property and it is checkable by any reader. It also makes a threat assignable and closable: the source design's own closure criterion requires a claim of implementation to cite a file and a location, and **evidence cannot be re-derived for a threat that names nothing.**

**Second**, the case for the register. The binding rule systematically removes operator, supply-chain, human-process and out-of-band-administration harms, each with an individually plausible logged reason. The source design's evaluator then explicitly refuses to deduct for missing threats, so **recall is unmeasured and the design declines to measure it.** The asymmetry is structural rather than accidental: precision is elaborately defended — anti-junk-drawer rules, semantic gates, a stated preference for leaving a threat unmapped — and nothing comparable guards recall. This session's response is not to weaken the rule but to require the discards be grouped into named categories, so that the shape of what the method cannot see fits on one page.

**Third**, the platform work. The vendor's workload-posture surface is the session's anchor because it is one of the few pieces of first-party documentation that names AI-native attack classes rather than restating classical ones, and because its **attack path analysis over "toxic combinations"** encodes the claim the module needs: a combination binds to a path rather than to a component. The workflow deliberately has learners read attack paths rather than recommendations, because **a recommendation is not a threat**, and converting one into the other without adding a component and a flow is how a registry fills with entries nobody can test.

The paper concedes three things. That the register depends on somebody choosing to read it, and that no gate this module is entitled to build would change that. That the session's prohibition on operational detail produces a thinner artefact than a practitioner would build — a constraint imposed from outside the design, which the paper declines to present as a pedagogical virtue. And that the anchor documentation publishes no detection rate, no false-positive rate and no coverage measurement of any kind, so nothing in the workflow's machine half is evidence that a scanner finds what is there.

## 1. The problem this session must solve

A learner asked to enumerate threats will produce entries that are true, serious, and about nothing in particular.

*Data could be intercepted in transit.* *An attacker could gain unauthorised access.* Each is correct. **None tells an engineer where to go, none can be joined to a control, and none can ever be closed**, because there is no place to look for evidence that it was addressed. The registry fills, the coverage looks good, and the artefact has become a hazard: it occupies the space where a real finding would have gone and reads, to anyone scanning, as work.

The binding rule solves that completely and creates a second problem in the same motion. **Real harms that involve no dataflow simply vanish** — the administrator acting outside the system, the dependency compromised upstream, the process step where a human is the control. Each is discarded individually, with a reason that is true, and nobody ever sees the pile.

The session's response is to enforce the rule strictly and to make the pile an artefact.

## 2. Entry state and prior knowledge

**Entry state: this-programme-only.** The session cannot start without [S051](/wiki/seminars/S051.md)'s numbered flows and characteristics checklist, and it uses the checklist rather than a mnemonic as the enumeration key.

It retrieves [S007](/wiki/seminars/S007.md), which is the same shape of question asked of people rather than of threats — who is not in the room — and [S032](/wiki/seminars/S032.md), whose pre-mortem is the only prior instrument the learner has for imagining a harm before it occurs. It assumes [S042](/wiki/seminars/S042.md)'s distinction between scope that grows and a backlog item that does not, because a registry entry is going to become a work item.

**The prior it works against is that coverage is the goal.** A learner arrives believing a good threat model is a long one, and the session's first assessed artefact is a list of things they deleted.

## 3. Theoretical basis

**3.1 Load profile.** Moderate intrinsic load, high evaluative load in the adversarial re-read. Applying the rule is mechanical; reading your own discard log as an adversary is not, because every reason in it was written by somebody who believed it.

**3.2 Opening item 3 is a generation phase whose product is designed to be destroyed.**

Productive Failure is two-phase and order-dependent: learners generate solutions to a problem targeting a concept they have not been taught, fail, and are then taught ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). This session uses an unusual variant. **The learner's generated answer is not wrong. It is correct and about to be excluded**, and the instructive failure is the exclusion rather than the answer.

That variant is defensible on the same mechanism. The **failed generation effect** holds that attempting an answer before instruction improves later learning even when the attempt is wrong; here the attempt is right and the *method* is what fails, which produces the same awareness of a gap between what the learner holds and what the method can hold. Kapur's own concession is relevant and is stated in his book: impasses are **sufficient but not necessary** for the awareness that drives learning. This session manufactures the awareness by a different route and claims no more warrant than that.

The consolidation condition binds exactly as elsewhere. **Presenting a correct answer beside a learner's own achieves nothing unless the learner is instructed to compare them**, which the book reports from Loibl and Leuders. Exercise 3 is that instruction and is mandatory rather than encouraged.

**The source's limits travel with it.** Trade book; school, university and laboratory evidence; **no adult-professional study in the reference list**; several underlying studies, including Loibl and Leuders, reported at second hand through the book rather than read directly.

**3.3 Why this session's difficulty is the right kind, and where that claim runs out.**

The error-management training meta-analysis distinguishes **analogical transfer** — applying a trained procedure to a familiar-shaped task — from **adaptive transfer**, defined as "using one's existing knowledge base to change a learned procedure, or to generate a solution to a completely new problem" ([research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md](/research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md)). Both effects were positive in that synthesis; the adaptive one was the larger contrast, and the authors are explicit that **the critical dimension is task distinctiveness rather than task difficulty.**

That matters for how this session is built. Applying the binding rule is analogical — the same operation over and over. **Reading your own discard log adversarially, and grouping losses into categories that name what a method cannot see, is adaptive**: it requires changing the procedure rather than executing it. The session spends its evaluative budget there deliberately, and that allocation is the design's use of this literature.

**The limits are severe and must be stated.** Every study in that pool compared error management training against *another* training method; **none used a no-training control**, so all effects are relative. **No study measured on-the-job performance** — every outcome is a laboratory task. The homogeneity test across the pool was not significant, so the moderators were theory-driven partitions of a set that did not statistically require partitioning, which the authors declare openly. **Direction only is used here and no magnitude is asserted anywhere.**

**3.4 Why binding is constitutive rather than optional.** A threat model is a claim about a particular system. A claim that would survive being pasted into a different system's model unchanged is not a claim about this one. **Binding is the cheapest available test of that property** and it is checkable by any reader, which is why the session states it as an artefact property rather than as a quality. The practitioner judgement underneath is sharper and comes from the source design: **generic mitigations are worse than absent ones, because they cannot be assessed, implemented or falsified** ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-agentic-threat-modelling.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-agentic-threat-modelling.md)). An absent mitigation is visibly missing; a generic one occupies the slot where the missing thing would have been noticed.

**3.5 Why the discard log is the informative artefact.** A reviewer handed a list of surviving threats will validate them, because plausible entries read as correct and there is nothing to notice. **The information is in what is absent, and absence is only reviewable if it was written down.** This is the same argument [S040](/wiki/seminars/S040.md) made for the unproven list, applied to threats.

**3.6 Why grouping is the addition that matters.** A log of individual discards, each with an individually sound reason, is unreadable as evidence about the method. **Grouped into categories, the same log becomes a statement of scope exclusion** — one page saying what this method cannot see. The source has the log and not the grouping, and the consequence is visible in it: nobody there notices what the rule removed, and no stage anywhere in fourteen definitions states a scope exclusion.

The production machine-learning literature supplies the general form of this argument in a different domain: **"a model with good performance 'on average' may still exhibit failure modes that are not apparent by looking at an aggregate metric"**, from which it derives the requirement to slice, and the observation that **which slices matter is a domain question rather than a statistical one** ([research/02-technical-foundations/crowe-machine-learning-production-systems.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/crowe-machine-learning-production-systems.md)). A registry with good aggregate coverage can be uniformly blind to one class, and no summary will show it. The reverse index and the grouped register are both slicing instruments.

**That source's limits are heavy.** Practitioner guidance from a commercial publisher: no study design, no comparison group, no measurement that its practices outperform anything; three of four authors at one large vendor; some foundational claims resting on **anonymous practitioner testimony**. Its informational-against-behavioural harms split, used in the session, is itself reported at second hand from a privacy research organisation. **Every quantity in it is a property of one system after one run; none appears here.**

**3.7 Why the candidate source must be dated.** The knowledge base behind an enumeration is a single point of failure whose currency is invisible. **A stale source does not fail loudly; it produces a complete-looking artefact.** Recording what the source was and when it was current is the only cheap countermeasure, and it is one the source design does not take anywhere in any output.

The retrieval-failure literature gives the general principle: without a stage-wise vocabulary "every failure looks like the same undifferentiated problem of the answer being wrong", and diagnosis therefore means locating a stage before explaining a cause ([research/02-technical-foundations/barnett-rag-failure-taxonomy.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/barnett-rag-failure-taxonomy.md)). *Our threat model was inadequate* names no stage. *Our threat model inherited a catalogue that predates agentic retrieval* names one. **That source is an experience report over three systems with no frequency data, no severity data and no controlled comparison, and its named failure points are recorded in this repository as reconstructed rather than quoted.**

**3.8 Why the platform half reads attack paths rather than recommendations.**

The workload-posture documentation names **attack path analysis**, whose subject is "toxic combinations": individually acceptable configurations that compose into a breach, with the specific concern that data may be exposed during **grounding** and **fine-tuning** ([research/06-microsoft-platform/purview-dspm-for-ai-and-defender-ai-spm-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/purview-dspm-for-ai-and-defender-ai-spm-docs.md)). Two things follow for the session's design.

**A combination binds to a path, not to a component**, which is why the workflow asks whether the registry has an entry bound to a *sequence* of flows rather than to a single one. And **grounding and fine-tuning are the two moments a model touches proprietary data**, which makes them the highest-value flows to have numbered — they are where data can leak by construction rather than by attack.

The same documentation names AI-native attack classes rather than restating classical ones: prompt injection, wallet attacks, model theft and data poisoning, alongside data breach and denial of service. **The wallet attack is the one worth teaching for its shape**: it drives cost rather than downtime, follows directly from usage-based billing, and violates availability by a route that leaves every service healthy and every log clean. Classical security models do not cover it because classical systems did not charge per unit of work.

**Its limits are stated flatly.** First-party and definitional. **No detection rate, no false-positive rate and no coverage measurement is published — none is asserted here or anywhere in this wiki.** One of the two pages behind the note is an explicitly superseded classic version whose replacement was not read; the other was read through search-returned excerpts rather than fetched in full, so its structure is reconstructed. Agent-level capability moves to a separate licence during the life of this material. Its stated operational bounds — scan scope, cadence and result latency — are **the resolution of the instrument**, and are paraphrased qualitatively throughout the session and this paper rather than reproduced.

## 4. Session design and its warrant

The eight sections run situation, artefact, material, workflow, standard, failure modes, exercises, limits — with one addition unique to this session: a stated restriction on operational detail, placed before the artefact.

**Opening item 3 asks for a harm involving no dataflow at all, and it is asked before the binding rule is stated.** The learner writes something real, and then adopts a rule that deletes it. **That sequence is the session**, and it is why the item cannot be moved after the instruction.

**Opening item 5 has one honest answer.** A threat model with no open questions and no discards is evidence that the rule was not applied, and a cohort that arrives believing it is evidence of thoroughness is the cohort this session is written for. **Be more suspicious, not less, of a threat model with nothing missing.**

**The enumeration key is the characteristics checklist rather than a mnemonic**, because a mnemonic asks the same questions of every system and the checklist asks the ones this system earned. That is [S051](/wiki/seminars/S051.md)'s artefact doing the work it was built for.

**Exercise 2 withholds the registry deliberately.** The reviewer is given only the discard log. This is the design's one executable check in the session — a person, not the author, looking at the thing the author has the strongest incentive to stop reading.

**The confidentiality restriction is stated inside the session rather than in a preamble.** A learner who meets an unexplained thinness will assume the material is thin; a learner told that operational detail exists and is deliberately withheld will read the artefact correctly. §8 concedes what that costs.

**4.1 Why the workflow reads attack paths rather than recommendations.** Step 6 sends the learner to the machine's *paths* and away from its *advice*, and the distinction is the workflow's whole teaching load. A recommendation is an imperative — *use private endpoints* — and pasting it into a registry produces an entry with no component, no flow and nothing to test. The corresponding threat is that a named component is reachable from a network it should not be reachable from, over a numbered flow. **A learner who cannot perform that conversion will fill a registry with controls and then, at [S053](/wiki/seminars/S053.md), map controls to controls and call the result coverage.**

**4.2 Why the machine step comes after the hand pass and not before.** A scanner's output is a strong anchor, and a learner who reads it first will enumerate against it rather than against their own characteristics checklist — which reintroduces the catalogue ceiling at the exact moment the session is trying to make it visible. The ordering is deliberate and follows the same logic as [S051](/wiki/seminars/S051.md)'s inventory step.

**4.3 Why the attack step withholds the registry from the agent.** The prompt gives an agent the compact index and the exclusion register only, and asks for a class of harm absent from both. Handing it the surviving threats would produce validation, for the same reason a human reviewer produces validation: **reading a list of plausible entries is not a test of anything.** The information is in the absences, and an instrument pointed at the absences is the only one that can find them.

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**The general threat.** *Countermeasure:* the copy-paste test in the standard — an entry that would survive being pasted into another system's model is not a finding about this one.

**The forced binding.** A real process or people threat attached to an adjacent component so that it survives. *Countermeasure:* the standard's warning sign — the flow number is arbitrary and you know it — plus the exclusion register, which gives the threat somewhere legitimate to go. **The register is what makes the honest discard cheaper than the dishonest binding**, and that is its main design purpose.

**The plausible discard.** *Countermeasure:* the adversarial re-read at workflow step 7, and exercise 2's inversion. **This is the design's strongest countermeasure and it is still weak**, because it depends on a reviewer who cares.

**The empty exclusion register.** *Countermeasure:* opening item 3, which guarantees every learner has at least one entry before instruction begins.

**The catalogue ceiling.** *Countermeasure:* the dated-source requirement. **This is a disclosure, not a fix.** Nothing here lets a learner see past their source.

**The recommendation wearing a threat's clothes.** A scanner's advice pasted in as a registry entry. *Countermeasure:* the standard's warning sign — the entry is phrased as an imperative — and exercise 5, which requires the learner to count these rather than merely avoid them. **Counting is the countermeasure**, because the conversion feels productive and produces no visible defect until two stages later.

**The operational drift.** *Countermeasure:* the stated restriction and the standard's warning sign — somebody could follow it.

## 7. Assessment and evidence generated

No entrustment evidence. Two constituents are assessed at [S055](/wiki/seminars/S055.md): that every surviving threat names a component and a flow number, and that the exclusion register exists, is grouped by category, and names its categories rather than its incidents.

**Neither the registry's completeness nor the correctness of any individual discard is assessed**, and §8 defends the first while §1 explains why the second is not available.

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: the exclusion register is a second document nobody will read, and adding it does not fix the problem it names.**

At its strongest: the source material already has a discard log, and the paper's own diagnosis is that nobody reads it. Grouping the same content into categories does not create a reader. **It creates a tidier artefact with the same fate**, and it lets the author feel the omission has been handled when nothing has been handled at all — which is worse than the untidy log, because the untidy log at least looks unfinished.

**Substantially conceded.** The register does not fix the omission; it makes the omission legible, which is a smaller claim and the only one available. Two things distinguish it from the log: it is assessed, so at least one person reads it; and it fits on a page, so a reader who wants to know what the method cannot see gets an answer rather than a search task. **The declined fix is to make the register a gate — nothing proceeds until every category has been dispositioned by a named person.** It is declined because a gate whose satisfaction condition is that somebody wrote something in a box is precisely the sign-off placeholder the source already has, and which does nothing there. **This module will not add a control it has just finished explaining is not one.** The residual dependence on a willing reader is real and unresolved.

**Objection 2: a threat model with no operational specificity cannot be acted on, and this session teaches a sanitised version of the job.**

At its strongest: knowing whether a control stops an attack requires knowing how the attack works. The distinctions this module insists on at [S053](/wiki/seminars/S053.md) — channel against payload, ingress against egress, data plane against management plane — are operational distinctions, and a learner who has never seen the operational form will collapse them exactly as the module says practitioners routinely do. **You are asking for judgement that requires knowledge you have withheld.**

**Conceded, and the paper declines to dress the constraint as a virtue.** The restriction is external: the source material is access-controlled, this repository is public, and reproducing operational detail is not a decision this design is entitled to make. **The declined fix is to teach the operational half in a closed setting alongside the public material.** It is declined because a programme whose artefacts are public cannot depend on a private supplement without becoming untranslatable to anyone reading it. **The gap is real, it is stated in the session rather than hidden, and a learner should assume their threat models will be thinner than a practising security engineer's until they acquire that knowledge elsewhere.**

**Objection 3: teaching the binding rule at all is teaching a method whose known failure direction is towards the harms that matter most, and calling the register a countermeasure is a rhetorical move rather than an engineering one.**

At its strongest: the paper concedes that binding removes operator, supply-chain, human-process and out-of-band harm. Those are not a minority of real incidents. A method whose known bias is towards the technically tractable and away from the organisationally consequential should be presented as a *partial* instrument used alongside something else — not as the constitutive discipline of threat modelling. **By making binding the rule and the exclusion register the consolation, the module has ranked them, and it has ranked them the wrong way round.**

**Partially conceded, and the ranking is deliberate rather than accidental.** The defence is narrow: binding is what makes the *technical* half of the analysis actionable, and this module's remit is the technical half. **What is not defensible is presenting the technical half as the whole**, which is exactly what the source design does by stating no scope exclusion anywhere. The register's job is to prevent that specific misreading, and the paper accepts that this is a smaller claim than the objection demands.

**The declined fix was to add a parallel non-dataflow threat method — an actor-and-authority analysis — as a second half of this session.** It is declined on the same grounds [WP-051](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-051.md) §8 gives: a module whose argument is that one artefact must be finished before the next begins cannot demonstrate that by running two incomplete analyses in parallel. **The cost is that a graduate of this module has one method and a written statement of what it misses, rather than two methods**, and this paper records that as an accepted cost rather than a solved problem.

**Objection 4: the workflow's platform half rests on a scanner whose recall is unpublished, in a session whose entire subject is unmeasured recall.**

At its strongest: step 6 sends the learner to compare their registry against machine-discovered attack paths. **The documentation behind that capability publishes no detection rate, no false-positive rate and no coverage measurement.** So the learner is asked to calibrate an instrument of unknown recall against another instrument of unknown recall, and any agreement between them is uninformative. A session that spent its first half explaining that recall is unmeasured should not spend its second half implying that a scan closes the gap.

**Conceded, and the session is written to avoid the implication rather than to escape the objection.** The workflow's step 6 asks specifically for two findings, and the *second* — items that are recommendations rather than threats — does not depend on the scanner's recall at all. That half of the exercise is about genre, not coverage, and is unaffected. The first half is exactly as weak as the objection says, and the session's own limits section says so. **The declined fix was to remove the machine comparison entirely.** It is declined because the recommendation-versus-threat confusion is the single most common defect in real registries, and a scanner's output is the cheapest place to meet it. **The cost is that one half of one workflow step generates a comparison the learner cannot interpret**, and it is labelled as such rather than quietly kept.

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** Most exclusion registers will list incidents rather than categories at first attempt. *Instrument:* the submitted register, coded *categorical* / *mixed* / *incident list* by a second reader.

**Prediction 2.** Learners will discard fewer candidates than a second reader applying the same binding rule to the same candidate list. *Instrument:* independent application of the rule by a second reader, compared against the learner's discard log.

**Prediction 3.** Reviewers given only the discard log will identify a wrongly discarded threat for most learners. *Instrument:* exercise 2's written outcome, recorded per learner as *found* / *not found*.

**Prediction 4.** Learners who record their candidate source and its currency will revise their registry after the adversarial second agent; those who do not will not. *Instrument:* the registry's revision record against the presence of a dated source statement, paired per learner. **If this fails, the dated-source requirement is disclosure with no behavioural effect and should be stated as such rather than taught as a countermeasure.**

**Prediction 5.** In exercise 5, a substantial proportion of the items learners initially transcribe from machine-generated posture output will be recommendations rather than threats — imperatives with no component and no flow. *Instrument:* the learner's own two counts, audited by a second reader against the submitted registry.

**Prediction 6.** Learners instructed to compare their item-3 answer against the taught criterion (exercise 3) will place unbindable threats in the exclusion register more often, and force-bind them into the registry less often, than learners who read the same criterion without the comparison prompt. *Instrument:* the registry and register together, coded for force-binding by a second reader, across two cohorts — or, absent two cohorts, a within-cohort split on whether exercise 3 was completed. **This is the session's only direct test of the mechanism §3.2 rests on.**

**Prediction 7.** Exclusion registers will be read by nobody except the assessor. *Instrument:* access or citation of the register in any downstream artefact — [S053](/wiki/seminars/S053.md)'s mapping, [S055](/wiki/seminars/S055.md)'s plan defence — counted per learner. **This predicts the failure of the session's own central countermeasure**, and it is included because §8's first objection is that the register creates a tidier artefact with the same fate as the log. If it holds, the register's value is confined to the act of writing it, and the paper should say so rather than claiming a reader it does not have.

## What a learner should take from this paper

**What the design claims.** That binding is what makes a threat model about your system, that the same rule blinds it in a predictable direction, and that the honest response is to write the blindness down rather than to weaken the rule.

**What the evidence licenses.** Nothing about recall. **The source design declines to measure it, the anchor documentation publishes no coverage measurement, and this session cannot measure it either.** The exclusion register makes visible only the omissions you watched happen; the threats nobody thought of remain invisible, and no artefact in this programme changes that. The teaching *method* has a reasonable warrant from two converging literatures, and that warrant is about ordering and difficulty — it says nothing whatever about whether threat models built this way find more real vulnerabilities.

**No magnitude appears anywhere in this paper**, including for the vendor material, which publishes none, and for the pedagogical sources, which state several that this repository does not reproduce.

**The concession worth remembering.** The reasons in your discard log will all be good ones. That is the failure mode, not the defence.
