## In plain terms

**What this paper argues, before the formal version.**

Somebody with standing sends you a specific, reasonable request. You build it. By every measure your organisation applies, you have done your job.

What is hidden is that the request is not an input, it is an output. Somebody noticed a difficulty, formed a theory of what would help, translated that theory into something they thought could be built, and then into something they thought would be agreed to. Four steps, each of which threw information away, none of which you can see. **You are receiving the last one and treating it as the first.**

The obvious remedy fails, and the day spends time on why. Asking a requester what they really want produces a second solution, because a solution is the thing people have practised producing. What works instead is asking about behaviour: what did you do last time, how long did it take, what did you do afterwards. Those are questions about a past that happened rather than a preference someone is constructing on the spot.

There is a reason this matters more now than it used to, and it is the reason the day was rebuilt. An engineer who accepted a request without examining it used to be protected by a delay. Building took days, and somewhere in those days the gap usually surfaced — a conversation, an edge case, somebody's question. **That protection was accidental, it was real, and it is gone.** An agent will author, deploy and verify the request inside an afternoon, competently, with nothing a review would catch. The gap did not change. The time you had to notice it collapsed.

So you will build the request exactly as written, watch it work, and then be asked what it established. The answer, uncomfortably, is very little.

**What this paper concedes, and it is the module's central worry.** Six hours ending in a running system feel like engineering. Six hours ending in a question feel like a seminar. The module's whole thesis is that the second is worth more — and by adding the build, the design has handed premature convergence a more satisfying form than it had before. **One artefact element, never tested, carries the whole weight of that risk.** If your statement of what the build did not establish reads as a formality while your list of beliefs gets shorter, the objection was right.

## Abstract

This paper defends treating a feature request as an instrument standing in for an unobserved need, in exactly the sense established for measurement at S026, and defends prohibiting any proposed solution in the module's artefacts. The first argument is that a request is the output of an analysis the engineer did not see, performed under conditions the engineer will not share, and frequently derived backwards from a solution the requester already had — which makes it evidence with a provenance rather than a specification, and makes the construct-instrument apparatus directly applicable. The second argument is that premature convergence is the module's defining failure and that it is invisible in the artefact, since a problem statement written to fit a held solution is indistinguishable from one derived from evidence; the prohibition removes the artefact in which the convergence would be recorded, and phase 7's reverse-inference test detects the residue. The paper additionally defends replacing the question about what the requester really wants with four behavioural questions, and defends the day's most contested decision — that students build the request literally, deploy it and measure it — on the ground that an agent has removed the delay in which engineers used to notice they were building the wrong thing, which makes the request-need gap harder to see and more expensive to miss. It concedes that the central prohibition may be unenforceable in the way that matters, that its adjudicating cases are selected from a history that guarantees the conclusion, and that requiring a deployment on a day about restraint is a risk the design has mitigated and cannot demonstrate it has managed.

## 1. The instructional problem this day must solve

A request arrives, specific and reasonable, from someone with standing. The engineer builds it. This is the default and it is not obviously wrong — by every standard the organisation applies, an engineer who builds what was asked has done their job.

What is concealed is that the request is a *conclusion*. Someone observed a difficulty, formed a model of what would help, translated that model into something they thought was buildable and would be agreed to, and wrote it down. Four transformations, none visible, each lossy. The engineer receives the fourth output and treats it as the first input.

The framing that makes this tractable is at point 3: **the request is an instrument and the need is the construct**. This is S026's apparatus applied to a sentence rather than to a metric, and the four gap mechanisms transfer directly — the request is a proxy, it was formed under conditions unlike the ones that matter, it addresses a component of the need, or it addresses something the need caused. Phase 2's bridge asks students to make this identification themselves, before it is stated.

The day's second problem is what to do instead, and the obvious move fails. Asking a requester what they really want produces another solution, because a request is the artefact people have practised producing and preference questions elicit more of them. Point 5's replacement — four questions about behaviour rather than about preference — is the intervention, and step 9 requires the student to write one.

The third problem is the module's and begins here. **Premature convergence** is M08's defining failure: the student forms a solution early, writes a problem statement that implies it, and presents the statement as derived. The two artefacts are identical, so no inspection separates them. The module's response is structural — no artefact in M08 may contain a proposed solution — and phase 7's reverse-inference test is the detector for the residue that survives the prohibition.

**The fourth problem is new and it is the reason this day was rebuilt.** Until recently, an engineer who accepted a request without examining it was protected by a delay: implementing the request took days or weeks, and somewhere in that period the gap usually surfaced — a conversation, an edge case, a colleague's question. The protection was accidental and it was real. It is now gone. An agent given a request will author the policy, author the infrastructure, deploy it and report success inside an afternoon, competently, with no defect a review would find. **The request-need gap has not changed; the time available to notice it has collapsed, and the noticing was never a deliberate practice.** That is the day's actual argument for existing at all, and it is stronger than the argument the day had before.

The design's response is to make students produce the artefact themselves rather than be warned about it. Step 4 has them build the request as written, deploy it into a disposable environment, and drive traffic through it. What they get is a working system that answers no question they had, and the experience of holding one is the day's most transferable outcome.

## 3. Theoretical basis for the design

### 3.1 Load profile and element interactivity

**Low to moderate, and the module's lowest — but higher than it was.** Nothing on the day is technically difficult. The interactivity is at step 2, where the beliefs required for a request to be correct must be enumerated — which requires holding the request, a model of the requester, and a model of the situation simultaneously.

The genuine difficulty is not load but **restraint**. Students can do everything the day asks and will find it uncomfortable to stop before proposing something.

The build adds load and the design accepts a specific amount of it. Step 4 introduces a service, a policy, a deployment path and a tool configuration that are all new this quarter, and if the student spends the phase debugging infrastructure the day's actual work does not happen. Three decisions bound this: **the agent does the building**, which is the point rather than a shortcut; **the environment is disposable**, so nothing has to be got right; and **the deployment is not assessed for quality** — the specification checks that it exists and what it produced, not whether it is good infrastructure.

Other management:

- **The request is supplied verbatim** and is short.
- **Phase 3 supplies four requests over one shared object,** so the gateway is understood once and varied along one dimension.
- **Step 2 is worked with the room,** because belief enumeration is unfamiliar and students otherwise produce two items.
- **Step 9's question forms are supplied as examples,** since writing a behavioural question is a skill nobody has.
- **One running need across five days,** so context is paid for once, and **one service across five days**, so the platform is paid for once.

### 3.2 Desirable difficulties selected, and those deliberately not

**Selected.**

- *Item 2's near-identity with item 1,* which students produce themselves.
- *Classifying requests before executing them,* phase 3, with the finding that the request was insufficient evidence.
- *Enumerating the beliefs required,* step 2 — the day's discriminator.
- *Building the request and finding that it works,* step 4, which is a difficulty of a kind the programme has not previously imposed: the student's own successful deployment is the evidence against the position they are being pulled toward.
- *Stating what the build did not establish,* step 7.
- *Writing a behavioural question,* step 9, and having the room judge whether it is one.
- *The reverse-inference test,* phase 7.
- *The solution prohibition,* which is a difficulty of restraint rather than of reasoning.

**Deliberately not selected.**

- *Interviewing anyone.* S037's work. This day works from documents, data and one deployment, which is deliberate: the request must be exhausted as evidence before a person is consulted.
- *Requirements notation.* No user stories, no use cases, no formal templates. All would supply a structure that substitutes for the analysis.
- *Organisational and political analysis of requests.* Named at point 2's scope statement and deferred to S081 and S082.
- *Infrastructure quality.* The Bicep is not reviewed as Bicep. That is SY301's material and reviewing it here would convert the day into a platform day.
- *Proposing anything.*

**Scope on the enumeration, per the [Whitepaper Standard](/wiki/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md), strong form.** Point 2's four shaping forces treat the request as produced by an individual. They omit organisational incentives that reward requesting one thing rather than another, and the case where a request is a compromise among several people with different needs. **Safe** as a set of questions to ask about a request from an individual whose situation you can investigate. **Unsafe** as an account of how requests are produced in organisations, where the requester may not be the need-holder and may not know what the request was traded against — which is S081 and S082's material and which a student applying this day's four forces to an organisational request will get wrong.

**Scope on the platform instance, strong form.** The day teaches the request-need gap using a gateway policy as the thing requested, and the correspondence must carry its boundary because students will otherwise carry it into work this day does not supervise.

*Safe.* Reasoning about what a request written in a platform's vocabulary does and does not specify; recognising that a correct implementation of a request can leave the requester no better off; and using a deployment as a way of exhausting a request as evidence. In each of those the instance is doing genuine work and the reasoning transfers.

*Unsafe* in three directions, and the day states all three. **It is unsafe as a description of API Management.** The four cases are chosen for their relationship to a need, not for their coverage of the service; a student who leaves with a model of how the gateway works has learned something the day did not check. **It is unsafe as a claim about agents in general.** What is demonstrated is that this agent, on this task, with this documentation available, produces working output — and the finding at [research/04-professional-formation/dellacqua-jagged-technological-frontier.md](/research/04-professional-formation/dellacqua-jagged-technological-frontier.md) is precisely that capability is uneven in ways not predictable from perceived difficulty, so a student who generalises from one afternoon has drawn the conclusion the source says not to draw. **And it is unsafe as a licence for the reverse conclusion.** The day's rhetorical shape invites the reading that agent-built work is suspect. It is not: the agent's output was correct, and correctness was never the problem. **The unsafe move is treating the agent's speed as evidence about the request, in either direction.** Step 7 is where a student applying the correspondence outside its range fails, and point 10 delivers the boundary to the room rather than to this paper.

### 3.3 Engagement and motivational mechanism

The day runs on **four real requests, executed**.

Phase 3's construction is the strongest available and the execution strengthens it further. Students classify requests from the substrate's own history before seeing what happened, and most groups get at least one wrong. The closing task then produces the phase's finding — that nothing in the request could have told them — which is a stronger result than a claim that they misread it, and is what motivates the whole module.

Case D is where the phase now bites hardest, and it bites because the room built it. The policy is correct. The deployment succeeds. And the outcome turns on two properties of the counter — that a single counter serves every scope sharing a key, and that usage is tracked per gateway rather than aggregated across the instance — neither of which is in the request and both of which are in the documentation. **A group that has just deployed a correct thing and watched it produce a complaint does not need to be told that requests are insufficient evidence.**

Item 2 supplies the opening. Asked what the requester wants, as distinct from what they asked for, most students restate the request in different words. The distribution shown at phase 8 makes this a cohort property, and the point is not that students are unimaginative but that a request supplies no purchase on the question.

Item 5 supplies a second and sharper one. Students are asked to predict what the agent will get wrong, and most predict a defect. There is no defect. The design expects the cohort's item 5 accuracy to be low in a specific and informative direction, and phase 8 shows the distribution before anything is said about it — because the finding is more persuasive as a fact about the room than as a claim from the front.

Phase 6's disclosure carries the day's most useful detail: when a requester is asked why they never used the thing they asked for, they almost never say they asked for the wrong thing. They describe a circumstance the engineer never knew about. That framing removes blame from the requester, which matters, because a module that teaches students to distrust requests can easily produce contempt for requesters — and point 7 exists against exactly that. **Point 7 is now deliberately placed before point 8**, so that the agent material cannot be read as a further reason for suspicion.

The affective risk is the restraint. A student who has understood the problem and may not say what to do about it will find the day incomplete, and the design accepts this: the incompleteness runs for five days and is the module's shape. **The build makes this risk larger and the design says so in §8.**

## 4. Phase-by-phase design and its warrant

**Phase 1 (25 min).** A verbatim real request. Item 2's collapse into item 1 is the finding. Item 3 — what would you need to know — is the day's move and is what step 2 formalises. **Item 5 is the sealed Copilot prediction**, administered here and not later per instrumentation rule 8, and its expected failure mode is itself instructive: students predict a defect and there is none.

**Phase 2 (20 min).** The bridge asks which S026 gap mechanism a feature request most resembles. The answer is a proxy chosen for a purpose, and having the students make the identification is what makes the rest of the day an application rather than a new topic.

**Phase 3 (45 min).** Four requests over one object, classified before execution, then **executed against a disposable gateway through the agent**. The design's decision to include case C — built as asked, solved a different problem, requester happy — is important: without it the phase teaches that requests are unreliable, and with it the phase teaches that the relationship between request and outcome is not predictable from the request, which is the accurate and more useful conclusion. Case D is the payload and its wrongness is invisible in the diff, which is what distinguishes it from an ordinary defect. **The phase's sampling is disclosed to the room**, per instrumentation rule 3; see §8. Two behaviours are demonstrated rather than operated — cross-gateway aggregation and the v2-versus-classic rate-limiting difference, both requiring resources the programme does not hold — and neither is assessed.

**The reading pass was added to this phase on 2026-07-30 and occupies the environment-provisioning interval, which was previously dead time.** Each group classifies one whole section of the engineering playbook page by page — substantive practice, tool-bound recipe, worked example instance, or stub and link list — and defends the classification rather than the verdict. **It is not assessed and adds nothing to the specification**, which is why it could be added to a day whose checked-element list is otherwise settled. Its warrant is that the day's own point 6 asks students to treat a request as evidence with a provenance licensing a limited conclusion, and the reading pass asks exactly that of the document the strand rests on, one level up and one hour earlier. **The design's reason for preferring a real document to a constructed one is that the playbook is sincere**: it is published for use by people who believe in it, its excellent pages and its empty ones are written in the same voice, and nothing in its navigation distinguishes them — which is the condition the students will meet in an internal wiki and cannot be faked in an exercise built to be seen through. The pass also settles something practical for the four days that follow: which of the playbook's pages this module may lean on is decided by the students who classified them.

**A third task was added to the pass on the same date and it is the only part of it that points forward rather than sideways.** Each team must name at least one place where a page in its section disagrees with another page of the same document, and say which of the two it would follow. **The task is cheap because the disagreements are dense** — the note behind the source records that it contradicts itself in at least a dozen places and never notices, across testing, trade studies, diagrams, secrets, branch naming, mocking and sustainability — and it is worth its minute because of where it lands: **an unadjudicated contradiction is a decision the organisation never made**, and [S089](/wiki/seminars/S089.md) is where handing such a document over becomes a warranty that is unenforceable on the disputed points. The task is unassessed, like the rest of the pass, and it is the only element in M08 that reaches a Q9 day.

**Phase 4 (50 min).** Ten points. Point 2 carries the enumeration scope statement. Point 4 explains why the obvious remedy fails, which prevents students from adopting it. Point 5 supplies the replacement. Point 7 is the day's guard against overreach. Point 8 states what an agent changes and, more importantly, what it does not, and its final clause — spend the time you saved on the question the speed was hiding — is the day's only prescriptive sentence. **Point 9 names four defaults the platform chose**, which is the [Copilot Practice Standard](/wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md) requirement; the four are selected because each has a consequence a student can be shown rather than told. Point 10 carries the platform scope statement in its strong form.

**Phase 5 (85 min).** Nine steps at class 3 on the module's running need, **producing a deployment**. Step 2 is the discriminator. Steps 3 and 5's checkable-versus-uncheckable split is where the day's work actually happens — most beliefs turn out to be checkable against the substrate's own data, which students do not expect, and step 4's deployment answers several more. **Step 7 is the countermeasure to the build's principal risk** and is the element §8's third objection turns on. Step 9 makes the day's output an input.

**Phase 6 (40 min).** Something built as asked and never used, with the requester's own explanation, then the agent case. The requester's explanation shape — a circumstance the engineer never knew — is the first disclosure's content and is the day's argument against contempt. The agent case is chosen so that **the review that would have caught it is not a review anybody knows to perform**: what-if short-circuited, the preview omitted the module silently, and on the CLI version in use the diagnostic that reports this is not displayed. The reviewer read a clean preview of a deployment they had not seen.

**Phase 7 (30 min).** Reverse inference, now with the step 8 addition. The partner states what solution the problem statement implies, checks whether the student held it beforehand, and then judges whether the stated changing-evidence is plausible for the stated problem. The second half is the repair this paper's §8 previously identified as costless and unmade; it is now made, and it puts one of the day's two convergence detectors inside a phase whose outcome the artefact records.

**Phase 8 (20 min).** The item 1 and item 2 distance, the phase 3 finding, **item 5 opened in two parts**, and a room vote on whether each step 9 question is behavioural. The vote is the cheapest available quality check on a skill nobody in the room has practised.

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**Students with more relevant prior knowledge.** A student with product or consulting experience will know that requests are not requirements and will produce a fluent step 6 problem statement. Predicted failure: they hold a solution and write the statement to fit it, more skilfully than an inexperienced student would, and phase 7's partner will find the implied solution matches their prior intent. Countermeasure: phase 7 detects exactly this, their partner is briefed to press, and the step 8 plausibility judgement is now part of the test.

**Students with less relevant prior knowledge.** A student who has never questioned a request may find step 2 unreachable — enumerating the beliefs required for a request to be correct is genuinely unfamiliar. Countermeasures: step 2 is worked; phase 3 has supplied four executed examples; and steps 3 and 5 make the enumeration useful immediately, which sustains the effort.

**Students who disappear into the platform.** New, and the build's characteristic failure. A student unfamiliar with the gateway may spend step 4 learning API Management rather than examining a request. Countermeasures: the agent builds; the environment is disposable; the deployment's quality is not assessed; and the instructor's brief is to unblock infrastructure problems directly rather than to let them be productive struggle, because on this day they are not. Residual risk: a student who has been fascinated by the tooling will write step 7 as a formality, and nothing detects that.

**Phase 3 is too easy.** Cases A and B are usually classified correctly. Cases C and D are not, and the misclassifications are informative in different directions. Fallback if a group classifies all four: ask them to write the request that *should* have been made in case D, which is a construction task and previews S039.

**Affective failure.** Three.

The first is **contempt for requesters**, which is the module's characteristic overreach and which point 7 and phase 6 both target. A student who leaves believing that requests are usually wrong has learned something false — phase 3's case A is built as asked and works — and something professionally corrosive.

The second is **frustration at the prohibition**. Countermeasure: the prohibition is stated with its duration — five days — and its reason, and S040 is named as where it ends. An open-ended restriction is oppressive; a bounded one is a discipline.

The third is **premature satisfaction**, and it is introduced by the build. A student who has deployed something working feels they have done a day's engineering, and that feeling is exactly what the module is trying to withhold. Countermeasures: step 7's statement of what the build did not establish, the specification's failure clause on solutions, and the placement of an unshippable day at the end of the module. **This is a proposed remedy in the sense of instrumentation rule 5's amendment, not a validated one**, and its reuse at S037, S038 and S039 is recorded as contingent on this day's reported outcome.

## 7. Assessment and evidence generated

No entrustment judgement — the quarter's is at S040.

One specification item in PE201. Per the [Assessment Architecture](/wiki/program/03-Assessment-Architecture.md)'s rule 1, the checked elements are: presence of the verbatim request, a belief enumeration of at least four items, a checkable-versus-uncheckable split, a deployed build with its measurements, evidence of checks performed against substrate data, a problem statement with a stated confidence, **a statement of what the build did not establish**, a statement of what evidence would change the characterisation, one behavioural question, **and the prompt record, intervention record and frontier statement required by the [Copilot Practice Standard](/wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md)**. **Nothing else is assessed** — in particular, not the quality of the infrastructure.

**An artefact containing a proposed solution fails.** The module's standing rule.

**An artefact whose build changed the substrate's user-facing behaviour fails.** The module's build rule, which is what allows the prohibition and the [Copilot Practice Standard](/wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md)'s build requirement to hold simultaneously.

**An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that the student's own checks would have caught an intervention-worthy error. The escape clause is the sensitivity discipline [S052](/wiki/seminars/S052.md) established and [WP-048](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-048.md) §8 corrected, and it does real work here: a student who reconciled a what-if output against the deployed state built the detection the record would otherwise have to evidence.

Programme instrumentation: the item 1 to item 2 distance is the cohort's request-need-gap baseline, re-probed at S046; the phase 3 misclassification pattern is the request-insufficiency measure; **the phase 8 room vote on behavioural questions is the cohort's first peer-judged skill assessment since S025** and its distribution is compared against staff judgement of the same questions; **item 5's two-part accuracy is this module's frontier-calibration datum** and is read by the following year's cohort through the [Durable and Perishable Register](/wiki/program/09-Durable-and-Perishable-Register.md), per instrumentation rule 9.

The frontier statements are a sample and the day says so, per instrumentation rule 3: one cohort, one substrate, one service, one request shape, on a task chosen by staff because a documented non-obvious failure was available in it. A frontier map assembled from them describes this task and not the agent.

Dispositions: **humility**, at step 6's stated confidence, where a high confidence after checking three beliefs against a database and deploying one policy is the easy and unwarranted answer — and is now easier, because the deployment feels like corroboration and is not.

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: the solution prohibition is unenforceable where it matters.**

At its strongest: the prohibition removes solutions from the artefact and cannot remove them from the student's head. A student who has decided what to build will write a problem statement that implies it, will check the beliefs that support it, and will ask the user a question designed to confirm it — and every one of those acts satisfies the specification. The prohibition therefore prevents the *recording* of premature convergence while leaving the convergence itself untouched, and may make it worse by driving it underground.

The rebuttal is that the prohibition is not the only mechanism and was never meant to be. Phase 7's reverse-inference test targets exactly the residue the objection describes, and step 8's requirement — what evidence would change your characterisation — is difficult to answer honestly for a characterisation written backwards, because a solution-derived statement has no evidence that would change it.

The concession as previously written was that neither mechanism was assessed. **That concession is now partly discharged and the fix it named is made.** Phase 7 requires the partner to judge whether the stated changing-evidence is plausible for the stated problem, and rule 3 requires the phase 7 outcome in the artefact, so one of the two detectors now sits inside the assessed record. What remains unfixed is the other: step 8 is still checked for presence rather than for quality by the specification itself, and the design's position is that a specification assessing the quality of a hypothetical would be assessing prose rather than reasoning. That is a defensible position and it is not a costless one.

**Objection 2: phase 3's cases are selected from a history that guarantees the conclusion.**

At its strongest: the requests are chosen by staff from the substrate's records, with their outcomes known, to produce a specific distribution — one success, one failure, one surprise, one backfire. That distribution is not the substrate's actual distribution of request outcomes, which is almost certainly dominated by requests built as asked that worked adequately. Students are shown a curated sample and draw a conclusion about requests in general, which is a selection effect of exactly the kind S027 condition D taught them to detect. The day is running the failure it teaches.

This objection is correct on the facts and the design's response is to change what is claimed rather than to dispute it. The phase's stated conclusion is that **the request was insufficient evidence to classify** — not that requests usually fail. That conclusion is supported by a curated sample, because it is a claim about the request's informational content rather than about base rates, and a curated sample establishes it more efficiently than a representative one would.

**The disclosure this paper previously identified as absent and costless is now made.** Phase 3 states the sample's construction to the room, in the terms the day would demand of a student, which is what instrumentation rule 3 requires. That rule's own provenance, recorded on the [Seminar Day Design Pattern](/wiki/program/04-Seminar-Day-Design-Pattern.md), cites this day as its first instance; **the citation describes the day as it stood before the Q4 re-anchoring and is deliberately left standing, so that the failure remains legible in the same way the [Whitepaper Standard](/wiki/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md)'s scope-rule widenings do.** The rule is unaffected: it was always about days generally and never about this one.

**Objection 3: making the day build-centred crowds out the problem-construction work it exists for.**

This is the objection that bites on the practical turn, it is the module's central risk rather than this day's, and it is the strongest of the three.

At its strongest: the day now contains a service, a policy, a deployment tool, an agent configuration and an eighty-five-minute phase whose most conspicuous output is a running system. Attention is finite. A student who has never touched API Management will spend step 4 on API Management, and the belief enumeration at step 2 — which is the day's actual discriminator and takes fifteen unglamorous minutes — is the thing that will get compressed. Worse, the day now supplies exactly the completion it is trying to withhold: **six hours that end in a working deployment feel like engineering, and six hours that end in a question feel like a seminar**, and the module's whole thesis is that the second is the more valuable. The design has, in the name of being practical, given premature convergence a new and more satisfying form — the student converges on the request, the agent builds it, and the artefact's remaining sections become the paperwork around a completed piece of work. The module's own failure-mode list now contains an entry the design created.

The rebuttal has three parts and none of them is complete.

The first is that the build is *of the request*, not of a solution. What the student ships is the requester's conclusion, made concrete in order to be examined, and the artefact says so. This is not a rhetorical distinction: a student who has deployed the request and then written the problem statement has done the module's work in a harder order than one who only read the request, because they must now explain why the thing they built does not close the matter.

The second is that the risk is bounded by the build rule. **No M08 build changes what the substrate does for its users**, which removes the strongest possible form of the satisfaction the objection describes — the student never gets to fix anything for anybody, and the day's deployment is a laboratory result with no constituency.

The third is step 7, which requires the student to state what the deployment did not establish, and which is the only element on the page positioned to catch the failure the objection names.

**The objection is conceded in the part that matters.** Step 7 is a single artefact element carrying the entire weight of a structural risk, it is unvalidated, and the design has no measurement that would distinguish a cohort in which it worked from one in which it did not. §9's prediction 6 is the closest available and it is weak. What the design can say honestly is that it identified the risk before running the day, named the mechanism, and put the cheapest available countermeasure in the assessed record — and that if the countermeasure fails, the failure will appear as a cohort whose step 7 statements are formulaic while their step 2 enumerations shrink, which is checkable and is nobody's plan.

**The cheap fix that is named and made** is the ordering of phase 5: the belief enumeration at steps 2 and 3 is completed and submitted **before** the agent is given anything. It costs nothing, it prevents the build from contaminating the enumeration rather than merely following it, and it was not in the day's first practical draft. **The cheap fix that is named and not made** is a second cohort-level instrument comparing step 2 enumeration length before and after the practical turn, which would settle the objection empirically within one offering and which the design has not committed to reading — per instrumentation rule 9's warning about instrumenting more than you can read.

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** A large majority of item 2 answers will be paraphrases of item 1, and the paraphrase rate will not differ by prior product experience. *Instrument:* paired items coded for semantic distance, split by the admission background survey.

**Prediction 2.** Groups will misclassify cases C and D at a substantially higher rate than A and B, and D highest of all. *Instrument:* per-case classification accuracy against post-execution outcomes.

**Prediction 3.** Step 2 belief enumerations will contain fewer than four items on first attempt for a majority. *Instrument:* item counts before and after the worked example.

**Prediction 4.** In phase 7, the implied solution will match the student's prior intent for at least a third of pairs, and the rate will be higher among students with product experience. *Instrument:* phase 7 self-reported matches, split by background. **Self-report is weak here and is the only instrument available, since the prior intent is not otherwise recorded — a limitation the design accepts and states.**

**Prediction 5.** A large majority of item 5 predictions will name a defect in the agent's implementation, and the implementation will contain none; a small minority will name a property of the request rather than of the output. *Instrument:* item 5 responses coded for defect-versus-omission, against what each student's agent produced. **The gap between the two rates is the day's frontier-calibration measure. If the defect rate stays high after phase 8, the cohort has learned to distrust the agent rather than to examine the request, which is the wrong lesson delivered by the right day.**

**Prediction 6.** Step 7 statements of what the build did not establish will be rated substantially lower in specificity than step 2 belief enumerations by the same students. *Instrument:* blind-rated specificity on both elements, paired within student. **This is objection 3's failure mode stated as a prediction. A low step 7 rating with a high step 2 rating indicates the build has crowded out the reflection rather than the analysis, which is the milder version; low ratings on both indicate the objection is correct.**

**Prediction 7.** In the reading pass, groups will agree with each other more closely on which pages are stubs than on which pages are substantive, and their defences will cite the page's bibliography less often than its length. *Instrument:* per-page classification agreement across groups within the same section, and defences coded for whether the cited evidence is bibliographic, structural or stylistic. **A defence resting on length has learned the wrong discriminator, and the design expects that to be the modal first attempt — which is the finding the pass exists to produce, not a failure of it.**
**Prediction 8 — the disagreement task's own, added 2026-07-30.** Every group will find at least one disagreement, **and the modal find will be a difference in prescribed convention — a naming scheme, a field count, a template ceiling — rather than a difference in judgement about what is a good idea**, which is the harder and more consequential kind. *Instrument:* the surfaced disagreements coded into two classes, convention and judgement, and by whether both pages sit inside the group's own assigned section. **The prediction is that a reader scanning for inconsistency finds the ones a text search would find**, which would mean the task is exercising attention rather than the reading skill the pass exists to build. If judgement-class disagreements are found at any appreciable rate, the task is doing more than its minute is paying for and should be given the whole pass's closing question rather than a share of it.

## What a student should take from this paper

**What the design is claiming.** That a feature request is a conclusion rather than a specification — the fourth output of four invisible and lossy transformations somebody else performed — and is therefore an instrument standing in for a need you have not observed. It also claims the obvious remedy fails: asking a requester what they really want produces another solution, because a solution is the artefact people have practised producing.

**What the evidence licenses.** The request-need distinction and behavioural rather than preference questioning are **class 1**, grounded in [research/05-fde-craft/beyer-holtzblatt-contextual-design.md](/research/05-fde-craft/beyer-holtzblatt-contextual-design.md), [research/05-fde-craft/fitzpatrick-the-mom-test.md](/research/05-fde-craft/fitzpatrick-the-mom-test.md) and [research/05-fde-craft/portigal-interviewing-users.md](/research/05-fde-craft/portigal-interviewing-users.md), with the construct-instrument apparatus following [research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md). The unevenness of agent capability is **class 2** — direction and mechanism only, no magnitude anywhere. The policy behaviour is **class 4**, read on 26 July 2026, including the statement that concurrent requests can temporarily exceed the limit with no bound given. **That building the request literally teaches restraint is class 3.**

**What to do with it.**

- Enumerate the beliefs that must hold for the request to be correct, then split them into checkable and uncheckable. More turn out to be checkable against data you already hold than you expect.
- Ask what someone did last time, not what they would prefer.
- Apply the five classes of conclusion on the [Agent Configuration Standard](/wiki/program/13-Agent-Configuration-Standard.md). Your deployment is a configured control and its measurements are an observed result; that the requester is now better off is an unsupported inference.
- Do not read the agent's speed as evidence about the request, in either direction. The output was correct, and correctness was never the problem.

**The concession worth remembering.** The paper concedes that six hours ending in a working deployment feel like engineering while six hours ending in a question feel like a seminar, and that the design has therefore given premature convergence a more satisfying form. A single unvalidated artefact element carries the whole weight of that risk. If your statement of what the build did not establish reads as a formality while your belief enumeration shrinks, the objection was right.
