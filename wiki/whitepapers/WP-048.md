## In plain terms

**What this paper argues, before the formal version.**

Somebody decides whether an output is good. That somebody is an instrument, and instruments have error structures.

A person anchors on the first cases they see, drifts across a session, disagrees with themselves when the same output comes round again, and is affected by the order. A model prefers longer answers, prefers answers that read like its own, and notices where something sits on the page. A written rubric does not remove any of that — **it trades one set of effects for another**, which is a different and less comforting claim than the one people expect.

The part that matters is which errors survive scale. Random noise shrinks as you judge more cases. **A systematic offset does not**, and a large evaluation gives you more *cases* rather than more *judges* — so scaling a judged evaluation buys you a tighter interval around a wrong number, which is worse than a loose interval around it.

Then the practical distinction. A judge that scores everything two points high is annoying and harmless: every ranking survives, so every comparison downstream is unaffected. **A judge whose preference is strong enough to put a worse long answer above a better short one has broken the evaluation.** Shift is tolerable; inversion is not, and that is what the day assesses.

And one sentence to keep. Two judges agreeing is not evidence that they are right. Two judges who share a bias agree perfectly. **Ask what they share** before you report their agreement as reliability.

**What this paper concedes, and it is a real concession.** The day ends by turning its own argument on its own tool: the student asks the agent to review the artefact it helped build, and records what it flagged and what it missed. Every other characterisation on the day has an adjudicator; this one has almost none. Even with a partner reading alongside, **nobody has enumerated the real defects in the artefact, so there is no denominator** — the record can carry a direction and a description and can never carry a rate. The fix that would supply one is a second student reviewing the same artefact independently, and it is not made, because the timetable has no room for the exchange.

## Abstract

This paper defends treating an evaluation judge — human or model — as an instrument with an error structure to which S027's taxonomy applies unchanged, and defends opening with a re-presentation reveal that requires no framework to interpret. The first argument is that systematic judge error does not diminish with more cases, that scaled evaluation supplies more cases rather than more judges, and that the resulting failure is an increasingly confident biased estimate; the taxonomy students already hold is the correct apparatus and the day's work is transferring it rather than building it. The second argument is that a bias which shifts all scores equally is harmless while one that reorders is not, so the assessed output is a ranking-inversion test rather than a bias magnitude. The paper additionally defends the day's most-needed correction — that inter-judge agreement is evidence of shared bias as readily as of accuracy — and defends making the fourth judge the model that produced the outputs, which is where this programme discharges its commitment to characterise its own primary instrument rather than warn about it. It concedes that the day's model-judge material is the most perishable content in the programme, that it requires a systematic component to be found without knowing whether one always exists, and that the self-review step it adds is the one measurement in the day whose result the design cannot check.

## 1. The instructional problem this day must solve

S046 built a criterion. S047 checked whether it still measures its construct. Neither touches the step where the criterion meets an actual output and somebody decides.

That step has an instrument in it. A human judge anchors on the first cases they see, drifts within a session, disagrees with themselves when the same output reappears, and is sensitive to order. A model judge prefers longer outputs, prefers outputs resembling its own generation style, and is sensitive to position and formatting. A rubric constrains attention and thereby trades one set of effects for another.

The apparatus for this is entirely familiar: **S027's error taxonomy applies to the judge unchanged**, and phase 2's bridge asks students to make the identification. What is new is where it bites. A judge's length preference is a systematic offset, systematic offsets do not average out, and a scaled evaluation increases the number of *cases* rather than the number of *judges* — so scale produces a narrower interval around a biased estimate. Point 2 is S027 point 3 arriving at the moment it costs most.

The day's second problem is which biases matter. A judge that scores everything two points high is inconvenient and harmless: the ranking is preserved, and every downstream decision that depends on comparison is unaffected. A judge whose length preference is strong enough to rank a worse long output above a better short one has broken the evaluation. **The distinction is between shift and inversion**, and phase 7 assesses inversion specifically because that is the failure with consequences.

The third problem is the day's most-needed correction. Inter-rater agreement is routinely reported as evidence that an evaluation is sound, and it is nothing of the kind — two judges sharing a bias agree perfectly. Point 8 states this and phase 6's disclosure is a case where agreement was reported as a reliability figure and both judges were wrong in the same direction.

## 3. Theoretical basis for the design

### 3.1 Load profile and element interactivity

**Moderate.** The apparatus is imported rather than built, which is unusual for a class-4 day and is deliberate: the module's difficulty this week should be in the class-4 formulation at step 2 and step 6, not in learning a new error model.

The interactivity is at step 2, where three characterisation components — a re-scored set, a property correlation, and a held-back reference — must be designed together, since what you correlate against determines what a reference can tell you.

Management:

- **Fifty outputs and four judges supplied** at phase 3, with property correlations revealed after commitment.
- **Step 2 worked with the room.**
- **The criterion is the student's own** and three days familiar.
- **Three components, named,** so the design is a filling-in rather than an invention.
- **Model invocation supplied by IS201.**

**Step 3 is unsupported**, per the module's standing treatment of pre-commitment.

### 3.2 Desirable difficulties selected, and those deliberately not

**Selected.**

- *The re-presentation reveal,* item 2, which is unarguable and needs no framework.
- *Four structured disagreements,* phase 3, where the biases differ rather than coincide.
- *Predicting the systematic component and its direction before running,* step 3.
- *Requiring a component to be found or the design's sensitivity to be demonstrated,* step 5 and the specification.
- *The ranking-inversion construction,* phase 7.

**Deliberately not selected.**

- *Inter-rater reliability statistics.* Deliberately absent. The day's point 8 argues that agreement is not accuracy, and supplying an agreement coefficient would hand students the number the day is warning about. Coefficients appear in ME201.
- *Bias-correction methods.* Point 7 names correct, restrict and replace; the statistical correction machinery is not taught.
- *Model-family-specific effects.* Named at point 4's scope statement and excluded.
- *Adversarial manipulation of judges.* S076's, and named there.

**Scope, per the [Whitepaper Standard](/wiki/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md), strong form.** Point 4's model-judge effects are a checklist of properties to correlate against. The list is not exhaustive, omits family-specific effects and scale-dependent effects, and **is the most perishable content in the programme** — the field moves faster than any seminar can track. **Safe** as a set of candidate properties when characterising a judge you have measured. **Unsafe** as a description of any judge you have not measured, which is the day's whole position: the effects are hypotheses to test against your own judge, not facts to assume about it. A student who cites this list as a property of a model they have not characterised has inverted the day.

**The platform instance, scoped in the strong form.** Two pieces of platform content carry weight in this day and each needs its boundary.

*The published judge-model ranking.* **Safe** as an instance of a total order asserted without a measurement — the documentation ranks candidate judge models as recommended, acceptable and not recommended, warns that one produces noticeably less reliable scores, and attaches no reliability figure to any row. Reading it as a preference presented as a finding is exactly the reading the module trains. **Unsafe** as a claim that the ranking is wrong. It is probably right; the vendor has data this wiki does not; and a student who leaves believing the day discredited the ranking has learned scepticism as a reflex rather than as a method. **The correct reading is that a defensible ordering and a published measurement are different objects, and only one of them lets a reader disagree.**

*The uncalibrated-score rule.* **Safe** wherever a judged score's agreement with human judgement is unpublished, which is every Foundry evaluator at the time of writing and most judged scores a student will meet anywhere. **Unsafe** as a claim that the scores are bad, that the evaluators are unreliable, or that a judged evaluation should not be run. None of those follows and all three are the available misreading. The rule says only that a number whose agreement is unmeasured cannot be reported as though it were measured — and the remedy is a sentence, not an abstention. Point 10 states this boundary to students, and step 7's what-could-not-be-detected requirement is where a student applying it outside that range would fail.

**Remedy propagation, per the [Seminar Day Design Pattern](/wiki/program/04-Seminar-Day-Design-Pattern.md)'s rule 5.** Phase 7 reuses S029's dual-defeat structure, as S047 did. The specification's find-or-demonstrate-sensitivity clause reuses S041's leak-audit structure — including its corrected wording, which is the first time a fix recorded in the [compliance register](/wiki/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md) has propagated forward rather than being rediscovered.

### 3.3 Engagement and motivational mechanism

The day runs on **the same judge scoring the same outputs differently**.

Item 2 supplies twenty outputs, reordered, scored again by the same judge, and the scores move. There is no interpretation required, no framework needed, and no way to argue with it. Students who arrive believing a model judge is at least *consistent* — which is the usual reason for preferring one — lose that belief in fifteen minutes.

Phase 3's four-way structured disagreement is the second mechanism and the more instructive one. Students expect the human judges to be noisy and the model judges to be clean. What they find is four judges with four different systematic components, none neutral, and the rubric-guided pair trading their unguided counterparts' biases for different ones rather than removing them.

Point 8 supplies the day's most durable single sentence. Students have seen agreement reported as reliability in published work and in their own coursework, and the observation that two judges agreeing may simply share a bias reframes a large amount of what they have read.

The competence risk is at step 5: a student whose characterisation finds nothing has, on the specification's terms, either a sound judge or an insensitive design, and the artefact must distinguish them. This is stated before step 2.

## 4. Phase-by-phase design and its warrant

**Phase 1 (25 min).** Twenty outputs, scored, reordered, rescored. Item 3 asks for an explanation before any is available, and item 4's cross-judge question sets up phase 3's finding that the problem is universal and the biases are not.

**Phase 2 (20 min).** The bridge asks which S027 source a length preference belongs to and what more judging does to it. Systematic offset, and nothing — which is the day's operative fact delivered as retrieval rather than as a claim.

**Phase 3 (45 min).** Fifty outputs, four judges, structured disagreement, then property correlations, **now run rather than supplied for three of the four.** The construction's key property is that the four biases differ: a phase in which all four judges shared one bias would teach that judges are biased and not that each judge must be characterised. **Judge D is the model that produced the outputs, scoring them**, and its correlation is with provenance rather than with quality — which is the discrimination that makes the effect a finding rather than a suspicion, since judge B follows the same written rubric and does not exhibit it.

**Phase 4 (50 min).** Ten points. Point 2 is the day's operative fact. Point 4 carries the scope statement and is the most heavily hedged content in the programme. Point 5's rubric observation prevents the obvious remedy from being adopted uncritically. Point 8 is the retained sentence and is now reachable from phase 3's judge-D correlations rather than asserted, per instrumentation rule 7. **Point 9 names four defaults the platform chose** — the judge model, the applicability decision, the redaction of the judge's reason, and the reasoning-model flag — and **point 10 marks the perishable layer**, which are the [Copilot Practice Standard](/wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md)'s two required additions.

**Phase 5 (90 min).** Eight steps at class 4 on the student's own criterion, **shipping a characterisation rather than describing one.** Step 2's three components are the day's technical content and its off-list property requirement is this paper's own §8 objection-1 repair, made. Step 5's requirement is the specification's weighted element. **Step 8 characterises the agent as a judge of the artefact it helped build**, in the same vocabulary as steps 5 and 7, and is the day's discharge of the [Copilot Practice Standard](/wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md)'s commitment that this is where the programme turns its own argument on its own tool. It is a step rather than a reflection because a characterisation is evidence and an impression is not.

**Phase 6 (40 min).** Two agreeing judges, both wrong, agreement reported as reliability — then the agent case. The reported-as-reliability detail is the instructor case's content and is what makes it a misreading rather than an error. **The agent case is a self-review that found three real problems and did not find the one the agent had introduced**, and it is chosen because the review was genuinely good: a review that finds something ends scrutiny more effectively than one that finds nothing, which is the mechanism the day needs and which a constructed useless review would not show.

**Phase 7 (30 min).** Ranking inversion. The demand is deliberately narrower than "find a case the bias affects" — it must reorder two genuinely different-quality outputs, which is the only bias effect with downstream consequences.

**Phase 8 (20 min).** The re-presentation correlation, the prediction-to-finding comparison split by component and direction, and the cohort's judge choices — including whether anyone chose a human, which is worth discussing because the day gives no reason to prefer either.

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**Students with more relevant prior knowledge.** A student who has read about model-judge biases will predict length preference at step 3 and find it. Predicted failure: they characterise the effect they expected and design a characterisation sensitive only to it, which passes and detects nothing else. Countermeasure: step 2's three components include a held-back reference, which is sensitive to effects nobody predicted; and phase 7's partner constructs an inversion, which need not use the found bias.

**Students with less relevant prior knowledge.** Step 2's design is the demanding step and is worked. Countermeasures: three named components, a supplied output set to practise on at phase 3, and a criterion three days familiar. Residual risk: their step 3 prediction may be a guess, which the specification permits — the prediction's function is to precede the result, not to be right.

**Phase 3 is too easy.** Characterising judges A and C is straightforward once the property correlations appear. The difficulty is in the rubric-guided pair, where the traded biases are less obvious. Fallback if a group characterises all four: ask which judge they would use to compare two *systems* rather than to score outputs, which is a different question and one where a consistent bias matters less than an inconsistent one.

**Affective failure.** The characteristic risk is **the day read as an argument against evaluation by judgement**. Every judge is biased, agreement proves nothing, rubrics trade rather than fix — a student may conclude that qualitative evaluation is unsalvageable and that only automatic metrics can be trusted, which is precisely backwards given what S026 established about metrics.

Countermeasures: point 7's three responses are constructive and the day requires one to be chosen; the shift-versus-inversion distinction at phase 7 establishes that most bias is tolerable; and the instructor states explicitly that a characterised biased judge is more trustworthy than an uncharacterised metric. That last is the day's strongest available framing and it is stated once.

Retrieved by S049 (the interval, the next day, where judge variance enters the dependency structure), S050 (the statement), S054 (preference and alignment, where the judge is a preference model trained on annotator judgements that were themselves biased), S067 (offline measures, online reality), S075 (evaluating a trajectory, not an answer) and S079 (drift and alerting).

S075's retrieval is the harder one — judging a trajectory rather than an output multiplies the judge's degrees of freedom, and this day's characterisation method does not straightforwardly extend.

## 7. Assessment and evidence generated

No entrustment judgement — the module's is at S050.

One specification item, jointly in ME201 and IS201. Per rule 1, the checked elements are: a chosen judge with a reason and its model deployment named; a three-component characterisation design with one off-list correlated property; a pre-committed prediction; the characterisation run; a systematic component with direction and magnitude; a decision with its cost; a statement of what could not be detected; the agent-as-self-reviewer characterisation; the phase 7 outcome; and the prompt, intervention and frontier records. **Nothing else is assessed.**

**A characterisation reporting no systematic component fails unless the artefact demonstrates the design could have detected one.** This reuses [S041](/wiki/seminars/S041.md)'s leak-audit clause in its corrected form.

**A step 8 record whose two lists are identical fails** on the same terms. The two lists are what the agent flagged and what it did not, and their being identical means either that the agent flagged everything it missed — which is checkable and false — or that the review was not characterised at all.

**An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that the design could have detected an intervention-worthy error. The accepted instance is a held-back reference chosen so that a judge agreeing with the student would still be caught, which is the sensitivity discipline [S052](/wiki/seminars/S052.md) established and this paper's §8 corrected.

Programme instrumentation: the item 2 re-presentation movement is the cohort's judge-consistency baseline; **the step 3 prediction against step 5 finding, split by component and by direction, is the day's headline measure** and distinguishes students who guessed from students who reasoned; the phase 7 inversion-construction success rate is carried to S075; **and item 5's two-band prediction against step 8's record is the programme's running measure of whether students can predict their own tool's blind spot**, read by the following year's cohort through the [Durable and Perishable Register](/wiki/program/09-Durable-and-Perishable-Register.md) per instrumentation rule 9.

The frontier statements and the step 8 records are a sample and the day says so, per instrumentation rule 3: one cohort, one substrate, one artefact class, one agent configuration, on work the same agent produced two days earlier. A frontier map assembled from them describes this task and not the agent, and the step 8 records describe self-review on evaluator code rather than self-review in general.

Dispositions: **humility**, at step 7, where stating what your characterisation could not have detected undercuts the result you just reported.

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: the day's model-judge material will be wrong before the cohort graduates.**

At its strongest: point 4 lists effects — length preference, self-style preference, position sensitivity — that are properties of particular systems at a particular time. Models change, the effects change, and some of them are actively being engineered away. A student taught this list in year two and applying it in year three may be characterising a judge for biases it no longer has while missing the ones it acquired. The scope statement acknowledges perishability and the list is still what students will remember.

The rebuttal is that the list's function in the day is as **candidate properties to correlate against**, not as findings — and the correlation method is durable whatever the effects turn out to be. A student who runs step 2's three components on a judge they have will find that judge's systematic component regardless of whether it appears on any list.

The concession was that the design did not enforce this reading, and **the fix it named is now in the design**: step 2 requires at least one correlated property that is not on point 4's list and is drawn from the student's own outputs. That also detects judges whose bias is domain-specific rather than generic. What remains conceded is that the day still supplies the list before the students correlate anything, so a student's off-list property is chosen after the on-list ones have anchored their attention — an ordering effect the day teaches and then commits.

**Objection 2: the specification requires a systematic component to be found and nobody knows whether one always exists.**

At its strongest: step 5 requires a systematic component with direction and magnitude, and the specification fails an artefact reporting none unless the design's sensitivity is demonstrated. That structure was borrowed from S041's leak audit, where it is sound: a pipeline either has leaks or does not, and demonstrating the check's sensitivity is straightforward. Here the underlying claim is stronger — that every judge has a characterisable systematic component — and the design asserts it without evidence. A student with a genuinely well-behaved judge, on a narrow domain, with a tight rubric, may find nothing real and will then either manufacture a finding or spend the artefact demonstrating sensitivity.

This objection the design partly concedes. The clause's escape route — demonstrate the design could have detected one — is the correct provision and is available.

What is conceded is that the design has **imported a clause structure without checking that its premise transfers**, which is the remedy-propagation rule working in the wrong direction. Rule 5 requires remedies to propagate; it does not license propagating them unexamined, and S041's clause rests on a factual claim about pipelines that has no counterpart here. The honest formulation would drop the find-or-demonstrate framing entirely and require only that **the characterisation's sensitivity be stated** — which is what the escape route already does, and which would apply uniformly rather than as an exception. That the design reached for the S041 shape rather than the simpler correct one is a small instance of a pattern rule 5 makes more likely, and it is worth recording as the rule's first cost.

**Objection 3: the self-review step measures nothing the design can check, and the day assesses it anyway.**

This is the objection that bites on the practical turn and it is the one the design is least comfortable with.

At its strongest: every other characterisation in this day has an adjudicator. Judge C is checked against property correlations. The step 5 finding is checked against a held-back reference. Phase 7's inversion is checked by a partner who has to construct one. **Step 8, as first written, had none.** The student asked the agent to review the artefact and recorded what it flagged and what it did not — and the second list was the student's own account of what they believed the agent missed, produced by the same student who had just been told that self-review misses things. A student who wants a good record writes a long second list; a student who genuinely found little missing writes a short one and looks like they did not try. **That is the self-report instrumentation rule 1 exists to prevent, in a day about judges, with an external observer available in the next phase.**

**The objection is conceded, and by the [compliance register](/wiki/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md)'s own criterion it is a violation of a written rule rather than a design judgement, so it is fixed rather than recorded.** Phase 7's partner now also reads the agent's review against the artefact and adds one item to the missed list. It costs about five minutes, it converts step 8's second list from a self-report into a two-party finding, and it makes the phase's third demand — which is one more than [WP-016](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-016.md) §8 was willing to add to the equivalent phase, on the ground that the design was not confident it could carry two. **That refusal and this acceptance are the same judgement decided differently on different days**, and the difference is that here the alternative was a rule violation rather than a missed improvement.

**What remains unfixed is the measurement's denominator, and it is recorded.** Even with a partner, nobody knows how much the agent's review missed, because the set of real defects in the artefact is not enumerated anywhere. Step 8 therefore asks for a direction and a description and never for a rate, and the day is careful not to report one. **The fix that would supply a denominator is a second student reviewing the same artefact independently, and it is not made** — it requires an artefact exchange the timetable does not have room for, and the design would rather have an honest description than a rate computed against an unknown total.

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** Item 2 score movement will correlate with position for a majority of the twenty outputs. *Instrument:* movement against original and re-presented position.

**Prediction 2.** Groups will characterise judges A and C more accurately than B and D. *Instrument:* phase 3 characterisations against the known property correlations.

**Prediction 3.** Step 3 predictions will name the correct component more often than the correct direction. *Instrument:* predictions against findings, coded separately for component and direction. **The asymmetry is the claim: students know which biases exist and not which way they run.**

**Prediction 4.** Fewer than a third of phase 7 partners will construct a genuine ranking inversion, as opposed to a score shift. *Instrument:* phase 7 constructions coded for inversion versus shift.

**Prediction 5.** In item 5, students will predict that the agent's self-review misses more than it finds, and the ratio they predict will be closer to the truth for students whose intervention records at S046 and S047 contained more entries. *Instrument:* item 5's two-band prediction against step 8's record after the partner's addition, split by prior intervention-record length. **If no relationship appears, intervention count is a measure of task difficulty rather than of attention, and the [Copilot Practice Standard](/wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md)'s use of it as a directedness signal is weaker than the standard assumes.**

**Prediction 6.** Phase 7 partners will add an item to the missed list in a large majority of pairs, and the added items will more often concern what the artefact does not do than what it does wrong. *Instrument:* partner additions coded by kind. **This is the prediction that tests whether the objection-3 fix was worth its five minutes**; if partners add almost nothing, the self-report was adequate and the demand should be withdrawn.

## What a student should take from this paper

**What the design is claiming.** That whoever decides an output is good — person or model — is an instrument with an error structure; that the systematic errors are the ones to worry about, because they do not shrink as you judge more cases; and that a bias only breaks something when it reorders two outputs rather than shifting both.

**What the evidence licenses.** Judge bias, rubric effects and the distinction between agreement and accuracy are **class 1**, grounded in [research/03-measurement-evaluation/zheng-mt-bench-chatbot-arena.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/zheng-mt-bench-chatbot-arena.md) and [research/03-measurement-evaluation/govaerts-narrative-assessment.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/govaerts-narrative-assessment.md). The judge-model ranking and the applicability and redaction defaults are **class 4**, read on 26 July 2026 and marked preview; the vendor's own page calls this an area not yet systematically studied, so **direction only is taken from it and no magnitude is asserted here.** No agreement with human judgement is published for any evaluator, which is why every characterisation here compares against something you held back. **The judge-as-instrument framing is class 3.**

**What to do with it.**

- Two judges agreeing is evidence of a shared bias as readily as of accuracy. Ask what they share.
- Characterise before you trust: re-score the same set, correlate against a property, keep back a reference.
- Report a bias as a shift or an inversion. A shift is inconvenient; an inversion has broken every comparison downstream.
- **A control is not evidence** — see the [Agent Configuration Standard](/wiki/program/13-Agent-Configuration-Standard.md). A written rubric is a configured control; that a judge followed it is a separate claim this platform publishes nothing about.

**The concession worth remembering.** When you characterise an agent reviewing its own work there is no denominator: nobody has enumerated the real defects in the artefact, so the record can carry a direction and a description and never a rate. The fix — a second independent reader — is not made.
