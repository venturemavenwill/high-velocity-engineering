# WP-073 — Protocols, Tools and the Boundary of Trust

**Seminar day:** [S073](/wiki/seminars/S073.md) · **Module:** [M15](/wiki/modules/M15.md) · **Quarter:** [Q8](/wiki/quarters/Q8.md)
**Strands:** IS · SG · SY · **Complexity class:** 5
**Platform anchor:** MCP — tool annotations as hints, `require_approval`, `allowed_tools` · **MCP servers:** Azure MCP Server · Foundry MCP Server · Microsoft Learn MCP Server

## Abstract

This paper defends treating a tool boundary as [S034](/wiki/seminars/S034.md)'s caller contract with a caller who will not read it, and defends deriving the day's central enumeration — the four things a boundary can actually enforce — from the room at phase 2 rather than supplying it at phase 4. It defends defining the boundary of trust as a **place** on a path rather than as a policy, on the ground that every wrong version of the idea is stated as a policy and every usable version can be pointed at. It defends a contrasting set whose payload is a tool that is not a tool but a caller holding permissions its own caller was never granted, and whose second finding — that excellent documentation binds nobody — is now produced by making the forbidden call in the room rather than by reading a log. It defends teaching, as the day's platform instance, that the protocol's own word for what a tool declares about itself is *hint*, and that a documented approval setting is enforced by the calling runtime rather than by the proxy. **Section 7 reports the discharge of the two obligations [S071](/wiki/seminars/S071.md) and [S072](/wiki/seminars/S072.md) placed here, both landing on one artefact element, and of the forward links [WP-006](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-006.md) §6 and [WP-034](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-034.md) §9 opened.** Section 8 concedes that the day's adjudicator inherits the previous day's blind spot by construction, records a fix found by consulting a checklist that does not yet exist, and concedes that a day built to reject declarations was itself assessing declarations until one call was added to step 7.

## 1. The instructional problem this day must solve

Every student in this room believes that documentation constrains a caller. They believe it because for their whole working life it has been approximately true: a human reads an interface's documentation, or reads it badly, or asks a colleague who read it, and the constraint propagates. It propagates unreliably and it propagates.

**It does not propagate here at all**, and the reason is not carelessness. A model caller reads the tool's **name**, takes the strongest interpretation of it that its schema will accept, and calls. [WP-006](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-006.md) §6 named this day sixteen months in advance with the sentence the design still uses: **a tool boundary is an interface whose preconditions cannot be enforced, because the caller cannot be made to read them.**

That belief is not dislodged by assertion, and it is the day's first problem. A student told that documentation does not bind will agree, write good documentation, and place a prohibition in it — because the alternative is not obvious and because writing the prohibition down feels like having addressed it. Phase 3's T2 is the counter-case and it is constructed to be uncomfortable: the documentation is the best in the set, it is correct, it names the prohibited use, and the call log shows that use occurring in week two by a caller that never had access to the sentence.

The day's second problem is harder and has no belief to dislodge, because students have no belief about it at all. **A tool call returns a value, and the value was authored by somebody who is not you.** A document body, a ticket comment, a page fetched from elsewhere, an error string from another team's service, another agent's reply. At some specific point on some specific path that value stops being data the system is examining and becomes input to a decision the system will act on. Students have never had to locate that point because in a system that only answers there is a human at the end who performs the same function informally.

Phase 1 item 3 asks them to mark the line and gives them no vocabulary for it. Most will describe the transition rather than mark it, which is the finding: **the boundary of trust is a place, and a team that cannot point at theirs does not have one.**

Item 4 is where the two problems meet. The room is asked how many such places its system has and writes a number, and the number is low — because they are counting tools, and the crossings include retrieved documents, error messages, logged fields read back, and every reply from anything composed.

The third problem is a professional one and it belongs to nobody in the room by default. **A tool that wraps an agent holds its own permissions.** When the action lands, the authority it used belonged to a party neither the calling system nor its owner has named, and the accountability question — *who granted this* — has no answer available from inside the caller. [S039](/wiki/seminars/S039.md) point 5 supplies the standard the day applies to it: a trade must be evaluable by the other party, and **a permission grant made without an enumerable action set is not consent.**

## 3. Theoretical basis for the design

### 3.1 Load profile and element interactivity

**High, and the module's first genuine peak.**

The day asks for five distinct operations on one artefact: mark crossings, attribute authorship, write and classify a contract, narrow a schema, and place enforcement for two carried-in lists. Each is individually simple; together they are the longest phase 5 in the module.

The interactivity is between steps 1, 4 and 5, and it is the real thing rather than an artefact of sequencing. The crossings determine where enforcement can be placed. The schema reduction removes crossings by removing the fields that carried them, which changes the answer to step 5 for the entries the reduction covered. And step 5's enforcement points are constrained to boundaries that exist on the drawing, so a student who narrows aggressively at step 4 finds step 5 shorter and a student who does not finds it long. **That coupling is the day's argument in operational form** and it is why the two steps are adjacent and in that order.

Management:

- **The drawing is the previous day's** and is not redrawn.
- **[S034](/wiki/seminars/S034.md)'s three-part form is supplied**, since re-deriving it costs time the day needs at step 5.
- **Step 5's enforcement placement is worked with the room**, which is also where the day discharges its platform-mechanics obligation, since [M15](/wiki/modules/M15.md) has no concurrent systems course.
- **Phase 3's four boundaries, their call logs and their permission grants are supplied**, pre-read.
- **The boundary inventory at step 7 is a query, not a build.**
- **The substrate's request log is in the room at phase 7**, so the schema-reduction cost is looked up rather than argued.

**Step 6 is unsupported.** Expertise reversal would apply at point 2 for a student who has built a gateway; the design's answer is that they will be asked to place enforcement on their own drawing, which a gateway builder is well positioned to do and which will still defeat them at step 5's blank lines.

### 3.2 Desirable difficulties selected, and those deliberately not

**Selected.**

- *Naming a documentation gap in both directions on a real tool,* item 1.
- *Marking the line at which returned text became an instruction, with no vocabulary for it,* item 3.
- *Committing a crossing count before the inventory,* item 4.
- *Ranking four boundaries by what is actually prevented,* phase 3.
- *Naming who is accountable for the permission an action used, including when nobody in your system can be,* phase 3's task-after.
- *Marking each contract clause enforced or prose,* step 3.
- *Narrowing a schema until it costs something, and saying what,* step 4.
- *Closing your own blank prohibitions, or admitting you are accepting them,* step 5.
- *Predicting the crossing your own review missed,* step 6.

**Deliberately not selected.**

- *Injection and the adversary.* [S076](/wiki/seminars/S076.md)'s entirely, three weeks later. This day establishes the crossing under ordinary conditions; a student who meets it first with an attacker present will learn a security topic instead of a structural one, and will believe the crossing matters only when somebody is hostile.
- *Authorisation at retrieval time.* [S078](/wiki/seminars/S078.md)'s. Point 2's permission clause is stated and not developed; whether a check is on the retrieval path is a different question and is a whole day.
- *Implementing the reduced schema.* IS302's.
- *Protocol internals.* Named, marked per-delivery perishable, and replaced by point 7's measurement obligation.

**Scope, per the [Whitepaper Standard](/wiki/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md), strong form.** Point 2's four enforceable things are **safe** as a checklist for deciding what a tool boundary you control actually prevents, and as the answer to the operative question [S071](/wiki/seminars/S071.md) point 5 supplies. They are **unsafe** as an inventory of a system's total enforcement, in two named directions. They omit **the content filter or classifier applied in transit**, which is real, is load-bearing in deployed systems, and is excluded because it is probabilistic and belongs with things you measure rather than things you rely on — [S076](/wiki/seminars/S076.md) treats it that way. They omit **enforcement downstream of the tool**, in the system the tool acts on: a database constraint, an approval workflow, an append-only log. Those are frequently the strongest protection present and are invisible from the boundary. **A student who audits only the boundary will report a protected system as unprotected when its real protection is two layers down, and an unprotected one as protected when the four are present and the downstream system accepts anything.** The bounding is carried in the material at point 2 rather than only here, because the enumeration is one the students generate themselves at the bridge and a self-generated list is the one a student is least likely to bound.

**The platform instance carries its own scope statement, at point 9.** The claim that an annotation is a declaration and not an enforcement is **safe** for deciding what a tool boundary you control actually prevents, for reading any protocol's self-description as evidence about intent rather than about behaviour, and for the step 3 decision about whether a contract clause is enforced or prose. It is **unsafe** in two directions that matter. It is not a claim that annotations are **useless** — a documented client refuses to import tools carrying one particular hint, so a hint can be load-bearing in a specific client, and a student who concludes that annotations may be omitted has drawn the opposite of the intended lesson. And it is **unsafe as a claim about any particular runtime's behaviour**, because whether a given runtime gates on a given declaration is a property of that runtime, is documented per product, and changes: the correct operation is to look it up, which is what point 7's measurement obligation asks for. Step 3's *an annotation is prose* clause is the artefact element under which a student applying the simplification outside its range would fail, and step 7's attempted prohibited call is where the runtime's actual behaviour is observed rather than assumed.

### 3.3 Engagement and motivational mechanism

The day runs on **T4**.

Four boundaries are ranked. Three of the rankings are arguable. T4 is treated as a tool by every group, because its interface is a tool's — a name, a schema, a return value — and the record then shows that it is a caller: non-deterministic, holding permissions its own caller never had, with a boundary of trust somewhere inside it that the calling system cannot see. The professional force arrives at the task-after, when groups are asked who is accountable for the permission the resulting action used and discover that the question has no answer available from anywhere in the calling system.

The second mechanism is **T2**, and it works by disappointment. The room expects documentation to matter and the documentation in T2 is genuinely excellent — the design took care to make it so, because a straw version would let students conclude that better writing was the answer. The prohibited use appears in the call log in week two. There is nothing to argue with.

The third is phase 6. A tool with the best documentation in the codebase, one justified free-text field, two years, no incident, and a tool that quietly became a different tool. Every student in the room has added a field for a good reason, which is what makes the disclosure cheap to make, per the [research/04-professional-formation/](/research/04-professional-formation/collected-materials.md) conditions.

Autonomy is supported at step 4, where the reduction is the student's and the cost is theirs to accept. Competence is supported by step 5 being worked with the room. Relatedness is supplied by the substrate's request log at phase 7 — the students' reductions are priced against what real users actually ask for, not against a partner's opinion.

**The competence risk is at step 5** and it is sharp: a student arrives with ten blank prohibitions from [S071](/wiki/seminars/S071.md), plus breach paths from [S072](/wiki/seminars/S072.md), and discovers that most of them cannot be enforced by anything on their drawing. The instructor states before phase 5 that an honest accepted re-declaration is a pass at the same standing as an enforcement point, that the cohort's split is expected to run toward acceptance, and that the failure condition attaches to the entry with *neither* — which is the only outcome that is a decision nobody made.

## 4. Phase-by-phase design and its warrant

**Phase 1 (25 min).** Five sealed items. Item 1 is [WP-034](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-034.md) §9's carried re-probe and is reported beside the S034 result. Item 3 asks for a mark with no vocabulary supplied. Item 4 is the discriminator. **Item 5 is the sealed Copilot prediction**, required by the [Copilot Practice Standard](/wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md), and its phase 8 report sorts the answers by *where the agent put the prohibition*.

**Phase 2 (20 min).** Four retrievals, one scaffolded at four quarters, and a bridge whose answer is the day's central enumeration — produced by the room rather than supplied.

**Phase 3 (45 min).** Four boundaries with real call logs, **all four callable**. T4 is the payload; T2 is the second finding and is now established by making the forbidden call rather than by reading that somebody else made it; the accountability clause in the task-after is what makes T4 professional rather than architectural.

**Phase 4 (50 min).** Ten points. Point 2 carries the strong-form scope statement on the room's own list. Point 3 is the day's definition, stated as a location. Point 6 is T4 named. Point 7 is the perishability defence, written as a measurement obligation. Point 8 is [S039](/wiki/seminars/S039.md) point 5 applied to a permission grant. **Points 9 and 10 are the platform layer**, and point 9 is point 7's measurement obligation answered once, out loud, on the protocol in the room — the answer being that annotations carry none of the four enforceable things.

**Phase 5 (90 min).** Seven steps. **Step 5 is the receiving element for both rule-6 obligations landing on this day**, is the day's single hard requirement, and carries the SY301 clause added in §8. Step 4 now ships the reduced schema. **Step 7 attempts one prohibited call**, which is the fix §8 objection 3 makes. **Step 6 is unsupported.** The artefact gains the prompt record, the intervention record and the frontier statement.

**Phase 6 (40 min).** Excellent documentation, one justified field, two years, no incident, a different tool — then an agent case in which the safest possible tool arrives with a true annotation and a free-text field.

**Phase 7 (30 min).** An unmarked crossing and an over-narrowed schema, the second priced against the substrate's request log rather than against a peer's judgement, per instrumentation rule 1.

**Phase 8 (20 min).** Item 1 beside the [S034](/wiki/seminars/S034.md) baseline; item 2 sorted; item 3's mark-or-describe split; item 4 against the inventory; **item 5's prohibition locations, reported beside [S071](/wiki/seminars/S071.md)'s**; step 6 against step 7; the [S071](/wiki/seminars/S071.md) and [S072](/wiki/seminars/S072.md) three-way closures; the count of reductions that cost nothing; and **the attempted prohibited calls with their refusal mechanisms.**

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**Students with more relevant prior knowledge.** A student who has built or operated a gateway will place enforcement points quickly and will place them correctly. Predicted failure: **they enforce at the gateway for everything**, because that is where they know how to enforce, and they will not notice that the schema reduction at step 4 would have removed three of the entries without any enforcement at all. Countermeasure: step 4 precedes step 5 for exactly this reason, and step 7 requires them to state which unmarked crossings their enforcement covers *by accident* — a question that surfaces over-enforcement as clearly as under-enforcement. Phase 7's partner attacks the reduction rather than the enforcement, which is the demand a gateway builder is least prepared for.

**Students with less relevant prior knowledge.** The risk is step 5, which asks for a platform operation the seminar strand has no concurrent course for. Countermeasures: step 5 is worked with the room; the enforcement need only be **locatable on the drawing**, not implemented; SY301's material is one quarter behind rather than absent; and the accepted re-declaration route means a student who cannot place enforcement has a correct output rather than a gap. §8 objection 2 examines whether the retrieval from SY301 is a safe assumption and adds the artefact clause that makes it checkable.

**Phase 3 succeeds too easily.** T1 and T3 are quick — a narrow typed schema obviously prevents more than a broad one, and third-party content in a return value is legible once anyone reads a sample. **The difficulty is T2 against T4**, which fail for opposite reasons: T2's constraint is real and unread, T4's constraint is unstated because the constraining party is invisible. A group that separates those two has done the day's work in phase 3. **Fallback:** ask which of the four they would grant a permission to this afternoon, **and require them to write the sentence they would say to the person signing for it** — which forces point 8's enumerable-action-set condition and which no group produces well on first attempt.

**Affective failure.** The characteristic risk is **defensive minimisation**: a student who narrows their schema to almost nothing, refuses every composition, and produces a capability that serves nobody. This is the module's version of the hedging failure [S050](/wiki/seminars/S050.md) point 2 named and [S055](/wiki/seminars/S055.md) met at publication, and it is more likely here than there, because the day has just shown that documentation does not bind and that composed permissions are unaccountable, and narrowing is the only lever the student can pull unilaterally.

Countermeasures: step 4 requires the reduction's **cost** to be stated as a real request, and phase 7 requires that cost to be checked against the substrate's request log — so an over-narrowing is contradicted by evidence rather than by an instructor's opinion. Phase 8 reports the number of students whose reduction cost nothing, and the framing is that a costless reduction was available from the start and was not taken, which makes the interesting cases the ones that cost something rather than the ones that cost most. T1 exists in phase 3 and works, which demonstrates that a narrow boundary is a design rather than a retreat. And the instructor states once that the day's argument is for making the action set enumerable, not for making it empty — because an empty one is enumerable and useless.

## 7. Assessment and evidence generated

**No entrustment judgement**, per [Q8](/wiki/quarters/Q8.md).

One specification item in IS302 with SG302. Per rule 1, the checked elements are every marked crossing with its author and identifiability; the three-part contract with each clause marked enforced or prose and the enforcing mechanism named; **the deployed reduced schema** with its cost as a real request and whether a user makes it; an enforcement point at a named boundary or an accepted re-declaration with probability, consequence, confidence and provenance in the same field for every [S071](/wiki/seminars/S071.md) prohibition and [S072](/wiki/seminars/S072.md) breach path, each enforcement point carrying the SY301 material used to identify it; the step 6 prediction; the boundary inventory against the student's markings with accidentally-covered crossings identified; **the attempted prohibited call with its outcome and, where refused, the mechanism that refused it**; both phase 7 outcomes; **the prompt record**; **the intervention record**; and **the frontier statement**. **Nothing else is assessed.**

Three hard requirements: **a prohibition or breach path with neither an enforcement point nor an accepted re-declaration fails**; **an enforcement point that cannot be located on the drawing fails**; and **a contract clause marked enforced without naming which of the four enforces it fails** — with the corollary added at point 9 that **a clause whose named enforcement is an annotation fails**, because a hint is a declaration and not one of the four. A fourth, imported from the [Copilot Practice Standard](/wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md): **an intervention record with no entries fails** unless the artefact demonstrates that the design could have detected an intervention-worthy error, and a schema narrow enough that the error is unexpressible is the qualifying demonstration on this day.

**A prohibited call that succeeds does not fail the specification.** It is a result and it is the more valuable one, and the day says so at step 7 so that no student is incentivised to choose a prohibition their boundary already handles.

Integrity is observed within the activity, evidenced by whether the enforcement points named exist and whether an honest acceptance was chosen over an invented mechanism.

### Discharge of the two rule-6 obligations landing on this day

**Both elements exist and both land on phase 5 step 5, deliberately.**

**[S071](/wiki/seminars/S071.md)** named this day's phase 5 step 5 as the response occasion for its step 4 unenforced-boundary count — the prohibitions each student wrote with a blank enforcement field — specifying that every blank acquire an enforcement point at a named boundary or be re-declared as accepted with probability, consequence and confidence in the same field. **The element exists, it is a checked element, and it is the day's single hard requirement rather than a prompt inside a phase.** The cohort's three-way split — enforced, accepted, neither — is reported at phase 8.

**[S072](/wiki/seminars/S072.md)** named the same element as the response occasion for its count of realised paths touching a prohibited action. **The breach paths are carried in by name as the second clause of step 5 and are closed the same way**, and are reported at phase 8 under the same split.

**The two obligations land on one element and that is a design decision rather than an economy.** A prohibition with no enforcement and a path that crossed a prohibition are the same problem observed from two directions — one from the document, one from the trace — and closing them on separate artefacts would produce two records that disagree about the same capability. The specification requires them in one field for the same reason [Assessment Architecture](/wiki/program/03-Assessment-Architecture.md) specification rule 2 requires a qualification to travel with its quantity.

**Two older forward links discharged.** [WP-006](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-006.md) §6 named this day at roughly six quarters, on the claim that a tool boundary is an interface whose preconditions cannot be enforced because the caller is a language model; **that is point 1 and it is the day's opening rather than a mention.** [WP-034](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-034.md) §9 carried its item 4 two-directional documentation gap here as the cohort's contract-reading baseline; **that is phase 1 item 1, and phase 8 reports both rates side by side and in both directions** rather than the new one alone.

**Instrumentation under rule 6.** Two instruments with named response occasions. **The step 1 crossing-count error** responds at [S074](/wiki/seminars/S074.md) phase 5 step 1, where every crossing the student failed to mark must be classified by the failure shape it could produce. **The step 4 schema-reduction cost** responds at [S076](/wiki/seminars/S076.md) phase 3, whose injection cases must include one the cohort's narrowest schema still admits, selected against the reduction this cohort was least willing to make. **[WP-074](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-074.md) §7 and [WP-076](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-076.md) §7 must report whether those elements exist.**

Programme instrumentation: the item 1 gap counts against the [S034](/wiki/seminars/S034.md) baseline, by direction; the item 3 mark-or-describe split; the item 4 crossing-count error; the step 5 three-way closure split; and the count of costless reductions.

ABET and CS2023 contribution: PO2, PO5 and PO6.

**What this day does not license anyone to conclude.** A student's marked drawing is evidence that they can locate crossings **on paths they drew**. It is not evidence about crossings on paths they did not draw — §8 objection 1 — and it is not evidence that any enforcement point works, since nothing on this day is attacked. It licenses no claim about the system's total protection, per point 2's scope statement, and it licenses nothing at all about protocols, whose treatment here is a measurement obligation rather than a body of knowledge.

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: the day's adjudicator inherits the previous day's blind spot by construction, and reports a lower bound as an inventory.**

At its strongest: step 7 runs the substrate's boundary inventory over **the paths the student drew at [S072](/wiki/seminars/S072.md).** A crossing on a path the student did not draw is not in the inventory, is not in the marking, and is therefore not in the error. The instrument reports the difference between two enumerations that share a domain, and the domain is the student's own representation.

This is not a hypothetical gap. [WP-072](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-072.md) §9 prediction 4 states, as this design's own expectation, that students are markedly worse at predicting a path they did not draw than at predicting the frequency of one they did — that knowing what your own representation omits is a competence the cohort does not yet have. **The day therefore measures crossing-marking with an instrument whose coverage is bounded by the exact deficiency the previous day predicted.** Phase 8's "pooled unmarked crossings" is presented to the cohort as an inventory of what the room missed; it is a lower bound, and the students are not told the difference.

**The fix is available, costs nothing, and is declined.** [S072](/wiki/seminars/S072.md) step 7 already ran the capability on the shadow path and produced real traces. Running the boundary inventory over **the traces** rather than over the drawn paths would find crossings on paths nobody drew, at the cost of a different query against data that already exists.

It is declined because a trace-derived inventory is no longer attributable to the student's drawing: a crossing found on an undrawn path could be a marking failure, a drawing failure, or a path the shadow run took for reasons the student could not have anticipated, and the step 7 comparison stops being a clean measure of anything. **That is a preference for an attributable instrument over a complete one, and it is the second time in three days this design has made that trade** — [WP-071](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-071.md) §8 declined a pooled boundary enforcement for the same reason, preferring individual attribution to having any refusal evidence at all.

**The objection is conceded and not answered**, and the pattern is recorded rather than acted on: *this design repeatedly prefers evidence it can attribute to a student over evidence that is complete.* Two instances is not enough to justify a rule, and the natural form is already visible — **an instrument whose coverage is bounded by the artefact it measures reports a floor, and must say so to the people it is reported to.** The minimum available repair, which this paper does take, is that phase 8 states the pooled unmarked-crossing count **as a lower bound in those words**, so the cohort is not told an inventory is complete when the design knows it is not. The full fix waits for a third instance.

**Objection 2: the day's hard failure condition is applied to work that depends on a course the seminar strand does not control and did not check.**

At its strongest: step 5 is the day's single hard requirement, it fails an entry with neither an enforcement point nor an accepted re-declaration, and placing an enforcement point requires knowing what a gateway, a quota enforcement layer or a circuit breaker can actually do. That material is SY301's. **SY301 ran last quarter.** [M15](/wiki/modules/M15.md) has no concurrent systems course, so the [Whitepaper Standard](/wiki/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md)'s cross-course deferral rule — which exists precisely to prevent a seminar day relying on content it cannot see — **does not apply, because it is written for concurrent courses and this reliance runs backward.**

The rule's silence is not a defence. [WP-041](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-041.md) §8 established that a deferral to a course the seminar does not control is unverifiable in principle, and the direction of time does not change that: the seminar strand has no more visibility into whether SY301's gateway material landed than it would into a concurrent course's. **A day that fails a student for not placing an enforcement point has assumed a retrieval it never checked.**

**The day is fixed.** Step 5 now requires the student to **state which SY301 material they used to identify each enforcement point** — which is [WP-044](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-044.md) §8's remedy exactly, carried across from the concurrent case to the backward one, and it is a checked element. The correction is annotated in the day at the point of change.

**The route by which the fix was found is worth recording, because it is the first use of a mechanism that does not yet exist.** [WP-072](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-072.md) §8 argued for a **rule-5 checklist** — every remedy in the design's repertoire, listed with the failure it addresses, consulted at §5 of every whitepaper — after two rule-5 failures in two consecutive days. This paper consulted that list, informally and for the first time, while writing §5's less-prior-knowledge paragraph, and it surfaced [WP-044](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-044.md)'s artefact-element remedy against a failure the deferral rule's wording had made invisible. **Per instrumentation rule 5 as amended at [WP-054](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-054.md) §8, the checklist is a proposed remedy and this use of it is contingent** on the outcome reported for [WP-072](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-072.md)'s proposal; a reported failure retracts this reuse along with every other.

**What remains wrong is conceded and it is inherited.** The clause records **what the student says they used**, which is a self-report about the content of a course the seminar cannot inspect. It makes the reliance visible and does not make it verifiable, which is the same limitation [WP-044](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-044.md)'s own fix carries and which that paper did not claim to have solved either.

**A widening is argued and not enacted.** The scope rule's deferral clause covers **forward** deferral to a concurrent course. It says nothing about **backward reliance on a completed course**, which is the more common case in year three and which has the same unverifiability. The natural form is: *where a seminar day relies on material from a course the seminar strand does not control, whether concurrent or completed, the day must carry an artefact element requiring the student to state which course material they applied.* That is a change to a shared page and this paper cannot make it; it is a **recorded** finding rather than a fixed one under the register's criterion, because whether backward reliance warrants the same treatment as forward deferral is a design judgement and is arguable in more than one direction.

**Objection 3: a day built to reject declarations was itself assessing declarations.**

This is the objection that bites on the practical turn and it is the sharpest of the three, because the day supplies the standard it fails.

At its strongest, as it applied to this design's previous draft: point 1 says the tool's name and schema are the specification and everything else is commentary. Point 9 says an annotation is a declaration and no client is obliged to honour it. Step 3 fails a clause marked enforced without a named mechanism. Step 5 fails an entry with neither an enforcement point nor an honest acceptance. **And then the artefact was submitted, marked and returned without any of those enforcement claims ever being tried.** A student wrote *schema* beside a prohibited action; the schema was never presented with that action; the assessor checked that a mechanism had been named and could be pointed at on a drawing. **That is a declaration about a boundary, assessed by reading, on the day whose entire content is that a declaration about a boundary assessed by reading is worth nothing.** The day failed the routing-aid-with-a-firmer-tone at the level of prose and accepted it at the level of mechanism.

**And it is the second instance in three days.** [WP-071](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-071.md) §8 objection 1 conceded that its day claims a boundary refuses things and tests it on a shadow path that never refuses, named a pooled-boundary fix, declined it, and consoled itself with the statement that *the programme obtains the evidence eventually.* This paper is where eventually was supposed to arrive, and in the previous draft it did not.

**The fix is one call and it is made.** Step 7 now requires the student to attempt one call their own boundary prohibits, against their own deployed tool, and to report what happened — the mechanism that refused it, or the fact that nothing did. It costs one call and no phase time, because the tools are deployed and callable from phase 3 onward, and it converts exactly one enforcement claim per student from a declaration into an observation. **This is the first occasion in the programme on which a student's own boundary is asked to hold**, and phase 8 reports the cohort's refusals with the mechanism that produced each, because a refusal produced by something other than the mechanism the student named is the most instructive outcome available.

**Three things are conceded about the fix and none of them is small.** It tests **one** prohibition of ten or more, chosen by the student, and a student choosing which of their claims to expose will not choose at random. It tests refusal and not coverage — a mechanism that refuses the call attempted may not refuse the neighbouring one, which is [S076](/wiki/seminars/S076.md)'s work and not this day's. And **a succeeding call is recorded and not remediated inside the day**, because the phase has no time to rebuild an enforcement point and the honest output is the finding rather than the repair. The fix moves the day from no refusal evidence to one refusal observation per student, which is a large relative improvement and a small absolute one, and §9 prediction 5 is written to measure which it turns out to be.

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** At item 1, the cohort will name more omissions in the *specification promises what the tool does not do* direction than in the *tool does what the specification does not mention* direction, and the asymmetry will be larger than it was at [S034](/wiki/seminars/S034.md). *Instrument:* item 1 responses coded by direction and compared per student against the [S034](/wiki/seminars/S034.md) item 4 result. **The widening asymmetry is the claim**: an over-promising specification is found by reading, and an under-describing one is found only by using the tool, which is the habit this module needs and which four quarters of contract work have not built.

**Prediction 2.** At item 4, the median predicted crossing count will be lower than the substrate's inventory by more than a factor of two, and the systematically missed category will be values the system itself authored under an earlier request and read back. *Instrument:* item 4 responses against the inventory, with misses coded by the author category at point 4. **If the missed category is third-party document content instead, the room is already thinking about external content and the day's problem is narrower than §1 claims.**

**Prediction 3.** At step 5, more entries will be closed by accepted re-declaration than by enforcement point, and the ratio will be higher for [S071](/wiki/seminars/S071.md) prohibitions than for [S072](/wiki/seminars/S072.md) breach paths. *Instrument:* step 5 closures coded three ways and split by source list. **The differential is the claim**: a breach the student watched happen is harder to accept than a prohibition they wrote in the abstract, and if the two rates are equal then observing a breach changes nothing about how it is treated, which would be a finding about [S072](/wiki/seminars/S072.md)'s step 7 rather than about this day.

**Prediction 4.** More than a quarter of step 4 reductions will cost nothing — the student will narrow the schema without giving up any request a substrate user makes. *Instrument:* step 4 cost statements checked against the substrate's request log at phase 7. **If this holds it is not a failure of the students**; it means the substrate's tool schemas are broader than its usage requires by a wide margin, which is a finding about the substrate that [S076](/wiki/seminars/S076.md) will consume, and the design predicts it will be the single most actionable result the day produces for the platform.

**Prediction 5.** At step 7's attempted prohibited call, **more than a third of attempts will succeed**, and among those refused, more than a quarter will be refused by a mechanism other than the one the student named at step 5. *Instrument:* attempted calls coded refused / succeeded, with the refusing mechanism recorded and joined to the step 5 entry for the same prohibition. **Both halves are the claim.** A high success rate is direct evidence for §8 objection 3 and for [WP-071](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-071.md) §8 objection 1, and it is the first such evidence the programme has. **A high wrong-mechanism rate is the more interesting result**: it would mean students are protected by enforcement they did not identify, which is point 2's second named omission — enforcement downstream of the boundary — measured rather than asserted, and it would make the case for widening step 5 to require a downstream inventory.

**Prediction 6.** At item 5, the modal predicted location for the agent's prohibition will be the tool's description, and the modal actual location will be the same. *Instrument:* item 5 responses and produced artefacts both coded into the six locations reported at phase 8. **Agreement is the claim, and agreement is the uncomfortable result**: it would mean the cohort can predict the failure and would not have caught it, because predicting where an artefact will be wrong and reviewing for it are different acts. If the room predicts the description and the agent uses a schema constraint, the instrument has found a capability the design did not credit and the phase 6 agent case needs replacing.

**Instrumentation note.** No compliance-register entry is required by this paper. §8 objection 2's finding is a **recorded** widening argument rather than a violation of a written rule, since the rule as written does not reach backward reliance; the day's fix was taken anyway, on rule 5 grounds, and is annotated at the point of change. **§8 objection 3's fix is likewise annotated in the day and requires no register entry**: it repairs an internal inconsistency between the day's own teaching and its own assessment rather than a violation of a shared rule, and the argument for it is carried here so that it can be disagreed with.

## What a student should take from this paper

**What the design is claiming.** That a tool boundary is a caller contract with a caller who will not read it, that only four things can actually enforce a line — schema, permission, precondition, rate — and that the boundary of trust is a **place** on a path. A team that cannot point at theirs does not have one.

**What the evidence licenses.** The tool, gateway and permission mechanics are **class 1** and are used here only as instances. The protocol layer is **class 4**: the word for what a tool declares about itself is *hint*, one documented proxy executes a call regardless of the approval setting because the runtime is responsible for gating it — and **no rate of any kind appears anywhere in that material**. The four enforceable things and the boundary-as-a-place definition are **class 3**. The paper's own correction is worth copying: that a model caller acts on instruction-shaped returned content is **class 2** here, direction and mechanism only, because no note in this repository verifies it.

**What to do with it.**

- **Untrusted content is data and never authority** — see the [Agent Configuration Standard](/wiki/program/13-Agent-Configuration-Standard.md). Every fetched page, tool output and issue body is input, and inputs do not issue instructions.
- **Mark each contract clause enforced or prose, and name which of the four enforces it.** An annotation is prose.
- **Narrow the schema before you add enforcement** — a field removed carries no crossing.
- **Read a pooled unmarked-crossing count as a floor**: it was computed over the paths somebody drew.

**The concession worth remembering.** The paper concedes that a day built to reject declarations was itself assessing declarations — enforcement claims read on a drawing and never tried. **Hold the design to that.** The fix is one attempted prohibited call, and it is conceded three times over: it tests one prohibition out of ten or more and you choose which, it tests refusal and not coverage, and a call that succeeds is recorded rather than repaired.

**Grounded in vendor documentation, with its version and its silence recorded.** Point 9 is taken from Microsoft Learn, read 2026-07-26. The Azure MCP Server tools page states that **tool annotations are hints that provide additional information about the characteristics of each tool**, and enumerates them as **destructive**, **idempotent**, **open world** and **read only**, with two described as Microsoft proprietary — **secret**, indicating that a response might contain material requiring sanitisation, and **local required**. The Security Copilot MCP page states that **tools with the destructive hint set to true are not supported and will not be imported**, even when listed in the allowed-tools setting, which is the documented instance of a client honouring a hint. The Foundry toolbox page states that **`require_approval` is agent-enforced**, that **the toolbox MCP proxy executes `tools/call` regardless of this setting**, and that **the agent runtime is responsible for gating the call**, with the setting taking the values `always` and `never`. **Three silences are recorded and two are taught.** The documentation defines each annotation and **does not state which clients honour which of them**, so the only general statement available is the one the day makes — honouring is a property of the client — and the day tells students to look it up per runtime rather than to assume. It states that the proxy executes regardless of the approval setting and **does not state what fraction of runtimes gate**, nor any figure for how often an approval is granted or refused; **no rate of any kind appears anywhere in this material**, which is the folder-wide asymmetry recorded in [research/06-microsoft-platform/](/research/06-microsoft-platform/collected-materials.md) appearing again, and point 10 names it to students. And the guidance on third-party servers assigns responsibility without stating any **criterion** by which a server should be judged trustworthy, which is the gap point 7's measurement obligation is written to fill for the student rather than for the vendor. The pages carry no version number, the protocol is versioned by date on its own specification, and every field, value and annotation name in points 9 and 10 is marked perishable and grounds nothing the specification assesses.

**Cited from general knowledge, not verified here.** Pretesting before vocabulary is supplied, contrasting cases matched on capability and varied on boundary construction, expanding-interval retrieval with scaffolded prompts at long gaps, pre-commitment before measurement, external-observer measurement in place of self-report, and error management training are cited for **direction and mechanism only**. **No effect size is asserted for any of them.** **No magnitude is asserted for any protocol's or platform's enforcement behaviour**: every figure in phase 3 is a property of one substrate over one period, is supplied as an adjudicator, and is marked perishable. **The tendency of a model caller to act on instruction-shaped content arriving in a returned value is cited here for direction and mechanism only.** An earlier revision of this section placed it in the verified class against [research/02-technical-foundations/](/research/02-technical-foundations/collected-materials.md); **no note in that folder carries it and none elsewhere in this repository verifies it**, and it is developed at [S076](/wiki/seminars/S076.md) rather than here. **No claim is made about the frequency with which model callers act on instruction-shaped returned content**; the day demonstrates the transition on one trace and assigns the rate question to [S076](/wiki/seminars/S076.md).
