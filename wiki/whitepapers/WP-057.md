## In plain terms

The most consequential sentence in any governance regime is the one deciding whether the regime applies, and it is the sentence nobody reads.

The regime this module draws on was found in two generations and **the change between them is the single best governance idea in the collection.**

**The older test asked whether the team had built or fine-tuned a model.** That is an effort test, and it was reasonable when the deliverable was a model.

**The newer test asks whether an artefact capable of AI behaviour crossed the boundary into the customer's control**, such that they can execute it, test it or operationalise it. That is a surface test, and it closes a hole the effort test cannot.

The hole is not hypothetical. **When the deliverable is a configured agent rather than a trained model, an effort test lets the highest-velocity work in modern delivery escape governance entirely** — no model work was done, so nothing applies, and the customer is now running something that acts. The surface test also survives the *next* change in how systems are assembled, because it asks about a boundary rather than about a technique, and boundaries persist while techniques do not.

**Then the same regime draws its line at authored artefacts and excludes configuration using existing controls.** That is defensible on ownership grounds and indefensible on harm grounds, and both halves are true at once: a carelessly configured off-the-shelf agent in a hiring workflow can do exactly the damage the regime exists to prevent, and it is out of scope.

The session's assessed work is that the learner writes their own test and then **breaks it twice** — once on harm, once on cost. A test whose author has found no fault in it has not been read by its author.

## Abstract

This paper defends teaching a governance scope test as a surface question about artefact transfer rather than an effort question about model work, and defends requiring learners to identify both a case their own test wrongly excludes and a case it wrongly includes. The argument is that an effort test lets agentic and low-code delivery escape governance without any rule being broken; that a boundary-based test survives changes in assembly technique because boundaries outlast techniques; and that a test which catches everything is routed around, so over-inclusion carries a real cost. The session installs a scope test in artefact-transfer form, a boundary located in credentials rather than in organisational description, a full classification of deliverables, two self-identified failures of the test, and a plan for the wrongly excluded case that does not depend on the regime applying. The paper concedes that artefact transfer is a diffuse and continuous trigger where model training was an observable one, that requiring a private supplement to a public rule carries its own hazards, and that no source establishes that any scope determination has ever prevented anything.

## 1. The problem this session must solve

Governance applies or it does not, and the decision is made once, early, by whoever fills in a form. **Everything downstream — assessment, review, approval, records — depends on a determination nobody revisits.**

An effort test makes that determination on the wrong variable. It asks how much model work was done, which was a reasonable proxy for risk when producing a model was the expensive and consequential act. **It is now a proxy for nothing.** The most consequential deliverable a team can hand over is frequently the one that took the least model work: a configured agent with tool access, an instruction set, an evaluation harness, a prompt file. Under an effort test all of that is out of scope, **and no rule has been broken.**

The second problem is that the corrected test has a boundary in it, and boundaries are easy to describe and hard to locate. **A team that can describe its boundary organisationally and not in credentials cannot apply its own test**, because the question *has this crossed?* becomes a matter of interpretation at exactly the moment interpretation favours the answer that avoids work.

The third is that scope tests fail in two directions and only one of them is ever discussed. **A regime that catches everything is routed around**, and an unenforced rule protects nobody while consuming the goodwill a narrower one would have had.

## 3. Theoretical basis

**3.1 Load profile.** Low intrinsic load, high classificatory load. The test is a paragraph; applying it honestly to a real deliverable list is where the session's time goes.

**3.2 Why a boundary outlasts a technique.** A test keyed to how a system was made must be rewritten every time systems are made differently, and the rewrite always lags the practice. **A test keyed to a transfer of control does not care how the artefact was produced.** That is the durability argument and it is structural rather than empirical.

**3.3 Why the boundary must be located in credentials.** *Can execute, test or operationalise* is a capability claim, and capability is held by whoever has the credentials and the ability to change the thing. **An organisational description of a boundary cannot answer the question and a credential map can**, which converts an interpretive judgement into a lookup.

**3.4 Why the test must be broken twice.** Over-inclusion and under-inclusion have different costs and different remedies, and a learner who checks only one direction produces a test that fails silently in the other. **The wrongly excluded case is a harm argument; the wrongly included case is a compliance-economics argument**, and this programme has already established, at [S053](/wiki/seminars/S053.md), that the same instinct can be right in one direction and disastrous in the other.

**3.5 Why the excluded case gets a plan anyway.** A harm you have identified does not stop being a harm because your rule missed it. **The professional position is that the rule bounds the organisation's obligation and not the engineer's knowledge**, which is an argument rather than a finding, and §8 gives it its strongest objection.

## 4. Session design and its warrant

The seven movements run situation, artefact, standard, failure modes, work, review, limits.

**Opening item 2 is the argument in a single question.** *You configured an agent, wrote no model code, and handed it over — in scope under an effort test?* The answer is no and every learner sees the consequence immediately. Nothing else in the session needs to persuade them.

**The work movement forbids softened verbs.** *Provided*, *shared* and *made available* describe things a customer cannot necessarily run, and a test using them is a test about documents. **The verb carries the whole determination**, which is why it is named as an object of attention rather than left to drafting.

**The classification starts with the awkward items on purpose.** A learner who classifies the obvious deliverables first arrives at the awkward ones with a pattern already established and applies it. Starting there makes the test do work.

**The review movement is independent classification rather than critique.** Two people applying the same paragraph to the same list, disagreeing, is the only cheap evidence available about the wording — **and the wording is the only part of a scope test that is ever used.**

**The reviewer's closing question is deliberately not a governance question.** *Which of these would you be uncomfortable being responsible for?* elicits a set built from intuition rather than from rules, and the gap between that set and the in-scope set is the session's most useful diagnostic.

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**The effort test in disguise.** The likeliest failure. *Countermeasure:* the standard's diagnostic — your test's answer depends on how much work you did.

**The unlocatable boundary.** *Countermeasure:* the credential requirement, and the warning sign — you cannot say who can change the artefact next month.

**The excluded configuration.** *Countermeasure:* the worked example of the regime's own authored-artefact line, given in the work movement so the learner sees a competent organisation make the mistake before looking for their own.

**The faultless test.** *Countermeasure:* both break-it elements are named artefacts rather than reflections, so their absence is visible.

**The everything test.** *Countermeasure:* the pricing requirement on the wrongly included case. **This is the design's weakest countermeasure**, because the cost of over-inclusion falls on people the learner will never meet.

**The orphaned exclusion.** *Countermeasure:* the final element, and the standard's warning sign — your answer is that it is out of scope.

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: artefact transfer is a diffuse and continuous trigger, and a test built on it is unenforceable.**

At its strongest: model training was an observable event with a log entry. Artefacts cross boundaries constantly and informally — a screenshot in a chat, a prompt pasted into a message, a notebook shared for a demonstration, a configuration exported so somebody could reproduce a result. **A test that catches all of those is unworkable; a test that draws a line somewhere in the middle is arbitrary, and arbitrariness is exactly what the effort test was criticised for.** The regime has traded an enforceable bad test for an unenforceable better one.

**Conceded in part, and the rebuttal is narrower than the objection.** The verb does more work than the objection allows: *can execute, test or operationalise* is a capability claim, not a receipt claim, and a screenshot conveys no capability. The credential requirement then makes the remaining determination a lookup rather than a judgement — whoever can run it and change it holds it. **The declined fix is to enumerate the artefact types in scope.** It is declined for the reason the session exists: **an enumeration ages exactly as fast as the delivery model changes**, which is the failure the effort test already demonstrated, and replacing one obsolescent list with another obsolescent list is not progress. **The residual enforceability gap is real, and the design's only answer is that a diffuse test applied honestly beats a crisp test that misses the consequential case.**

**Objection 2: requiring a plan for the wrongly excluded case teaches that governance is optional and that private engineering judgement supersedes an organisational rule.**

At its strongest: an organisation cannot function if every engineer maintains a personal supplement to its compliance regime. The supplementary work is unbudgeted, unreviewed and unaccountable, and a harm handled privately by a well-meaning engineer is handled by somebody with no mandate, no specialist knowledge and no oversight. **That is worse than a known gap in a public rule, because the gap can at least be argued about.**

**Conceded in part, and the design's answer turns on where the plan is recorded.** The element does not ask for a private supplement. **It asks for the excluded case and its handling to be written into the same assessment**, where a reviewer sees them, where the organisation can adopt or refuse them, and where they become the evidence a regime needs in order to widen its own test. That is the opposite of private. **The declined fix is to require escalation of the gap instead of handling it.** It is declined because escalation with no proposed handling is a complaint, and because the regime this module draws on has no route for receiving one: its non-conformance state covers an inability to comply, not an inadequacy of the test itself. **A learner who escalates into that regime has filed nothing, and the harm remains unhandled while the paperwork looks correct.**

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** Most first tests will be effort tests in new wording. *Instrument:* the submitted test, with a second reader determining whether its answer varies with the amount of work done.

**Prediction 2.** Independent classification will disagree on at least one deliverable for most learners. *Instrument:* the paired classifications from the review movement, compared item by item.

**Prediction 3.** Wrongly included cases will be absent more often than wrongly excluded cases. *Instrument:* the presence of each named case, coded per learner. **If this fails, the over-inclusion cost is more salient to learners than the design expects and §3.4's symmetry argument needs revisiting.**

**Prediction 4.** The set of deliverables a reviewer would be uncomfortable owning will differ from the learner's in-scope set. *Instrument:* the two sets compared per learner, with the difference recorded in both directions.

## What a learner should take from this paper

**What the design claims.** That a scope test keyed to a boundary crossing outlasts one keyed to a technique; that the boundary has to be locatable in credentials to be usable; and that a test its author cannot break has not been read by its author.

**What the evidence licenses.** Nothing about harm prevented. **No source available reports a case in which a scope determination stopped anything**, and the regime this is drawn from publishes no evidence that any assessment it triggered ever changed a system. The comparison between its two generations is evidence about coverage, obtained by reading both, and is not a measurement of consequence.

**The concession worth remembering.** The best governance idea in the material has a hole its own authors chose, on ownership grounds, knowing it. Your test will have one too, and finding it is the only part of this you control.
