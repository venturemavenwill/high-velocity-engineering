## In plain terms

A spike is not a rough first attempt at the system. It is an instrument pointed at one uncertainty, and everything that distinguishes it from a rough first attempt is written down before the work starts.

Six things go in that writing: the question, the decision waiting on it, the bound, what evidence would count, where the code will live and what happens to it, and what the spike will not decide. **None of the six can be reconstructed afterwards.** A question written after the answer is a description; evidence named after it is found is a rationalisation; and a disposal rule written at the end of a hard week, next to something that works, will not be written at all.

The two hard rules come from the openly published playbook and both are prohibitions rather than techniques. **The goal of a spike is fact-finding, not decision-making or recommendation.** And **the code written for a spike is not going to serve as the code for the final solution.** Together they stop a spike becoming an unreviewed prototype in production, which is the failure that makes the whole instrument counterproductive.

The session's real difficulty is that the tooling has made the distinction invisible from the outside. An investigation now routinely produces something that runs, looks finished and could be demonstrated tomorrow. **The difference between that and the beginning of a system is entirely in the charter**, which is why the charter is the assessed artefact and the code is not.

## Abstract

This paper defends the spike as a chartered instrument rather than an exploratory activity, and locates the whole of its discipline in a document written beforehand. The argument is that a feasibility investigation's value depends on properties — a falsifiable question, a declared bound, pre-specified evidence and a disposal rule — none of which can be established retrospectively, and that generative tooling has removed the physical friction that previously enforced the last of them. The session installs a six-part charter, a bound with a stop rule in both directions, and a peer review that tests whether the charter can distinguish an investigation from the start of a system. The paper concedes that the disposal rule is a prohibition with no measurement behind it, that the playbook supplying the protocol offers no comparative evidence for any of its practices, and that the corpus contains a flagship anecdote which the design cannot distinguish from the failure it warns against.

## 1. The problem this session must solve

A design has an uncertainty in it and the team knows which one. The available responses are to start building, to argue about it, or to investigate.

Investigation is obviously right and is nonetheless the least stable of the three, because an investigation has no natural boundary. It ends when the person doing it feels finished, it produces whatever it produces, and its output is reported in whatever form the reporter chooses — which is very often a recommendation, because a recommendation is what the room asked for.

**Each of those three degradations is separately fatal and they compound.** An unbounded investigation consumes the schedule the proof was supposed to protect. An investigation reported as a recommendation absorbs its own evidence, so that a reader who disagrees with the conclusion cannot use the observations. And an investigation whose code is retained converts a cheap enquiry into an expensive foundation that nobody reviewed.

The session's response is to move all three decisions to a point before the work, where they can be made cheaply and checked by somebody else.

## 2. Entry state and prior knowledge

**Entry state: this-programme-only.** [S030](/wiki/seminars/S030.md) is required, and the requirement is structural rather than conventional: a spike's charter must name the decision waiting on it, and until a design exists at the scope of its decision there is no such decision. A learner who spikes before framing produces a competent investigation of something nobody was going to act on, which §5 records as the second-commonest failure.

[S020](/wiki/seminars/S020.md) is also assumed, for the stopping rule. The prior it works against is that a technical investigation is somehow exempt from the discipline a discovery is held to — that *we will know when we have it* is an acceptable bound for a spike, though it was rejected for a discovery ten sessions earlier. **The two are the same rule and the session says so.**

## 3. Theoretical basis

**3.1 Load profile.** Moderate. The charter's six parts are individually simple. The load is evaluative rather than intrinsic: several of the parts require a judgement about what the answer might be, made before there is one, and learners find that uncomfortable rather than difficult.

**3.2 Why pre-specification is not merely tidy.** A question, a bound and an evidence standard written after the investigation are all consistent with any outcome, because they were selected in the presence of it. Written before, each of them can fail. The mechanism is the same one behind [S020](/wiki/seminars/S020.md)'s stopping rule and behind the falsifiability requirement at [S022](/wiki/seminars/S022.md), and this session is its third application rather than a new claim.

**3.3 Why the bound needs a stop rule in both directions.** A bound that only stops on success is a target with a deadline attached, and it will be extended. A bound that stops on exhaustion produces a second finding — that the question is more expensive than it looked — and that finding is frequently more valuable than the answer, because it changes the design rather than confirming it.

**3.4 Why disposal is enforced by location rather than by intention.** The rule survives if the code lives somewhere that would have to be deliberately moved, and does not survive if it lives where it could simply be merged. **This is a claim about mechanism design, not about character**, and it is the only form of the rule this paper is prepared to defend.

## 4. Session design and its warrant

The seven movements run situation, artefact, standard, failure modes, work, review, limits.

**The opening items are the instrument and item 3 is the one the design cares about.** Asked what happens to the code, most learners say it is kept, and they say it without hesitation because the alternative sounds wasteful. The gap between that answer and the playbook's flat statement — that spike code is not going to serve as the final solution's code — is the session's actual content, and it is much more effective as a surprise than as an instruction.

**The charter is assessed and the code is not**, throughout the module. This is a deliberate inversion of what learners expect and it is defensible on one narrow ground: the code is the part that does not survive, and a curriculum that marked it would be teaching that it does.

**The review movement removes the disposal line and asks a peer to classify the charter.** If the other five parts cannot distinguish a spike from the beginning of a system, the charter is decorative — which matters because the charter is the only part of a spike that outlives it.

**The corpus's contradiction is handed over rather than resolved.** It states, as a headline rule, that the tooling is for making each step better and not for skipping steps; its customer-facing material's proudest anecdote is a prototype generated on day one from a reusable starting point with no discovery activity described at all. **Neither document notices.** The session presents both and asks the learner what charter, if any, would have made the second one legitimate. **There is a defensible answer and the design does not supply it.**

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**The spike that becomes the product.** The likeliest failure, and the one the whole session exists for. *Countermeasure:* the disposal line, its date, and the location rule — the code lives somewhere that would have to be deliberately moved. **This is partial and the paper does not overstate it.** A line in a charter does not prevent a merge under deadline pressure; [S035](/wiki/seminars/S035.md) makes retention an assessed disclosure rather than a prohibition, precisely because a rule enforced by virtue is not enforced.

**The unactionable question.** The learner spikes something interesting. *Countermeasure:* the Monday question in the review movement — what changes if the answer is no — asked by a peer rather than by the learner.

**The recommendation smuggled into the goal.** *Countermeasure:* the standard's warning sign, and §4's separation of fact-finding from decision-making, which is reinforced as an assessed element at [S035](/wiki/seminars/S035.md).

**The generated foundation.** An agent produces something that runs, and running is treated as the finding. *Countermeasure:* the requirement that evidence be specified before it exists. If the charter says the evidence is a comparison over inputs the learner did not choose, an artefact that runs on a chosen input does not satisfy it.

**Refusal to bound.** A learner declines to set a limit on the grounds that the question is unpredictable. *Countermeasure:* none available in one session. The bound is revisited at [S035](/wiki/seminars/S035.md), where reaching it without an answer is an explicit pass.

## 7. Assessment and evidence generated

No entrustment evidence. Three specification items at [S035](/wiki/seminars/S035.md): that the bound was declared before the work and its outcome reported; that the finding states facts rather than recommendations; and that disposal is reported as an act.

**Never assessed:** whether the answer was yes, the quality of the spike's code, or how many spikes were run.

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: discarding working code is waste, and a curriculum that teaches it is teaching a superstition that will not survive commercial pressure.**

At its strongest: the code works. It was written by a competent engineer against a real question. Deleting it and rewriting it costs a week that nobody has, and the argument for doing so — that it was not written to production standards — is an argument for reviewing it, not for destroying it. In an environment where generating the code was the cheap part, the retained artefact is nearly free and the rewrite is the expensive one. **A rule that made sense when code was costly to produce has been left standing after its economics were removed.**

**Substantially conceded, and the concession changes the rule's form rather than its content.** The design's rebuttal is narrow: the hazard is not retention, it is *undisclosed* retention. Spike code enters production having skipped the review that production code receives, and it does so at exactly the moment when everybody is relieved. So [S035](/wiki/seminars/S035.md) does not prohibit retention — it makes retention an assessed disclosure with a named reviewer and a named authority. **The declined fix is to drop the disposal line from the charter and rely on the disclosure alone.** It is declined because the line is what makes the disclosure noticeable; without it, retention is the default and there is nothing to report. **Whether that holds under real delivery pressure is untested and the paper does not pretend otherwise.**

**Objection 2: the design cannot distinguish its own recommended practice from the corpus's flagship anecdote, and it says so instead of solving it.**

At its strongest: a spike is a fast investigation producing a working fragment. A day-one prototype from a reusable starting point is a fast investigation producing a working fragment. The session's answer is that one has a charter and the other does not, which makes the entire distinction a piece of paperwork — and a sufficiently motivated team will write the paperwork afterwards. **A distinction that can be manufactured retrospectively is not a distinction.**

**Conceded without qualification.** The charter can be backdated and the design has no defence against a team that chooses to. What it has is the claim that the charter cannot be backdated *honestly*, because its evidence clause and its exclusion clause both become obviously convenient once the answer is known — and that a peer reading a charter can usually tell. **That is an argument about detectability, not about prevention.** The declined fix is to require an external time-stamp or a supervisor's counter-signature on every charter. It is declined because a ceremony that must be witnessed will be run for the smallest possible number of spikes, which produces fewer charters rather than more honest ones. **The reasoning is design reasoning and is not warranted by anything.**

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** A large majority of learners will answer opening item 3 by saying the spike's code is kept, before instruction. *Instrument:* the written answers, coded *kept* / *discarded* / *conditional* by a second reader.

**Prediction 2.** In the review movement, peers will be unable to classify charter-versus-system for a substantial share of charters. *Instrument:* the peer's binary judgement against the author's declared intent, with disagreements counted.

**Prediction 3.** Charters whose bound states a stop rule in both directions will more often report a bound reached without an answer at [S035](/wiki/seminars/S035.md) than charters whose bound states only a target. *Instrument:* the charter's bound clause, coded, against the [S035](/wiki/seminars/S035.md) finding's reported outcome.

**Prediction 4.** The disposal discipline will transfer to [S036](/wiki/seminars/S036.md) less often than to [S035](/wiki/seminars/S035.md) — learners who discarded spike code will nonetheless retain and refine their concept artefact. *Instrument:* the [S036](/wiki/seminars/S036.md) stripping record, coded for whether the second artefact is an amendment of the first, against the [S035](/wiki/seminars/S035.md) disposal statement. **If this fails, the module's claim that disposal is a transferable discipline rather than a code-specific rule has support it currently lacks.**

## What a learner should take from this paper

**What the design claims.** That a spike's value lives in a document written before it, that its two prohibitions are what keep it cheap, and that the tooling has removed the friction which used to enforce the second one.

**What the evidence licenses.** A protocol, published openly by an organisation that holds itself to it, with **no comparative evidence that it beats an alternative.** Everything about outcomes is unwarranted.

**The concession worth remembering.** The disposal rule survived for decades partly because retaining spike code was inconvenient. That inconvenience is gone, and this paper's response — convert the prohibition into a disclosure — is a repair, not a proof that the original rule was right.
