## In plain terms

**What this paper argues, before the formal version.**

You choose an algorithm by whether the problem looks like one you have seen before. That is what you were trained to do, it is fast, and it works right up until it does not — because **looking alike and being alike are different things.** Two scheduling problems can differ by one constraint. The greedy algorithm runs on both. On one of them it gives you the best answer and on the other it gives you a perfectly reasonable, feasible, plausible answer that is worse than the best one by an amount nobody ever measures.

That is the important part. **Nothing crashes.** There is no exception, no error, no obviously wrong output — just answers that are quietly not as good as they should be. Your own tests will not catch it, because you wrote them while believing the algorithm was right. Finding it needs either a proof or a comparison against the true answer, and neither of those happens by accident.

The way out is that every one of these methods has a property it needs the problem to have, and **each of those properties can be checked individually and cheaply.** They just never are, because picking-by-resemblance never produces a claim that anyone would think to check. So the day's demand is: say what structural property you are assuming, then check it — and it grades you on the check rather than on the choice, because a right choice and a lucky guess produce identical working code.

The obvious complaint is that if you already chose correctly, the check is a bureaucratic formality. **The day answers that by making the check a single query parameter on a live search service.** You can ask the index for its best guess or for the true answer over exactly the same data, and if the two disagree you have just watched your own assumption fail on your own data in about four seconds. It is hard to call that ceremony.

**What this paper concedes, and it is the one that should follow you out of the room.** All the persuasive evidence on the day comes from four things you actually run, and those four reach three of the methods. **The fourth — dynamic programming, the one you probably hold most confidently and check least — is never demonstrated at all**, because the service has no instance of it. So you will leave having watched three assumptions become checkable in public, and you will assume the fourth is too, on no evidence. The paper says the omission is a decision made because the day was full, and that it is presented in the teaching as though it were a fact about the service.

## Abstract

This paper defends framing algorithmic paradigms as falsifiable hypotheses about problem structure rather than as techniques matched to problem types, and defends requiring an explicit hypothesis check whether or not the paradigm chosen turns out to be correct. The first argument is that paradigm selection by resemblance to remembered examples succeeds on problems resembling the examples and fails silently otherwise, producing fast wrong answers that no test suite constructed by the same student will detect; and that the structural properties licensing each paradigm are individually checkable, which converts an act of recognition into an act of verification. The second argument is that assessing the check rather than the choice is the only way to distinguish a student who reasoned from one who guessed correctly, since both produce working code. The paper additionally defends extending the day's failure discipline from correctness to detectability, applying the signal requirement established at S018 to a class of error that produces no operational symptom; and defends running the day's contrasting cases as four retrieval configurations over one live search index, on the ground that the index supplies both an instance of the greedy hypothesis and, on the same field, the exhaustive adjudicator that can falsify it. It concedes that the hypothesis framing is a reconstruction that may not describe how paradigm selection is actually performed, that the day's silent-failure emphasis rests on a claim about detection that the design has not evidenced, and that the platform instance covers three of the four paradigms while being presented as though it covered the taxonomy.

## 1. The instructional problem this day must solve

Students select algorithmic paradigms by resemblance. They have seen a dynamic programming problem, a greedy problem and a divide-and-conquer problem; a new problem is matched against the remembered set, and the closest match supplies the method. This procedure is fast, it is what the standard curriculum trains, and it fails in a specific and expensive way.

The failure is that resemblance is a surface property and paradigm applicability is a structural one. Two scheduling problems can differ by a single constraint — whether jobs may be preempted, whether weights are uniform, whether the deadline structure is nested — and one admits a greedy solution while the other does not. The problems look alike. The greedy algorithm runs on both. On the second it returns an answer that is feasible, plausible and not optimal.

This is the day's central point about *how* the failure presents. There is no crash, no exception and no obviously wrong output. The system produces schedules; the schedules are worse than they could be by an amount nobody measures. Detection requires either a proof or a comparison against an optimum that is expensive to compute, and neither happens by accident. A student's own test suite will not find it, because the tests were written by the person who believed the algorithm was correct — S009's argument, applied here to a structural assumption rather than to an interface.

The remedy is that each paradigm's licensing property is individually checkable. The greedy-choice property admits an exchange argument. Optimal substructure with overlap can be exhibited. Subproblem independence can be shown. None of these checks is expensive; they are simply never performed, because resemblance-based selection does not produce a claim to check.

**The day's third problem is the one the platform anchor was chosen to solve, and it is a problem about the first two.** WP-021's original design asserted that the check was cheap and that the failure was silent, and asked students to accept both. A student who has selected correctly and been told to prove it anyway experiences the requirement as ceremony — §5 predicts this as the day's characteristic affective failure — and no amount of explanation dislodges it, because the explanation is what sounds like ceremony.

A vector search index removes the argument. The same field can be queried two ways: by descending a graph, which is a greedy selection whose correctness rests on the greedy-choice property holding on that graph, or by comparing against everything, which is the exhaustive limit and returns the true answer. **The check is a query parameter.** A student who runs both and finds the sets differ has watched their own greedy hypothesis fail, on their own data, in about four seconds, and the ceremony argument does not survive it.

The index also supplies a stronger version of the silent-failure claim than the design could previously make. An approximate vector query returns the requested number of results **whether or not anything in the corpus is relevant** — the documentation says so plainly, and notes that nonsensical and off-topic queries return results too. A method that structurally cannot return nothing is a method whose hypothesis failure has no output signature at all, and that is not an anecdote about a production system; it is a documented property of the service in front of the room.

The day's fourth problem is what to do when the check cannot be completed. Point 9 requires the hypothesis to be recorded as an assumption with a detection signal attached — which is the S018 discipline moved from a capacity threshold to a correctness property, and is the harder case, because a correctness violation has no operational symptom to monitor. Here too the platform helps and the help is honest about its limits: the exhaustive comparison is a real detection signal, it is cheap, and running it continuously would cost more than the approximation saves, so what students build is a sampled signal — which is the shape most real correctness monitoring takes.

## 3. Theoretical basis for the design

### 3.1 Load profile and element interactivity

**Moderate to high, concentrated at point 4 and step 3.** The exchange argument requires holding a hypothetical optimal solution, a greedy choice, and a transformation between them, and reasoning about all three jointly. It does not decompose and it is the module's first genuinely new proof technique.

The rest of the day is lower. Identifying structural properties is largely a matter of asking specific questions of the problem statement, and phase 3's four configurations are constructed so that each question has a clear answer.

**The platform anchor adds load and the design pays for it deliberately.** A new service, a new query shape and a new vocabulary arrive on the module's first day. Three decisions contain the cost:

- **The index is built once, by the cohort, and kept for all five module days.** Comprehension is paid on day one for the module, which is the same argument the module already makes for its single problem domain.
- **Phase 3 varies one thing.** Four configurations over one index, one query set, one embedding, one distance metric. Cases B and D differ by a single query parameter, which is the tightest controlled contrast in the module.
- **No index internals.** Graph construction, level assignment, pruning, quantisation and shard behaviour are all omitted. The day needs *greedy descent through a structure built at indexing time* and nothing more.

Other management:

- **The counterexample is supplied at item 2** rather than requested, so the pretest tests analysis rather than search.
- **The exchange-argument template is supplied at step 3,** and point 4 works one completely.
- **Step 1 worked with the room.**

### 3.2 Desirable difficulties selected, and those deliberately not

**Selected.**

- *Stating the structural property being claimed,* phase 3 and step 2 — the day's defining demand.
- *Distinguishing configurations B and D in problem terms rather than configuration terms,* phase 3's closing task.
- *Recording a failed check,* step 4, rather than silently discarding the attempt.
- *The partner's violating-input construction, executed,* phase 7.
- *Constructing a detection signal for a correctness assumption,* step 7, which is materially harder than S018's capacity signal.
- *The sealed Copilot prediction at item 5,* which requires a student to commit a position about a tool before seeing its output.

**Deliberately not selected.**

- *Withholding the four hypotheses.* Point 2 names them as checkable properties. They are vocabulary and criteria.
- *Deriving the exchange argument.* Worked at point 4.
- *Constructing counterexamples in the pretest.* Supplied, per the load decision above.
- *Approximation quality.* Deferred to S025; today a wrong answer is simply wrong. **This is now a two-day deferral with a named consumer**: S025's step 2 requires the exhaustive adjudicator this day introduces, so the deferral is discharged in an artefact rather than in a lecture.
- *Index internals and tuning.* Omitted per §3.1.
- *Assessment stakes on the same day.*

**The platform instance, scoped in the strong form.** The day teaches a graph traversal as an instance of greedy selection, and the simplification must carry its safe and unsafe conditions, because students will otherwise carry it into work this day does not supervise.

*Safe.* Reasoning about why an approximate search can fail to return a document that exists in the corpus; about why the exhaustive configuration is the right adjudicator for that question; about why a method that always returns a full result set cannot signal its own failure; and about which of the two configurations pays in memory and which pays in time. In every one of those the correspondence between greedy selection and graph descent is sufficient and correct.

*Unsafe.* Reasoning about **how often** the approximation misses, about what parameter values would fix it, about any other vector service by analogy, or about behaviour at a scale the cohort has not run. The day measures one corpus with one embedding at one index size on one service version. **The correspondence is a correspondence of structure and not of magnitude**, no miss rate may be inferred from the day's own measurements, and the unsafe condition includes, as it nearly always does, any version other than the one in front of you: the parameter names, their defaults and their permitted ranges are product decisions that can be changed in a release note.

The boundary is stated to students at point 10 rather than left to this paper, per the widening recorded on the [Whitepaper Standard](/wiki/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md), and **step 6's failing input class and step 7's detection signal are the artefact elements under which a student applying the simplification outside its safe range would fail** — a student who writes "the approximation is accurate" instead of naming an input class has left the range in the assessed artefact.

### 3.3 Engagement and motivational mechanism

The day runs on **an algorithm that is wrong and looks right**.

Item 1 supplies a greedy algorithm with a justification that reads well. Most students accept it. Item 2 supplies an input that breaks it, and the break is undeniable once seen and invisible before. That sequence — plausible argument, then concrete refutation — is the same structure as S004's argument C and is used deliberately, because by week twenty-one students have a category for it.

Phase 3's B-versus-D pair supplies the day's more durable engagement, and it is stronger than the paper variant it replaces. Two configurations of one field, differing by one parameter, one of which is exact and one of which is not, is a fact students find genuinely surprising and remember — and unlike the constructed problem variants, **nobody designed the surprise.** The closing demand, state the difference in problem terms rather than configuration terms, is what converts it into a transferable criterion.

**Round two is the day's cheapest and most reliable moment.** Three queries whose answers are not in the corpus, four configurations, and three of the four return a full page of confident scored results. Students arrive at *a method that cannot say no is a method whose failure has no signature* several minutes before phase 4 names it, and they arrive by observation rather than by argument.

Phase 7 is the day's most enjoyed phase by observation. Constructing an input that breaks someone's stated hypothesis is a bounded puzzle with a clear win condition, and it is the S006 breaking-caller game in a new domain, which means it requires no explanation. Requiring the query to be run rather than described sharpens the win condition without changing the game.

The competence risk is low. The failures on this day are failures of a method students were taught to use, and the day says so explicitly at point 1: resemblance-matching is what the standard curriculum trains, and its failure is not a personal deficiency. The same framing is extended to the agent at point 5 and phase 6 — the tool's confident wrong configuration is normal tool behaviour, not a trap laid for the student.

## 4. Phase-by-phase design and its warrant

**Phase 1 (25 min).** A plausible wrong greedy algorithm, a supplied counterexample, and then the day's move at item 3 — what property would the problem need. Item 3 is answered by very few, which is the pretest's finding. **Item 5 is the sealed Copilot prediction**, administered here and not later per instrumentation rule 8, and it is the day's contribution to the running frontier map. Its construction is deliberate: the instruction the students will give the agent is a single sentence containing the word *relevant*, which is the shape of instruction real teams give and contains no checkable criterion at all.

**Phase 2 (20 min).** A nine-week bridge asking which S008 obligation a dynamic programming base case discharges. The answer is establishment, and the item's function is to make the recurrence an inductive object rather than a formula.

**Phase 3 (45 min).** Four configurations, one index, one query set, executed. Configurations B and D differ by one query parameter and are the payload; a group that explains why they can differ has the greedy hypothesis, and a group that does not, does not. **Round two — three queries with no answer in the corpus — is the phase's second and cheaper finding**, and it is the one the design would keep if it could keep only one, because it converts the silent-failure claim from an assertion into an observation. The asymmetry between the two vector configurations is noticed here and explicitly not explained; it is S023's subject and pre-empting it would spend the next day's opening.

**Phase 4 (50 min).** Ten points. Point 1's two-sentence framing is the day's central design decision. Point 4 works the exchange argument completely, because S023 requires it. Point 5 maps phase 3's cases onto the taxonomy **and names what the mapping does not cover** — term-overlap scoring is not one of the four, and dynamic programming has no instance on the day's service. That admission is the enumeration-scope rule applied to the platform instance rather than to the taxonomy, and it is the first time this design has had to scope a *demonstration* rather than a list. Point 8 names four defaults the platform chose. Point 10 carries the strong-form scope statement and the perishability marking.

**Phase 5 (90 min).** Seven steps at class 2, **shipping a retrieval configuration** rather than describing a paradigm choice. Step 3's check is the specification's weighted element and is now executable: the exhaustive query on the same field is the adjudicator, and it costs a parameter. Step 4's recorded failed check is the honesty requirement and is the day's equivalent of S007's reconciliation. Steps 6 and 7 are the input-class and detectability pair the module carries throughout, and they are also the artefact elements enforcing §3.2's scope boundary.

**Phase 6 (40 min).** Silent wrongness in production, then an agent failure. The instructor case's attention to *how long it ran* and *what made the wrongness invisible* is the phase's content, and it is the hardest kind of error to disclose because there is no incident to point at. The agent case is chosen so that **the review that would have caught it is not the review a student knows to perform**: the configuration was well-formed, the results were plausible, the explanation was accurate, and the defect was the absence of an output nobody was looking for. It is the same failure shape as the instructor case, one layer up, and the pairing is deliberate.

**Phase 7 (30 min).** Violating-input construction, executed. Directly parallel to S006 phase 5 step 2 and deliberately unremarked, since the design wants the move to generalise without being labelled.

**Phase 8 (20 min).** Three rates from the pretest — identified the algorithm as wrong, found the break, stated the required property — which decompose the day's competence into three levels and show the cohort where it sits on each. Then item 5's sealed predictions, scored on both halves.

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**Students with more relevant prior knowledge.** A student with competition-programming or strong algorithms background will identify the paradigms correctly and quickly. Predicted failure: they select correctly by pattern recognition and skip step 3, because their pattern recognition is reliable and the check feels redundant. Countermeasure: the specification fails a paradigm choice with no check regardless of correctness, which is stated in advance and is the only clause that reaches this student. Phase 7's partner also targets the stated hypothesis rather than the choice, so a correct choice with a vague hypothesis is attackable. **The platform anchor supplies a second countermeasure the paper version lacked**: this student's pattern recognition was trained on problems, not on a graph traversal over embeddings, and phase 3's B-versus-D result is as new to them as to anyone.

**Students with less relevant prior knowledge.** The exchange argument is the module's first new proof technique and arrives on the module's first day, now alongside a new service. Countermeasures: point 4 works the argument completely; step 3 supplies a template; step 3 offers three alternative check forms, so a student who cannot construct an exchange argument may instead exhibit overlap or show independence; and the index is built once for the module rather than per day. Residual risk: they may choose a method they can check rather than the one the query class supports, which is a real distortion and is partially caught at step 6.

**Phase 3 is too easy.** Configuration A's behaviour is predicted correctly by most groups and round two's result is legible immediately. The B-versus-D discrimination is the difficulty and it is robust, because the two configurations are identical in every respect the students can see. Fallback if a group explains it: ask them to predict, before running it, which *kind* of query will show the largest divergence, which requires reasoning about where the graph's structure is thin and is genuinely hard.

**Affective failure.** The characteristic risk is **the check experienced as bureaucracy**. A student who has selected correctly, implemented correctly and measured correctly is then required to prove something they already believe, and the requirement can read as ceremony. This was the day's most likely disengagement and the paper version's countermeasure — phase 1's broken greedy algorithm — was argumentative. **The platform anchor replaces it with a structural one.** The check is now a query parameter on a service the student is already using, and the question is not whether the check is worth the ceremony but whether they ran it. Whether that survives to phase 5 remains the day's open question, and it is now a cheaper question.

**Agent-confidence adoption.** New with the platform anchor and predicted to be common. A student who runs the agent before forming a view will adopt its configuration and its confidence together. Countermeasure: item 5 is sealed before the agent is run, and phase 3 requires groups to commit their paradigm assignments before any query is issued. Both are pretest structures and both work on the same mechanism, which is that a written prior is harder to abandon than an unformed one.

## 7. Assessment and evidence generated

No entrustment judgement.

One specification item in CF103 requiring the full chain from structural properties to detection signal, **plus the prompt record, the intervention record and the frontier statement**.

**A paradigm choice with no hypothesis check fails, whether or not the choice was correct.** This is the day's central assessment decision and it is the only way to distinguish reasoning from successful guessing, since both produce working implementations.

**An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that the design could have detected an intervention-worthy error. The escape clause is the sensitivity discipline [S052](/wiki/seminars/S052.md) established and [WP-048](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-048.md) §8 corrected, and it is load-bearing here: a student whose configuration ships a sampled comparison against the exhaustive adjudicator has built the detection the record would otherwise have to evidence, and penalising them for not having intervened would penalise the better artefact.

Programme instrumentation: the phase 1 three-rate decomposition is the cohort's baseline on identify / falsify / characterise, re-probed at S043 — **and if that rate is low, S043's phase 3 carries the remediation**, per instrumentation rule 4. Step 4's recorded-failed-check rate measures whether the honesty requirement is being met or whether students are only recording checks that succeeded; a rate below a fifth triggers the phase 7 cross-check described at §9 prediction 3 in the same offering. The phase 7 violating-input success rate is the hypothesis-specificity measure. **Item 5's sealed prediction accuracy is this quarter's first frontier-calibration datum and its reader is the following year's cohort, through the [Durable and Perishable Register](/wiki/program/09-Durable-and-Perishable-Register.md)**, per instrumentation rule 9.

**The frontier statements are a sample and the day says so**, per instrumentation rule 3: one cohort, one index, one corpus, one embedding model, on a task staff chose because a known failure was available. A frontier map assembled from them describes this day's task and not the agent.

Dispositions: **integrity**, at step 4, where the incentive to omit a failed check is direct and the omission is undetectable.

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: the hypothesis framing is an expert's reconstruction and does not describe how paradigm selection is done.**

At its strongest: no competent algorithm designer works by enumerating structural properties and testing them against a hypothesis catalogue. They recognise the problem, often instantly, on the basis of extensive exposure — and the structural account is what they produce when asked to justify a judgement that was made another way. Teaching the justification as the method gives students a slow, laborious procedure that experts do not use, and may interfere with the pattern recognition that actually develops competence.

The rebuttal is the same one WP-008 made about the inductive account of recursion, and it has the same shape: the premise about experts is probably true and the inference does not follow. Expert recognition is fast and it is also *checked* — the difference between an expert and a student is not that the expert skips verification but that the expert's verification is quick because they know what to look for. The day teaches what to look for. Point 4's exchange argument is not a substitute for recognition; it is what an expert does in thirty seconds and a student does in ten minutes.

The concession is that the day does not say this. It presents the check as the method, when the accurate framing is that recognition proposes and the check disposes — and the omission risks producing students who believe fast selection is illegitimate. That is a live risk given phase 5's step 4, which rewards recording a failed check and may be read as rewarding indecision. One sentence at point 1 would fix it and is not present. This is now a familiar category in this design's whitepapers: a correct position stated without its scope.

**Objection 2: the silent-failure emphasis rests on an undefended claim about detection.**

At its strongest: the day asserts repeatedly that a wrong paradigm produces answers that are plausible and undetected, and this assertion carries most of the day's motivational weight. But it is an empirical claim about real systems and the design offers one instructor anecdote as its evidence. In practice many structural violations *are* detected — by users noticing poor results, by comparison against alternative implementations, by anomalies in downstream metrics. The day may be overstating an unusual failure mode into a general one, and if it is, the check requirement is being justified by a rarity.

This objection the design does not fully answer, and the platform anchor answers part of it in a way worth stating precisely. That structural violations produce *no crash* is true by construction and needs no evidence. That an approximate vector search returns a full result set regardless of whether anything relevant exists is now **documented rather than asserted**, which converts one link in the chain from anecdote to citation. What remains unevidenced is the organisational claim — that nobody notices — and that depends entirely on whether anyone is measuring result quality, which is a question the programme takes up at S066 and S068 and which S021 still pre-empts without warrant.

The honest position is unchanged: the day should claim only the first two and should defer the third, and step 7's detection-signal requirement is better justified by the first two anyway — a violation with no output signature needs a signal *because* nothing else will surface it, regardless of how often that circumstance arises. Restating point 3 and the phase 6 framing in those terms is a small change and is still not made.

**Objection 3: the platform instance covers three of four paradigms and is staged as though it covered the taxonomy.**

This is the objection that bites on the practical turn and it is the one the design finds hardest.

At its strongest: the day's argument is that *each paradigm* is a hypothesis, and its evidence is four executed configurations. But those four instantiate greedy selection, exhaustive search and an independence claim, and one of them — term-overlap scoring — is not in the taxonomy at all. **Dynamic programming, which is the paradigm students hold most confidently and check least, has no instance on the day's service and is never executed.** So the day's most persuasive evidence is silent on its most misapplied hypothesis, and the persuasion runs in the dangerous direction: a student who has watched three hypotheses become checkable on a real system will believe the fourth is too, without having seen it, and will carry a confidence the day did not earn. The design has, in effect, generalised from a convenience sample of the taxonomy — which is precisely the sampling failure S028 exists to teach students to detect, committed by the day that teaches hypothesis-checking.

The objection is conceded. The rebuttal available — that the day never claims the service instantiates the taxonomy — was true of this paper and was not true of the day as first drafted, where phase 3's cases were mapped to paradigms and nothing marked what the mapping omitted.

**The cheap fix is one point and it is made.** Point 5 now states which hypothesis each executed case asserts, states that term-overlap scoring is not one of the four, and states that **dynamic programming has no instance on the day's service and that the taxonomy is not the world.** That is a scope statement about a demonstration rather than about a list, which is a register the [Whitepaper Standard](/wiki/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md)'s scope rule does not currently name, and this paper records it as a fourth register the rule should cover: **wherever a day's evidence is a set of executed instances, the day must state which of its claims the instances do not reach.**

What remains unfixed is smaller and is recorded rather than repaired. The day has no dynamic programming instance and could construct one — the reranking stage is a constrained selection over a candidate set, and there is a version of this day in which that is worked. It would cost phase 4 a point and phase 3 a case, the design is not confident phase 3 can carry a fifth configuration at this load, and the honest statement is that the omission is a capacity decision presented at point 5 as a fact about the service.

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** The three phase 1 rates will be strictly ordered — identifying the algorithm as suspect will exceed finding the break, which will exceed stating the required property — with a large gap at the second step. *Instrument:* per-item rates.

**Prediction 2.** Students with strong algorithms backgrounds will choose paradigms correctly at a much higher rate and will produce hypothesis checks rated no better than other students. *Instrument:* choice correctness and blind-rated check quality, split by the admission background survey. **This is the §8 objection-1 test: if check quality also tracks background, the check is a skill exposure develops and the day's necessity is weaker.**

**Prediction 3.** Fewer than a quarter of artefacts will record a failed check at step 4. *Instrument:* step 4 completion rates. **A very low rate is consistent with either accurate first choices or omitted failures, and phase 7's violating-input outcomes are the cross-check: students whose hypotheses were broken at phase 7 and who record no failed check are the diagnostic group.**

**Prediction 4.** At S043, students will ask what structural assumption a model family asserts, unprompted, at a higher rate than a comparison cohort. *Instrument:* an S043 protocol coded for unprompted structural questioning. Untestable until a variant delivery exists.

**Prediction 5.** A large majority of item 5 sealed predictions will state that the agent will choose a fused configuration and assert that it is relevant, and a small minority will name the absence of an empty-result condition, or any other specific defect, as the thing it gets wrong. *Instrument:* item 5 responses coded for generic-versus-specific failure identification, against what each student's agent actually produced. **The gap between the two rates is the day's frontier-calibration measure. If the specific rate is high, the cohort's frontier model is better than the design assumes and the phase 6 agent case is redundant; if the generic rate is also low, students are not predicting agent failure at all and the sealed item is measuring nothing.** The result is read by the following year's cohort, per instrumentation rule 9, and a low generic rate is carried to S022's item 5 as a briefing note rather than being re-discovered.

**Prediction 6.** In phase 3 round two, more than half of groups will state, unprompted and before phase 4, that a method returning results for a query with no answer cannot signal its own failure. *Instrument:* group artefacts from round two, coded for unprompted articulation. **This is the platform anchor's own test. If the rate is low, round two is producing an observation without an inference and point 3 must do work the design currently assigns to phase 3.**

## What a student should take from this paper

**What the design is claiming.** That choosing an algorithmic paradigm is asserting a structural hypothesis about the problem, that the hypothesis is individually checkable, and that a wrong choice does not crash — it returns plausible answers nobody measures.

**What the evidence licenses.** The search index's behaviour sits in **class 4**, read on a stated date, and one item does real work: the documentation states that an approximate vector query returns the requested number of results even when the nearest neighbours are not similar, so nonsensical queries return results. That converts one link of the silent-failure argument from anecdote into citation. The retrieval and cost material sits in **class 1**. **The paradigms-as-hypotheses framing, and the claim that an executable check dissolves the objection that checking is ceremony, are class 3** — design reasoning with no external warrant. You may say the approximation can miss a document that exists in the corpus. You may not say how often, because no recall figure is published at any setting.

**What to do with it.**

- **State the structural property before you implement.** A choice with no stated hypothesis is a guess that happened to compile.
- **Run the adjudicator.** On this service the exhaustive comparison is a query parameter, so the excuse that checking is expensive is not available to you here.
- **Record the check that failed.** The incentive to omit it is direct and the omission is undetectable, which is precisely why it is the assessed element.
- **Separate the classes of conclusion.** Under the [Agent Configuration Standard](/wiki/program/13-Agent-Configuration-Standard.md), a retrieval setting you configured is a configured control, the exhaustive comparison is an executable check, and results that merely looked plausible are neither.

**The concession worth remembering.** The paper concedes that the day's most persuasive evidence — four executed configurations — reaches three of the four paradigms and never touches dynamic programming, which is the one students hold most confidently and check least. **Hold the design to that.** A student who has watched three hypotheses become checkable on a real system will believe the fourth is too without having seen it, and the paper records that the omission is a capacity decision presented at point 5 as a fact about the service.

The algorithmic and cost material follows [research/02-technical-foundations/malkov-yashunin-hnsw.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/malkov-yashunin-hnsw.md). **Grounded in vendor documentation, with its version and its silence recorded.** Phase 3's four configurations and points 5 to 10 are taken from Microsoft Learn's Azure AI Search relevance and vector index pages, read 2026-07-26. What the documentation states: exhaustive nearest-neighbour search performs a brute-force scan and finds the exact nearest neighbours, is computationally intensive, and does not consume vector index size quota; the approximate algorithm builds a hierarchical graph at indexing time, requires its data points to be resident, and is navigated by moving to closer candidates level by level with a bounded candidate queue; a field configured for the approximate algorithm accepts an exhaustive query at query time and a field configured for exhaustive search **does not** accept an approximate query, because the structures do not exist; the graph's bi-directional link count defaults to 4 with a range of 4 to 10, the indexing-time neighbour count defaults to 400 with a range of 100 to 1,000, and the search-time neighbour count defaults to 500 with the same range; a vector field must be searchable and must not be filterable, facetable or sortable; the vector score is a monotone transformation of the distance and not the distance itself; when the number of results is unspecified the default is fifty; and **the search always returns the requested number of results even when the nearest neighbours are not similar, so nonsensical or off-topic queries return results.** The exhaustive configuration is named in the same documentation as the way to build a ground-truth set for evaluating an approximation's recall, which is the warrant for step 3 and for S025's step 2.

**Two silences are recorded and one is taught.** The documentation states no recall figure for the approximate algorithm at any parameter setting, on any corpus, at any index size — **so nothing in it licenses a claim about how often the greedy hypothesis fails, and none is made here or on the day**; that is the silence point 10 names to students and it is the reason §3.2's unsafe condition is drawn where it is. The documentation also recommends the hybrid-and-reranking combination on the ground that it "consistently produced the most relevant results" in benchmark testing, **and names no benchmark, no metric, no baseline and no interval**, which makes the claim uncitable under this programme's rules; the day does not repeat it, and S025 uses it as an example of guarantee language that guarantees nothing. Consistent with the folder-wide asymmetry recorded in [research/06-microsoft-platform/](/research/06-microsoft-platform/collected-materials.md), the pages state latencies, sizes, ranges and defaults throughout and state no accuracy anywhere. All parameter names, defaults, ranges and score ranges are marked perishable at point 10 and are expected to be superseded.
