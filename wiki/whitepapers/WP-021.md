# WP-021 — A Requirement Is a Business Statement

## In plain terms

A discovery tells you about a situation. A requirement commits somebody to something. The step between them is where most of the discovery is lost, and the loss is silent because a badly written requirement looks exactly like a well written one.

Two things go wrong at this step, and they are different failures with different causes.

**The first is that the requirement restates the solution.** The customer arrived with an answer, the discovery examined the problem behind it, and the requirement document quietly returns to the answer, now phrased as an obligation. This is not laziness; it is gravity. The answer is the thing everybody in the room can already picture.

**The second is that the requirement is written in the wrong language.** Not the wrong jargon — the wrong *ownership* of language. A requirement written in the engineer's vocabulary can only be checked by the engineer. A requirement written in the customer's unaltered vocabulary inherits every ambiguity that vocabulary carries, and specialist jargons carry a great many. **The working answer is neither: the customer's language, sharpened, with the customer holding a veto over the sharpening.**

That pairing is taken from the domain-modelling literature, where it is argued from the cost of translation rather than from tidiness. **A team where one or two people are bilingual has a bottleneck, and the bottleneck's translations are inexact.** That is the mechanism, it is legible, and it is not measured anywhere.

## Abstract

This paper defends opening the framing stage with language rather than with structure. The argument is that a requirement is a commitment expressed in somebody's vocabulary, that the choice of whose vocabulary determines who can check the commitment, and that the working answer is the customer's terms sharpened under the customer's veto. It defends the requirement that at least one false cognate be found and recorded, on the grounds that the words which cause damage are ordinary rather than technical and are therefore never checked. It adopts the corpus's rule that engineering artefacts belong as text in the repository, and records that the corpus asserts it without evidence. The paper concedes that its principal source declares two prerequisites — iterative development and continuing access to domain experts — which many engagements do not satisfy, and that the session's discipline is therefore frequently applied outside the conditions its source claims for it.

## 1. The problem this session must solve

A practitioner who has just finished a good discovery is asked for requirements, and the request arrives with a shape attached: a numbered list, due Friday, one line each.

**That shape is the adversary.** A numbered one-line list cannot hold a contradiction between informants, cannot hold an observation that was not reported, and cannot hold the sentence *we do not yet know*. Everything the previous stage worked to preserve is inexpressible in the format the next stage asks for, and so it is dropped — not decided against, dropped.

The session's response is to change what the artefact is before changing what it says. A requirement set that comes with a glossary, that names who would notice each requirement's absence, and that lives as reviewable text in the repository, has somewhere to put the things a numbered list has nowhere to put.

There is a second problem underneath. **A requirement is the point at which the engagement acquires a vocabulary, and the vocabulary is acquired by accident.** Whoever writes first decides. If that person writes in the engineer's terms, the customer becomes a reviewer of a document they cannot check; if they write in the customer's terms unaltered, the document inherits ambiguities that will surface as defects.

## 2. Entry state and prior knowledge

**Entry state: either.** A practitioner arriving from industry has written requirements before and can do this session; a learner arriving from [S020](/wiki/seminars/S020.md) brings a discovery packet and can do it better.

The prior it works against is universal and rarely examined: that a requirement is a thing you write down after you know what to build. **In this programme it is the instrument by which you find out whether you know**, and the session is designed so that the finding-out is uncomfortable.

Learners also arrive with an unexamined belief that vocabulary problems are technical. They are not. The terms that cause damage — *order*, *complete*, *approved*, *customer*, *site*, *shift* — are ones everybody is confident about, which is precisely why nobody checks them.

## 3. Theoretical basis

**3.1 Load profile.** Intrinsic load is low. The session's difficulty is social rather than conceptual: asking somebody what they meant by a word they have used for twenty years is awkward, and the awkwardness is the work. The surplus is spent on the repeated-why dialogue, which cannot be hurried, and on the rubric, which is the session's only piece of genuinely technical writing. This is a design judgement and carries no external warrant.

**3.2 The opening items are a generation phase, and the design is warranted rather than stylistic.**

Productive Failure is a two-phase, order-dependent design: learners generate solutions to a task targeting a concept they have not been taught, fail, and are then taught ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). The mechanism this session leans on is the **failed generation effect** — attempting an answer before instruction improves later learning *even when the attempt is wrong*. Item 4 creates exactly that situation, and the design wants most learners not to be able to answer it.

Two of the stated design conditions bind here and they are conditions rather than preferences. **The generation phase must not be scaffolded**, which is why the release rule gates §§4, 5 and 9 — a version of this session that helped learners towards a good false cognate during the opening would be a worse Productive Failure rather than a gentler one. And **the consolidation phase must explicitly prompt comparison**, because presenting a correct answer beside a learner's wrong one achieves nothing on its own; only the condition instructed to compare them learned more. Exercise 4 exists to satisfy that condition and is a comparison prompt rather than a recall prompt.

**Its limits.** The evidence is school, university and laboratory work; no adult-professional study appears in the source's reference list, and the author's claim that the cycle works in corporate training is uncited. **Direction and mechanism only; no magnitude is asserted here or anywhere in this wiki.**

**3.3 Why translation is treated as the cost rather than ambiguity.** The domain-modelling source frames the problem as translation rather than as vocabulary hygiene: where two languages exist, a small number of people become bilingual, they become bottlenecks of information flow, and their translations are inexact ([research/05-fde-craft/evans-domain-driven-design.md](/research/05-fde-craft/evans-domain-driven-design.md)). **The failure is structural rather than lexical**, which is why the remedy is a shared language rather than a bigger dictionary — and why it bites hardest in forward-deployed work, where the engineer is the bilingual bottleneck by default rather than by accident.

**3.4 Why the customer keeps a veto.** The same source is explicit that the language derives from the experts' own jargon but is cleaned up — sharper and narrower — and equally explicit that the experts should object if the cleaning diverges from what the term means in their field. **Both halves are needed and dropping either produces a different, worse practice.** Dropping the sharpening gives you their ambiguity; dropping the veto gives you a model they will not use, which the same source names as the thing you must never adopt however elegant it seems.

The source's own worked failure is the reason this is stated as a rule rather than as advice. An object at the centre of a syndicated-loan design carried a name that was not a term in the business, the domain experts said more than once that they did not understand it, and they were overruled — not by argument but by deference to technical authority. **Deference is the failure signal, and it is granted to a forward-deployed engineer as a condition of the role.** A discipline that relies on the customer volunteering an objection has to be told, out loud, that objecting is expected.

**3.5 Why the repeated-why dialogue is taught as a dialogue.** The corpus teaches it as a worked conversation rather than as a principle, and that is the right way to teach it: the move is a sequence, its value comes from the second and third repetition rather than the first, and a principle stated once does not convey that. **The corpus's own worked example turns a request about lighting into a requirement about what happens when an alert fires**, which is the shape the move produces and is not obvious from a description of it.

**3.6 Why a rubric is required in the same session as the requirement, and not in the next one.**

This is the addition that gives the session its platform anchor, and it rests on two independent literatures agreeing.

The practitioner evaluation literature argues that most failed products built on foundation models share one root cause — no robust evaluation system — and that the consequence is arrested iteration rather than poor quality: a team can change behaviour and cannot tell whether the change helped ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/husain-your-ai-product-needs-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/husain-your-ai-product-needs-evals.md)). The decomposition that makes a check writable at all is features and scenarios, and each scenario becomes an assertion. **A requirement that resists that decomposition has not been written at a level anybody can act on.**

The book-length treatment names the same practice and states its precondition in a form that belongs to this session rather than to [S022](/wiki/seminars/S022.md): "Creating a clear evaluation guideline is the most important step of the evaluation pipeline. An ambiguous guideline leads to ambiguous scores that can be misleading" ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/huyen-ai-engineering.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/huyen-ai-engineering.md)). The guideline is written in words, the words are the ones agreed with the customer, and **a rubric that cannot be written in glossary vocabulary is evidence about the glossary rather than about the tooling.** That is the whole mechanism, and it converts a claim about language into a physical test.

The same source supplies the counterweight this paper is obliged to carry, in the author's own voice: defining evaluation criteria first is "similar to looking for the lost key under the lamppost (at night). It's easier to do, but it doesn't mean we'll find the key." The module's response is the unverifiable list — kept, carried forward, never deleted — introduced at [S022](/wiki/seminars/S022.md) and assessed at [S025](/wiki/seminars/S025.md). Without it, this session's discipline would silently shrink the requirement set to the checkable part of it.

**Their limits.** The essay rests on a single sustained case with no control condition, no measured before-and-after and no released dataset. The book is a practitioner text whose evaluation figures are largely reported second-hand, and whose strongest claim about evaluation is marked by its author as a belief. **No magnitude from either appears anywhere in this session or this paper.**

**3.7 What the platform contributes that prose cannot.**

The Agent Framework's evaluation abstraction is three types — an item wrapping a conversation and deriving a query and response from it by a split strategy, an evaluator that scores items, and results aggregating pass and fail with per-item detail ([research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-framework-and-evaluations-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-framework-and-evaluations-docs.md)). Two properties of that surface are doing pedagogical work here and neither is about the tool.

First, a judged evaluator's rubric is a sentence about the customer's business that a stranger applies. It is the first artefact in the programme where the learner's vocabulary is executed rather than read, and **an instrument has no social reason to agree with you**, which is the property the session is buying.

Second, the documentation states plainly that "how you split determines *what you're evaluating*." The requirement's demand that it name who would notice its absence, and the evaluation's demand that it name a unit of analysis, are the same question in two registers. A learner who has answered one and not the other has answered neither.

**Its limits, and they bound §7.** First-party product documentation, read through search-returned excerpts rather than full page fetches; many evaluators are preview; one consulted page is a superseded classic variant; surfaces differ by language. **No agreement rate with human judgement is published for any evaluator, no calibration data, and no threshold rationale.** This paper asserts none.

## 4. Session design and its warrant

Eight movements: situation, artefact, material, workflow, standard, failure modes, exercises, limits.

**The opening items are the instrument and item 4 is the one the design cares about.** Naming a word you and the customer both use and mean differently is a request most learners cannot satisfy, and the inability is the session's opening content. It is placed fourth rather than first because items 1 to 3 establish enough context for the question to be answerable in principle. Items 1, 3 and 5 are not filler: they produce confident answers, and the contrast between them and item 4's blankness is what makes the blankness legible to the person who wrote it.

**Three artefacts are required rather than two, and the rubric is the newest and the most load-bearing.** A requirement set without a glossary has resolved its ambiguities silently, in the author's head, which is where they will stay. A requirement set with a glossary and no rubric has agreed a vocabulary and never tested whether the vocabulary can carry a decision. The requirement that the glossary contain at least one genuine disagreement is a forcing function: a glossary of undisputed terms can be produced without talking to anybody.

**The review movement uses speech deliberately.** The source's argument is that speech is the cheapest modelling instrument available and that rough edges are easier to hear than to see. The session's version is narrower and more checkable: **a requirement you cannot say without inserting an explanatory clause has a missing definition, and the clause you inserted is that definition.** That converts a general claim about modelling out loud into a mechanical test, and exercise 3 runs it.

**The workflow ends with the learner attacking their own sentence, and the instruction matters more than the tool.** The agent is asked to satisfy the requirement in a way the author would reject. That is not review, and it is not improvement. It is the cheapest available falsification of a document nobody has an incentive to falsify — and it works because the instrument is literal: it will not infer the unstated constraint, will not fill the gap out of professional courtesy, and will not tell the author the requirement was clear when it was not. **A learner whose agent could not satisfy the requirement badly supplied their intentions alongside their words, which is not the test.**

**The repository requirement is adopted from the corpus and its warrant is thin.** The corpus states that engineering artefacts should move out of documents and slides into text in the repository because that is what makes them actionable, and evidences it nowhere. The mechanism is legible — text can be diffed, reviewed and referenced from a decision record, and a slide cannot — and the mechanism is all there is.

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**The restated solution.** The likeliest outcome. *Countermeasure:* the repeated-why dialogue, applied to the learner's own sentences rather than to the customer's, which is the only place in the session where the move can be practised without a customer present.

**The undisputed glossary.** A learner produces a glossary of terms nobody ever disagreed about and satisfies the letter of the requirement. *Countermeasure:* partial. The specification asks for a resolved disagreement with a date and a name attached, which raises the cost of fabrication without preventing it. **[S025](/wiki/seminars/S025.md) checks the same item and cannot distinguish either.**

**The imposed vocabulary.** The learner invents a term because it is tidier, the customer defers to their technical authority, and the model silently stops matching the business. *Countermeasure:* the veto requirement, plus the workflow's step 3, which surfaces invented terms by requiring every non-ordinary word in the rubric to be matched against the glossary. This catches the invention and not the deference.

**The rubric written for the machine.** A learner who cannot express the criterion in the customer's vocabulary quietly adopts the evaluator's, and the requirement acquires a second, private language nobody negotiated. *Countermeasure:* the `invented_for_the_machine` field in the workflow's configuration, which makes the substitution a recorded line rather than an unnoticed drift. **This is a weak countermeasure against a strong temptation**, and prediction 5 in §9 predicts it failing.

**The comfortable review.** The learner reads their requirements to a colleague who agrees with them. *Countermeasure:* the adversarial workflow step, which supplies a reviewer with no social stake, and [S022](/wiki/seminars/S022.md), which makes the human reviewer's job adversarial too. That is why the two sessions are adjacent.

Retrieved by [S022](/wiki/seminars/S022.md), [S025](/wiki/seminars/S025.md), [S027](/wiki/seminars/S027.md) and [S030](/wiki/seminars/S030.md).

The requirement set returns at [S030](/wiki/seminars/S030.md) as the design's inputs.

## 7. Assessment and evidence generated

**No entrustment evidence.** Two specification items at [S025](/wiki/seminars/S025.md): that no requirement names a technology unless the technology is an owned constraint, and that the glossary records at least one word the two sides meant differently, with the adopted meaning and the person who agreed.

**Neither item marks whether the requirements are good.** They mark whether the requirement is about the business and whether the vocabulary was negotiated. That is a deliberately narrow claim about what the session can establish, and it follows the entrustment literature's distinction between an activity that can be handed over and an outcome that cannot be held constant ([research/08-assessment-epas/ten-cate-epa-framework.md](/research/08-assessment-epas/ten-cate-epa-framework.md)). *Writing a requirement in the customer's negotiated vocabulary* is a bounded activity with a beginning and an end. *Writing a good requirement* is a judgement about facts the assessment cannot hold constant, and marking it would assess the learner's agreement with the assessor.

**The rubric is deliberately not assessed anywhere.** It is the session's most technical artefact and the one a learner is most likely to optimise. Marking it would produce rubrics written to be markable, which is precisely the substitution §5 predicts and §9 tests for. Its function is diagnostic: it exists to make a hole in the glossary visible on the day, to the person who made it.

**Nothing else is assessed.**

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: the language discipline is being lifted out of the conditions its source declares for it.**

At its strongest: the source states two prerequisites and states them flatly — development is iterative, and developers have a close continuing relationship with domain experts. It says the book makes no claim without them. **Forward-deployed engagements frequently satisfy neither.** Access to the people who actually understand the domain is often mediated, rationed, or supplied through a project manager who is not one; and delivery is often a fixed scope against a fixed date with one demonstration at the end. Teaching a language discipline whose author disclaims it outside those conditions, to a cohort who will mostly work outside them, is teaching a method that will not hold.

**This is conceded, and it is the strongest objection to the session.** The rebuttal is narrow: the discipline degrades rather than fails, and it degrades informatively. A team that cannot get access to a domain expert to negotiate a term has learned something specific about the engagement — and the glossary entry that records *nobody available to arbitrate this* is a more useful artefact than a definition invented in its place. **The declined fix is to gate the session on the prerequisites and skip it where they are absent.** It is declined because absence of access is the common case, and a programme that teaches nothing about language in the common case has surrendered the field to whoever writes first.

**Objection 2: requiring a false cognate manufactures one.**

At its strongest: a specification item that requires a disagreement will produce a disagreement, and the disagreements produced to satisfy an assessment will be small, safe and discovered by asking a colleague rather than a customer. **The item measures compliance and calls it discovery.**

**Partly conceded.** The item cannot distinguish a found disagreement from a manufactured one and nothing in this design can. The rebuttal is that the base rate is favourable: in any engagement of real size the ordinary words genuinely are overloaded, so a learner who looks will find one, and a learner who fabricates has still had to think about which word would plausibly be contested. **The declined fix is to require the disagreement be attested by the customer.** It is declined because it makes the assessment depend on a customer's availability, which the programme cannot guarantee, and an assessment that fails on unavailability teaches learners to avoid the item.

**Objection 3: attaching an evaluation surface to a session about language teaches learners that a requirement is only real if a machine can score it.**

At its strongest, and this is the objection the rubric requirement most deserves: the session's own source concedes the lamppost problem in the author's own words, and then the session hands learners a tool that shines a light on exactly one patch of ground. A learner who cannot write a rubric for a requirement will, under time pressure, rewrite the requirement rather than record the difficulty. The result is a requirement set that is entirely checkable and has quietly lost the things that mattered — and the loss will look like rigour, because every surviving line has a criterion attached.

**Partially conceded, and the concession has a cost this paper is obliged to name.** Three defences, none complete. The rubric is not assessed, here or anywhere, so there is no mark to optimise. The module carries an unverifiable list that is kept rather than deleted, and [S025](/wiki/seminars/S025.md) checks that it is non-empty or that its emptiness is defended. And the session states the lamppost concession in its own material rather than leaving it to this paper. **The declined fix was to defer all evaluation material to [S022](/wiki/seminars/S022.md) and keep this session purely linguistic**, which was the shape of the previous design. It is declined because the rubric is the only instrument in the module that makes a vocabulary hole *visible on the day the vocabulary is agreed*, and moving it one session later moves it past the point where the glossary is being written. **The cost of that decision is that a learner meets the checkability pressure before they meet the unverifiable list**, and prediction 5 is the only thing that would detect the resulting distortion.

**Objection 4: the adversarial workflow step depends on an instrument whose behaviour is neither stable nor characterised.**

At its strongest: the session's cheapest and most memorable move — ask the agent to satisfy your requirement in a way you would reject — rests on a capability that changes without notice, differs between hosts and models, and has no published characterisation of any kind. A cohort running it in one term and a cohort running it in the next are not doing the same exercise, and neither cohort can say what the exercise measures.

**Conceded, and not repaired.** The mitigation is that the step's *finding* is recorded in the learner's own words — which word was exploited, and whether it was in the glossary — so that the durable artefact is a sentence about the requirement rather than a transcript of the instrument. **The declined fix was to replace the agent with a scripted adversarial reviewer using a fixed script**, declined because a fixed script cannot exploit the specific word this learner left undefined, which is the only thing the step is for.

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** Most learners will be unable to answer opening item 4 before instruction, and most of those who can will name a technical term rather than an ordinary one. *Instrument:* the written answers to item 4, coded *absent* / *technical term* / *ordinary term* by a second reader.

**Prediction 2.** A substantial number of first-draft requirements will be satisfiable by exactly one named product, and their authors will be able to name it when asked. *Instrument:* the first draft against a single question put to the author, recorded before revision.

**Prediction 3.** Requirements revised after the repeated-why dialogue will be stated at a different level of the problem than their first drafts, and the difference will be visible to a reader who has not seen the dialogue. *Instrument:* paired first and revised drafts, presented unlabelled to a second reader who is asked which of each pair is more general. **If the reader cannot tell, the dialogue movement is decorative and should be rewritten as a checklist.**

**Prediction 4.** Glossaries whose disagreement is dated and attributed to a named role will be rarer than glossaries whose disagreement is undated. *Instrument:* the glossaries, counted by presence of date and role.

**Prediction 5.** A majority of learners attempting the rubric will introduce at least one term that is in neither the glossary nor ordinary English, and a majority of those will leave the `invented_for_the_machine` field empty. *Instrument:* the submitted rubric, with its non-ordinary terms extracted by a second reader and matched against the glossary, compared with the learner's own declaration. **This predicts the failure of §5's countermeasure**, and it is included because a countermeasure whose failure rate is unmeasured is an assumption.

**Prediction 6.** Learners instructed to compare their item-4 answer against the taught criterion (exercise 4) will produce glossary entries judged complete on all five properties more often than learners who read the same material without the comparison prompt. *Instrument:* a second reader's five-point completeness check, split within cohort on exercise-4 completion. **The same prediction is made at [WP-022](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-022.md), [WP-023](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-023.md) and [WP-024](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-024.md), so a null across all four would indict the comparison pattern rather than this session.**

**Prediction 7.** Learners who ran the adversarial step will more often name a specific word as the exploited ambiguity, where learners who ran only the spoken review will more often name a whole sentence. *Instrument:* the closing two sentences from workflow step 7, coded *word* / *sentence* / *neither*. **If the two groups are indistinguishable, the platform anchor is buying nothing that speech was not already buying**, and the workflow should be cut back to exercise 3.

## What a learner should take from this paper

**What the design claims.** That a requirement is a commitment in somebody's language; that whose language it is determines who can check it; that the words which cause damage are the ordinary ones; and that a criterion you cannot write is evidence about the vocabulary rather than about the tool.

**What the evidence licenses.** Nothing about outcomes. The language discipline's source makes **no quantitative claim of any kind** and declares prerequisites many engagements do not meet. The repository rule is the corpus's assertion with no evidence behind it. The evaluation literature establishes a pattern from a single case and a practitioner book that reports its figures second-hand. The platform documentation accurately describes what the tooling computes and **publishes no evidence that what it computes is right**, because no agreement with human judgement is published for any of it.

**The concession worth remembering.** This session teaches a discipline outside the conditions its author claims for it, deliberately, because the alternative is teaching nothing. And it introduces a checkability pressure one session before it introduces the list that protects against it. Both are choices, both are recorded here rather than smoothed over, and §8's third objection is the one to hold on to.

**The silences that matter.** **No agreement rate with human judgement is published for any evaluator in this catalogue, no calibration data, and no account of where any default threshold came from.** That is load-bearing for this session, because the rubric a learner writes will be applied by a model-based judge whose correspondence with a domain expert is unmeasured by anybody — which is why the workflow directs learners to read the `reason` strings rather than the scores, and why nothing in the session is assessed on an evaluator's output. The documentation also says nothing at all about how a rubric should be worded, whose vocabulary it should use, or what happens when a rubric term is ambiguous to the judge; **the entire language argument of this session sits in a gap the documentation does not acknowledge exists.** Many evaluators are preview and may change or disappear; one consulted page is an explicitly superseded classic variant; API surfaces differ across programming languages, so examples are not interchangeable; and cloud evaluation requires a project with a model deployment and incurs cost, which is why every step of the workflow except one runs locally.
