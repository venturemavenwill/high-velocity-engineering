# WP-028 — Sampling, Intervals and What a Number Licenses

**Seminar day:** [S028](wiki/seminars/S028.md) · **Module:** [M06](wiki/modules/M06.md) · **Quarter:** [Q3](wiki/quarters/Q3.md)
**Strands:** ME · MS · **Complexity class:** 2
**Platform anchor:** Microsoft Foundry cloud evaluation — the aggregate pass rate and its sampling · **MCP servers:** Microsoft Learn MCP Server · Azure MCP Server (read)

## Abstract

This paper defends teaching statistical inference as a question about which population a number describes rather than as a question about precision, and defends assessing the day on a written sentence stating what a number does not license. The first argument is that sample size is the only limitation students reach for spontaneously, that it addresses one of at least four ways a number can fail to support a claim, and that the intuition "more data, stronger claim" is actively harmful when the additional data comes from the same unrepresentative source. The second argument is that a reporting sentence travels without its analysis, so the only meaningful test of one is whether a reader with the sentence alone draws the intended conclusions — which phase 7 operationalises by supplying the sentences and withholding the work, and which phase 5 now makes mechanical by requiring a harness that cannot emit the number without them. The paper additionally defends running the day's four sampling schemes against a real evaluation service, on the ground that the platform's own default output shape is the one whose interval is wrong. It records that this day implements the magnitude requirement that [WP-026](wiki/whitepapers/WP-026.md) §8 identified as the module's most serious omission. It concedes that the day teaches an inferential apparatus it refuses to derive, that its central sentence-writing exercise has no defensible standard of correctness, and that the practical turn has left the day's most important point as its only unstaged one.

## 1. The instructional problem this day must solve

A number exists and someone is about to act on it. Everything this day teaches is a component of one question: what is that person entitled to conclude?

Students have one answer available — that a bigger sample supports a stronger claim — and it is the wrong axis. Sample size governs the width of the interval around an estimate of the population you sampled. It says nothing about *which* population that is, and the gap between the population sampled and the population the decision concerns is where nearly every consequential error lives. A week's observations from current users cannot be extended to next quarter's users by taking more of them, and the belief that they can is what makes the error invisible: the analyst who adds data feels they are strengthening the claim.

Phase 1 is built to expose this in three steps. Item 2 rewards the size intuition, which is correct as far as it goes. Item 3 changes the target population and leaves the sample untouched, and the intuition has nothing to offer.

The day's second problem is clustering, which is the most common way a correctly computed interval is wrong. Observations that arrive in sessions, batches or bursts are not independent, the effective sample is smaller than the count, and the interval computed from the count is too narrow. Nothing in the reported output reveals this — the arithmetic is correct — which is why phase 6's disclosure emphasises that the analysis was reviewed and passed.

The third problem is one the module created. [WP-026](wiki/whitepapers/WP-026.md) §8 concluded that S026 taught students to identify a construct-instrument divergence and supplied no way to judge whether it mattered, calling this the day's most serious omission and specifying the repair: return to the S026 case and bound it. Point 8 and step 6 implement that repair. This is the second occasion in this design where a whitepaper's identified defect has been fixed in a subsequent day rather than recorded and left, and the design's position is that a repair inside the module is preferable to a note.

## 3. Theoretical basis for the design

### 3.1 Load profile and element interactivity

**The module's peak.** Three demands stack. The inferential arithmetic is unfamiliar. The population reasoning is abstract and has no procedural check. And the day's conclusions are almost uniformly negative, which imposes a motivational load on top of the cognitive one.

Management, which is the module's most aggressive:

- **The interval is computed for one case with the room** and thereafter by supplied tooling. Derivation is refused entirely.
- **Phase 3 supplies all five schemes with their numbers and intervals.** Groups reason about populations; they compute nothing.
- **Step 6's bounding is modelled once,** because it is a new demand introduced late in the module.
- **The measurement, the instrument and the error budget are all the student's own** and are one to two days old, so no context is new.
- **One quantity across phase 3.**

The design accepts that this is the hardest day in Q3 and that some students will complete step 7 poorly. Step 7 is the assessed output, and tokens are available.

### 3.2 Desirable difficulties selected, and those deliberately not

**Selected.**

- *Item 3's population change,* which the size intuition cannot address.
- *Scheme E's clustering,* invisible in its output.
- *Ranking claims by usefulness rather than by interval width,* phase 3's closing task.
- *Bounding the S026 divergence,* step 6.
- *Writing what the number does not license,* step 7.
- *Supplying only the sentences at phase 7,* withholding the analysis.

**Deliberately not selected.**

- *Deriving the interval.* Refused; MS103's material.
- *Hypothesis testing.* Entirely absent. The day is about estimation and licensing, and significance testing would supply a decision procedure that substitutes for the judgement being taught. It appears at S049 and not before.
- *Multiple comparisons and power.* S049's material.
- *Constructing sampling schemes.* Supplied.

**Scope, per the [Whitepaper Standard](wiki/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md).** Point 3's three exclusions from the interval are a selection. They omit model misspecification and the dependence of an interval's coverage on assumptions that may themselves fail. Both are real, both are ME101's and MS103's material, and both are named in the seminar page. The selection criterion is that systematic error, drift and selection are the three exclusions students actively violate by treating the interval as a total uncertainty statement.

**The platform instance, scoped in the strong form.** The day treats a Foundry evaluation's aggregate pass rate as its worked example, and students will apply the reading to every quality number they meet.

*Safe.* Reasoning about which population a reported rate estimates; identifying that rows drawn one per turn from conversation-structured traffic are not independent and that an interval computed from the row count is too narrow; distinguishing a sample constructed to cover unusual cases from one constructed to represent a distribution; and refusing to extend a rate computed on one period to another. All four follow from how the sample was assembled and hold for any evaluation service, any monitoring pipeline and any benchmark.

*Unsafe.* Two readings. **First, treating the platform's aggregate as wrong.** It is not wrong; it is an average over the rows it was given, which is exactly what it claims to be. The defect is in the reader who supplies a population it was never told about. A student who concludes that cloud evaluation reports bad numbers has misplaced the fault and will look for a better tool rather than a better sentence. **Second, treating the clustering correction as a general repair.** Adjusting for conversation-level clustering fixes one dependence structure. Turns also cluster by user, by tenant, by retrieved document and by release, and the day corrects for one of these because one is visible in the data the students hold. **A student who reports a clustering-adjusted interval as though it accounted for dependence has replaced a known error with an unknown one**, and step 4's requirement to name the row shape is where that must be said out loud.

The boundary is stated to students at point 10 rather than left to this paper, and step 4's requirement to state what the rows actually are is where a student applying the correction outside its range would fail.

### 3.3 Engagement and motivational mechanism

The day runs on **an intuition that works and then stops working**.

Item 2 rewards the size intuition and is meant to. Item 3 does not defeat it by showing it wrong; it changes the question so that the intuition is irrelevant, which is a different and more instructive experience than being contradicted. Students report the item 3 moment as a reframing rather than a correction.

Scheme E supplies the day's second engagement and it is the one with professional force. An interval that is correctly computed, reviewed, passed and wrong is genuinely unsettling, and it is the case that makes the day's apparatus feel necessary rather than pedantic.

Phase 7's construction — hand over only the sentences — has an effect the design did not initially anticipate and now relies on: students discover that their own sentences, stripped of the analysis they hold in their heads, say much less than they thought. That discovery is the closest thing the day has to a constructive payoff.

The competence risk is the module's highest here. Countermeasures are the fading, the supplied tooling, and the fact that step 7 is assessed on completeness of limitation rather than on elegance.

## 4. Phase-by-phase design and its warrant

**Phase 1 (25 min).** Three escalating conditions on one number, plus a calibration item and a sealed Copilot prediction. The design's decision to reward the size intuition at item 2 before making it irrelevant at item 3 is deliberate: an intuition defeated immediately is defended, and an intuition rewarded and then bypassed is revised. **Item 5** is administered here per instrumentation rule 8, and its expected answer — the agent reports the aggregate and answers the ship question without naming a population — is the day's whole subject performed by a machine in ten seconds.

**Phase 2 (20 min).** The bridge asks which S027 source an interval accounts for and which three it does not. Answering before the interval is taught means the apparatus arrives pre-bounded, which is the design's standard defence against a technique being over-applied.

**Phase 3 (45 min).** Four schemes, one quantity, **four evaluations actually run**, populations reasoned about rather than computed. Scheme D is the payload and its platform grounding is what the practical turn contributes: the row-per-turn shape that makes its interval wrong is not a construction, it is **what the evaluation level produces by default**, so the students are not being shown a cautionary case but the ordinary output of the ordinary configuration. Scheme C is the day's second surprise — narrowest interval, least useful claim — and reliably inverts the ranking groups commit to. The closing task's distinction between usefulness of the claim and width of the interval is the phase's abstraction, and its second half is the §8 objection-3 repair.

**Phase 4 (50 min).** Ten points. Point 2 introduces the interval operationally, which is a deliberate refusal of rigour in favour of correct use. Point 3 carries the scope statement. Point 5 states extrapolation's limit as a matter of what was sampled rather than of confidence, which is the formulation students need. Point 8 is the module's self-repair. **Point 9 names four defaults that chose the student's population for them** and is the [Copilot Practice Standard](wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md) requirement; the strongest of the four is that the platform's default evaluation level produces exactly scheme D's defective row shape, which means the wrong interval is the default interval. The split strategy is named there as a default and its mechanism is explicitly handed to ME201, whose anchor it is. Continuous evaluation and intelligent sampling are **demonstrated rather than operated** and are excluded from assessment. Point 10 separates durable claim from instance.

**Phase 5 (85 min).** Eight steps at class 2. Steps 1 and 2 — the population sampled and the population asked about — are the day's cleanest exercise and are frequently different in ways students had not noticed. Step 6 closes S026. Step 7 is the assessed output. **Step 8 is the build** and is the day's answer to its own phase 6: a limitation that travels by convention does not travel, so the harness is required to emit the number and its sentences together, and a number that can be copied out alone is a defect in the harness.

**Phase 6 (40 min).** A reviewed, passed, correctly computed, wrong interval, then an agent failure. The review detail is what makes clustering a structural problem rather than a competence problem. The agent case is chosen because Copilot did not *remove* the limitation — it summarised a table in which the limitation was a separate column and the headline was a single figure, and the limitation was never attached to the number in the first place. That is the argument for step 8 in one sentence.

**Phase 7 (30 min).** Sentences only, three questions, revision. Withholding the analysis is the phase's critical design decision and is the only honest test of a reporting sentence.

**Phase 8 (20 min).** The item 3 distribution, the sealed Copilot predictions opened, and the step 6 bounding rate compared against S026's phase 8 confidence, which is the module's internal check on whether the repair worked.

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**Students with more relevant prior knowledge.** A student with statistics background will compute correctly and quickly. Predicted failure: their step 7 sentence will be technically precise and will name only sampling limitations, because their training treats the interval as the uncertainty statement and the other three S027 sources are outside its frame. Countermeasure: the specification fails a does-not-license sentence naming only sample size, and their phase 7 partner is asked what decision the sentences support — which a sampling-only limitation statement will over-license.

**Students with less relevant prior knowledge.** The arithmetic is new and the population reasoning is abstract. Countermeasures: supplied tooling, one worked computation, a modelled bounding, and their own two-day-old measurement as the object. Residual risk: step 6's bounding may be impossible for them, and the day's fallback — say what would bound it — is explicitly permitted and passes, which is the correct professional answer in many real cases.

**Phase 3 is too easy.** Schemes A to D are usually reasoned about correctly. Scheme E is not. Fallback if a group identifies the clustering: ask them to estimate the effective sample size, which requires reasoning about the correlation structure and is genuinely hard without the apparatus.

**Affective failure.** The characteristic risk is **the day experienced as the removal of every claim**. Three days of the module have now each subtracted something: the instrument may not measure the construct, the error may not be reducible, and now the number may not describe the population asked about. A student who reaches step 7 believing nothing can be said will write a sentence that disclaims everything, which is as useless as claiming everything.

Countermeasures: step 5's licensing sentence is a positive claim and is written before step 7's negative one, deliberately; phase 3's closing task ranks claims by usefulness, which requires some to be useful; and S029, the following day, is entirely constructive. The design's position — stated identically in WP-026 and WP-027 — is that the module's fatalism risk is cumulative, peaks here, and resolves at S029. This is now the third consecutive whitepaper making that claim, and if S029 does not resolve it the module has a structural problem rather than three local ones.

## 7. Assessment and evidence generated

No entrustment judgement — the module's is at S030, two days later.

One specification item, jointly in ME101 and MS103, now carrying a shipped reporting layer and the three [Copilot Practice Standard](wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md) elements. **A does-not-license sentence naming only sample size fails.** This targets the day's specific reflex and is checkable.

**An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that the design could have detected an intervention-worthy error — step 8's coupling of number to sentences is such a demonstration and is accepted.

Programme instrumentation: the phase 1 item 3 distribution is the cohort's population-reasoning baseline; **the step 6 bounding rate is the module's self-repair check** and is compared directly against S026's phase 8 confidence figures; the phase 7 over-licensing rate is the reporting-sentence measure carried to S050 and S083; **item 5's sealed prediction accuracy is the day's frontier-calibration datum**, read by the following year's cohort through the [Durable and Perishable Register](wiki/program/09-Durable-and-Perishable-Register.md), per instrumentation rule 9.

The frontier statements are a sample and the day says so, per instrumentation rule 3: one cohort, one corpus, one evaluator, on a task where the population substitution was known to staff to be available.

Dispositions: **humility**, at step 7, where a complete limitation statement makes the student's own work look weaker.

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: the day teaches an apparatus it refuses to derive, which produces users who cannot tell when it does not apply.**

At its strongest: point 2 introduces the interval operationally — the range of population values consistent with the observations — computes one, and hands the rest to tooling. A student who does not know where the interval comes from cannot know which assumptions it rests on, and will therefore apply it where those assumptions fail. Phase 3's scheme E is precisely such a case, and the day's remedy is to warn about clustering specifically rather than to equip students to recognise assumption failures generally. That is instruction by exception list, and exception lists do not generalise.

The rebuttal is that the derivation would not supply what the objection wants either. A student who has followed a derivation can recite the independence assumption and will still not notice that session-clustered observations violate it, because noticing requires reasoning about the data-generating process rather than about the formula. The day attacks that directly: phase 3 asks which population each scheme estimates, which is a question about the process, and scheme E is diagnosable from the sampling description without any distributional knowledge.

The concession is about scope rather than about the argument. The day handles one assumption failure and the general skill of recognising others is left to S049 and MS103, neither of which the seminar coordinates with. And the day's own point 3 scope statement admits that an interval's coverage depends on assumptions that may fail — which is the objection, conceded in the seminar page and not addressed in the design. A day that names a limitation in its scope statement and builds nothing to handle it has documented a gap rather than closed one.

**Objection 2: step 7 has no defensible standard of correctness.**

At its strongest: the assessed output is a sentence stating what a number does not license. There is no complete answer — a number fails to license indefinitely many things — so completeness cannot be the standard. Relevance cannot be the standard either, since relevance depends on decisions not yet made. The specification's only concrete clause is negative: a sentence naming only sample size fails. Everything above that floor is assessor judgement, on an artefact the programme's own assessment material warns will be judged narratively and inconsistently. Two students could write materially different sentences, both defensible, and receive different outcomes for reasons neither could reconstruct.

This objection the design does not fully answer. Two partial mitigations exist. Phase 7's decision-elicitation test provides a functional standard that is not assessor-dependent: if a reader with the sentence alone identifies a decision the analysis does not support, the sentence is incomplete in a specific and demonstrable way. And the specification's floor catches the dominant failure.

Neither yields a standard for the middle of the range, and the design should say what it is doing instead. The honest position is that step 7 is assessed as **present, floor-passing and phase-7-survivable** — three binary conditions — rather than as good, and that the specification should state those three conditions explicitly rather than implying a quality judgement it cannot make. That restatement is not in the specification, and its absence means an assessor reading the current wording will believe they are judging quality and will do so, which is precisely the drift the programme's assessment material warns about.

**Objection 3: the practical turn strengthened three of the day's points and left the most important one as the only unstaged one.**

This is the objection that bites on the practical turn, and it is a defect the revision created rather than one it inherited.

At its strongest: the day's four executed schemes all draw differently from a corpus the programme already holds. That is what makes them executable, and it is exactly why none of them can stage point 5. Extrapolation — the claim that no sample supports a statement about a population it did not draw from, including the future — is the day's most consequential point and is now the only phase 4 point with no artefact behind it. Points 1, 3 and 4 each have a scheme; point 5 has item 3 of the pretest and a sentence. **Instrumentation rule 7 binds precisely here**: item 3 is the day's own record that students hold the resisted belief, extrapolation has an obvious engineering consequence, and the design predicted the resistance and then declined to meet it. Before the revision this was a symmetry — all five schemes were supplied, so no point was staged and none was privileged. After it, three points are demonstrated against a live service and the fourth is asserted, which makes the asserted one look like the soft claim. **The design has made its weakest-supported point weaker by strengthening the others.**

The objection is conceded and the rebuttal available is thin. It is true that no design can execute a sample of next quarter, and true that the impossibility is itself the point — but "we cannot demonstrate this because it is undemonstrable" is a claim about the world that a student has to take on trust, which is the position the module spends five days teaching them to refuse.

**The cheap fix is a second closing task at phase 3 and it is made.** Groups take the scheme they ranked most useful, name what would have to be true for it to license a claim about next quarter, and then **check the corpus for whether the sampled period was itself internally stable** — whether the model deployment changed, whether the question mix shifted, whether the index was rebuilt. This is executable on data the students hold, it costs ten minutes, and it converts point 5 from an assertion into a finding of the strongest kind available: the students discover that the week they sampled was not a single population either, which makes the claim about next quarter follow rather than be asserted. It is also the honest form, because it teaches the substitute an engineer actually has.

What remains unfixed is recorded. The check establishes instability where instability happens to exist in this corpus, and a stable week would leave the task with nothing to find. The design does not know how often the substrate produces a stable week, has not measured it, and has no fallback prepared for the case where a cohort's corpus is quiet. That is a dependency on the substrate's behaviour that the day has not characterised — which is, one level up, this module's own subject applied to this module's own apparatus, and it is recorded rather than repaired.

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** A large majority will give a substantive answer at item 2 and a majority will give a non-answer or an incorrect one at item 3. *Instrument:* per-item response coding.

**Prediction 2.** Scheme C will be ranked as supporting a more useful claim than scheme D by a majority, on the basis of its narrower interval. *Instrument:* phase 3 rankings with stated reasons.

**Prediction 3.** Fewer than half the cohort will produce a numeric bound at step 6; a majority of the remainder will produce a substantive statement of what would bound it. *Instrument:* step 6 outcomes in three categories. **This is the module's self-repair check; a low rate in both categories means the [WP-026](wiki/whitepapers/WP-026.md) repair has not worked.**

**Prediction 4.** In phase 7, over-licensing will be more common than under-licensing, and the gap will be larger for students with prior statistics training. *Instrument:* phase 7 outcomes by direction, split by the admission background survey.

**Prediction 5.** A large majority of item 5 sealed predictions will not anticipate that the agent answers the ship question at all; most will predict that it hedges or refuses. *Instrument:* item 5 responses coded for predicted refusal versus predicted answer, against what each agent produced. **The design's claim is that students over-estimate the agent's reticence on questions of consequence, which is the opposite of the failure they are usually warned about, and that discovering this on a question they can adjudicate is worth more than being told.**

**Prediction 6.** In phase 3's second closing task, a majority of groups will find at least one instability in the sampled period, and groups that find one will produce step 7 sentences naming extrapolation at a higher rate than groups that do not. *Instrument:* second-task findings against step 7 sentences coded for extrapolation content. **This is the §8 objection-3 repair's own check, and its failure mode is stated there: a corpus with a genuinely stable week leaves the task with nothing to find, and the design has not characterised how often that happens.**

## Evidence status

**Verified in this repository.** Sampling, intervals, clustering, extrapolation limits and the entitlement-of-conclusion position are grounded in [research/03-measurement-evaluation/](research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md), whose note on Miller records clustered standard errors, paired comparison and power analysis as the standard defences; those are cited for **direction and mechanism only** and no clustering ratio or question count from that source is asserted here. The reviewed-and-passed failure at phase 6 follows [research/05-fde-craft/](research/05-fde-craft/collected-materials.md). The assessor-drift warning invoked in §8 is drawn from [research/08-assessment-epas/govaerts-narrative-assessment-literature.md](research/08-assessment-epas/govaerts-narrative-assessment-literature.md). The magnitude requirement implemented at point 8 and step 6 responds to [WP-026](wiki/whitepapers/WP-026.md) §8.

**Grounded in vendor documentation, with its version and its silence recorded.** The evaluation behaviour used at phase 3 and points 9 and 10 is taken from Microsoft Learn's cloud evaluation how-to, built-in evaluators reference, Agent Framework evaluation page and agent monitoring dashboard how-to for Microsoft Foundry, all read 2026-07-26. Specifically: **the evaluation level defaults to `turn`** when omitted, and turn-level evaluators score each turn independently; conversation-level evaluation is preview, requires a `messages` array, and all evaluators in a run must support the same level; an aggregate result over a dataset is **the average pass rate across examples**; JSONL supports turn-level and conversation-level rows while **CSV is turn-level only**; the Agent Framework's conversation split defaults to the last turn, with full and per-turn alternatives, under the documentation's own heading that **"How you split determines what you're evaluating"**; continuous evaluation samples production at a set rate; and trace evaluation offers **intelligent sampling**, described as a MinHash farthest-first diversity algorithm that preserves trace diversity so that **"edge cases, error paths, and varied conversation patterns are included in the evaluated set."**

**Three silences are recorded and one is taught.** Intelligent sampling is documented as producing a **"representative subset"** and, in the same passage, as selecting for diversity — and **the documentation does not say which property it means, nor whether an aggregate computed over such a subset estimates anything.** That is the silence point 9 names to students, and it matters because the two readings license opposite conclusions from the same number. The aggregate pass rate is documented as an average across examples and **nothing states what dependence structure, if any, the reported figure assumes**, which is why scheme D's interval is wrong without anything being incorrect. And, consistent with the cross-source asymmetry recorded in [research/06-microsoft-platform/](research/06-microsoft-platform/collected-materials.md), **no accuracy or agreement figure appears anywhere in this documentation**, and none is asserted here. Conversation-level evaluation, trace evaluation, intelligent sampling and continuous evaluation are all marked preview; the last two are demonstrated rather than operated and are not assessed; and the defaults are marked perishable at point 10.

**Cited from general knowledge, not verified here.** Contrasting cases with a fixed quantity, escalating-condition pretests, retrieval practice at expanding intervals, cognitive load under stacked unfamiliar demands, worked-example fading, and error management training are cited for **direction and mechanism only**. No effect size is asserted for any of them, and none is asserted for AI-assisted productivity anywhere in this paper.

**Design reasoning with no external warrant.** The escalating pretest and the decision to reward the size intuition before bypassing it; the four executed schemes and the choice of the platform's own default row shape as the payload; the usefulness-versus-width ranking task and its stability follow-up; the ten points of phase 4 and the operational introduction of the interval; the eight-step phase 5 and the coupled-output build at step 8; phase 7's sentences-only protocol; the specification's sample-size-only failure clause; and all six §9 predictions are this design's own reasoning, untested. The §8 objection-1 concession states that the day names an assumption-failure limitation in its own scope statement and builds nothing to address it. The objection-2 concession states that the day's assessed output has no defensible quality standard, specifies the three binary conditions the design is actually applying, and notes that the specification's current wording implies a quality judgement the design cannot make. **The objection-3 concession states that the revision made the day's most important point relatively weaker by staging the other three, records the phase 3 stability check as the fix, and records that the fix depends on a substrate behaviour the design has not characterised.**
