# WP-054 — Preference, Alignment and What Optimisation Selects For

**Seminar day:** [S054](wiki/seminars/S054.md) · **Module:** [M11](wiki/modules/M11.md) · **Quarter:** [Q6](wiki/quarters/Q6.md)
**Strands:** IS · ME · MS · **Complexity class:** 4
**Platform anchor:** Foundry fine-tuning — supervised, direct preference optimisation (preview), reinforcement · Foundry model catalogue · **MCP servers:** Foundry MCP Server · Microsoft Learn MCP Server

## Abstract

This paper defends treating a preference dataset as a criterion with everything S046 required present and nothing recorded, and defends delivering the day's central claim — that the annotators' systematic component becomes the training target — as a one-minute consequence of a fact the cohort established six days earlier rather than as new content. It defends an opening that supplies choices, then instructions, then choices defeating both, on the ground that the gap between the written and the operative criterion is the only thing on the day that needs no framework to see. It defends holding the optimisation fixed while varying the annotator population, and defends placing the student in every role the day critiques. **Its principal addition is that the day's argument now arrives partly as a file format**: the platform's preference training schema admits exactly two completions per record, so a criterion that was a ranking, a rejection or a continuous judgement is compressed into a binary choice by a schema, and the compression is legible in every record and recorded nowhere. The paper reports discharge of the obligation S052 placed on this day under instrumentation rule 6. It records **a defect in the rule amended two days ago: rule 5's validated-versus-proposed distinction is unsatisfiable at design time**, and amends it with a contingency clause in the same chain. It concedes that the day teaches its own answer before administering the instrument that measures it, and opens that as a pattern-level observation; and it concedes that the day's anchor is a preview capability the programme may not be able to run, which makes the strongest half of the practical turn optional.

## 1. The instructional problem this day must solve

Students arrive able to use the word *aligned* and unable to complete it.

The word is two-place at minimum and three-place in practice: aligned to what, from whom, under what instructions. Used without those, it means approximately *nice*, and it is used that way in almost everything the students have read. Repairing that is the day's most portable output and it is the sentence most likely to be used in rooms outside this programme.

The mechanism underneath is a compression the students are equipped to decompress and have not been asked to. [S046](wiki/seminars/S046.md) established what a criterion is: constructed, by named people, from evidence, for a purpose, with trades recorded. A preference dataset has all of that — someone chose the annotators, someone wrote instructions, thousands of choices were made — and **records none of it.** [S048](wiki/seminars/S048.md) established that every judge carries a systematic error component that does not average out with more judging. A preference dataset is a pile of judgements. The systematic component did not evaporate; it became an objective.

Phase 2's bridge asks exactly that — *where did the systematic component go* — and the room answers it in under a minute. **The day's central claim is therefore a retrieval item, not an exposition**, which is the strongest position a claim can occupy.

The second problem is the turn. Optimising against a proxy improves the true objective, then stops improving it, then damages it, and the proxy reports improvement throughout. Detecting the turn requires a measure the proxy does not control — which is a structural requirement, not a matter of diligence, and step 7's second clause is where the day tests whether students have understood that.

The third problem is attribution. A behaviour observed after preference optimisation could come from the annotators, from the optimisation dynamics, or from the base model, and the three are not distinguishable by looking. This is [S052](wiki/seminars/S052.md) point 4 in a new domain and it is why S052 named this day as its rule-6 response occasion.

**The fourth problem is one the platform supplies and the earlier design could not.** The day's central claim — that a criterion is compiled into weights with its construction discarded — was, in the first draft, an argument. It is now a file. The platform's preference training format takes three fields per record: the input, the preferred output, and the non-preferred output, **and the documentation states that you can only provide two completions.** Whatever the criterion actually was, the schema will accept it only as a sequence of binary choices. An expert who wanted to say *neither of these* cannot. A user whose preference was expressed by accepting something never compared two things at all, and their behaviour was reconstructed into pairs by whoever built the file.

That is the discarding, enforced, visible in every record, and impossible to argue with. It is also the reason the vendor's own account of the technique's appeal is worth reading aloud: teams reach for it because they *often have preference data already collected based on user logs, A/B tests, or smaller manual annotation efforts* — which is to say, **the easiest data to obtain is the data nobody constructed on purpose**, and the format's convenience is what makes the criterion invisible.

## 3. Theoretical basis for the design

### 3.1 Load profile and element interactivity

**High, and the module's second peak after S053.** The day's technical content is modest; its load comes from phase 5, which requires the student to write instructions, annotate forty pairs, recruit a peer group, measure disagreement, and then analyse their own annotations against their own instructions — five operations that are individually easy and collectively long.

The interactivity is between steps 1, 2 and 5: the criterion determines the instructions, the instructions shape but do not determine the annotations, and step 5's implied criterion is recovered from the annotations against both.

Management:

- **A pair-generation harness is supplied** so no time is spent constructing candidate outputs.
- **Step 2 is worked with the room**, including the instruction-writing, which is where most students would otherwise stall.
- **Forty pairs is deliberately small.** It is enough to recover a shape and little enough to annotate inside the phase.
- **Peer annotation is twenty pairs, not forty.**
- **Phase 3's four deployments and its probe set are supplied**, and the groups query them rather than building anything.

**Step 3 is unsupported**, and §8 objection 2 argues that it has nonetheless been contaminated.

### 3.2 Desirable difficulties selected, and those deliberately not

**Selected.**

- *Inferring a criterion from choices before seeing instructions,* item 1.
- *Five pairs defeating both your inference and the instructions,* item 3.
- *Characterising four models from outputs before the populations are revealed,* phase 3.
- *Producing two explanations and a distinguishing test for every behaviour,* phase 3.
- *Writing instructions before annotating,* step 2, enforced as a fail condition.
- *Having peers annotate under your instructions and measuring the gap,* step 4.
- *Arguing that your detection evaluation is not controlled by your criterion,* step 7.

**Deliberately not selected.**

- *The optimisation algorithms themselves.* MS201's and IS202's. The day is indifferent to which method is used and says so.
- *Constitutional and rule-based approaches.* Named as an alternative family and excluded; they relocate the criterion-construction problem rather than removing it, which is a point made in one sentence.
- *Safety-specific alignment.* [S078](wiki/seminars/S078.md)'s.
- *Multi-objective and personalised preference.* Named and excluded.

**Scope, per the [Whitepaper Standard](wiki/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md), strong form.** Point 6's list of selected-for properties — length, confident tone, formatting, refusal patterns, agreement — is the fastest-moving content in the module. **Safe** as a set of candidate properties to look for in a preference set you are analysing, and safe as an existence claim: unintended selection happens and is not rare. **Unsafe** as a description of any particular system, and specifically unsafe as a claim about which properties current systems exhibit — training practice changes, several of these are actively worked against, and a student citing this list as a property of a deployed model has asserted something they have not measured. The mechanism is durable; every item on the list is not.

**The platform instance, scoped in the same form.** Points 9 and 11 teach the three fine-tuning techniques and the preference schema as the operable form of the criterion argument.

*Safe.* Reasoning that a training format is a constraint on what a criterion can express; that a binary comparison discards ordering, rejection and magnitude; that a technique choice encodes a theory about what kind of object your criterion is; that a hyperparameter default is a decision nobody made; and that a filter applied to your model at a threshold you cannot see is a criterion somebody else wrote. All five are properties of the class of system and hold wherever preference data is compiled into weights, on any platform.

*Unsafe.* Reasoning about **which technique produces a better model**, which is a question about a task and a dataset and which the day does not measure. Unsafe as a claim that the two-completion constraint is a defect — it is a design decision with a stated rationale, namely that binary preferences avoid fitting a separate reward model, and a student who leaves calling it a mistake has misread a trade as an error. And unsafe as a claim about **availability**: preference fine-tuning is preview, its supported base models are a named and moving list, and a student who plans a system around its availability has planned around a preview. That last unsafe condition is why the day's specification does not assess the training submission.

The boundary is stated to students at points 9, 10 and 11, and step 8 — name what your criterion lost in the compression — is where a student applying the compression argument outside its range would fail, because a student who answers "nothing" has either a genuinely binary criterion, which is possible and must be argued, or has not read their own file.

### 3.3 Engagement and motivational mechanism

The day runs on **item 3**.

Students infer a criterion from twenty choices. They are then given the annotator instructions and check their inference — an ordinary and satisfying exercise. Then five more pairs arrive in which the choice contradicts both the instructions and the inference. There is nothing to interpret. **The written criterion and the operative criterion are different objects**, and the room has just watched the difference.

The second mechanism is **P4** at phase 3. Its model scores highest of the four on the judge the cohort themselves characterised at S048, and worst on the domain experts' criterion. Students who have spent a day learning to characterise a judge now watch a system optimise directly into that judge's errors, and the sequencing is the argument: this is not a new failure, it is S048's failure with an optimiser pointed at it.

Phase 6 supplies the affective content, and the detail that carries it is the six weeks. The satisfaction metric and the completion metric were both being collected. One was on the dashboard. **The turn was visible the entire time to anyone who looked at the other number.**

The competence risk is at step 4. A student whose peers disagree substantially with their own annotations may read that as evidence they annotated badly. The instructor states before phase 5 that high disagreement under identical instructions is the expected result, is the day's most important measurement, and is a finding about instructions rather than about annotators.

## 4. Phase-by-phase design and its warrant

**Phase 1 (25 min).** Choices, then instructions, then choices defeating both. Item 4's prediction is collected for phase 8 and for §9 prediction 1. **Item 5 is the sealed Copilot prediction** and is administered here per instrumentation rule 8; the design expects a schema-valid file whose generation procedure introduces a systematic difference between the two completions unrelated to the criterion.

**Phase 2 (20 min).** Four retrievals and the bridge. The bridge is the day's highest-value minute.

**Phase 3 (45 min).** Four annotator populations, one optimisation, **four real deployments queried by the groups**, characterisation before revelation, and **two explanations plus a distinguishing test for every named behaviour** — the rule-6 element. **The training files are then opened**, which is the phase's platform payload: the compression to two completions is visible in every record and groups are required to find one record per file where they can tell it happened.

**Phase 4 (50 min).** Eleven points. Point 5 is the mechanism, point 6 the retained list, point 7 the portable sentence. **Point 9 is the technique account and is placed after point 8 so that the choice reads as a consequence of the attribution problem rather than as a menu. Points 10 and 11 are the required perishable point and the required defaults**; point 11's last default is point 4 recursing, since the thing that checks your model before deployment is itself a criterion at a threshold you cannot see.

**Phase 5 (90 min).** Eight steps in which the student occupies every role the day critiques, **shipping a validated preference training set in the platform's own format**. **Step 4 uses an external observer per instrumentation rule 1** — a peer group is present for other reasons, so the disagreement is theirs to measure and not the student's to estimate. Step 6's added constraint — a property not on the point 6 list — answers §8 objection 2. **Step 8 is new and converts the compression argument from something taught into something the student can point at in a file they wrote.** Submitting a training job is demonstrated rather than operated and is not assessed; §8 objection 3 examines what that costs.

**Phase 6 (40 min).** Six weeks, two metrics, one dashboard — then an agent-built generator whose defect exists only in the distribution and not in any record.

**Phase 7 (30 min).** Alternative explanation and distinguishing intervention, **contingent** under the amended rule 5 — see §8.

**Phase 8 (20 min).** Item 4 against measurement; step 3 against step 6; the cohort's disagreement distribution; **item 5 opened and coded for distributional versus record-level defect**; step 8's compression losses read aloud.

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**Students with more relevant prior knowledge.** A student who has read alignment work will name agreement at step 3 immediately and find it at step 6. Predicted failure: they produce the expected answer and learn nothing about their own set. Countermeasure: step 7's independence argument is not answerable from reading, and phase 7's partner attacks the attribution rather than the finding. §8 objection 2 argues this countermeasure is insufficient.

**Students with less relevant prior knowledge.** The risk is step 2's volume. Countermeasures: the harness; instruction-writing worked with the room; forty pairs rather than a realistic number; peer annotation capped at twenty.

**Phase 3 is too easy.** Characterising P1 and P2 is straightforward. The difficulty is P3, which has no instructions at all and whose implied criterion must be recovered entirely from behaviour, and P4, whose model looks best by the measure the students trust least. Fallback: ask which of the four they would deploy on the substrate today, which forces the trade rather than the characterisation.

**Affective failure.** The characteristic risk is **cynicism about the whole enterprise** — if every preference set encodes an unrecorded criterion, and optimisation finds the proxy's errors, and the word *aligned* means little, a student may conclude that alignment work is theatre. This would be a serious misreading and the day creates conditions for it.

Countermeasures: P1 exists and is better than the others on the criterion its annotators were instructed for, which demonstrates that careful construction works; point 5 is stated as a turn rather than as futility, with a detectable location; step 7 requires a constructive answer rather than a critique; and the instructor states once that the day's argument is for doing this work explicitly rather than for not doing it.

## 7. Assessment and evidence generated

No entrustment judgement.

One specification item in IS202 with ME202. Per rule 1, the checked elements are the traceable criterion; the preference set with instructions written first, emitted and validated in the platform's training format; the pre-committed prediction; measured peer disagreement; the implied criterion recovered from choices; one unintended selected property with evidence, not drawn from the taught list; the over-optimisation detection with its independence argued; the compression statement with its record; the phase 7 outcome; **the prompt record, the intervention record and the frontier statement.** **Nothing else is assessed.**

**Submitting a training job is not assessed.** Preference fine-tuning is preview, is supported on a named and moving list of base models, and is subject to regional and quota availability outside the programme's control; where the tenant does not hold it, the submission is demonstrated rather than operated. Per the [Copilot Practice Standard](wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md) the specification does not assess a case the programme cannot guarantee it can run. §8 objection 3 examines what that costs the day.

Three hard requirements: **a detection evaluation controlled by the criterion it checks fails**; **instructions written after annotation fail**; and **an intervention record with no entries fails** unless the artefact demonstrates that the design could have detected an intervention-worthy error.

**Rule-6 discharge, as required by [WP-052](wiki/whitepapers/WP-052.md) §7.** S052 named this day as the response occasion for its phase 7 alternative-explanation rate, specifying that phase 3's preference datasets be constructed to admit multiple explanations of one outcome. **The element exists and is a required output of the phase: two explanations and a distinguishing test for every behaviour a group names, produced before the populations are revealed.** It is not a prompt and not an aside. **The practical revision strengthens it rather than diluting it**: because the four models are queried by the groups rather than supplied as output tables, the behaviours a group names are behaviours it found, and an explanation offered for a behaviour you found is a different object from one offered for a behaviour you were shown. The obligation is discharged.

**Instrumentation under rule 6.** The step 4 peer-disagreement distribution responds at [S056](wiki/seminars/S056.md) phase 5, where the same disagreement is measured on relevance judgements; the step 3-against-step 6 accuracy responds at [S060](wiki/seminars/S060.md), whose failure-mode statement must include one unintended selection property. **WP-056 §7 and WP-060 §7 must report whether those elements exist**, per WP-051 §8.

Item 5's hit rate joins the running frontier map per instrumentation rule 9. Per rule 3 the day states its sampling: one cohort, one substrate, four annotator populations chosen by staff.

**Phase 7's structure is proposed, not validated**, per the amended rule 5, and its use here is contingent. §8 examines whether that classification is coherent.

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: the amended rule 5 cannot be satisfied at design time, and this day is where that becomes visible.**

At its strongest: [WP-052](wiki/whitepapers/WP-052.md) §8 amended instrumentation rule 5 to distinguish a **validated** remedy — one that has run at least once and whose outcome has been reported — from a **proposed** one, and ruled that only validated remedies enter the repertoire. The amendment was correct in its diagnosis: rule 5 had been used to launder an untested structure into the design's repertoire.

But the amendment is unsatisfiable in the situation it was written for. **No day in this programme has run.** The entire design is written before any cohort exists, so no remedy can ever move from proposed to validated during design, and every countermeasure the design invents is permanently proposed. Applied literally, the amended rule forbids reuse of any new structure anywhere in the remaining thirty-six days — including S052's phase 7 structure, which this day uses.

The design has to choose between three positions: ignore the rule it wrote two days ago, refuse to reuse any new structure for the rest of the programme, or fix the rule. The first is what the design would have done a quarter ago and is what the compliance register exists to catch. The second is absurd on its face — it would mean that a good idea found at S052 may never be applied at S054 because the calendar has not yet happened.

**The rule is therefore amended again, and the amendment is specific rather than a weakening.** At design time a proposed remedy may be reused, provided that **every reuse is recorded as contingent on the proposing day's reported outcome**, and that **a single reported failure retracts every contingent reuse at once.** The retraction is what makes the concession safe: the design is not permitted to accumulate dependents on an untested structure and then find the cost of removing it too high, which is the ordinary way an unvalidated pattern becomes load-bearing. S054 phase 7 is marked contingent on S052's outcome in the seminar page itself.

What this episode shows, and what is worth recording beyond the fix, is that **the amended rule was itself an untested design change reused immediately** — WP-052 wrote it and this day is its first application. The design's rule-making is subject to the same defect its rules address, one level up. That observation is recorded and not acted on further; a rule governing the making of rules is not obviously worth its cost, and the compliance register already catches the failures it would prevent.

**Objection 2: the day teaches its own answer before administering the instrument that measures it.**

At its strongest: step 3 asks the student to predict what their preference set will select for that they did not intend. That prediction is collected in phase 5, **after phase 4 has spent fifty minutes naming five such properties at point 6, with agreement identified as the canonical one.** A student who predicts "agreement" at step 3 and finds it at step 6 has performed a recall exercise. The day's headline instrument — step 3 against step 6 — measures whether students remember the list.

Worse, step 6 requires a property "with evidence" and does not require it to be one the list omits, so the same contamination reaches the assessed artefact. The specification cannot distinguish a student who analysed their own annotations from one who searched them for the properties phase 4 supplied.

This is conceded without qualification. Two fixes are available and the cheaper one is better. Requiring step 6 to name **one property not on the point 6 list**, in addition to any that are, costs one clause and forces analysis rather than search. Moving step 3 before phase 4 is cleaner in principle but impossible in practice, since the student has no criterion until step 1.

**The finding generalises and is opened as a pattern-level observation at one instance: this design repeatedly places its instruments downstream of its own instruction.** The pretest structure exists precisely to avoid this and is used well at phases 1 of every day; the failure appears at *phase 5* instruments, which are written as part of the learning task and are not thought of as measurements until §7 of the whitepaper claims them as ones. The observation is recorded and left standing. If it reaches three instances it becomes an instrumentation rule, and the natural form is already visible: **an instrument administered after its answer has been taught measures recall.**

**Objection 3: the day's strongest practical element is the one the programme may not be able to run.**

This is the objection that bites on the practical turn.

At its strongest: the day's platform anchor is preference fine-tuning, and the argument for anchoring here is that the technique makes the day's abstraction concrete — a criterion becomes a file, a compression becomes a schema constraint, an unintended selection becomes a measurable property of a deployment. **All of that depends on four tuned deployments existing.** They are staff-prepared, which the design treats as sufficient. But the capability is preview, its supported base models are a moving list, and its regional availability is not the programme's to control. A university lab that cannot obtain it has a day whose phase 3 reverts to supplied outputs and whose phase 5 produces a file that is never compiled into anything.

Worse, the specification has already conceded the point by declining to assess the submission. **So the day's assessed artefact is a dataset and an analysis — which is exactly what the pre-revision day assessed** — and the practical turn has added apparatus that carries the argument without carrying any of the assessment. A reader could reasonably say the turn is decorative here in a way it is not at S051 or S053, where the built thing is the assessed thing.

The rebuttal is real. **The two-completion constraint is a documentation fact, not a runtime one**, and it does its instructional work whether or not a job ever runs: a student who writes a file in the format and finds that their criterion does not fit has learned the day's central claim from a schema, at no availability cost. Step 8 is written to depend on the format and not on the training run, deliberately. And the four deployments, where they exist, are reused by [S055](wiki/seminars/S055.md) as its model-swap arm, so the preparation is amortised across two days rather than spent on one.

**But the objection is conceded on its narrower and truer form: the day's phase 3 degrades to a reading exercise where the capability is unavailable, and the design does not say what the degraded day looks like.** Every other day in this module states its demonstrated-rather-than-operated boundary in the day's own text — S052 at phase 3 arm A, S053 at its opening. This day states it only for the submission, and not for the four deployments phase 3 depends on.

**The cheap fix is one paragraph and it is not made.** The day should state, in phase 3, that where four tuned deployments cannot be obtained the phase runs on supplied outputs, is demonstrated rather than operated, **and that the training-file inspection continues regardless** — because the files are files and require nothing to be deployed. That would preserve the day's central platform payload under the worst availability case, would cost nothing, and would bring this day into line with the two before it. It is not written, and the reason is that the design wrote the availability caveat for the submission and did not ask the same question one level up, **which is the same locality failure instrumentation rule 5 exists to prevent and which this whitepaper is in the middle of amending the rule about.**

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** At item 4, before any instruction, the most-predicted unstated property will be length rather than agreement. *Instrument:* item 4 predictions coded by property. **The claim is that length is the visible proxy and agreement is the invisible one**, and the gap between item 4 and step 3 measures what phase 4 supplied.

**Prediction 2.** Step 4 peer disagreement under identical written instructions will exceed one in five pairs for a majority of students. *Instrument:* per-student disagreement rates, reported as a distribution.

**Prediction 3.** More than half of step 7 detection evaluations will fail the independence requirement on first submission. *Instrument:* step 7 submissions coded against the fail condition, before and after revision. **This is the day's hardest element and the prediction is that it is too hard as scaffolded.**

**Prediction 4.** Step 3 predictions naming a point 6 property will be confirmed at step 6 far more often than predictions naming a property not on the list. *Instrument:* step 3 and step 6 pairs coded for list membership. **If the gap is large, §8 objection 2 is correct and step 6's fix is required**; if it is small, the contamination is less severe than argued and the concession is overstated.

**Prediction 5.** At step 8, more than half of students will name a compression loss of the form *my criterion was a ranking and the format took a comparison*, and fewer than a quarter will name one of the form *my criterion admitted rejecting both*. *Instrument:* step 8 statements coded by loss type. **The claim is that ordering is the visible loss and rejection is the invisible one**, because a student who cannot express *neither* tends to conclude they should have written better candidates rather than that the format cannot hold their criterion. If the rejection rate is high, the two-completion constraint is teaching more than the design assumes and step 8 could carry more weight.

## Evidence status

**Verified in this repository.** Preference data as an implicit criterion, divergence between annotator instructions and annotator behaviour, annotator population effects on resulting model behaviour, reward models as judges, over-optimisation against a learned proxy, and unintended selected properties including agreement with the user's premise are grounded in [research/02-technical-foundations/ouyang-instructgpt.md](research/02-technical-foundations/ouyang-instructgpt.md) and [research/02-technical-foundations/rafailov-dpo.md](research/02-technical-foundations/rafailov-dpo.md) and in [research/03-measurement-evaluation/zheng-mt-bench-chatbot-arena.md](research/03-measurement-evaluation/zheng-mt-bench-chatbot-arena.md), [research/03-measurement-evaluation/shankar-evalgen.md](research/03-measurement-evaluation/shankar-evalgen.md) and [research/03-measurement-evaluation/govaerts-narrative-assessment.md](research/03-measurement-evaluation/govaerts-narrative-assessment.md). The criterion-construction frame is [S046](wiki/seminars/S046.md)'s and follows [research/04-professional-formation/](research/04-professional-formation/collected-materials.md).

**Grounded in vendor documentation, with its version and its silence recorded.** The fine-tuning material at phase 3 and points 9 to 11 was read from Microsoft Learn on 2026-07-26.

*What the documentation says.* Microsoft Foundry offers three fine-tuning techniques: **supervised**, which trains on labelled input-output pairs and is described as working best where there are finite ways of solving a problem; **direct preference optimisation**, described as adjusting model weights based on human preferences, as **not requiring the fitting of a reward model**, as using binary preferences, and as computationally lighter and faster than reinforcement learning from human feedback while being equally effective at alignment; and **reinforcement fine-tuning**, which uses reward signals from graders and is described as suiting problems with many possible solutions. The techniques may be stacked, supervised first and preference second. The preference training file uses three top-level fields — the input, the preferred output and the non-preferred output — and **the documentation states that you can only provide two completions.** The technique's stated appeal is that organisations which struggle to assemble a supervised dataset *often have preference data already collected based on user logs, A/B tests, or smaller manual annotation efforts.* A hyperparameter governs the weight of the penalty between the tuned model and its reference, with **higher values increasing that penalty**, and the documentation recommends defaults for initial experimentation. Training tiers are documented as a standard tier providing in-region data residency, a global tier that is cheaper with a faster queue, and a developer tier that is pre-emptible and intended for experimentation. The transparency note records that fine-tuning can reduce a model's robustness and generalisation, that poorly fine-tuned models may regurgitate training data, and that **a model's logic and reasoning can become more opaque after fine-tuning, such that customers may not be able to explain how or why the model arrived at its outputs**; it further records that **fine-tuned model evaluation filters are set to predefined thresholds and cannot be modified by customers.**

*What version.* No page carries one. **Direct preference optimisation is documented as preview**, on a named list of supported base models that changed twice during the writing of this page, with regional availability that is not enumerated on the same page.

*What it does not report that a reader would need.* **The documentation states that the format admits exactly two completions and states nothing about what to do when your criterion does not fit** — no guidance on representing a rejection, an ordering, or a tie, and no acknowledgement that the constraint is a modelling decision rather than a neutral encoding. That silence is the day's step 8. Second, the transparency note states that fine-tuning safety filters run at predefined thresholds and **does not publish the thresholds or any detection figure for them**, so a refusal a student observes cannot be attributed to a filter with any confidence — which is the deployment-configuration kind [S055](wiki/seminars/S055.md) names as an omission in its own taxonomy. Third, and consistent with the folder-wide asymmetry recorded in [research/06-microsoft-platform/](research/06-microsoft-platform/collected-materials.md), the material states techniques, formats, tiers, hyperparameters and cautions throughout and **states no accuracy anywhere**; nothing in it licenses a claim about how much any technique improves anything, and none is made here. **In particular no claim is made that preference optimisation is equally effective at alignment — that is the documentation's characterisation of the technique and is reported as such, not adopted.**
