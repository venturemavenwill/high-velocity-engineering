# WP-035 — Breaking a System That Appears to Work

**Seminar day:** [S035](/wiki/seminars/S035.md) · **Module:** [M07](/wiki/modules/M07.md) · **Quarter:** [Q4](/wiki/quarters/Q4.md)
**Strands:** SY · ME · PE · CF · **Complexity class:** 3
**Platform anchor:** Bicep and the `what-if` operation as the review instrument · **MCP servers:** Azure MCP Server (write, disposable environments only) · Microsoft Learn MCP Server

## Abstract

This paper defends allowing second-year students to degrade a live service with real users on it, and defends assessing the day on a report whose most heavily weighted section records what the student did not predict. The first argument is that the competence being built — deciding what would count as a system working, and observing whether it does — cannot be exercised against a system that cannot be disturbed, and that every substitute the design considered removes the property that makes it instructive: that the consequence is not arranged by the instructor. The second argument is that a break's value is entirely in the gap between prediction and observation, so an artefact with nothing unpredicted records an experiment that was not observed rather than one that went well. The paper additionally defends making the quarter's review instrument the day's subject: `what-if` is characterised as an instrument with documented false positives and documented false negatives, which is the programme's answer to the risk that a student deploys infrastructure they could not have written and cannot review. It concedes that the day's safety rests on a staff veto whose exercise is unmeasured, that the design has no principled account of what degradation the substrate's users have consented to, and that characterising one review instrument does not establish that a student will characterise the next one.

## 1. The instructional problem this day must solve

The module has produced four documents and one deployed gateway: a specification, an evidence argument, a failure-handling decision and a contract. Each is a claim about how something behaves, and none has been tested against the substrate component behaving.

The competence the day builds is prior to breaking anything. **What would count as this system working, and how would you know if it stopped?** Students cannot answer this. Asked whether a component is working, they say yes and cite the absence of complaints — which is S027's selection effect and S032's disappearance fallacy in their most ordinary form. Item 1 exposes this in the first five minutes.

Without a criterion, a break produces an event. Something changes, somebody notices or does not, and the student has learned that the system is fragile or robust in a way they cannot state. With a criterion committed in writing beforehand, the same ninety minutes is an experiment whose result is interpretable whichever way it falls.

The day's second problem is what makes the criterion hard: it must be observable *before* the break, not only after. Point 5 names this and it is the part students omit. A criterion that can only be evaluated retrospectively — "users were not affected" — is not a criterion, because the state it compares against was never recorded.

The third problem is the report's structure. A student who breaks something, repairs it and reports that the criterion held has produced nothing. The valuable content is the gap between what was predicted and what occurred, and it exists in every case, because a system under disturbance always does something nobody modelled. Point 10's five-part report makes that section mandatory and the specification fails an empty one — **an empty section means it was not looked for**, which is a stronger claim than the design usually makes and is defended in §8.

**The fourth problem is the quarter's and it arrives here because there is nowhere else for it to go.** Four days ago the agent began writing to Azure. The quarter's governing sentence is that an architecture you can describe and cannot deploy is a diagram, and an architecture the agent deploys that you cannot review is a liability — and the second half has been asserted for four days without being taught. It cannot be taught by exhortation, because "review the agent's work carefully" is advice every student already agrees with and none can act on. It can be taught by characterising the review instrument, because the instrument is documented, its failure modes are published by its own vendor, and its most dangerous failure mode is **silence**: a resource excluded from the analysis appears in the output as nothing at all. That is [S029](/wiki/seminars/S029.md)'s discipline applied to the one tool a student is about to stake a production change on, and it is the only form of the argument the design has found that a student can execute rather than agree with.

The fifth problem is one of institutional nerve. The programme has committed, in the [Substrate and Infrastructure](/wiki/program/10-Substrate-and-Infrastructure.md) page's withdrawal commitment, to teaching this material against a real system or not teaching it. This is the first day where that commitment costs something.

## 2. Entry state and prior knowledge assumptions

- **Four module artefacts** about the component, none tested against a disturbance.
- **S009's falsification discipline,** twenty-six weeks old, now applied to a running system.
- **S028's licensing discipline** and **S029's instrument design**, which the criterion and the before-state record require — **and which point 7 now applies to a tool rather than to a measurement**, which is the day's principal transfer demand.
- **S033's blast-radius thinking,** two days old but not under that name — the unhandled-failure consequence estimate is the same quantity.
- **No experience of production consequence.** Every prior artefact was adopted, assessed or documented; none was executed against users.
- **The S020 load-change comparison,** reported in Q3, which phase 8 revisits.
- **Four days of Azure write access in disposable environments,** one deploy-and-destroy cycle, and Bicep read but not authored. **No student has reviewed a deployment preview**, and the design assumes none holds any model of what such a preview can fail to show — which is what item 5 measures and phase 3 supplies.

## 3. Theoretical basis for the design

### 3.1 Load profile and element interactivity

**Low intrinsic, extremely high situational.** The technical work is small — a bounded change to a component the student has studied for four days — and everything difficult about the day comes from executing it under consequence, in a shared window, with a staff member accountable and a rollback available.

Management, which is unusually procedural:

- **Consolidation precedes the window,** reversing the assessment-day pattern. The window is an execution and students must not be learning during it.
- **The criterion is committed and reviewed before the window opens,** so no design work happens under time pressure.
- **One change at a time, sequenced,** with the system returned to a known state between them. Concurrency between student breaks would make every observation uninterpretable.
- **The rollback is not the student's decision,** which removes a judgement they are worst placed to make while the experiment is running.
- **A named staff member may decline any break without appeal,** which bounds the space of what can go wrong before anything is attempted.
- **The agent does not apply the change.** Authoring, reasoning and reviewing are agent-assisted; application is a human act. This is a load decision as well as a safety one: an agent applying the change would make the window's most consequential moment the one the student attends to least.
- **The instrument's characterisation is supplied at point 7, not discovered at step 3.** Phase 3 produces the surprise and phase 4 supplies the model, so that no student is deriving a tool's failure modes while a production window is open.
- **The report is written the following day,** not in the room.

### 3.2 Desirable difficulties selected, and those deliberately not

**Selected.**

- *Stating a working criterion observable before the break,* item 2 and step 1.
- *Item 3's unanswerable question* — what will your break do that you have not predicted — revisited against reality at phase 8.
- *Item 5's harder unanswerable question* — what could be wrong with your change that your evidence cannot show — which asks a student to reason about an instrument they have not been told is one.
- *Proposal D,* a well-designed experiment against a promise nobody made.
- *Reading four `what-if` outputs and forming a judgement from them,* immediately before learning that one was silent.
- *Recording the before-state,* step 2, which students omit — **now with the additional demand that it be independent of the tooling.**
- *Stating what your review does not cover for your particular change,* step 3.
- *The mandatory unpredicted section,* point 10 and the specification.
- *Estimating blast radius before and measuring it after,* steps 1 and 7.

**Deliberately not selected.**

- *Fault injection tooling.* Named and not used; the breaks are direct changes, because a tool would supply the fault taxonomy the student should be choosing from.
- *Unannounced degradation.* Refused absolutely. The window is announced to users.
- *Student-held rollback.* Refused, per point 6.
- *Agent-applied substrate change.* Refused, per the production conditions.
- *Unbounded blast radius.* The staff veto's principal function.
- *Concurrent student changes.*
- *Authoring Bicep modules.* The refactor that produces short-circuiting is shown at phase 3 and phase 6 and is not written by students, because the day's competence is reviewing a change rather than structuring one.
- *Measuring how often `what-if` is incomplete.* The documentation publishes no rate and the day does not invite students to estimate one from four proposals, which would be a sample of four presented as a property.

**Scope, per the [Whitepaper Standard](/wiki/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md), strong form.** The day teaches a break as a bounded change to a single component under an announced window. **Safe** as a method for testing a stated contract against a component you own. **Unsafe** as a model for resilience testing generally, which requires fault taxonomies, steady-state hypotheses over aggregate metrics, and blast-radius control the day does not teach — and which is taken up nowhere in this programme, since S080 works incidents rather than experiments. That last clause is the more important half: students should not leave believing they have learned resilience engineering.

**The platform instance, scoped in the strong form.** The day teaches `what-if` as a characterised instrument.

*Safe.* Deciding how much weight a deployment preview can bear; knowing which independent observation to take alongside it; recognising that an output containing no deletions is not evidence that nothing will be deleted; and generalising the discipline — an artefact that reports what a tool could compute is not a report of what will happen — to agent-authored infrastructure at large.

*Unsafe.* **As a claim about any other platform's preview tooling.** The failure modes taught here are this operation's, published by this vendor, and a student who assumes another product's preview short-circuits the same way has done precisely what the day exists to prevent. It is equally unsafe to read the characterisation as a completeness claim: the day names the failure modes the documentation names, and **the documentation publishes no frequency for any of them**, so "characterised" here means the directions are known and the rates are not.

Step 3's requirement to state what the review does not cover *for this particular change* is the artefact element under which a student applying the simplification outside its safe range would fail, which is the form the scope rule's latest widening requires.

### 3.3 Engagement and motivational mechanism

The day runs on **consequence that is not arranged**.

Every prior high-stakes day in the programme has been an assessment: the stakes were institutional and the institution controlled them. Here the stakes are external. Users are on the system, a staff member is accountable, and the rollback exists because something might actually go wrong. Students report this as categorically different from an assessed defence, and the design relies on it.

Phase 3's adjudication is the second mechanism and is unusually strong: all four proposals were run on this system in previous years, and one caused a real incident. Judging break proposals whose actual outcomes are known, on the system you are about to break, concentrates attention in a way constructed cases do not.

**Phase 3's second finding supplies a third mechanism the previous design did not have, and it is the day's most durable moment.** The room reads four preview outputs, forms judgements, and is then told that the proposal that caused the incident had the cleanest output — not because the tool was wrong, but because the tool was silent, and silence and safety are indistinguishable on a screen. Students who have just used an artefact as evidence are the correct audience for the news that the artefact was blank, and the design's observation is that this lands harder than any account of agent unreliability, because **the villain is not the agent and not the reviewer but the evidence.**

The competence risk is asymmetric and worth stating. A student whose break produces nothing observable has not failed — the criterion held, which is a result — and a student whose break causes the rollback to be used has also not failed, because the rollback's use is explicitly not held against them. **A student whose review turns out to have been incomplete has also not failed, provided they said what it did not cover**; the specification is explicit that whether the output was complete is outside it. The design has arranged the day so that **the only failure mode is not observing**, which is what the specification's empty-section clause enforces.

The affective risk is the reverse of the module's earlier days: not despondency but exhilaration. Breaking production is exciting and excitement is not attention. Phase 6's placement immediately afterwards, before the report, is partly a countermeasure — it converts adrenaline into disclosure while the memory is undistorted by what the student intends to write.

## 4. Phase-by-phase design and its warrant

**Phase 1 (25 min).** Five items on the student's own component. Item 1's evidence for "it is working" is nearly always the absence of complaints. Item 3 asks for the unpredicted and is answered with speculation, which is the point — the speculation is compared at phase 8 against what occurred. **Item 5 is the sealed Copilot prediction** and is the module's hardest, because it asks a student to name a limit of an instrument they do not yet know is an instrument. Its expected hit rate is the module's lowest, and that is its function: it establishes, before phase 3 supplies the answer, that the cohort holds no model of what its review tool cannot see.

**Phase 2 (25 min).** The module's cumulative set with a synthesis item and a boundary item. The boundary item — name something about this component none of the four days would have told you — is the module bounding itself, matching S030's construction and now a recognisable convention.

**Phase 3 (40 min).** Four break proposals with real outcomes, **each expressed as a Bicep change and each previewed by the groups themselves.** Proposal D remains the design payload and the module's characteristic waste: everything specified, everything executed, and the claim under test was never promised. Its resolution requires the S034 contract, which is why it appears the day after. **The executed payload is separate and is the day's argument**: the proposal that caused the incident produced the cleanest preview, because its dangerous change was inside a module that short-circuited and was therefore omitted from the analysis entirely, on tooling old enough to emit no diagnostic. The two payloads are deliberately not merged — one is about what to test and the other is about what your evidence can show, and a student needs both.

**Phase 4 (40 min).** Eleven points, before the window. Point 1 is the day's principle. Point 5's before-observation is the omitted step. Point 6 justifies the external rollback rather than merely stating it. **Points 7 to 9 characterise the review instrument, and are placed immediately after point 6 because both concern the same idea in two registers — that the judgement you most want to make yourself is the one you are worst placed to make.** Point 8 is the day's sharpest default and is unusual among the module's defaults in that it governs not a behaviour but **whether you are told the truth about your own evidence**: diagnostics for an incomplete analysis are emitted only by recent tooling, and on earlier versions the same omission occurs silently. Point 10 specifies the report.

**Phase 5 (90 min).** The window. Seven steps, sequenced across students. The commitment and the veto at step 1 are what make it an experiment and a safe one respectively. **Step 3 is the quarter's thesis reduced to an artefact element: the review is submitted alongside the change, with its own limits stated for that change.** That is the only form in which an agent-authored infrastructure change can honestly be described as reviewed, and it is checkable — either the student says whether a module short-circuited or they do not. **Step 2's independence from the tooling is the countermeasure phase 3 earned**, and it is the operational form of point 9's conclusion that you should not rely on a single instrument for a claim you cannot afford to be wrong about.

**Phase 6 (45 min).** Extended, immediately after the window, **before the report is written**. This ordering is the day's most deliberate procedural decision: a disclosure made before the student has decided what to submit is a different artefact from one made afterwards, and the firewall's guarantee is what makes the difference exploitable. **The agent case is the quarter's terminal one and is chosen because every human in it behaved correctly** — the agent refactored competently, the team reviewed the diff, ran the preview and read the output, and the output was clean. An agent failure a careful person would have caught teaches carefulness, which students already believe in and cannot execute. This one teaches instrument characterisation, which is the programme's actual answer and is a procedure rather than a virtue.

**Phase 7 (25 min).** The unpredicted, compared across the cohort and grouped on the board. Individual surprises are anecdotes; a grouped set of things this system does that nobody models is a finding, and it goes to the substrate's documentation.

**Phase 8 (20 min).** Item 3 against reality, item 4's rollback prediction, item 5 opened, blast-radius ratios, and the S020 revisit. The S020 revisit is the programme's second long-horizon loop closing and is placed here because a student who has now disturbed the system has a basis for saying what their capacity model missed.

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**Students with more relevant prior knowledge.** A student with production experience will propose a competent bounded break. Predicted failure: their criterion will be an existing dashboard metric, chosen because it is available rather than because it captures what the contract promises — which is S026's construct failure at the moment it matters most. Countermeasure: step 1's review by the accountable staff member checks the criterion against the S034 contract, and a criterion that measures something the contract does not promise is proposal D and is declined. **A second predicted failure is new: an experienced student is the most likely to treat a clean preview as a clean bill of health**, because their working practice has rewarded exactly that, and step 3 requires them to say what it did not cover.

**Students with less relevant prior knowledge.** The window is intimidating and the sequencing means it happens in front of the room. Countermeasures: the criterion is committed and reviewed beforehand, so nothing is designed under pressure; the break is small; the rollback is not theirs; the agent does not apply the change, so no student is watching a tool act on production; and the staff veto means an unsafe proposal never reaches the window. Residual risk: a student may propose something so conservative that nothing is observable, which is not a specification failure and is a wasted window. The reviewer is briefed to push in the other direction.

**Phase 3 is too easy.** Proposals B and C are usually rejected. Proposal D is usually accepted, which is the phase's finding. Fallback if a group rejects D correctly: ask what proposal D *would* be worth testing against, which requires identifying a promise nobody made and deciding whether it should be made — and previews S039. **A second fallback covers the executed payload: if a group identifies the short-circuit unprompted from the preview output, ask them to say what a complete output would have looked like and how they would tell the two apart, which is genuinely hard and is the question point 9 answers with "you cannot, from the output alone."**

**The tooling reports differently on the day.** The diagnostics that reveal an incomplete analysis depend on the version of the command-line tooling in use, so the phase 3 finding's visibility is a property of the lab image. Countermeasure: **phase 3 is run deliberately on a version that does not emit the diagnostic**, and point 8 then names the version threshold at which it would have. This is a staged condition and the seminar page states it as one; presenting a silent failure and then revealing that a newer tool would have spoken is a better sequence than the reverse, and the design does not pretend the condition was accidental.

**Affective failure.** Three, and the first two are unusual for this programme.

The first is **exhilaration displacing observation**. Countermeasures: the before-state record at step 2, which is mechanical and must be complete before the break; and the clinic's placement immediately afterwards.

The second is **guilt where the rollback is used**. A student whose change affected users, however briefly, may take it hard, and the day has arranged for this to be nobody's fault — the staff member decided, the window was announced, the rollback worked. The countermeasure is that the accountable staff member states, in the room, that the rollback's use is a system working as designed. Whether that lands is not something the design can guarantee.

The third is new and is the practical turn's: **generalised distrust of tooling**. A student who learns that a preview can be silently incomplete may conclude that no automated check is worth running, which is the fatalism WP-012 identified in a new setting. Countermeasure: point 9 states the response explicitly and it is constructive rather than sceptical — do not rely on a single instrument for a claim you cannot afford to be wrong about — and step 2's independent before-state is what that response looks like in practice. The design's position is that a student who leaves with a second observation rather than with a worse opinion of their tools has learned the right thing, and the specification rewards the second observation and is silent about the opinion.

## 6. Retention architecture

Retrieved by S039 (requirements as negotiated constraint), S045 (a model you are willing to defend), S060 (shipping retrieval into production), S066 (the release gate as an evaluation artefact), S068 (designing for detectability) and S080 (blameless forensics on a real incident).

S080 at roughly four quarters is the principal target, and the relationship is complementary rather than repetitive: S035 is a disturbance you designed and S080 is one you did not. The design's claim is that a student who has predicted a blast radius and measured it can reason about an incident's blast radius, and one who has not cannot.

S066's release gate is the criterion-first principle applied to a change you want to make rather than one you want to test, and the correspondence is drawn there. **It gains a second connection from this day's platform content: a release gate that reports what it could evaluate is the same artefact as a preview that reports what it could compute**, and S066 is the correct place for that to be generalised.

**The frame S068, S080 and S086 require is the documentation and observability frame** — the committed criterion, the before-state, the estimated-and-measured blast radius, the five parts, and phase 7's grouped observations entering the substrate's documentation. The seminar page states that this frame is durable in full and is not weakened by the platform anchoring, because all three downstream days depend on it and none of them depends on `what-if`.

Retained content: the criterion-first principle, blast radius as estimated and measured, rollback as an external decision, the five-part report, and **the discipline of characterising a review instrument before relying on it**. Five items, no numbers.

Own schedule: next morning, day 3, then the module set in the inter-module gap.

## 7. Assessment and evidence generated

No entrustment judgement — the quarter's is at S040.

One specification item in SY201, the module's terminal artefact.

Per the [Assessment Architecture](/wiki/program/03-Assessment-Architecture.md)'s rule 1, the checked elements are: presence of a committed criterion observable before the break; presence of a before-state record taken independently of the tooling; **presence of the `what-if` output with a statement of what it does not cover for this change**; presence of an observation record; confirmation of repair; presence of both estimated and measured blast radius; a non-empty unpredicted section; and the **prompt record, intervention record and frontier statement**. **Nothing else is assessed.** The quality of the break, the size of the effect, whether the rollback was used, and **whether the review output turned out to have been complete** are all outside the specification — the last of these deliberately, because a student who reported a limitation that did not bite has done exactly the assessed work and must not be penalised for the tool having behaved.

**A report with an empty unpredicted section fails.** This is the day's sharpest clause.

Programme instrumentation: item 1's evidence-for-working responses are the cohort's absence-of-complaints baseline; the blast-radius estimate-to-measurement ratios are the cohort's consequence-calibration measure, carried to S064 and S070; **phase 7's grouped unpredicted observations enter the substrate's documentation** and are the day's only output the institution retains; the item 4 rollback prediction against actual use is a cohort risk-perception measure; **item 5's specific-blindness rate is this day's frontier datum and is expected to be the module's lowest**, read by next year's cohort through the [Durable and Perishable Register](/wiki/program/09-Durable-and-Perishable-Register.md) per instrumentation rule 9.

The frontier statements are a sample and the day says so, per instrumentation rule 3: one cohort, one substrate component, one tooling version chosen by staff to be silent, on breaks the accountable staff member approved.

Dispositions: **integrity**, in whether the reported blast radius is the measured one, which is checkable against the substrate's own records.

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: the mandatory unpredicted section will be satisfied with trivia.**

At its strongest: the specification fails an empty section, so no student will leave it empty. What appears instead will be whatever is easiest to write — a log line that looked odd, a graph that moved slightly, an observation with no bearing on the criterion. The clause guarantees content and cannot guarantee value, and the design's justification for it, that something is always unpredicted, is doing double duty: it is used both to argue that the section is always fillable and to argue that an empty section proves inattention. Those cannot both be safe.

The rebuttal is that they can, and the distinction is what phase 7 tests. A trivial unpredicted observation is one the student's partner cannot say anything about; a substantive one is one that other students recognise from their own breaks or can say would not have appeared in theirs. Grouping the cohort's observations on the board is what separates them, and it happens before the report is written.

The concession stands and is unrepaired. Phase 7's separation is not carried into the specification, which checks that the section is non-empty and nothing more, so a trivial entry passes. The available repair is to require the report's unpredicted section to state whether the observation appeared in any other student's break — which phase 7 has already established and which costs nothing to record. **It is still not in the design.** This is a case where a discrimination the day performs is discarded before assessment, which is a specific and avoidable waste, and it is now the second consecutive revision at which it has been recorded without action.

**Objection 2: the design has no account of what degradation the substrate's users have consented to.**

At its strongest: the day's safety argument rests on an announced window, a bounded blast radius and a staff veto. None of these is consent. The users of this service are, per the [Substrate and Infrastructure](/wiki/program/10-Substrate-and-Infrastructure.md) page, real people using a real service — and they are being told that a maintenance period is occurring, not that second-year students will deliberately break the component they depend on to find out what happens. The distinction matters, the programme's own professional-formation strand would insist on it in any other context, and the design has not addressed it anywhere.

This objection the design does not fully answer and it is the most serious concession in this paper. The substrate page's risk 4 names harm to users and mitigates it with a domain constraint and staff escalation; it does not address informed consent to deliberate degradation, which is a different question from harm.

What can be said is narrow. The degradation is bounded, announced and reversible, and the domain is chosen so that failure is inconvenient rather than harmful. That establishes proportionality and not consent.

What should exist and does not: a statement, in the service's own terms and visible to its users, that the system is operated as a teaching substrate and that its behaviour is deliberately disturbed during announced windows. That is a one-paragraph disclosure, it is obviously right, and its absence means the programme is running an experiment on people who have not been told they are part of one — while teaching a module whose fourth day is about what you owe the person on the other side of an interface. The self-refutation is exact and the design has no defence for it beyond not having thought about it, which is not a defence.

**The practical turn does not touch this objection and the design will not pretend otherwise.** Nothing about characterising a review instrument, restricting the agent from applying substrate changes, or improving the artefact makes the users of the substrate better informed. **The disclosure belongs on the [Substrate and Infrastructure](/wiki/program/10-Substrate-and-Infrastructure.md) page, which this revision does not edit**, and its absence is carried forward unchanged and unmitigated.

**Objection 3: characterising one review instrument does not produce a student who characterises the next one, and the day's evidence for its own central claim is a single well-chosen example.**

This is the objection that bites on the practical turn.

At its strongest: the day's argument is that the answer to agent-authored infrastructure is instrument characterisation. It demonstrates this on one tool, whose failure modes are unusually well documented, on a platform whose vendor happens to have written them down. **That is close to the easiest possible case and the day treats it as representative.** A student who leaves knowing that `what-if` short-circuits has learned a fact about `what-if`. The design claims they have learned a discipline, and the only evidence for the claim is that the day framed it as one. Worse, the day's most memorable content is a specific and perishable blind spot in a specific tool — exactly the shape of content the programme's own perishability register exists to demote — and the design has placed it at the emotional centre of the module's terminal day. **The programme has spent four days warning that the platform instance is a vehicle, and has then built its answer to the quarter's hardest question on top of one.**

The objection is conceded in part and the concession is the most uncomfortable in this paper after objection 2.

What is defensible: the discipline being taught is not new here, it is [S029](/wiki/seminars/S029.md)'s, and the day is its fourth application rather than its first — which is why point 7 is framed as a transfer demand in §2 rather than as instruction. Step 3 assesses the transfer and not the fact: a student must state what their review did not cover *for their change*, which requires applying the model and cannot be satisfied by reciting the failure modes. And the seminar page's perishability register puts the specific blind spots in the perishable column and the discipline in the durable one, which is the programme's standing structure and is applied here without exception.

What is conceded: **the day has no instrument that would detect a cohort that learned the fact and not the discipline**, and the obvious one is cheap. A phase 8 item asking students to name a *different* automated check they rely on and say what it cannot show would separate the two directly, would take three minutes, and would produce the programme's first datum on whether instrument characterisation transfers at all. **It is not in the design**, because phase 8 already carries item 3, item 4, item 5, the blast-radius distribution and the S020 revisit, and the design judges it full.

**That is the fourth omission attributed to phase compression in five days** — after [WP-032](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-032.md) §8 twice and [WP-033](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-033.md) §8 once — and at four instances it stops being a series of local judgements. **The finding is that M07's days are budgeted with no slack for the measurements their own whitepapers identify**, and that the design has responded each time by recording the omission rather than by removing something. What should be removed is a question for the module and not for this day; recording it here is the fourth and last time this revision will do so without acting.

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** A large majority of item 1 responses will cite the absence of complaints or an existing dashboard as evidence that the component works. *Instrument:* item 1 responses coded by evidence type.

**Prediction 2.** A majority of groups will accept proposal D. *Instrument:* phase 3 judgements per proposal.

**Prediction 3.** Blast-radius measurements will exceed estimates for a majority, and the ratio's spread will be wider than the S020 capacity-prediction spread. *Instrument:* estimate-to-measurement ratios, compared against the recorded S020 distribution.

**Prediction 4.** Unpredicted observations that recur across three or more students will be rated more substantive than singletons, blind-rated. *Instrument:* phase 7 grouping against blind substantiveness ratings. **This is the §8 objection-1 discrimination stated as a testable claim; if recurrence does not track substance, phase 7's grouping cannot serve as the filter the rebuttal claims.**

**Prediction 5.** No group will identify, from the phase 3 preview outputs alone, that one analysis is incomplete. *Instrument:* phase 3 judgements coded for any statement that an output may be missing resources. **If a group identifies it, the executed payload's force is lost for that cohort and the §5 fallback applies. A group that identifies it while using tooling that emits no diagnostic has done something the design believes is not possible, and that would be the more interesting result.**

**Prediction 6.** Fewer than a tenth of item 5 sealed predictions will name a specific blindness of the review operation, and the rate will not correlate with prior cloud experience. *Instrument:* item 5 responses coded for specific-versus-general, against the admission background survey. **A correlation with experience would indicate the blindness is known in industry and the day is teaching an unfamiliar rather than a novel fact, which would weaken §8 objection 3 by making the example less special than the objection assumes.**
