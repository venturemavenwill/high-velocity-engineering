## In plain terms

**What this paper argues, before the formal version.**

Your program does not run. It is scheduled — given the processor in slices, by a policy you did not choose, interleaved with work you cannot see. Correcting that misconception takes about a minute and is not what the day is for.

**The day is for the thing that follows it.** Scheduling is usually taught as a list of algorithms with a performance figure attached to each, which quietly implies that one of them is best. None of them is. The goals genuinely conflict: making the average wait short means starving the long jobs, guaranteeing fairness costs you throughput, favouring interactive work punishes batch work. You will build two schedules by hand in the first half hour and discover the conflict yourself rather than being told about it.

Once there is no best answer, the engineer's job changes shape. **You pick a goal, and picking a goal is deciding whose work is allowed to wait.** That decision is normally invisible because it is reported as an average, and an average can improve while a specific group's experience gets worse. So the day requires you to name the person — with a number and with who submitted the job — and then has your partner write that person's complaint in their own voice. Writing as somebody is harder to do dishonestly than naming them.

**What this paper concedes, and it is the one to hold on to.** The requirement to name a victim can be satisfied by naming a *convenient* victim — someone whose disadvantage is small, or already accepted, or easy to justify. The number will be accurate. The submitter will be real. And it will be the wrong person, because the real victim of a prioritisation decision is very often a class of work you never thought about, which is precisely why it is the real one. **The paper admits that a student who does this competently will pass**, that all three of the design's defences depend on somebody happening to notice rather than on any mechanism, and that the obvious fix — show the student every affected class from the measurement, after they have committed — is straightforward, named in this paper, and not built.

## Abstract

This paper defends teaching scheduling as an allocation decision with an identifiable victim rather than as a family of algorithms with performance characteristics, and defends the requirement that the victim be named with a magnitude and a submitter. The first argument is that scheduling objectives conflict pairwise and admit no dominant solution, so the competence being taught is not optimisation but the selection and defence of an objective — which is a class-2 decision and is indistinguishable in structure from the representation choice at S015. The second argument is that a policy's cost falls on a person, that aggregate metrics systematically conceal this, and that a programme certifying judgement about deployed systems must install the habit of asking whose work waits. The paper further defends the phase 7 requirement that a partner write the victim's complaint in the victim's own voice, as a stronger operation than naming, and defends running the day's four arrangements against real Foundry deployment types, where the deprioritised class is a product with a published turnaround and a published discount. It concedes that the day's professional content may be received as moralising bolted onto a systems topic, that its central instrument — a named victim — can be satisfied by identifying a convenient victim rather than the real one, and that the platform's most consequential victim is one the day cannot identify at all.

## 1. The instructional problem this day must solve

Students hold a model in which their program runs. It does not run; it is scheduled, in intervals determined by a policy they did not choose, interleaved with work they cannot see, sharing hardware state whose contents S016 has just shown them to be decisive.

Correcting this is easy and is not the day's difficulty. The difficulty is that scheduling is conventionally taught as a set of algorithms with associated metrics — first-come-first-served has this average waiting time, shortest-job-first has that one — which frames the subject as an optimisation with a best answer. It is not. The objectives conflict: minimising average waiting time starves long jobs, guaranteeing fairness costs throughput, prioritising interactive work degrades batch work. There is no schedule that is best on all, and phase 1 item 4 establishes this by construction within the first twenty-five minutes.

Once that is established, the day's actual content follows: the engineer selects an objective, and the selection is a decision about which work is allowed to wait. That is a class-2 decision of exactly the shape S015 introduced, and the module's second instance.

The day's second problem is the one that justifies its place in this programme. A deprioritised job belongs to somebody. In a deployed system with real users, a scheduling or prioritisation policy determines whose requests are slow, and aggregate metrics conceal this by construction — an average improves while a minority's experience degrades, which is S013's tail argument in a new setting and with a person attached.

Students do not naturally ask who is worse off. They optimise the metric they were given, which is what their training has rewarded for years. Step 4's requirement to name the victim with a magnitude and a submitter is the intervention, and phase 7's complaint-writing is what stops the naming from being nominal.

The third problem is technical continuity. S016's hierarchy is one day old and a context switch is, in the terms that day supplied, an operation that discards locality. Phase 2's final item requires students to make that connection themselves, and point 6 formalises it. This is the module's first internal transfer test.

## 3. Theoretical basis for the design

### 3.1 Load profile and element interactivity

**High, but lower than S016's and S019's.** The day sits in the module's load trough by design, between two peaks.

The intrinsic interactivity is moderate: a policy, a workload and an objective interact, but each can be varied while holding the others, which is exactly what phase 3 does. The genuine difficulty is that the *conclusion* depends on all three jointly, so no single-factor reasoning suffices — this is the reversal phase 3 produces.

Management:

- **Phase 1's workload is eight jobs,** small enough to schedule by hand, which makes the objective conflict a construction rather than an assertion.
- **The same substrate component and workload across the module.**
- **Four policies, two workloads,** which is the minimum that produces a reversal.
- **Step 1 worked; step 5's template supplied.** Fading around method, removed from choice.
- **Scheduler implementation is supplied.** Students configure and measure; they do not implement. Implementation is SY102's conventional work.

### 3.2 Desirable difficulties selected, and those deliberately not

**Selected.**

- *Scheduling by hand under two objectives,* phase 1, which produces the conflict rather than describing it.
- *The workload reversal,* phase 3, which invalidates a conclusion groups have just formed.
- *Naming the victim with a magnitude and a submitter,* step 4.
- *Writing the victim's complaint in their voice,* phase 7.
- *Stating the workload change that would invalidate the choice,* step 7 — the module's standing expiry discipline.

**Deliberately not selected.**

- *Implementing schedulers.* Refused; SY102's work.
- *Queueing analysis.* Visible in the measurements, deferred to S019, named as deferred.
- *Real-time scheduling and deadline guarantees.* A genuine and distinct topic; omitted.
- *Withholding the objective taxonomy.* Point 2 names the five conflicting quantities. Vocabulary.
- *Multi-core and parallelism.* Deliberately excluded — the day's illusion is about interleaving on one core, and adding true parallelism would obscure it. S031 takes it up.

**The platform instance, scoped in the strong form.** The day teaches deployment types as scheduling classes, and the simplification must carry its safe and unsafe conditions.

*Safe.* Deciding which class of work belongs in which path; predicting which class suffers when a mix changes; reasoning about why isolating bulk work protects interactive work; and defending an allocation split to the person whose work is on the wrong side of it. In all of these the class-and-victim structure is sufficient.

*Unsafe.* Reasoning about **what the service does inside a class**. The day teaches that a policy is chosen; on a managed deployment the policy inside the class is not yours, is not published, and cannot be inferred from the outside. A student who carries the taxonomy across the boundary will describe a first-come queue where the vendor documents only a target turnaround, and will make commitments the platform has not made. It is also unsafe for reasoning about **cost as a proxy for priority**: the batch path is cheaper and slower, and it is tempting to read price as a general ordering. It is not one; priority processing is documented as using the same quota as standard processing and costs more, so price, quota and latency are three axes and not one.

The boundary is stated to students at point 3 rather than left to this paper, and step 7's invalidating-change clause is where a student applying the taxonomy outside its range would fail.

### 3.3 Engagement and motivational mechanism

The day runs on **a conflict students construct with their own hands in the first half hour**.

Phase 1 gives eight jobs and asks for two schedules under two objectives. Both are constructible in ten minutes. The schedules differ, the victims differ, and item 4's question — is there a schedule best on both — is answered by the students' own work before anyone says anything. That is a stronger foundation than any exposition, and it makes point 2's taxonomy a formalisation of something already known rather than new material.

Phase 3's reversal supplies the second engagement. Groups form a conclusion on the first workload, commit it, and watch it fail on the second. This is the module's standard mechanism and by S017 it is familiar, which the design intends — the format should be transparent by now.

Phase 7 is the day's distinctive moment. Writing a paragraph in the voice of the person your policy disadvantages is uncomfortable in a specific and useful way, and students report it as the part of the day they remember. It is also the earliest instance of a move the programme uses heavily from M17 onward: representing an absent stakeholder concretely rather than abstractly.

The competence risk is low — the arithmetic is easy — and the affective risk is at phase 7, discussed in §5.

## 4. Phase-by-phase design and its warrant

**Phase 1 (25 min).** Two objectives, one small workload, hand construction. Item 3's identification of the worst-off job under each schedule is the day's move introduced before the day's content. Item 4's expected-yes is the misconception the phase corrects using the students' own artefacts. **Item 5 is the sealed Copilot prediction,** administered here and not later per instrumentation rule 8.

**Phase 2 (20 min).** The final item asks what a scheduler saves and restores and which S016 property makes it expensive — a one-day transfer test, answered by a majority, and the module's first evidence that S016 took.

**Phase 3 (45 min).** Four arrangements, one workload, measured adjudication, then a second workload that reverses the ranking — **executed against real deployment types rather than a simulated scheduler**. The second workload is the phase's payload and the reason the phase exists at forty-five minutes rather than twenty-five. The closing task — what property of the workload determined the ranking — is the abstraction, generated rather than supplied.

**Case D is demonstrated and not operated,** because priority processing is in preview and available by invitation and the programme holds neither. It is excluded from assessment, per the [Copilot Practice Standard](/wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md). Its exclusion is used rather than apologised for: the arrangement that would plausibly have won is one the room cannot obtain, which is a condition every practitioner meets and which no scheduling chapter contains. Students are told in explicit words which cases they ran and which they read, because a student who cannot draw that line will report both as experience.

**Phase 4 (50 min).** Eight points, with the professional content last. Point 8's placement is deliberate: stated first it would read as framing and be discounted, stated last it lands on an established technical base and is a consequence rather than an assertion. Point 5's rule — a policy recommendation without a workload characterisation is not a recommendation — is the day's retained sentence. **Point 7 names the default the platform chose:** one allocation control sets two limits, at a model-dependent ratio, so every allocation is two allocations and one of them was not considered.

**Phase 5 (90 min).** Seven steps at class 2, **shipping a two-class client** against the substrate's real workload. Step 4 carries the day. The *who submits it* clause is the specification's weighted element and is what distinguishes this from an abstract trade-off exercise; the outside-victim clause added at §8 is what stops the platform version being easier than the original.

**Phase 6 (40 min).** A prioritisation decision that improved aggregates and degraded a group's experience, then an agent failure. The attention to *how long it took to notice* is the phase's forward content, seeding S068's detectability material and S079's alerting material, and it is the third M04 disclosure to carry a monitoring dimension. **The agent case is unbounded concurrency**, chosen because it is correct against the instruction given, clean, tested, and starves everything sharing the allocation — so the review that would catch it is not a review of the code at all but a review of the code against an allocation, which nobody performed.

**Phase 7 (30 min).** The partner writes the victim's complaint. Writing in the affected person's voice is materially different from naming them: it requires imagining a specific experience, which resists the abstraction that makes trade-offs comfortable. The follow-up question — does your objective still look right — is genuinely open and some students change their step 2.

**Phase 8 (20 min).** The cohort's victims collected and shown, then the sealed predictions opened. Overlaps are the interesting result: when many students' policies disadvantage the same class of work, that class is structurally disadvantaged by the system rather than by any one choice, which is a systems observation students reach themselves.

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**Students with more relevant prior knowledge.** A student who has taken an operating systems course will know the four policies and their standard characteristics. Predicted failure: they answer phase 3's first workload from memory, are correct, and disengage before the reversal. Countermeasure: the second workload is chosen so that textbook expectations fail, and these students are asked to explain the discrepancy rather than the result. Their phase 7 assignment is the victim class with the least sympathetic profile, which is the harder complaint to write honestly.

**Students with less relevant prior knowledge.** The day is unusually accessible — phase 1 requires only arithmetic — so this is the module's most equitable day. Residual risk is at step 1's workload characterisation, where *shape rather than averages* is a demand students without statistical exposure find vague. Countermeasure: step 1 is worked with the room and the characterisation template asks for specific quantities rather than for a description.

**Phase 3 is too easy.** The first workload's ranking is predicted correctly by many groups, which is intended. Fallback if a group also predicts the reversal: ask them to construct a workload on which round-robin beats shortest-first on *average* completion time, which is possible under specific conditions and is hard.

**Affective failure.** Two, and they are the day's most likely failures.

The first is **the professional content read as moralising**. A student who came for a systems day and receives, at point 8 and phase 7, an argument about whose work waits may experience this as an ethics lecture attached to a technical topic, and will discount it. Countermeasures: point 8 arrives last and as a consequence; step 4 is a technical requirement with a magnitude, not a sentiment; and phase 7's complaint must be *specific and accurate*, which makes it an analysis rather than an expression of concern. Whether this is sufficient is the substance of §8's first objection.

The second is **discomfort in phase 7 producing evasion**. A student writing the complaint of a victim their own choice created may write it weakly, or ironically, or in a way that makes the victim unreasonable. Countermeasure: the complaint is written by the *partner*, not by the student who made the choice, which removes the incentive to weaken it. This is the phase's central design decision and it is the reason the roles are assigned that way.

## 7. Assessment and evidence generated

No entrustment evidence.

One specification item in SY102 requiring the full chain from workload characterisation to invalidating change, **plus the prompt record, the intervention record and the frontier statement**.

**A named victim without a magnitude and a submitter fails.** The clause exists because "batch jobs are worse off" is a description and "batch jobs submitted by the reporting team wait an additional forty minutes at the ninety-fifth percentile" is a finding, and only the second can be acted on or disputed.

**An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that the design could have detected an intervention-worthy error. A client that enforces and logs its own concurrency bound is the accepted demonstration, and it is deliberately the same artefact that would have prevented the phase 6 agent failure.

Priority processing is demonstrated rather than operated and is **not assessed**. Nothing in the specification depends on it.

Programme instrumentation: step 5's numeric predictions extend the calibration series; the phase 8 victim overlap is a structural finding about the substrate that staff act on independently of the teaching; the phase 7 complaint quality, blind-rated, is the cohort's first stakeholder-representation measure and is carried to S082; **item 5's sealed prediction accuracy extends the frontier series opened at S016**, whose reader is named at instrumentation rule 9 as the following year's cohort. The frontier statements are a sample of one cohort on one task with one model family, and the day says so per instrumentation rule 3.

Dispositions: **integrity**, at step 4, in whether the victim named is the real one or the convenient one.

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: the victim analysis is moral content wearing a technical costume, and students will correctly identify it as such.**

At its strongest: point 8 and phase 7 are not systems material. They are an ethical position — that engineers should consider who bears the cost of their decisions — inserted into a scheduling day because the programme wants it somewhere. Students are perceptive about this. A day that spends five hours on measurable trade-offs and then asks them to write a complaint in someone's voice will read as a bait-and-switch, and the technical credibility built over the first five hours will be spent rather than extended. Worse, the assessment requires it, which makes the ethical position a compliance item.

The rebuttal is that the distinction the objection draws does not survive contact with the material. The technical question — which objective should this policy optimise — has no technical answer. Point 2's five quantities conflict pairwise and nothing in systems theory adjudicates between them; the choice is made on grounds external to the system, and those grounds are about whose work matters. An account of scheduling that stops before the objective selection has stopped before the decision, and has taught students to optimise a metric supplied by someone else. That is precisely the competence this programme refuses to certify.

The concession is about reception rather than content. The design's protections — last placement, a required magnitude, an analytical rather than expressive complaint — are all aimed at making the material land as technical, and the design does not know whether they work. If students report the day as having a moral appended, the protections have failed and the failure is instructional rather than philosophical. Prediction 3 in §9 is the instrument, and it is a weak one because it relies on self-report about a topic students have an incentive to answer diplomatically.

**Objection 2: step 4's requirement can be satisfied by naming a convenient victim, and the design cannot tell the difference.**

At its strongest: a student who chooses a policy and must name its victim has an obvious move available — name a victim whose disadvantage is small, defensible or already accepted. The specification asks for a magnitude and a submitter, both of which the student supplies, and both of which can be accurate about the wrong victim. The real victim of a prioritisation decision is often a class of work the student did not consider at all, which is precisely why it is the real victim, and no requirement to name *a* victim will surface it.

This objection the design does not fully answer. The available countermeasures are partial. Phase 7 assigns the complaint to a partner, who may identify a different victim — and often does, which is the phase's most useful unplanned output. Phase 8's cohort collection shows overlaps, which surfaces structurally disadvantaged classes that individuals missed. And the dispositions observation names exactly this, which means an assessor who notices a convenient victim can record it.

None of these is a mechanism. All three depend on someone else noticing, and the student who names a convenient victim competently will pass. The structurally sound fix would be to supply, after step 4 is committed, a list of *all* work classes affected by the chosen policy, derived from the measurement rather than from the student — which would make the omission visible without requiring anyone to catch it. That instrumentation is technically straightforward, is not currently built, and would materially improve the day. It is recorded here as an unimplemented fix.

**Objection 3: the platform dissolves the day's moral argument by pricing it, and hides the only victim who did not consent.**

This is the objection that bites on the practical turn, and it does so from two directions at once.

At its strongest: the day's professional claim is that deprioritised work belongs to a person and that choosing a policy is a decision about them. The platform's deprioritised class is a **product**. It has a published turnaround target, a published discount, and a customer who chose it — so the victim consented, in writing, at half price. A student who moves bulk work into the batch path has not made a decision about somebody; they have bought a cheaper service on that somebody's behalf with that somebody's agreement. Phase 7's complaint, written in the voice of a workload class that opted in, is a complaint with no grievance, and students will notice. Far from making the day's argument concrete, the platform instance is the strongest available demonstration that the argument does not apply: **contention that has been priced has been settled.**

And the direction that genuinely bites: the one victim who did not consent is invisible. A standard deployment draws on a pool shared across customers, and when demand across that pool rises the service may temporarily reduce a deployment's effective rate limit. That is the real victim of a student's burst — unconsenting, unaware, and *structurally unidentifiable*. The student cannot name them, cannot measure their loss, cannot apologise, and will never be told. The day's entire instrument is *name the victim*, and the platform's most important victim cannot be named.

The objection is conceded in both halves. The first half is more than a reception problem: pricing genuinely does settle some allocation disputes, and a design that pretends otherwise is teaching a grievance where a contract exists.

**The cheap fix is one clause and it is made.** Step 4 now requires **two** victims, one inside the subscription and one outside it, and phase 7's complaint is written for the outside one. The inside victim is the priced, consenting, nameable case and it is where the technical analysis lives. The outside victim is the case where naming fails, and the required output is not a name but a statement of *what you would have to be told to know you had harmed them* — which, on this platform, is nothing. **Making unidentifiability the deliverable is a better lesson than the one the clause replaced**, because a student who has written a complaint on behalf of somebody they cannot identify has learned the actual shape of shared infrastructure rather than a stylised version of it.

What is conceded and not fixed is that the design cannot tell a thoughtful outside-victim statement from a fluent one, for the same reason the student cannot: there is no ground truth. Prediction 5 measures the rate at which students identify the shared pool at all, which is a weaker thing to measure than the quality of what they say about it, and it is the only thing available.

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** A majority will answer phase 1 item 4 affirmatively before constructing the schedules, and a large majority will answer negatively after. *Instrument:* item 4 responses, collected before and after the phase 1 construction.

**Prediction 2.** Groups will predict the first workload's ranking substantially better than the second's, and the gap will be larger for students with prior operating-systems coursework. *Instrument:* per-workload prediction accuracy against the admission background survey. **The interaction is the claim: prior instruction supplies expectations that the reversal is designed to defeat.**

**Prediction 3.** Fewer than a quarter of students will describe phase 7 as the least valuable phase of the day in the end-of-module review. *Instrument:* the module review's phase-value item. **A high proportion would indicate the §8 objection-1 reception failure.**

**Prediction 4.** In phase 7, partners will name a different victim than the student did in at least a third of pairs. *Instrument:* phase 7 victim identification compared against step 4. **This is the §8 objection-2 estimate; a low divergence rate would mean either that step 4 is accurate or that partners are anchoring, and the design cannot distinguish these.**

**Prediction 5.** Before point 8, fewer than a quarter of students will identify the shared-pool neighbour as a party affected by their choice; after it, a large majority will, and their outside-victim statements will nonetheless be rated less specific than their inside-victim statements. *Instrument:* step 4's two victim statements, blind-rated for specificity, against a phase 1 free-response probe on who is affected. **The gap is the §8 objection-3 estimate. If the two are rated equally specific, students are writing about the unidentifiable victim with a fluency the situation does not support, which is a worse result than the gap.**

## What a student should take from this paper

**What the design is claiming.** That scheduling has no best answer, and that choosing one objective over another is a decision about whose work is allowed to wait. Naming that person, with a magnitude and a submitter, is treated here as a technical requirement rather than as a sentiment.

**What the evidence licenses.** The learning mechanisms the day runs on — contrasting cases with a reversal, prediction before adjudication, perspective-taking as an operation distinct from identification — sit in **class 2**: direction and mechanism only, no magnitude. The deployment behaviour at phase 3 sits in **class 4**, read on a stated date, with three silences recorded: no distribution behind the batch turnaround target, no published policy inside a class, and no notification when a shared pool reduces your effective limit. **The claim that this arrangement installs the habit is class 3** — design reasoning with no external warrant. You may say the objectives conflict; you may not say the day is known to work.

**What to do with it.**

- **Write the victim as a finding, not a description.** A magnitude and a submitter can be disputed; "batch jobs are worse off" cannot.
- **Ask whether the victim you named is the real one or the convenient one.** Neither the specification nor an assessor who is not looking can tell the difference.
- **A control is not evidence.** A client that enforces its own concurrency bound is a configured control under the [Agent Configuration Standard](/wiki/program/13-Agent-Configuration-Standard.md); that nothing starved is an observed result only if you measured it.
- **Do not read price as priority.** Cost, quota and latency are three axes on this platform, and the documentation says so.

**The concession worth remembering.** The paper concedes that step 4 can be satisfied by naming a convenient victim, that a student who does so competently will pass, and that all three countermeasures depend on somebody noticing rather than on a mechanism. **Hold the design to that.** The fix — supplying every affected work class from the measurement once the student has committed — is named in this paper, is straightforward, and is not built.
