## In plain terms

A pre-mortem is a post-mortem run before the work, on a failure that has not happened.

The trick is grammatical and it is the whole device. Asked *what could go wrong*, people produce hedged possibilities, because forecasting is uncomfortable and hedging is the socially safe form of it. Told *it failed — why*, they produce accounts, because explaining a stated outcome is a different cognitive act and a much easier social one. **You are not asking anybody to predict a disaster. You are asking them to explain one that has already been supplied.**

The session's second device is that everybody writes before anybody speaks. A discussion-first pre-mortem produces the most confident person's list, with the room's names attached, and the causes that most needed saying are exactly the ones that do not survive being said aloud first.

Its third is that a cause is not finished until it has been routed. Most causes are settled by asking somebody, looking at something, or checking a constraint that was recorded as a belief rather than as a fact. **Routing everything to an investigation is how a pre-mortem becomes a quarter of work**, and it is the commonest way the device destroys its own value.

The last column is the uncomfortable one. Some causes will be accepted rather than acted on, and an accepted cause with no owner has not been accepted — it has been set down.

## Abstract

This paper defends running a structured failure analysis before a feasibility investigation rather than after a delivery, and locates its mechanism in the difference between explaining a stated outcome and forecasting an unstated one. The argument is that the causes which matter most are socially expensive to raise, that anonymous generation before discussion lowers that cost, and that a cause is only useful once it has been assigned to the cheapest instrument capable of settling it. The session installs a four-column record with an owner on every row including the accepted ones. The paper concedes that the practice corpus behind it records no failure of any kind and therefore cannot supply a case in which a pre-mortem's causes were checked against a real outcome, that the anonymous-generation claim has no external warrant, and that the exercise's own most likely failure is producing a list nobody routes.

## 1. The problem this session must solve

The causes of a failed engagement are usually available to somebody on the first day, and they do not get said.

The reasons are structural rather than personal. The cause that implicates the plan implicates the person who wrote it. The cause that implicates the sponsor is career-adjacent. The cause that implicates the team's own capability is the one nobody can raise without appearing to volunteer. And the cause that is merely embarrassing — we do not actually know how their system works — is suppressed by the same mechanism that makes people nod in a briefing.

**A risk register does not solve this, because a risk register is a list of things it is safe to say.** Its items are conditional, hedged and generic, and they are generic precisely because a specific risk names somebody.

The session's response is to change the grammar, remove attribution during generation, and require every cause to acquire either an instrument or an owner. **A list with neither is a document, not a control.**

## 2. Entry state and prior knowledge

**Entry state: either.** This is one of very few sessions in the stage a learner can enter from ordinary professional experience. Anybody who has been in a risk workshop has the raw material, and the device's mechanism does not depend on anything this programme has taught.

The two programme retrievals sharpen it rather than enable it. [S006](/wiki/seminars/S006.md) supplies constraints that were recorded as beliefs and marked as untested — the cheapest routing target in the exercise, because those rows are settled by asking one person. [S007](/wiki/seminars/S007.md) supplies the header: who was in the room, who was invited and did not come, and who was not invited. **A learner arriving without those substitutes their own team's equivalents and loses nothing structural.**

The prior the session works against is that a pre-mortem is a mood. Learners arrive expecting an exercise in pessimism and produce one unless the record's routing and ownership columns force the output to be actionable.

## 3. Theoretical basis

**3.1 Load profile.** Low to moderate, and deliberately so. The session is placed second in the module because [S031](/wiki/seminars/S031.md) is cognitively heavier and because the pre-mortem's output is what makes [S033](/wiki/seminars/S033.md)'s question set possible.

**3.2 Why the past tense.** Prospective hindsight — stating an outcome and asking for its causes — changes the task from prediction to explanation. Explanation draws on a different and more fluent process, and it removes the social cost of appearing to forecast disaster. **The direction is well attested; no magnitude is asserted here or anywhere in this wiki.**

**3.3 Why generation precedes discussion.** A group discussion converges early on the first plausible account offered, and the first account is disproportionately likely to come from the most senior or most confident person present. Independent generation followed by anonymous collection preserves the tail of the distribution, and the tail is where the useful causes are. **This is a mechanism argument with no measurement behind it in this repository.**

**3.4 Why the routing column exists.** A cause without an instrument is an anxiety. The routing discipline is [S020](/wiki/seminars/S020.md)'s, applied to the team's own plan rather than to the customer's problem, and the cheapest instrument is nearly always a question to a named person rather than an investigation.

**3.5 Why acceptance needs an owner and a reopening condition.** A risk accepted by a group is accepted by nobody, and an acceptance with no condition attached is indistinguishable from having forgotten about it. The two fields together are what make the acceptance auditable later, at [S063](/wiki/seminars/S063.md) and [S088](/wiki/seminars/S088.md).

## 4. Session design and its warrant

The seven movements run situation, artefact, standard, failure modes, work, review, limits.

**Opening item 1 is the device, administered before it is explained.** The learner is told the engagement failed and asked why. Item 3 then asks which of their own causes somebody would have been reluctant to say out loud, which is the session's actual subject arriving as a self-report rather than as a lecture.

**The instructor puts a cause implicating the design on the list first.** This is a re-norming move and it is the only countermeasure the session has for the suppression problem that does not depend on anonymity holding. The learning-design source behind this programme's ordering makes the same point about its own generation phase: the norms are a layer of the design rather than a preamble to it, and educators sharing their own failures is one of the named operations.

**Agent use is bounded to extension.** The learner produces five causes alone, then asks a model for causes they did not name, biased towards the non-technical, and records what was kept against what was discarded. **The discard rate is the point.** A model's list of project risks is long, fluent and mostly about any project, and the session is the first place in the programme where a learner is asked to quantify how much of an assistant's output was generic — a habit [S049](/wiki/seminars/S049.md) will need.

**The review movement targets the accepted rows only**, because they are the ones that will otherwise never be looked at again. A reader who cannot state the reopening condition for an accepted cause has found a row that was set down rather than accepted.

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**The risk register in fancy dress.** The likeliest failure. Causes come back hedged and generic. *Countermeasure:* the past-tense requirement, enforced in the record's own column heading, and the standard's warning sign — the words *may*, *could*, *potentially*.

**The polite pre-mortem.** The real cause is known and unsaid. *Countermeasure:* anonymous collection, plus the instructor's own uncomfortable cause going first. **Both are partial and the paper does not overstate them.** Anonymity in a small cohort is nominal, and a learner who fears attribution will still self-censor.

**The all-technical list.** *Countermeasure:* the standard's requirement that at least one cause be organisational, political or commercial, and the corpus's own observation that resistance originates in ownership of process rather than in technical incapacity.

**The ratified plan.** The exercise is run after the decision. *Countermeasure:* placement before [S033](/wiki/seminars/S033.md) and before any investigation, and the standard's requirement that the record changed something nameable.

**The unrouted list.** Forty causes, no instruments, filed. *Countermeasure:* the standard's warning sign — the record took longer to produce than the cheapest test on it would have taken to run — and the review movement, which is about outputs rather than about causes.

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: this is a ritual with an appealing story attached, and the story is doing all the work.**

At its strongest: nobody has shown that a pre-mortem improves any outcome in any setting resembling this one. The mechanism is plausible, the exercise is enjoyable, and the output is a list — and lists are the least effective control in engineering practice, which is why the discipline invented tests. A curriculum that spends a session on a facilitation device and then admits it has no evidence for it has taught a superstition with unusually good manners.

**Substantially conceded.** No source behind this session measures its effect, and the corpus that supplies the practice context records no failure at all, so it cannot supply a single case in which a pre-mortem's causes were later checked against an outcome. The rebuttal is narrow and is about cost rather than efficacy: the exercise is short, it is run before anything has been spent, and its output is required to be routed to instruments that are themselves cheap. **A cheap control with a plausible mechanism and no evidence is worth running; it is not worth believing in**, and the session's limits movement says exactly that. **The declined fix is to remove the session and fold the failure question into [S033](/wiki/seminars/S033.md)'s question set.** It is declined because the question set is written by somebody who already believes the design works, and the grammatical device is the only thing in the module that reliably produces a cause its author would not have volunteered.

**Objection 2: anonymity is theatre in a cohort of this size, and pretending otherwise teaches learners a false safety.**

At its strongest: in a room of people who know each other's projects and writing, an anonymous cause about the design is attributable within seconds. Telling learners that anonymous collection protects them is worse than not offering it, because they will rely on it in a workplace where the same reasoning fails and the consequences are real.

**Conceded without qualification, and the design's response is a substitution rather than a defence.** The session does not claim anonymity works; it claims that the instructor putting an uncomfortable cause first does more than anonymity does, and it makes that the primary mechanism with anonymity as a secondary. **The declined fix is to run the generation phase through an anonymous digital channel with genuine unlinkability.** It is declined because the mechanism the design actually wants is a norm that survives into a workplace, and a technical guarantee teaches learners to depend on infrastructure that will not be there. **That is design reasoning and nothing warrants it.**

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** Answers to opening item 1 will be markedly more specific than the same learners' answers to a conventionally worded *what could go wrong* prompt administered as a control. *Instrument:* the two sets of written causes, coded *specific* / *generic* by a second reader who does not know which prompt produced which.

**Prediction 2.** Most learners' pre-instruction lists will be entirely technical. *Instrument:* the five written causes, coded by domain.

**Prediction 3.** The cause a learner nominates at item 3 as hardest to say will more often be organisational than technical. *Instrument:* the item 3 nomination, coded by domain, against the same learner's item 2 list.

**Prediction 4.** A substantial share of causes will be routed to a spike where a single question to a named person would have settled them. *Instrument:* the routing column, with each row independently re-routed by a second reader and the disagreements counted. **If this fails, the module's claim that over-routing is the device's characteristic failure is unsupported**, and the session's emphasis should move to generation instead.

## What a learner should take from this paper

**What the design claims.** That stating the failure produces better causes than asking for risks, that the valuable cause is usually the suppressed one, and that a cause is not finished until it has an instrument or an owner.

**What the evidence licenses.** A device and its mechanism, plus one organisation's published decision to place it at the opening of a feasibility investigation. **Nothing about outcomes.**

**The concession worth remembering.** The corpus this stage draws on has never recorded a failure. Everything this session teaches about anticipating one is therefore reasoning, not experience, and the reasoning is cheap enough to be worth running anyway.
