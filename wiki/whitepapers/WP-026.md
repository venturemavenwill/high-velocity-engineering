# WP-026 — What Are You Actually Measuring?

**Seminar day:** [S026](wiki/seminars/S026.md) · **Module:** [M06](wiki/modules/M06.md) · **Quarter:** [Q3](wiki/quarters/Q3.md)
**Strands:** ME · SY · PE · **Complexity class:** 2
**Platform anchor:** Microsoft Foundry built-in evaluators (`relevance`, `coherence`, `groundedness`) · **MCP servers:** Microsoft Learn MCP Server · Azure MCP Server (read)

## Abstract

This paper defends opening the programme's measurement module with a fifteen-minute public correction of the preceding quarter's own practice, and defends organising the day around a fixed instrument and varying constructs rather than the conventional reverse. The first argument is that a day teaching construct validity which passed over the programme's own five instances of construct-free measurement would be self-refuting, and that the correction is also the strongest available demonstration that the programme's S001 commitment to instrumenting itself in public is load-bearing rather than rhetorical. The second argument is that holding the instrument fixed while varying the construct isolates the construct-instrument gap, which the conventional construction — one construct, several candidate instruments — obscures by making the construct look like the settled term. The paper additionally defends running that fixed instrument as a live Foundry evaluator rather than describing one, on the ground that an evaluator named after a construct and scoring something narrower is the clearest instance of the day's subject available anywhere, and that the divergence is more convincing when the students produce it. It defends testing a reporting sentence by attempted misuse rather than by comprehension. It concedes that phase 0 spends assessed teaching time on an institutional act whose instructional value it cannot evidence, that the day's central distinction may be unfalsifiable as students will apply it, and that a day teaching students to distrust unvalidated instruments now hands them one and asks them to trust its output for the length of an afternoon.

## 1. The instructional problem this day must solve

A measurement has two parts and students see one. There is the construct — the property you want to know about — and the instrument, which is the procedure you actually run. They are never identical, and the entire discipline of measurement lives in the distance between them.

The gap is not exotic. Response time is not perceived speed; a test pass rate is not correctness; an accuracy figure is not usefulness. In every case the instrument is real, the arithmetic is right, and the claim being made is about a construct that was not measured. There is no error to find. This is what makes the failure resistant to the verification habits the programme has spent two quarters building: S009's falsification discipline operates on claims about instruments, and a construct-instrument gap produces no false claim about the instrument at all.

The day's second problem is the design's own. Five whitepapers across Q2 recorded that the programme required students to measure before teaching them what a measurement is, and [WP-019](wiki/whitepapers/WP-019.md) §8 concluded that the accumulation was a curriculum-sequencing error rather than a series of defensible local compromises. A module on construct validity that ignored this would be teaching a discipline it had visibly failed to apply to itself, in front of students who took those measurements.

Phase 0 exists for that reason. It is fifteen minutes, it is not assessed, and it names three specific defects in how Q2's measurements were used. Its instructional content is close to nil; its institutional content is the whole point.

The third problem is what happens to a proxy that becomes a target. This is the day's most operationally consequential idea and it is the mechanism behind the evaluation failures at S047, S059 and S079. The mechanism is simple and worth stating precisely: the cheapest way to move an instrument is rarely the way that moves the construct, so once anyone is rewarded for the instrument, the cheap route is taken and the relationship that made the proxy useful is destroyed by the act of using it.

## 2. Entry state and prior knowledge assumptions

- **Five Q2 measurements taken without a stated construct,** which phase 0 revisits and phase 2 uses as an example.
- **The S020 capacity argument,** which contained an unexamined construct claim and which phase 2's bridge exposes.
- **S025's checkability requirement,** one day old, which point 6's reporting obligation extends.
- **S009's falsification discipline,** which the day must explicitly distinguish from construct validity, since neither catches the other's failures.
- **No measurement vocabulary.** Construct, instrument, proxy and validity are all new as terms, though the phenomena are familiar.
- **ME101 running concurrently** and supplying the formal treatment.
- **Copilot in agent mode and the Learn MCP Server since Q1; the Azure MCP Server in read configuration since Q2.** Students have shipped builds against a metered service. **No student has yet called an evaluation service, and the design assumes none can state what an evaluator returns.** The `score`, `label`, `threshold` and `reason` fields are new objects on this day.

## 3. Theoretical basis for the design

### 3.1 Load profile and element interactivity

**High, and of an unfamiliar kind.** The construct-instrument relation is not a computation and cannot be checked by procedure; judging whether an instrument measures a construct requires holding both, plus the conditions of use, plus the ways they could diverge. The interactivity is irreducible and the day's difficulty is that students have no prior structure to attach it to.

Management:

- **Phase 0 is not instruction and is not assessed,** so it imposes no load; it is deliberately placed before the pretest so that the day's cognitive work begins at phase 1.
- **The instrument is fixed throughout phase 3.** Five constructs, one instrument, which removes instrument comprehension from the comparison entirely.
- **Divergence evidence is supplied at phase 3,** so groups judge rather than investigate.
- **One question across all five module days.** Students meet it here and defend an answer at S030, so the question's context is paid for once.
- **Step 1 worked; step 4's construction modelled once.**
- **Phases shortened proportionally** to accommodate phase 0 without extending the day.

### 3.2 Desirable difficulties selected, and those deliberately not

**Selected.**

- *Item 3's gap identification,* where "none" is the expected and revealing answer.
- *The two opposite-direction constructs* in phase 3.
- *Constructing the divergence case,* step 4 — the day's discriminator.
- *Predicting the optimisation consequence,* step 6.
- *The partner's misuse construction,* phase 7.

**Deliberately not selected.**

- *Formal validity theory.* Content, criterion, convergent and discriminant validity are ME101's material. The day uses one distinction.
- *Investigating divergence unaided in phase 3.* Evidence supplied.
- *Reliability.* Deliberately absent; it is S027's and S048's material and conflating it with validity is a common error the day avoids by omission.
- *Instrument construction.* Deferred to S029.

**Scope, per the [Whitepaper Standard](wiki/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md).** Point 2's four gap mechanisms are a selection. They omit instruments that alter the construct by being applied, and constructs that are not single quantities and therefore admit no single instrument. Both are named in the seminar page and deferred to S029 and S046 respectively. The selection criterion is that these four generate the failures students actually produce at this stage; the omitted two require either a systems-level account of measurement effects or a treatment of multi-dimensional constructs, neither of which the day can carry.

**The platform instance, scoped in the strong form.** The day teaches a Foundry evaluator as the instance of a construct-instrument gap, and students will carry that reading into work this day does not supervise. The boundary must therefore be stated.

*Safe.* Reasoning about what an evaluator's output does and does not support. Concluding that a `relevance` pass says nothing about truth; that a threshold is a policy decision separable from the score it binarises; that an ordinal judgement collapsed to a binary has discarded information the collapse cannot restore; that the conditions of use stated in an evaluator's documentation bound its readings; and that a score whose agreement with human judgement is unpublished must be reported as uncalibrated. Every one of those is derivable from the documentation the students read on the day and holds for any judged score from any vendor.

*Unsafe.* Three readings. **First, concluding that the evaluators are bad.** Nothing on the day establishes that. They are uncalibrated, which is a statement about what is published, not about what is true — and an uncalibrated instrument may be excellent. A student who leaves believing Foundry's evaluators are inaccurate has asserted exactly the kind of unwarranted claim the day exists to prevent, in the opposite direction. **Second, generalising the specific gaps to other evaluators by analogy.** That `relevance` does not score truth is a fact about `relevance`'s documented definition, looked up, not derived; the next evaluator's gap must be looked up too. **Third, and most consequential, treating the day's four executed cases as evidence about how often the gap occurs.** They were selected by staff because they diverge. The day establishes that the gap exists and is reachable, and establishes nothing whatever about its base rate.

The boundary is stated to students at point 9 rather than left to this paper, per the widening recorded on the [Whitepaper Standard](wiki/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md), and step 7's requirement to name the threshold and say whether the student chose it is where a student applying the simplification outside its range would fail.

### 3.3 Engagement and motivational mechanism

The day runs on **the programme admitting a fault in front of the people it affected**.

Phase 0's effect is not instructional and is not primarily motivational in the ordinary sense. It is evidential: students have been told since S001 that this programme instruments itself and reports what it finds. Phase 0 is the first occasion on which the programme reports a finding against itself that it was under no external pressure to report, about work these students did. The design's expectation is that this materially affects how the module's later claims are received, and it cannot demonstrate that.

The day's ordinary engagement mechanism is phase 3's opposite-direction constructs. An instrument that moves *backwards* relative to a construct it is routinely used for is more arresting than one that is merely uninformative, and it produces the correct affective response — not that measurement is unreliable, but that this particular pairing is wrong.

Item 3's near-universal "none" supplies the opening finding in the students' own handwriting, which by week twenty-six is a familiar construction and is used deliberately for that reason: the format should cost nothing.

The competence risk is that the day's content sounds like a warning rather than a technique. Point 6's four-part reporting obligation and step 7's sentence are the constructive output, and the design places them late so that the day ends on something producible.

## 4. Phase-by-phase design and its warrant

**Phase 0 (15 min).** Three named defects in the programme's own prior practice. Unassessed, non-instructional, placed before the pretest. Its structural function is to make the module's authority conditional on the programme's own compliance, which is the only basis on which a construct-validity module can honestly claim authority.

**Phase 1 (25 min).** A real question from a real person, the student's proposed measurement, the asker's own words, and the gap. Item 2's requirement to state the construct *in the asker's words* is the load-bearing constraint — a student who paraphrases into engineering terms has already closed the gap by fiat. **Item 5 is the sealed Copilot prediction**, administered here and not later, per instrumentation rule 8. Its wording is deliberately the wording a real requester would use — *evaluate whether our assistant's answers are good* — because the day's claim is that this sentence contains a construct nobody will state and the agent will not ask for.

**Phase 2 (20 min).** The bridge asks which part of the S020 capacity argument was a construct claim and whether anyone checked it. The answer is that the latency target was a construct claim, nobody checked it, and the assessors did not ask. Using the students' own assessed work as the day's first example is deliberate and uncomfortable.

**Phase 3 (40 min).** One instrument, five constructs, **four response sets executed against a live evaluator**. The inversion of the conventional construction is the phase's principal design decision: varying instruments against a fixed construct trains instrument selection and leaves the construct looking settled, which is exactly the misconception the day targets. The change from supplied divergence evidence to produced divergence evidence is the practical turn's contribution and it is not cosmetic — a group that has watched a correct refusal score below a fabrication has an experience, and a group that has read that it would is being told. Case D is the payload and its selection is defended in §8. The closing task — what would it take to measure the construct properly, and why does nobody — is where the gap's persistence is explained, and the honest answer on this platform is that measuring truth requires ground truth and ground truth requires people.

**Phase 4 (45 min).** Ten points. Point 2 carries the scope statement. Point 3 rescues proxies from the day's own argument, which matters: a day that left students believing proxies are illegitimate would produce paralysis. Point 4 is the operational core. **Point 7 names five defaults the platform chose** and is the [Copilot Practice Standard](wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md) requirement; the automatic addition of a tool-accuracy evaluator when tool definitions appear is the sharpest of them, because it is a case where the *shape of the input* silently changes what is measured. **Point 8 is the module's central real-world instance** and is reachable from case D's artefact rather than asserted, per instrumentation rule 7. Point 9 separates durable claim from platform instance and delivers the §3.2 boundary to students. Point 10 names the four downstream instances in one sentence and does not elaborate.

**Phase 5 (85 min).** Seven steps at class 2 on the module's question, **now shipping a working evaluation rather than proposing one**. Step 1's two-form construct statement is the day's cleanest single exercise. Step 4 is the discriminator and step 5's execution is what converts it from an assertion into a finding — the design's expectation is that a substantial minority of divergence cases will not survive being run, because they were constructed against what the student assumed the evaluator scores. Step 6 makes point 4 personal. Step 7's requirement to name the threshold *and say whether you chose it* is the artefact element that binds the §3.2 boundary.

**Phase 6 (35 min).** A metric accurate for a year and used for a decision it could not support, then an agent failure. The attention to *when the distinction was last written down* is the phase's content: construct-instrument gaps open by attrition — the original author knew, the knowledge was not recorded, and successive users inherited the number without the caveat. The agent case is a `groundedness` evaluation whose `context` field was mapped to the assistant's own prior turn, and it is chosen because **every element of it is correct except the one nobody was checking**: the code ran, the service was real, the evaluator was the right one, the number was plausible, and the field mapping made the measurement circular. Its lesson is about review rather than about the agent — a reviewer with no construct statement in front of them is reduced to checking that the words match, and the words matched.

**Phase 7 (30 min).** Misuse construction. Harder than S024's comprehension test and more appropriate here, because a reporting sentence's failure mode is not being misunderstood but being correctly understood and over-applied.

**Phase 8 (20 min).** The "none" rate, the actual gaps, and the sealed Copilot predictions opened in two bands. The commitment names a number the student currently relies on whose construct they have never stated, which nearly everyone has.

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**Students with more relevant prior knowledge.** A student with research-methods or psychometrics exposure will hold the distinction already. Predicted failure: they use the vocabulary fluently and produce a step 4 divergence case that is theoretical rather than substrate-reachable, because the vocabulary permits abstract discussion and their training rewarded it. Countermeasure: step 5 requires substrate reachability and phase 7's partner constructs a *decision*, not a scenario, which resists abstraction.

**Students with less relevant prior knowledge.** The distinction is genuinely difficult to hold and has no procedural check. Countermeasures: the fixed-instrument phase 3; the two-form construct statement; supplied divergence evidence; one question across the module. Residual risk: step 4's construction may be beyond them, and the fallback is to select a divergence case from phase 3's supplied evidence and adapt it, which is weaker and passes.

**Phase 3 is too easy.** Groups usually identify the one construct the instrument measures. The two opposite-direction constructs are where the phase bites, and groups typically classify them as merely uninformative rather than as inverted. Fallback if a group gets all five: ask them to construct a sixth construct for which the instrument would be a *good* measure, which requires reasoning from the instrument's mechanism and is hard.

**Affective failure.** The characteristic risk is **generalised suspicion of measurement**, which is the module's version of the fatalism this design has now met four times. A student who concludes that all instruments are invalid has learned the opposite of the intended lesson and will be worse at measurement, not better. Countermeasures: point 3's rehabilitation of proxies; the day's constructive output at point 6 and step 7; and phase 3's closing task, which explains why imperfect instruments persist for good reasons. The design's position is that this risk is highest at S026 and declines across the module as the constructive apparatus accumulates, and that S029 is where it should resolve.

## 6. Retention architecture

Retrieved by S027 (error structure, where the instrument's own variability is decomposed), S029 (instrument design, where the day's diagnosis becomes a construction), S046 (where do criteria come from — the same question with an evaluation rubric as the instrument), S047 (criteria drift and validating the validator), S059 (the benchmark trap) and S067 (offline measures, online reality).

S046 at roughly two quarters is the principal target and the correspondence is exact: an evaluation rubric is an instrument, the quality it purports to measure is a construct, and the gap between them is the whole of S046 and S047.

Point 4's optimisation mechanism is the day's most widely retrieved idea — it recurs at S047, S054, S059 and S079 — and is the one the design most wants to survive.

Retained content: the construct-instrument distinction, the four gap mechanisms, the proxy discipline, the optimisation mechanism, and the four-part reporting obligation. Five items, which is high and is justified by the module's dependence on all of them.

Own schedule: next morning, day 3, week 2, week 6.

## 7. Assessment and evidence generated

No entrustment judgement — the module's is at S030.

One specification item in ME101, now carrying an executed run and the three [Copilot Practice Standard](wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md) elements. **An artefact in which the construct and the instrument are the same sentence fails.** This is the module's structural test and it is checkable without judgement: either two distinct statements appear or they do not.

**An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that the design could have detected an intervention-worthy error. The escape clause is the sensitivity discipline [S052](wiki/seminars/S052.md) established and [WP-048](wiki/whitepapers/WP-048.md) §8 corrected, and it is load-bearing here: a student whose harness asserts that the reported threshold matches the threshold actually applied has built the detection the record would otherwise have to evidence.

Programme instrumentation: the phase 1 item 3 "none" rate is the cohort's construct-awareness baseline, re-probed at S046; step 6's optimisation predictions are collected across cohorts and compared against what actually happens to substrate metrics that become targets; the phase 7 misuse-construction success rate is the reporting-sentence quality measure, carried to S050; **item 5's sealed prediction accuracy is this module's first frontier-calibration datum and is read by the following year's cohort through the [Durable and Perishable Register](wiki/program/09-Durable-and-Perishable-Register.md)**, per instrumentation rule 9.

The frontier statements are a sample and the day says so, per instrumentation rule 3: one cohort, one substrate, one evaluator family, on a task chosen by staff because a construct gap was known to be available in it. A frontier map assembled from them describes this task and not the agent.

Dispositions: **humility**, at step 3, where the incentive is to claim identity between instrument and construct rather than to characterise a proxy relationship.

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: phase 0 spends teaching time on an institutional gesture whose value cannot be shown.**

At its strongest: fifteen minutes of a six-hour day is given to an apology. It teaches nothing — the three defects it names are all taught properly in the following five hours — and it is unassessed, so no student is accountable for attending to it. Its claimed value is that it makes the module's authority credible, which is unmeasurable and is exactly the kind of claim the day itself would reject: "students receive the module's claims differently" is a construct, and no instrument for it is proposed. The design is doing, in its own defence of phase 0, the thing the day exists to prevent.

The rebuttal is that the objection's final move is the strongest argument *for* phase 0 rather than against it. The design is asserting an unmeasured construct, and it says so here rather than concealing it — which is the reporting obligation at point 6 applied to the design's own claim. A programme that could only justify practices it had instrumented would be a narrower programme than this one intends to be.

The concession is real nonetheless and has two parts. Fifteen minutes is a genuine cost and the design has no evidence for the return. And phase 0's placement before the pretest, while defensible on load grounds, means it is the least-attended fifteen minutes available — students are arriving, and an unassessed institutional statement at the start of a day is the classic position for content that is not received. If the design believes phase 0 matters, placing it at phase 8, after the day has established why it matters, would almost certainly be more effective. That change is not made and the reason is that phase 8 is already the day's most crowded phase, which is a scheduling reason rather than a pedagogical one.

**Objection 2: the construct-instrument distinction as students will apply it is unfalsifiable.**

At its strongest: any instrument can be said to differ from any construct, because no instrument is the thing itself. A student who has learned the distinction can produce a plausible gap statement for every measurement they encounter, and nothing in the day tells them when a gap is small enough not to matter. The result is a rhetorical move that is always available and never wrong — which is precisely the shape of an unfalsifiable claim, and the programme has spent M05 teaching students to distrust exactly this.

This objection the design does not fully answer. Step 4's divergence-case requirement is the intended discipline: a gap you cannot exhibit with a concrete case is a gap you have asserted rather than found, and step 5's reachability requirement narrows it further. Those two steps do real work and they bound the *identification* of a gap.

What they do not supply is any account of **magnitude**. A student who exhibits a reachable divergence case has established that the gap is real and has established nothing about whether it matters, and the day offers no vocabulary for the second question. This is a substantive omission and it is the day's most serious one, because the professional question is almost never whether a proxy is imperfect — it always is — but whether it is imperfect enough to change a decision.

The apparatus for magnitude is exactly what S027 and S028 supply, which suggests the correct repair is a fifth step at S028 requiring the student to return to their S026 divergence case and bound its size. That would close the gap within the module and is not currently in either day's design. Until it is, S026 teaches an identification move with no stopping rule, and students who over-apply it are doing what the day taught.

**Objection 3: the day hands students an unvalidated instrument and requires them to trust it for an afternoon, which is the failure it exists to prevent.**

This is the objection that bites on the practical turn and it is the sharpest of the three.

At its strongest: phase 3's entire construction depends on the evaluator's readings being *real* — case D is the payload because the correct refusal genuinely scores worst. But the evaluator producing those scores is a language model prompted to grade, its agreement with human judgement is published nowhere, and the day itself says so at point 8. So the day's central demonstration rests on readings from an instrument the day declares uncalibrated. If the evaluator's score on case D is wrong — if a competent human would have scored the refusal highly and the judge simply erred — then the students have watched an instrument malfunction and been taught that they watched a construct gap. **The day cannot distinguish those two situations, and it teaches students that distinguishing them is the whole discipline.** Worse, the rhetorical force runs the dangerous way: a live service is more persuasive than a handout, so the students will believe the demonstration *more* precisely where the design can warrant it least.

The objection is conceded. Two mitigations exist and both are partial. The gap in case D is derivable from documentation rather than from the reading — `relevance` is defined against the query, an abstention addresses the query only in a sense the definition does not cover, and the platform's own later `Quality Grader` adds abstention as a separate dimension, which is a vendor concession that relevance did not capture it. A student can therefore reach case D's conclusion from the definitions without trusting the score at all. And case B's result is over-determined: a fabrication that is fluent and on-topic passes `relevance` by construction, whatever the judge's reliability, because there is no path from the evaluator's inputs to the fact that the content is false.

Neither mitigation covers case C, whose whole content is a reading, and neither addresses the general point that the day's persuasive force exceeds its evidential force.

**The cheap fix is one artefact element and it is made.** Phase 3's closing task now requires each group to state, for the one case they found most convincing, **whether their conclusion survives the assumption that the evaluator's score on that case was simply wrong.** For case B it does; for case D it does, via the definitions; for case C it does not, and the group must say so. This costs five minutes, it converts the objection into the day's exercise, and it is the only construction available in which a student learns to separate an instrument's reading from an instrument's warrant while holding a reading in their hand.

What remains unfixed is recorded rather than repaired. The design has no way to check whether a group performing that separation has understood it or has learned the phrase, because the separation is now demonstrated at phase 3 before it is required anywhere — instrumentation rule 8's problem in a new place. The repair would be to elicit it at phase 7 from the partner instead. It is not made, because phase 7 already carries the misuse-construction demand and the design is not confident it can carry two.

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** A large majority will answer "none" or equivalent at phase 1 item 3, and the rate will not differ materially by prior research-methods exposure. *Instrument:* item 3 responses coded, split by the admission background survey.

**Prediction 2.** In phase 3, groups will classify the two inverted constructs as uninformative rather than inverted at a higher rate than they misclassify any of the other three. *Instrument:* per-construct classification with error direction.

**Prediction 3.** Step 4 divergence cases will fail step 5's reachability test for a substantial minority, and failure will correlate with prior research-methods exposure rather than against it. *Instrument:* step 5 outcomes split by background. **This is the abstraction failure from §5 stated as a prediction.**

**Prediction 4.** At S046, students will state a construct separately from an instrument without prompting at a higher rate than at S026, and the improvement will be larger for students whose S026 step 4 case was substrate-reachable. *Instrument:* an S046 protocol coded for unprompted separation, conditioned on S026 step 5 outcomes.

**Prediction 5.** A large majority of item 5 sealed predictions will state that the agent will get something wrong, and a small minority will name the omission of a construct statement specifically; the most common named error will instead be a coding or configuration error, which will not occur. *Instrument:* item 5 responses coded into three categories — generic error, code-level error, construct-level error — against what each student's agent actually produced. **The design's claim is that students predict agent failure in the register they have been taught to review in, which is correctness of code, and that the frontier is somewhere else entirely. If the construct-level rate is high, the cohort's frontier model is better than the design assumes and phase 6's agent case is redundant; if the generic rate is also low, students are not yet predicting agent failure at all and the sealed item is measuring nothing.**

**Prediction 6.** A substantial minority of step 4 divergence cases will not reproduce when executed at step 5, and the failure will be concentrated in cases asserting that the evaluator measures something it in fact does not measure at all — that is, students will over-predict divergence rather than under-predict it. *Instrument:* step 5 execution outcomes coded for direction of error. **This is the objection-2 over-application failure appearing in a form the day can finally observe, and it is the practical turn's contribution to a question the paper record has carried unanswered since S026 was first written.**

## Evidence status

**Verified in this repository.** Construct validity, the proxy-optimisation mechanism, and the reporting obligations are grounded in [research/03-measurement-evaluation/singh-leaderboard-illusion.md](research/03-measurement-evaluation/singh-leaderboard-illusion.md) and [research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md](research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md). The metric-attrition failure at phase 6 follows [research/05-fde-craft/](research/05-fde-craft/collected-materials.md). The four biases of language-model judges — position, verbosity, self-enhancement and limited reasoning — are recorded in the measurement folder's note on Zheng et al., at abstract-verification depth, and are cited here for **direction and mechanism only**; no agreement figure from that source is asserted. The claim that vendor documentation states latencies and sampling bounds and no accuracies is [research/06-microsoft-platform/](research/06-microsoft-platform/collected-materials.md)'s own cross-source finding. The Q2 sequencing defects named at phase 0 are recorded in [WP-011](wiki/whitepapers/WP-011.md), [WP-012](wiki/whitepapers/WP-012.md), [WP-016](wiki/whitepapers/WP-016.md), [WP-018](wiki/whitepapers/WP-018.md) and [WP-019](wiki/whitepapers/WP-019.md).

**Grounded in vendor documentation, with its version and its silence recorded.** The evaluator behaviour used at phase 3 and points 5, 7 and 8 is taken from Microsoft Learn's built-in evaluators reference, general purpose evaluators page, RAG evaluators page and cloud evaluation how-to for Microsoft Foundry, all read 2026-07-26. Specifically: an evaluation result carries `label`, `score`, `threshold`, `reason` and optional `details`; the label is binary and is described as "similar to a unit test's output"; quality evaluators score on a five-point scale, content-safety evaluators on a seven-point scale and textual-similarity evaluators as floats between 0 and 1, and **any nonbinary score is binarised to the label at a default threshold the user may override**; the default pass threshold for general-purpose and RAG evaluators is **3**, with scores at or above it passing; `relevance` is defined against the query and `groundedness` against a supplied context; `Quality Grader` scores relevance, abstention and answer completeness as separate dimensions; the evaluation level defaults to `turn`; and the Agent Framework's Foundry evaluator set defaults to relevance, coherence and task adherence and **adds tool call accuracy automatically when items contain tool definitions**. The custom-evaluator page supplies the requirement that a code-based evaluator name a model deployment it never calls.

**Three silences are recorded and two are taught.** The general-purpose evaluators page states that **scoring reliability "might vary for very short responses (under approximately 20 tokens)"** and does not quantify the variation or say in which direction it runs — the caveat is the whole of what is published, and it is the silence phase 3's case C exposes. The same page states that both evaluators "currently support English-language responses" and does not say what happens to responses in other languages, which is a documented boundary with an undocumented failure mode. And, most consequentially, **no page consulted states any figure for how well any evaluator agrees with human judgement**, for any evaluator, in any category. Microsoft's own AI playbook page on metrics for evaluating language-model output states that evaluation by language model "is an emerging area of research and has not yet been systematically studied" and lists positional, verbosity and self-enhancement bias among identified reliability problems — which is the vendor recording the absence rather than filling it. Alignment machinery for judged evaluators exists elsewhere in the same documentation estate, for Azure Databricks MLflow judges, and **not for Foundry's built-in evaluators**. Point 8 names this silence to students, and no claim about any evaluator's agreement with human judgement is made anywhere in this paper. The pages are dated by content rather than by version number, several of the agent and rubric evaluators are marked preview, and the day's numbers are marked perishable at point 9 for that reason.

**Cited from general knowledge, not verified here.** Contrasting cases with a fixed comparison term, the pretesting effect, retrieval practice at expanding intervals, cognitive load and irreducible relational interactivity, worked-example fading, and error management training are cited for **direction and mechanism only**.
