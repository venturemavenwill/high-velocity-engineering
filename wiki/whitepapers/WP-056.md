# WP-056 — The Lexical Baseline You Must Beat

**Seminar day:** [S056](wiki/seminars/S056.md) · **Module:** [M12](wiki/modules/M12.md) · **Quarter:** [Q6](wiki/quarters/Q6.md)
**Strands:** IS · ME · **Complexity class:** 4
**Platform anchor:** Azure AI Search full-text keyword retrieval — BM25 scoring, analysers, the shard · **MCP servers:** Azure MCP Server (Search) · Microsoft Learn MCP Server

## Abstract

This paper defends opening a retrieval module with the method the cohort believes is obsolete, and defends spending the day's second half on relevance judgement rather than on ranking. Its central argument is that which retrieval method wins is determined by the query population, the corpus and the relevance criterion before it is determined by the method, and that the only way a cohort learns this is to watch one ranker pair produce four different winners on four query sets over a fixed corpus. It defends making the unfiltered query log the phase-3 payload on the ground that nobody in the room has read one. It defends requiring a written relevance criterion and then measuring how far two people diverge applying it, because that divergence is the ceiling on every subsequent measurement in the module. **It defends running the day against a real search service, and shipping a real index, on the ground that a baseline the students did not build is a baseline they will treat as a rhetorical device.** The paper reports discharge of the two obligations S051 and S054 placed on this day under instrumentation rule 6. It concedes that the phase-3 payload depends on a contingent property of the substrate's own traffic; concedes without qualification that the ceiling it teaches is estimated from a sample its own S049 material would reject; and concedes that the day's platform apparatus is doing more instructional work than its perishability register admits.

## 1. The instructional problem this day must solve

The problem is not that lexical retrieval is unfamiliar. It is that it is *filed*.

Students arrive with the method already placed on a timeline: term matching was what people did, dense retrieval is what people do, and the interesting work is downstream of that settlement. Nothing in their reading contradicts it, because the published comparisons that produced the settlement are real and were correctly run. The belief is not a misconception in the ordinary sense — it is a correct summary of a body of evidence, generalised past the conditions that produced it.

That makes it harder to displace than an error. An error is defeated by a counterexample; a correct generalisation applied out of scope is defeated only by making the scope visible. This is why the day's structure varies the query population rather than the method: a student shown one query set on which the baseline wins concludes the set was chosen to make it win, and they are right. A student shown four sets, one of which is the traffic their own system receives, has to account for the ordering changing.

The second problem is that the entire module's evidence rests on a quantity nobody has looked at. Every comparison in [M12](wiki/modules/M12.md) is against relevance judgements. Those judgements come from people applying a criterion, the criterion is usually unwritten, and where it is written two competent people still disagree at a rate that is not small. **A cohort that has not measured that rate on their own judgements will spend the rest of the module reporting differences smaller than their own noise**, which is [S049](wiki/seminars/S049.md)'s failure with the annotator as the cluster and is the specific error this module is most exposed to.

The third problem is affective and belongs to the module rather than the day: the baseline must be treated with respect, or the day teaches contrarianism. It is a cheap, inspectable, strong system, and the reason to know that is not that new methods are overrated but that **you cannot report an improvement without a number to improve on.**

## 3. Theoretical basis for the design

### 3.1 Load profile and element interactivity

**High, and the module's heaviest day for its position.** The design accepts that deliberately: the relevance criterion written at step 2 is used by every subsequent day in the module, so the cost is paid once rather than five times.

The interactivity is between steps 1, 2 and 3. The information need determines the criterion; the criterion determines what counts as a relevant result; the query sample determines whether the criterion is ever exercised on a hard case. A student who writes a criterion for one need and samples queries expressing another produces an evaluation in which nothing is measured, and the failure is invisible in the artefact.

Management:

- **The corpus, both rankers, the four query sets and all phase 3 judgements are supplied**, and the phase 3 index is pre-loaded so that groups issue queries rather than build.
- **Step 2 is worked with the room**, including a supplied criterion for a different need as a worked example.
- **Sixty queries is deliberately small**, and peer judgement is capped at twenty pairs.
- **No student implements a ranker.** IS203 carries that, and the service carries the scoring.
- **The index schema at step 5 is produced by the agent and read by the student**, which removes authoring load and leaves the decision load, which is the part being assessed.
- **Phase 3's four sets come with their judgements already made**, so the phase is characterisation and not annotation.

**Step 4's prediction is unsupported**, per the programme's standing pre-commitment discipline.

### 3.2 Desirable difficulties selected, and those deliberately not

**Selected.**

- *Marking relevance before articulating the rule used,* item 1.
- *Two unnamed rankers with no basis for preferring either,* items 2 and 3.
- *Characterising four query sets before their construction is disclosed,* phase 3.
- *Stating what deployment each set would be the right evaluation for,* phase 3.
- *Writing a criterion another person must apply,* step 2.
- *Disclosing your own sampling,* step 3.
- *Deciding a field's attributes before knowing which of them affects ranking,* step 5.
- *Having peers judge under your criterion and measuring the gap,* step 6.
- *Adjudicating between your criterion and your intent,* phase 7.
- *Predicting what an agent will get wrong before watching it,* item 5.

**Deliberately not selected.**

- *Implementing a scoring function.* IS203's, and the day says so.
- *Query expansion, stemming and analysis pipelines.* Named and excluded; they are the baseline's tuning surface and they change the day into an optimisation exercise.
- *Learning to rank.* Named and excluded. It requires the judgement infrastructure this day is building.
- *Dense retrieval training.* [S057](wiki/seminars/S057.md)'s. The embedding ranker here is [S051](wiki/seminars/S051.md)'s E2, used unchanged.
- *Index capacity, replication and partitioning.* Named and excluded; they belong with [S063](wiki/seminars/S063.md)'s memory material, and the day mentions sharding only because the ranking statistics depend on it.

**Scope, per the [Whitepaper Standard](wiki/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md), strong form.** Point 3's list of what the baseline discards and point 4's identification of vocabulary mismatch as its failure mode are a tractable enumeration and are stated as one. **Safe** as a diagnostic when query language and document language are the same kind of text and the question is whether a semantic method has anything to contribute — which is the substrate's case and the case most students will meet first. **Unsafe** as an account of when term matching fails: it omits aggregation failures over long documents, queries whose intent is not lexical at all, and cases where the corpus rather than the matching is the problem. A student who carries the short version into work this day does not supervise will ship a baseline into a case it cannot serve and will look for a vocabulary problem that is not there. The boundary is stated to students at point 10 and repeated in the day's perishability register.

**The platform layer carries a second scope statement, and it is the one more likely to be violated.** Points 8 and 9 name a scoring algorithm, two of its parameters and their defaults, a per-shard statistics behaviour, a match cap and a uniform-score-means-unranked signal. These are **safe** for reasoning about the student's own index on the version in front of them: what to change, what accepting a default committed them to, and why two similar documents scored differently. They are **unsafe** in three directions. They are unsafe as a description of any other search product, whose parameter names may coincide and whose behaviour will not. They are unsafe as a description of this product next year, since one of the facts taught — which scoring algorithm is available at all — is a function of when the service was created and is therefore already inconsistent across deployments today. And they are unsafe for reasoning about **why** a default sits where it does: the parameters' *meaning* is derived in [research/02-technical-foundations/](research/02-technical-foundations/robertson-zaragoza-bm25-and-beyond.md) and can be reasoned to, while their *values* are product decisions and can only be looked up. **A student who treats the vendor's exposure of `k1` and `b` as evidence for the monograph's account has mistaken an instance for a proof**, and the correspondence runs the other way: the monograph explains what the switch does, and the switch demonstrates nothing about the monograph.

### 3.3 Engagement and motivational mechanism

The day runs on **Q4**.

Phase 3's first three query sets behave. The identifier set favours term matching, the mismatch set favours the embedding, the question set favours the embedding, and groups characterise all three quickly and correctly. Then the fourth set — which is simply what the substrate's users typed last month — reverses the ordering, and it reverses it because the real distribution is dominated by a query shape nobody weighted. There is no methodological trick to find and nothing to dispute. **The room's model was not wrong about the methods; it was wrong about the queries, and it had never looked.**

The second mechanism is item 1's ordering. Students mark relevance and then write the rule they used, and the rule they write does not reproduce their markings. That is a small, private, immediate finding, and it arrives before the word *criterion* is used.

**The third is item 5 and it is new.** Students predict what the agent will produce when asked to build a search index, and the design's expectation is that the room will predict a wrong parameter and the agent will produce a wrong field attribute. That asymmetry is the day's cheapest lesson about the frontier: **the agent is reliable on the things students know to check and unreliable on the things they do not know exist.**

Phase 6 supplies the affective content and is constructed to be unblameable: a published comparison, an internally-run evaluation, no error anywhere, and eleven weeks of worse outcomes for the people the system exists for. The detail that carries it is the hour — reading the query log would have taken one, and the team's competence was never the issue. The agent case beside it is constructed on the same principle: the review was performed, the review was reasonable, and the review was structurally incapable of finding the fault.

The competence risk is at step 6. A student whose peers disagree substantially with their judgements may read that as evidence that their criterion is bad. The instructor states before phase 5 that substantial disagreement under a written criterion is the expected result, that it is the day's most important measurement, and that the number's job is to bound what the student may later claim rather than to grade the criterion.

## 4. Phase-by-phase design and its warrant

**Phase 1 (25 min).** Twelve queries, mark-then-articulate, two unnamed rankers, **and a sealed Copilot prediction**. **The set's composition is selected by a two-branch rule against [S051](wiki/seminars/S051.md)'s item 1 distribution**, so that whichever intuition the cohort actually holds is the one defeated. This is the day's first rule-6 discharge and it is a change in content rather than a mention. Item 5 is administered here and not later, per instrumentation rule 8.

**Phase 2 (20 min).** Three retrievals and a bridge reaching back four quarters. The bridge converts a retrieval score into an observed distribution over a sampled population, which is the day's whole argument in one question.

**Phase 3 (45 min).** Four query sets, fixed corpus, fixed rankers, characterisation before disclosure, then the deployment-fit task. **The queries are issued against a live index rather than supplied as tables**, which is what makes the score-comparability finding available for free: both rankers report on the same response field and the two numbers are not the same kind of number. The construction disclosure is required by instrumentation rule 3 and is not optional on a day that demands the same of students two phases later.

**Phase 4 (50 min).** Ten points. Points 5 and 6 are the retained pair. Point 7 is the portable sentence. **Point 8 is the perishable platform point and point 9 names the defaults**, per the [Copilot Practice Standard](wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md). Point 10 carries the strong-form scope statement in both its content and platform forms.

**Phase 5 (90 min).** Eight steps, **producing a shipped index rather than a report.** Step 3 applies rule 3 to the student. Step 5 makes field attributes a decision. **Step 6 is the second rule-6 discharge and uses an external observer per instrumentation rule 1.** Step 8 is the discriminator. The three Copilot Practice Standard elements attach here.

**Phase 6 (40 min).** Eleven weeks, a clean evaluation, an unread log — then an agent-generated schema that ran, returned results, and scored nothing but the title.

**Phase 7 (30 min).** Bilateral construction and adjudication; the second part is a proposed structure and is marked as such.

**Phase 8 (20 min).** Item 4 against measurement; step 4 against step 7 by class; **item 5's sealed predictions against what each agent produced**; **the cohort's relevance-disagreement distribution shown beside [S054](wiki/seminars/S054.md)'s preference-disagreement distribution.**

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**Students with more relevant prior knowledge.** A student who has built search will know the baseline is strong and will predict Q4 correctly. Predicted failure: they treat step 2's criterion as boilerplate, because in their experience judgements are outsourced. Countermeasure: step 6 puts their criterion in someone else's hands within the same phase, and phase 7 requires them to adjudicate a case where their own words and their own intent diverge. Neither is answerable from experience. A second predicted failure is new: a student fluent with this service will accept the agent's schema because it matches what they would have written, which is exactly the condition under which an intervention record has no entries — and the escape clause is available to them only if their build asserts something about itself.

**Students with less relevant prior knowledge.** The risk is step 2. A first criterion is usually a restatement of the information need and is not applicable by a second person. Countermeasures: worked with the room; a criterion for a different need supplied as a worked example; the instructor's statement that an unusable first criterion is the ordinary outcome and that noticing it is the skill; and step 6's peer judgement, which surfaces the problem cheaply rather than at the end. The build is not an additional risk for this group and is arguably a reduction: the agent produces a working index for anybody, and what the specification reads is the decisions recorded around it.

**Phase 3 is too easy.** Q1, Q2 and Q3 are straightforward and are meant to be — they exist to establish the expected ordering that Q4 breaks. If a group predicts Q4 correctly and quickly, the fallback is to ask **what the substrate's query log would have to look like for the ordering to reverse again**, which is a question about the user population rather than about retrieval and has no supplied answer.

**Affective failure.** The characteristic risk is **contrarianism** — a student who concludes that the new method is overrated, that the baseline is secretly best, and that the lesson is scepticism about progress. This is a worse outcome than the belief the day is displacing, because it is unfalsifiable and it travels.

Countermeasures: Q2 and Q3 are cases where the embedding ranker wins decisively and the day does not soften them; point 7 is stated as an ordering of terms rather than as a verdict on methods; step 7 asks for the class on which the baseline wins rather than for a winner; and the instructor states once that the purpose of a baseline is to make an improvement reportable, which is an argument for building the new thing rather than against it.

## 6. Retention architecture

Retrieved by [S057](wiki/seminars/S057.md) (where the relevance criterion becomes the training signal's target), [S058](wiki/seminars/S058.md) (where the per-class reporting discipline is applied to recall loss), [S059](wiki/seminars/S059.md) (where the disagreement rate becomes a ceiling on a published claim), [S066](wiki/seminars/S066.md) (where the criterion becomes a release gate) and [S078](wiki/seminars/S078.md) (where relevance and authorisation interact and a relevant document may still not be returnable).

**S059 at roughly two weeks is the primary target**, and the link is the module's spine: this day measures how far two people diverge on the student's own judgements, and S059 requires that number to be stated as a floor beneath which no claimed improvement may be reported.

S066's retrieval is the longest-range and the most consequential in practice. A release gate built on relevance judgements inherits every property established here, and the gate is the artefact that will still be running when nobody remembers who wrote the criterion.

Retained content: ranking rather than matching, what the baseline discards, vocabulary mismatch as its failure, relevance as a judgement, the disagreement ceiling, and the four-term ordering at point 7. Six items.

Own schedule: next morning, day 3, week 2, week 5.

## 7. Assessment and evidence generated

No entrustment judgement. The quarter's is at [S060](wiki/seminars/S060.md).

One specification item in IS203 with ME202. Per rule 1, the checked elements are the information need and its decider; the written relevance criterion; the query set with its sampling and period; the pre-committed prediction; **the shipped index with its field attributes, analyser and scoring configuration recorded as decisions**; the measured peer disagreement; per-class results for both rankers; the baseline-wins class with its explanation; the phase 7 outcome including the adjudication; **and the prompt, intervention and frontier records**. **Nothing else is assessed.**

Four hard requirements: **a query set whose sampling is not stated fails**; **a result reported only in aggregate fails**; **an accepted default recorded as an absence rather than as a decision fails**; and **an intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that its own design could have detected an intervention-worthy error. The last carries the sensitivity escape [S052](wiki/seminars/S052.md) established and [WP-048](wiki/whitepapers/WP-048.md) §8 corrected, and its accepted instance here is a build that checks every field named in the student's criterion is in fact searchable — which is the [S056](wiki/seminars/S056.md) phase 6 agent failure, detected by the artefact instead of by the student.

**Rule-6 discharge, first obligation — [S051](wiki/seminars/S051.md)'s item 1 grouping distribution.** WP-051 §8 committed this paper to stating in §7 whether the day carries the artefact element S051 named, and where. **It does. The element is phase 1's construction rule**, which selects the twelve query-result sets by a stated two-branch decision against the S051 distribution: a cohort whose unaided similarity was behavioural receives a set weighted toward queries whose clicked result is wrong, and a cohort whose similarity was semantic receives a set weighted toward queries where a fluent paraphrase match is wrong. Which branch runs is decided from the S051 data before delivery. **The response is a change in what the phase contains, not a sentence acknowledging the measurement**, and that distinction is what WP-051 §8 said rule 6 could otherwise be satisfied without. The obligation is discharged.

**Rule-6 discharge, second obligation — [S054](wiki/seminars/S054.md)'s step 4 peer-disagreement distribution.** WP-054 §7 named this day's phase 5 as the response occasion, specifying that relevance judgements be collected and the same disagreement measured on a retrieval task. **The element exists, is assessed, and is a checked specification element: step 6 requires a peer group to judge twenty of the student's query-result pairs under the student's own written criterion, and phase 8 shows the two distributions side by side.** The obligation is discharged, and the paired display is the addition — the value is not either rate but the comparison between a preference task and a relevance task under identical conditions. **The step number moved from 5 to 6 when the build was inserted; the phase and the element are unchanged.**

**Instrumentation under rule 6.** The step 6 disagreement distribution responds at [S059](wiki/seminars/S059.md) phase 5, where each student states the ceiling their judgements imply against the difference they are claiming; the step 4 prediction accuracy by query class responds at [S057](wiki/seminars/S057.md) phase 3, whose configurations are evaluated on the class the cohort mis-predicted worst. **WP-057 §7 and WP-059 §7 must report whether those elements exist**, per the commitment WP-051 §8 established.

**Frontier instrumentation.** Item 5's sealed prediction accuracy is the module's first frontier-calibration datum, coded for whether the named error concerned a parameter or a field attribute, and is read by the following year's cohort through the [Durable and Perishable Register](wiki/program/09-Durable-and-Perishable-Register.md) per instrumentation rule 9. Per instrumentation rule 3 the day states its sample and this paper repeats it: **one cohort, one corpus, one service, one agent version, on a task staff chose because a silent failure was known to be available in it.** A frontier map assembled from these predictions describes this task and not the agent.

Dispositions: **humility**, at step 8 and at phase 7's adjudication.

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: the day's central finding is contingent on a property of one substrate, and the design has committed to it.**

At its strongest: phase 3's payload works because the substrate's real query log is dominated by short identifier-bearing navigational queries. That is a fact about this deployment. A substrate whose users write natural-language questions produces a Q4 on which the embedding ranker wins, the four sets produce the expected ordering, and the phase's designed defeat does not occur. [M12](wiki/modules/M12.md) states that if phase 3 does not produce a case where the older method wins on the real distribution, the phase has failed — so the design has staked the module's opening finding on traffic it does not control and cannot predict from year to year.

Worse, the failure is silent in a specific way. Staff who know the payload is supposed to be Q4 and find that it is not have an obvious and wrong repair available: reweight the sample until it is. The day would then teach the intended lesson from a constructed distribution, which is the exact error phase 6 is about.

The rebuttal covers part of this. The phase's *structure* does not depend on which set wins — four query populations over a fixed corpus and a fixed ranker pair produce four orderings whatever the substrate looks like, and "the winner changes with the population" is the point 7 claim. The lesson survives; only the specific defeat is contingent.

**What is conceded is that the design wrote the contingent version into the module's failure criterion, and that the fix is one query set.** Adding **Q5, an unfiltered log sample from a different deployment with different users**, makes the finding structural rather than local: two real populations, two orderings, neither chosen by the designer. It costs one supplied set and no additional phase time, it removes the incentive to reweight Q4, and it would let the day state the claim it actually wants — that the ordering is a property of the population — without depending on which way this year's traffic runs. It is not in the design.

**Objection 2: the ceiling this day teaches is estimated from a sample the day's own material would reject.**

At its strongest: step 5 measures disagreement on twenty query-result pairs, judged once, by one peer group, under a criterion those peers did not write and have no stake in. That number is then carried into [S059](wiki/seminars/S059.md) as a floor beneath which no claimed improvement may be reported. [S049](wiki/seminars/S049.md) point 6 requires a design to state the smallest effect it could detect before it runs; [S049](wiki/seminars/S049.md) point 2 requires the clustering structure to be argued, and twenty pairs from one annotator group is a single cluster. **The day computes a quantity from a sample whose properties it teaches students to interrogate, and then uses it as a bound.**

There is a further problem the design did not notice until writing this section. Peers annotating under someone else's criterion are performing a different task from the one the ceiling is meant to bound, which is *the criterion's author and a colleague both judging in good faith*. Uninvested annotators will diverge more, so the estimate is biased in the direction that makes the ceiling look higher, which makes the day's rhetorical point more strongly and the number less usable.

**This is conceded without qualification.** The cheap fix is the reciprocal: **require each student also to judge twenty pairs under a peer's criterion.** It doubles the data at no additional elicitation cost, it puts every student on both sides of the task, and — the part that matters — comparing a student's disagreement-as-author against their disagreement-as-annotator separates criterion quality from annotator variance, which is the decomposition the number needs before it can bound anything. The design did not take it because phase 5 is already the module's longest, and that is a scheduling reason offered against a methodological objection, which is not an answer.

**Objection 3: the platform apparatus is carrying more instructional weight than the perishability register admits, and the register is the design's only defence.**

This is the objection that bites on the practical turn.

At its strongest: the day's stated durable content is six items and no numbers. Its actual persuasive structure now runs through a specific vendor's product. The room discovers score non-comparability because two rankers report on one field of one response. It discovers that a field attribute can silently disable scoring because that is how this service's schema works. It discovers that ranking statistics are per-shard because this service shards by default. **Each of those is a genuine engineering fact and none of them is a fact about retrieval** — they are facts about a product, arriving in the most memorable slots the day has, and the perishability register's response is to list them in a table and mark them perishable.

Marking is not the same as bounding. A student who leaves with six durable claims and eleven vivid product facts has a memory whose *proportions* are wrong even if its labels are right, and the design has no instrument that would detect this. Worse, the day's most quotable new sentence — that the score of 1 means nothing was scored — is pure product trivia with no durable analogue at all, and it is the sentence students will repeat.

The rebuttal has real content. The alternative is the design this revision replaced, in which the baseline was a described method and the room agreed it was probably strong; that day produced no finding a student would act on, which is why the module was judged too theoretical. **A vivid product fact that produces a correct engineering habit is worth more than a durable claim that produces nothing**, and point 9's four defaults exist to produce exactly one habit: enumerate what was chosen for you. That habit is platform-independent and is what [S060](wiki/seminars/S060.md) point 7 assesses at the end of the module.

**What is conceded is that the design has no evidence for the proportions and one cheap way to get some.** The day's retention schedule already includes a next-morning ten-minute retrieval on point 7. **Adding a single free-recall prompt — *what did you learn yesterday that will still be true in five years, and what will not* — and coding the answers by durable-versus-instance would measure the ratio directly**, at a cost of two minutes, on an occasion that already exists. It would also give the [Durable and Perishable Register](wiki/program/09-Durable-and-Perishable-Register.md) its first outcome measure rather than its current process measure. It is not in the design, and until it is, the claim that the platform layer is a vehicle rather than the content is this paper's assertion and not a finding.

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** Step 6's relevance-judgement disagreement will exceed [S054](wiki/seminars/S054.md)'s step 4 preference-annotation disagreement for a majority of students. *Instrument:* the two per-student distributions, paired and reported together at phase 8. **The claim is that relevance is a harder criterion to write than preference**, and if the rates are indistinguishable the day's separate treatment of relevance is not warranted.

**Prediction 2.** At step 4, a majority of students will predict that the embedding ranker wins on the real-log query class. *Instrument:* step 4 predictions coded by class against step 7 results. **This is the prediction the phase 3 payload is built to defeat**, and if it fails — if students predict the baseline correctly — the cohort arrived already holding point 7 and phase 3 is redundant for them.

**Prediction 3.** Fewer than a third of item 1's written rules will be applicable by a second reader without further information. *Instrument:* item 1 rules given to a different student to apply to the same twelve queries, with agreement scored. **The measure is of criterion writability, not of relevance**, and it is the cheapest available baseline for step 2.

**Prediction 4.** At phase 7's second part, a majority of students will choose to amend the criterion rather than the intent. *Instrument:* phase 7 adjudications coded for direction. **If the majority amend the criterion whenever it is inconvenient, the criterion is decorative** and step 2 is producing a document rather than a commitment — which would mean the module's entire evidentiary base rests on something students revise at will.

**Prediction 5.** At item 5, a large majority will name a parameter as the agent's likely error and fewer than a fifth will name a field attribute; and the agent's actual errors will be field attributes more often than parameters. *Instrument:* item 5 responses coded parameter-versus-attribute, against the errors each student's intervention record in fact contains. **The gap between the two is the day's frontier-calibration measure.** If students predict attributes correctly, this cohort's model of agent failure is better than the design assumes and the phase 6 agent case is redundant; **if the agent's errors are overwhelmingly parameters, the design has mis-characterised where the frontier sits on this task** and points 8 and 9 should be reordered.
