# WP-015 — Choosing a Representation for a Caller You Have Met

**Seminar day:** [S015](/wiki/seminars/S015.md) · **Module:** [M03](/wiki/modules/M03.md) · **Quarter:** [Q2](/wiki/quarters/Q2.md)
**Strands:** CF · PE · SY · **Complexity class:** 1 → 2
**Platform anchor:** four candidate representations run against a caller's derived mix, one a Foundry embedding deployment; a repository custom instructions file as an executable specification · **MCP servers:** Microsoft Learn MCP Server · Azure MCP Server (Read only = true)

## Abstract

This paper defends effecting the programme's first complexity-class transition on a day whose adjudicating criterion is supplied by a real person rather than by a written brief, and defends removing fading from the choice while retaining it around the choice. The first argument is that a class-2 decision requires a criterion the student did not author, that a written brief is always authored by the assessor and is therefore read as a puzzle with an intended answer, and that a live caller supplies an underdetermined criterion which is what the professional situation actually presents. The second argument is that fading is scaffolding for a method, and that when the method is the thing being withheld, retaining fading *on the choice* would contradict the transition — while retaining it elsewhere costs the transition nothing. The paper additionally defends making the day's deliverable an instructions file rather than a document: a specification checked by execution is the same object the programme has assessed since S006, in the one register where a defective specification announces itself the same afternoon. It also defends the requirement that rejected alternatives be paired with the specific conditions under which each would have won, now measured rather than asserted. It concedes that the day's central resource — a real caller — is the least reproducible element in the programme, that its removal would change what the day teaches rather than degrade it, and that the design has no adequate fallback.

## 1. The instructional problem this day must solve

The programme's complexity classes are defined by what the student decides. At class 1 the task is specified and the method given; at class 2 the student chooses the method and defends the choice. That transition has to happen somewhere, and where it happens determines whether it takes.

The obvious failure is to effect it with a written brief. A brief is composed by the person who will assess the response, which means it contains exactly the information needed for the intended answer and no more. Students read this correctly and treat the brief as a puzzle: what does this want? The resulting decision is a decoding exercise, and the competence practised is inference about assessors rather than judgement under uncertainty.

A live caller removes that structure entirely. The caller does not know what the options are, cannot be asked which one they want, describes their work rather than their requirements, and answers questions honestly including when the honest answer is that something has never come up. The criterion the student ends up with is one they derived, from evidence they gathered, with gaps they must decide how to fill. That is the class-2 situation, and no written artefact reproduces it.

The day's second problem is the module's synthesis. S011 supplied an operation mix, S012 a comparison cost, S013 a claim type, S014 a problem shape. Each was taught as a separate consideration and none was ever assembled. Point 4 assembles them, and phase 5 requires the assembly to be used. If the module is four days of related material rather than one method, phase 2's synthesis item and phase 5 both fail visibly.

The third problem is what students owe back. Step 8 requires a one-page answer in the caller's language stating what they are getting, what it guarantees and what it costs them — and the caller reads three of them at phase 8 and says which they would rather receive. That is the first time in the programme that a student's work is judged by its recipient rather than by an assessor, and the design considers it the module's most important twenty minutes.

The fourth problem is the one the platform layer makes tractable for the first time. The programme has assessed specifications since S006, and every one of them has been assessed by *reading*. A reader can approve a specification that would not survive contact with anyone trying to conform to it, and the student learns nothing about the gap because nobody ever tried. An instructions file closes that loop: the student writes the rules, the agent conforms to them, and the output either matches the caller's need or does not — within ninety minutes, without a marker's opinion in between. **The instruction that fails is almost never the one that is wrong; it is the one that is unexaminable**, and unexaminable instructions are what students write. S006's rule 1 says state what is actually checked. This is the day where a student finds out what "actually" was doing in that sentence.

## 2. Entry state and prior knowledge assumptions

- **Four days of module inputs,** none yet assembled.
- **The S007 bet framing,** used at point 6 and step 7 without re-teaching, now on its fourth application.
- **S014's user-facing answer requirement,** one day old, which step 8 extends from a question-answer to a recommendation.
- **PE102's contextual-inquiry material,** running concurrently and used directly at phase 3. This is the module's only within-quarter cross-course dependency and it is load-bearing.
- **One prior encounter with a real user's words,** at S014, but not with a real user.
- **Copilot agent mode used for four days on this module's builds,** and never yet steered by a written rule set rather than by a conversational prompt. The instructions file at step 8 is the first.
- **S006's specification rules,** eight weeks old, retrieved four times, and never yet checked by execution.
- **No experience of an underdetermined criterion.** Every prior task had a determinate right answer.

## 3. Theoretical basis for the design

### 3.1 Load profile and element interactivity

**High, and the load is of a kind the module has not previously imposed.** The intrinsic interactivity is comparable to S013's — four inputs that constrain each other, plus an option space — but the day adds two loads that are not intrinsic to the material.

The first is **social**: conducting a twenty-five-minute inquiry with a real person, in a group, without leading them, is demanding for students who have never done it. The second is **decisional**: for the first time there is no answer the instructor is holding, which removes a support students have relied on for fifteen weeks even when they were not conscious of it.

Management, given that fading is removed from the choice and retained around it:

- **The caller's description is captured in writing at phase 3 step 3,** individually and in silence, so that the derived mix is an artefact rather than a memory.
- **Point 3 works the derivation technique on a real student's notes,** which is the single most useful support available and is provided immediately before it is needed.
- **The module dataset and component persist,** so no comprehension load is added.
- **Step 8 is one page,** bounded deliberately, and the instructions file has a supplied skeleton with its rules named and its content empty.
- **The four representations were all built earlier in the module** and are re-run at step 4 rather than re-implemented, so the measurement costs minutes.
- **The option space is unbounded but the module supplies four familiar candidates,** so a student who cannot generate options has a floor.

The design accepts that this is the module's hardest day and that some students will not complete step 8 well. That is treated as the transition's cost rather than as a failure of the day.

### 3.2 Desirable difficulties selected, and those deliberately not

**Selected.**

- *Removal of fading from the choice,* which is the transition itself.
- *Rejected alternatives with specific winning conditions,* step 6. The operational test of whether a choice was made.
- *Deriving a mix from a description* rather than receiving one.
- *The partner's argument constrained to the caller's own words and the step 4 measurements,* phase 7.
- *Item 3's anticipation demand* — what will they say that you have not anticipated — which is a prediction about one's own blind spot.
- *Fixing the specification rather than the output,* step 8. The day's hardest instruction and the one most reliably violated.

**Deliberately not selected.**

- *Withholding the assembly.* Point 4 states the four-input procedure explicitly. The class transition is about choosing, not about discovering the method of choosing.
- *A second caller.* One caller, one component. Comparing callers is S039's work.
- *Writing the component.* No implementation code is authored by the student. The day is a decision, its justification, and the specification that produces the caller's artefact.
- *Assessor-supplied requirements.* Refused, per §1.
- *Any technical novelty.* All four representations are already known and were all built earlier in the module.
- *Provisioning anything.* The Azure MCP Server is read-only at step 3 and the day inspects an estate it does not alter.

**The scope statement on the platform instance, in strong form.**

The day teaches that an instructions file is a specification checked by execution. That claim has a safe range and an unsafe one, and both are stated at point 7 and enforced at step 8.

It is **safe** as a claim about *conformance*: whether a rule you wrote produced the work you wanted is a question execution answers, and answers cheaply, repeatedly and without an assessor. It is safe as the basis for the day's diagnostic move — a non-conforming output indicts the specification first.

It is **unsafe** in three conditions. It is unsafe as a claim that **a conforming output is a correct one**: the agent conforming to your rules says nothing about whether your rules serve the caller, which is what the caller's own phase 8 judgement is for and why that judgement is not delegated to the agent. It is unsafe as a claim about **the agent's stability** — the same file may produce different output on a different day, and one conforming run is one observation, which the students have no framework for until S027 and are told so. And it is unsafe **at any version other than the one in front of you**, which on this day means both the model behind the agent and the MCP server options at point 8, none of which carries a stability guarantee.

The artefact element enforcing the boundary is the intervention record's second clause: **an intervention record in which every entry fixed the output rather than the specification fails.** A student who treats the generated page as the deliverable has applied the simplification outside its safe range, and the record makes that visible without anyone needing to read the page.

### 3.3 Engagement and motivational mechanism

The day runs on **a person in the room**, and nothing else in the module compares.

The mechanism is not novelty. It is that the caller's presence makes the work non-hypothetical in a way that survives the day: students who have asked someone about their work, and heard them describe something frustrating, produce different artefacts than students working from a brief. The design's observation is that step 8 quality correlates with phase 3 engagement more than with technical background, and phase 8's caller preference is the reason — students know a real person will read three of these.

The phase 8 return is the design's strongest single motivational device in Q2. The caller reads three anonymised answers and says which they would rather receive and why. Their reason is frequently not the technically strongest answer, which is the point: it is usually the clearest about what it costs them. That outcome, delivered by the recipient rather than by an instructor, is worth more than any amount of instruction about audience.

The competence risk is that a student's choice is argued down at phase 7 and they revise. Countermeasure: revision is stated in advance as the expected outcome and is a disposition credit rather than a specification failure. The framing is that a choice which survives an argument constrained to the caller's own words is a strong choice, and a choice which does not should not have survived.

Autonomy is at its highest for the module: the representation may be any, including one not taught.

## 4. Phase-by-phase design and its warrant

**Phase 1 (25 min).** Deliberately insufficient information, undisclosed. Item 1 forces a committed choice on a brief; item 2 asks what would be needed; item 3 asks what the student cannot anticipate; item 4 predicts survival. The whole phase exists to be falsified by phase 3, and item 2 becomes the measure of the conversation's quality.

**Phase 2 (20 min).** The module's cumulative set with a synthesis item asking which outputs are inputs and which are constraints. Answered well by a minority, which is expected — point 4 is where it is resolved.

**Phase 3 (45 min).** The caller. Ten minutes describing their work without being asked about technology, twenty-five minutes of questions, ten minutes of silent individual capture. The prohibition on asking the caller to choose is absolute and is stated to students in advance: a caller who is asked which representation they want will answer, and the answer will be worthless. **This phase does not carry the pattern's executed contrasting cases and the day says so**; the four cases are run at step 4, where the caller's derived mix is available to run them against, and manufacturing a phase 3 benchmark would mean a real person sitting in a room while students waited for measurements.

**Phase 4 (50 min).** The room's item 1 choices shown against the phase 3 mixes, unattributed, as the first act. That gap is the day's instruction and it is evidence rather than assertion. Point 2's distinction — callers know their work and their pain, not their frequencies — is the technique's foundation. Point 4 is the module's assembly. Point 7 is the specification-as-executable claim and is placed after the assembly rather than before it, because a student who has not yet made a choice has nothing to specify. Point 8 carries the perishable material and its scope, and is deliberately about *defaults in a tool you trusted* on the day the student first decides on somebody else's behalf.

**Phase 5 (90 min).** Eight steps, class 2, fading removed from the choice and retained around it. Step 3's read-only estate check is the day's cheapest correction of a common professional error — defending a choice against an assumed system. Step 4's measurement is what converts step 6's winning conditions from invention into evidence. Step 6 is the specification's weighted element. Step 7 applies S007's bet framing to a decision made under acknowledged uncertainty, which is what class 2 requires and class 1 never did. Step 8 is the terminal artefact and the programme's first specification assessed by execution.

**Phase 6 (40 min).** Two disclosures of the same failure. An accurate description that produced a wrong derived mix, chosen because the day's real risk is not choosing badly but deriving badly, and because inquiry alone does not prevent it — the attention is on *which question was not asked*. Then the agent case: an instructions file saying "avoid jargon", a page using the word *embedding* four times, and two readers who checked tone because the instruction had been about tone. Both failures are the same shape — **an unexaminable requirement produces work nobody can examine** — and the pairing is what makes point 7 land as a consequence rather than as a rule.

**Phase 7 (30 min).** Adversarial argument constrained to the caller's own words and the step 4 measurements. The constraint is what keeps the phase evidential; without it, students argue from preference and the phase becomes a debate about structures.

**Phase 8 (20 min).** The Copilot prediction opened, then the caller returns. Three anonymised answers, their preference and their reason. This is the module's terminal event and is deliberately placed where a calibration item would normally sit, because the caller's judgement is the calibration.

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**Students with more relevant prior knowledge.** A student with strong technical background will produce a competent step 4 choice quickly and may treat phase 3 as requirements-gathering to be got through. Predicted failure: they ask closed questions that confirm a choice they made in phase 1, obtain agreement, and derive a mix that reflects their own assumptions. This is the exact failure phase 6's disclosure targets. Countermeasure: phase 3's first ten minutes forbid technology questions entirely, and phase 4 point 1's unattributed gap display makes assumption-confirmation visible at cohort level. Their phase 7 partner is also constrained to the caller's words and the measurements, which neutralises technical fluency as an argumentative advantage.

**Students with less relevant prior knowledge.** A student with a weaker technical background may conduct an excellent inquiry and be unable to convert it into a defensible choice. Countermeasures: point 3's worked derivation; the four familiar candidate representations as a floor; the instructions-file skeleton; and the specification's weighting on step 6, which rewards reasoning about alternatives rather than optimality of the selection. Residual risk: with fading removed from the choice, a student who stalls at step 1 has little to fall back on, and the instructor's mid-phase check is the main mitigation. This is the least-supported point in the module and the design accepts it as the transition's cost.

**Phase 3 is too easy.** Not applicable in the usual sense; the risk is the reverse. If the caller is unusually articulate and volunteers frequencies unprompted, the derivation step is trivialised. Countermeasure: callers are briefed to describe work rather than requirements and not to volunteer quantities, and if a caller does so anyway the instructor introduces a second workflow the caller has not described, which restores the derivation demand.

**The agent produces a conforming page on the first attempt.** Possible, and it removes the day's specification lesson entirely, because a specification that is never violated teaches nothing about what it checks. Countermeasure: step 8 requires both versions to be kept, and where there is only one the student is asked to produce a rule they could delete without changing the output — which is the same diagnostic in a subtractive form and is harder. A rule whose deletion changes nothing was not load-bearing, and a file made entirely of such rules is the failure the day is looking for.

**Affective failure.** Two. The first is **anxiety in phase 3** — students who have never interviewed anyone may not speak at all, and the group format means silence is survivable and therefore likely. Countermeasure: each student is required to ask at least one question, allocated in advance, and the first five questions are assigned rather than volunteered. The second and more serious is **deference**: students treat the caller's stated preferences as requirements and abandon judgement, producing a choice that is an obedience rather than a decision. This is the professional-formation failure the day is most likely to produce and phase 6's disclosure names it, but the countermeasure is a story rather than a mechanism.

## 6. Retention architecture

Retrieved by S016 (the memory hierarchy, one day later, which explains the constant factors this module has been asserting), S029 (designing an instrument for a question), S034 (consistency models as a contract with the caller — the same relationship at class 3), S037 (contextual inquiry with a real user, which is this phase 3 at class 3 and with the inquiry itself as the object), S039 (requirements as negotiated constraint) and S089 (the handover artefact and its warranty, which is step 8 at class 6).

S037 is the closest structural repeat and the relationship is deliberate: S015 does inquiry as a means to a technical decision, S037 does inquiry as the object of study. Students meet the practice before they study it.

S089 at roughly two years is the long-range target. Step 8 — what you are giving them, what it guarantees, what it costs them — is a warranty statement in miniature.

Retained content: the four-input assembly, the caller-knows-work-not-frequencies principle, the rejected-alternatives discipline, and the specification-checked-by-execution equivalence. Four items.

Own schedule: next morning, day 3, week 2, and the module cumulative set in the inter-quarter gap.

## 7. Assessment and evidence generated

No entrustment judgement, per the programme's position that none is made before Q3.

One specification item in CF102 requiring all eight steps and the three artefact elements, with weighting on step 6 and step 8. **A rejected alternative without a specific winning condition fails**; **a step 8 page using representation vocabulary fails**; **an intervention record in which every entry fixed the output rather than the specification fails.**

The third clause is the day's own, and it is the only assessment clause in the module that measures a *diagnostic habit* rather than an artefact property. Its defence is that the habit is the whole difference between owning a specification and editing its output, and that it is cheaply checkable because the record already exists for other reasons.

Dispositions: **humility**, observed at phase 7 in whether the student revised, and observed as a positive rather than as a concession.

Programme instrumentation: the phase 1 item 4 survival prediction against actual survival is the cohort's assumption-durability measure; the phase 3 question quality, rated against item 2's stated needs, is the inquiry baseline carried to S037; **the caller's phase 8 preference and stated reason is recorded verbatim across cohorts** and is the programme's only recipient-side quality signal in Q2; and the phase 1 Copilot prediction, scored at phase 8, closes the module's frontier map. Rule 4 branch for the frontier item: a cohort that cannot predict which of its own instructions the agent will satisfy in letter only receives the letter-versus-substance distinction as an explicit S021 pretest item.

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: the day's central resource does not scale and is not reproducible.**

At its strongest: it requires a real caller, willing, available, briefed not to over-specify, and prepared to return at phase 8 and judge three answers in front of the cohort. With multiple parallel sections, that is multiple callers per delivery, each of whom must be comparably articulate and comparably briefed. Caller variance then becomes a fairness problem — a section with a vague caller has a harder day and a section with an articulate one has an easier one — and the design has made this variance load-bearing rather than incidental. Any programme that cannot supply callers reliably cannot run S015, which means the module's terminal day is the least portable thing in it.

The rebuttal is that the design accepts the constraint knowingly and that the alternative degrades the day into the thing it was built to avoid. A written brief, a staff member playing a caller, or a recording all reintroduce an authored criterion, and an authored criterion is decoded rather than derived. The [Substrate and Infrastructure](/wiki/program/10-Substrate-and-Infrastructure.md) page's withdrawal commitment applies here in principle: if the resource cannot be supplied, the honest response is to change what is claimed rather than to simulate it.

The concession is substantial and is not resolved. Caller variance is real, it is not controlled, and the design has no mechanism for equalising it across sections. Worse, the fallback the substrate page implies — teach a reduced version — would remove the class-2 transition's only genuine underdetermination, which means S015 without a caller is not a degraded S015 but a different day teaching something else. The design does not currently name what that different day would be, and it should, because the probability that some delivery lacks a caller is not small.

**Objection 2: removing all fading at the point of maximum difficulty is the opposite of what the design's own load principles require.**

At its strongest: the programme's reconciliation rule is to raise difficulty in retrieval and selection and lower it in representation and attention. S015 raises difficulty in selection, which is consistent — and then also removes every support in representation and attention, on a day that adds social load and decisional load simultaneously. The expertise-reversal argument does not apply, since these students are not experts in this task; they have never done it. The predictable result is that the weakest students receive the least support at the moment they most need it, and the day's own §5 concedes that a student who stalls has nothing to fall back on. That is not a transition, it is a cliff.

**The concession is discharged.** This paper previously argued that the reasoning for removing fading is sound about the choice and does not extend to the rest of the day, that point 3's worked derivation was in fact such a support, and that **the seminar page's claim of removed fading was therefore inaccurate and should be corrected.** It is now corrected: the day states that fading is removed from the choice and retained around it, and names what is retained — the worked derivation, the four candidate representations as a floor, the instructions-file skeleton, and the re-run rather than re-implementation of the four representations at step 4. The repair is annotated at the point of change. What is *not* fixed is the residual: a student who stalls at step 1 still has only the instructor's mid-phase check, and adding a support there would supply the method the transition exists to withhold. The design accepts that residual and states it as accepted rather than solved.

**Objection 3: making the deliverable an agent-generated page hands the student's terminal artefact to the agent, on the one day the programme most needs to see the student's own judgement.**

At its strongest, and this is the objection that bites hardest on the practical turn: the module has spent five days building toward one page written by a student for a person they met. §1 calls the caller's reading of that page the module's most important twenty minutes. The revised day replaces it with a page written by a language model from a file of rules, and then asks a real human being to say which of three machine-generated documents they would rather receive. Whatever that measures, it is not the student's ability to write to a caller. Worse, the artefact elements are all *about the process* — prompts, interventions, frontier — so a student can now pass the module's terminal specification without ever having written a sentence intended for another person to read. The programme's stated claim is that a graduate can say what a system does badly; it does not follow that a graduate need never say anything themselves.

**This objection is conceded, and the cheap fix is named.** The rebuttal available — that the instructions file *is* the student's writing, and is writing for a reader who will act, which is exactly PE102's anchor — is true and does not answer the objection, because writing rules for an agent and writing prose for a frightened non-technical colleague are different competences and the programme needs both. **The cheap fix is to require the student's own one-page answer as well, written first, before the instructions file is drafted, and to have the caller judge a mixed set at phase 8 without being told which are which.** It costs twenty minutes of phase 5, it preserves the module's terminal demand intact, and it converts the day's central platform claim into something falsifiable: if the caller systematically prefers the generated pages, the design learns something uncomfortable and worth knowing; if they systematically prefer the hand-written ones, the design learns something about what the specification register cannot carry. **That fix is in the design as §9 prediction 5's instrument and is not yet in phase 5's step list**, because adding it costs twenty minutes the phase does not currently have, and the design has not decided what to cut. That is an unresolved trade and it is recorded as unresolved rather than settled.

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** A large majority will predict at item 4 that their item 1 choice survives, and a majority of choices will not survive. *Instrument:* item 4 predictions against phase 5 step 4 selections compared with item 1.

**Prediction 2.** Phase 3 question quality will correlate more strongly with step 8 caller-facing quality than technical background does. *Instrument:* blind-rated question quality and step 8 quality, against the admission background survey.

**Prediction 3.** The caller's phase 8 preference will cite clarity about cost or limitation, rather than technical merit, as the reason — across cohorts. *Instrument:* verbatim caller reasons coded for cited criterion. **A caller who cites technical merit would indicate the anonymised answers are not differing on the dimension the design believes they differ on.**

**Prediction 4.** Students who revise after phase 7 will produce step 6 statements rated higher in specificity than those who do not, controlling for pre-revision quality. *Instrument:* step 6 specificity before and after, against revision status.

**Prediction 5.** A caller shown a mixed set of hand-written and agent-generated one-page answers, unlabelled, will not distinguish them reliably, and will prefer on the same criterion in both. *Instrument:* the mixed-set phase 8 protocol named in §8 objection 3. **This prediction is currently unmeasurable, because the hand-written page is not in phase 5's step list; it is stated so that the design's largest untested platform claim has a named instrument rather than a hope, and so that the twenty-minute trade §8 leaves unresolved has a stated payoff if it is ever made.**

**Prediction 6.** A majority of intervention records will contain more output fixes than specification fixes on first submission. *Instrument:* intervention record entries coded by target. **The direction is the claim; a majority of specification fixes would mean point 7 lands without the phase 6 disclosure, and the disclosure could be shortened.**

**Grounded in vendor documentation, with its version and its silence recorded.**

*What the documentation says.* Microsoft Learn documents the Azure MCP Server's start parameters, including **Read only** — "Whether the MCP server should be read-only. If true, no write operations are allowed" — with a **default of `false`**; **Mode**, with values `namespace` (default), `consolidated`, `all` and `single`, controlling which tools are exposed; and **Learn mode**, which "enables command and parameter discovery without executing Azure operations", **disabled by default**. It states that the server uses the caller's own Azure credentials and that access is secured through Azure RBAC, and it documents six per-tool annotation hints — Destructive, Idempotent, Open world, Read only, Secret and Local required ([Azure MCP Server tools](https://learn.microsoft.com/azure/developer/azure-mcp-server/tools/)). The read tools used at step 3 are documented individually: **List subscriptions** returns subscription ID, display name, state, tenant ID and an `isDefault` flag resolved from the Azure CLI profile or the `AZURE_SUBSCRIPTION_ID` environment variable ([subscription tools](https://learn.microsoft.com/azure/developer/azure-mcp-server/tools/subscription)); **List resource groups** and **List group resources** return names, IDs, types and locations from Azure Resource Manager, both annotated Read Only and Idempotent ([resource group tools](https://learn.microsoft.com/azure/developer/azure-mcp-server/tools/resource-group)).

*At what version and date.* All pages retrieved 2026-07-26. None of the Azure MCP Server pages carries a version stamp in its own text, which is why point 8's unsafe condition is stated as *any version other than the one in front of you* and why the option names as much as their defaults are treated as perishable.

*What the documentation does not report that a reader of this day would need.* Three silences. **It does not say what happens when a read-only server is asked to perform a write** — whether the tool is hidden, refused or errored — so a student cannot predict what their own guard rail looks like from the inside, and the day therefore teaches the default rather than the enforcement. **It publishes no guidance on writing repository custom instructions that produce conforming work**, and no conformance rate for any instruction form; the day's entire point 7 is grounded in the programme's own S006 specification rules, which are this repository's, and no vendor claim is made or implied about them. **And it reports no stability property for agent output** — nothing states whether the same instructions file yields the same result twice — which is precisely the unsafe condition point 7 names and the reason the day treats one conforming run as one observation. No magnitude, rate or agreement figure is asserted anywhere in this paper for any agent or evaluator.

**Design reasoning with no external warrant.** The decision to effect the class transition against a live caller; the four-item phase 1 with undisclosed insufficiency and the sealed Copilot prediction; the phase 3 structure including the ten-minute technology prohibition and the silent individual capture, and the decision to state plainly that this phase carries no executed contrasting cases; the display of the cohort's pre-conversation assumptions as phase 4's first act; the four-input assembly at point 4 and the specification-as-executable claim at point 7; the eight-step phase 5, the read-only estate check at step 3, the four representations re-run at step 4, the rejected-alternatives-with-conditions requirement, and the instructions file at step 8; the fix-the-specification-not-the-output rule and its assessment clause; the conforming-on-first-attempt fallback in §5; the caller's phase 8 return and preference statement; and all six §9 predictions are this design's own reasoning, untested. The §8 objection-1 concession states that caller variance is uncontrolled, that no fallback day is specified, and that one should be. The objection-2 concession is **now discharged in the day** — the seminar page states that fading is removed from the choice and retained around it, and names what is retained — with the residual stall risk recorded as accepted rather than solved. The objection-3 concession accepts that the module's terminal artefact is now generated rather than written, names the mixed-set protocol as the cheap fix, and records that the fix is not in phase 5's step list because the twenty-minute trade has not been decided.
