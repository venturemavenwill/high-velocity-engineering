# WP-023 — Reduction as an Argumentative Move

**Seminar day:** [S023](wiki/seminars/S023.md) · **Module:** [M05](wiki/modules/M05.md) · **Quarter:** [Q3](wiki/quarters/Q3.md)
**Strands:** CF · MS · **Complexity class:** 2
**Platform anchor:** Azure AI Search — the exhaustive-and-approximate asymmetry, and rank fusion · Foundry `document_retrieval` · **MCP servers:** Azure MCP Server (read) · Microsoft Learn MCP Server

## Abstract

This paper defends teaching reduction as an argument with a direction rather than as an algorithmic transformation, and defends requiring the three licensing requirements to be verified separately and explicitly. The first argument is that the direction error is not a knowledge gap but a language-induced misreading — ordinary usage of "reduce" implies simplification while the technical usage implies the opposite — and that errors caused by language are corrected by making the argument's form explicit rather than by repetition of the definition. The second argument is that the transformation is the visible part of a reduction and the other two requirements are invisible, so a student who verifies what is visible produces a complete-looking argument with an unverified premise; separate verification is the only structural remedy, and shipping it as three separate executable checks makes the failure visible in a diff rather than in prose. The paper additionally defends devoting the day's most important instructional point to what a hardness result does *not* license, on the grounds that the module's characteristic failure is overreach rather than error. It concedes that the day's three-requirement formulation is a simplification of a more technical condition, that its central professional claim is asserted from practice rather than evidenced, and that the day's most persuasive moment — a rejected query that enacts the direction rule — is a product decision with the rhetorical force of a proof.

## 1. The instructional problem this day must solve

Reduction is confused more reliably than any other idea in an undergraduate complexity course, and the confusion has an identifiable cause. In ordinary English, reducing A to B suggests that A has been simplified into B — that A was the harder thing and B is what remains. The technical meaning runs the other way: a reduction from A to B shows that a solver for B yields a solver for A, and therefore that **B is at least as hard as A**.

Students who have learned the definition still make the error, because the definition competes with a linguistic intuition that operates automatically. Repetition does not fix this; the intuition survives repetition. What fixes it is making the argument's *form* explicit — if I can solve B then I can solve A, therefore B is at least as hard — because the form has no ambiguity and can be checked against the intended conclusion.

This is why the day sits in the argumentative strand rather than the algorithms one, retrieves from S004 rather than from S021, and asks at phase 2 which S004 failure class an inverted reduction commits. The answer is assuming what you set out to prove, and reframing the day's central error as an instance of a failure class students have held for nineteen weeks is the design's main lever.

**The design's second lever arrived with the platform anchor and is cruder and faster.** The module's index carries the asymmetry in its own configuration. A field built for approximate search accepts an exhaustive query at query time, because the exhaustive query needs nothing the approximate field lacks. A field built for exhaustive search does not accept an approximate query, because the graph the approximate query traverses was never constructed. A student who sets the comparison up the wrong way round receives a rejection in about a second.

That is not a proof of the direction rule and the day is careful to say so — see §8 objection 3. What it is, is a **legible instance of a direction mattering**, delivered by a party with no pedagogical interest, in a register a student cannot argue with. The paper version of this day had to persuade students that direction was not a presentational choice. This one shows them a case where it is not, and then makes the theoretical argument to a room that has stopped resisting it.

The day's second problem is that a reduction has three requirements and only one of them is visible. The transformation is on the page; the direction is a property of what the transformation is used to conclude; the generality is a property of which instances the transformation covers. A student who constructs a correct transformation has done the visible work and produced an argument that looks finished. Reduction 4 in phase 3 is the case that makes this concrete: right direction, efficient transformation, and it covers only a special case, so the conclusion is weaker than stated and the artefact is indistinguishable from a correct one at a glance.

**Here too the platform contributes something the design could not previously supply.** Foundry's `document_retrieval` evaluator returns, alongside its ranking metrics, a count of how many retrieved documents carry no relevance judgement at all. That is a generality failure with a number attached, produced by a tool rather than by an instructor, and it converts phase 3's hardest case from an argument the room must be talked into into a quantity the room can read.

The third problem is overreach. A hardness result is the module's most rhetorically powerful output and students will use it to conclude more than it supports — that no algorithm works, that their instances are hard, that approximation is hopeless. Point 6's three prohibitions are the day's most important content, and phase 6's disclosure attaches a cost to them: six months of work not attempted.

## 3. Theoretical basis for the design

### 3.1 Load profile and element interactivity

**The module's peak.** A reduction argument requires holding the source problem, the target problem, the transformation, the direction of inference and the generality of coverage simultaneously — and an error in any one invalidates the whole, with no local signal.

Management, which is the module's most aggressive:

- **Step 2 supplies a shortlist of three candidate source problems.** Choosing what to reduce from is a research skill and is not the day's target.
- **Step 3's transformation is worked with the room** on a different problem before the independent one.
- **Step 4 decomposes the verification into three separate acts,** which is both the day's discipline and its principal load reduction: three simple checks replace one complex judgement. **The three check shapes are named and their implementations are not**, which is where the day's fading now sits.
- **Phase 3 supplies four complete reductions.** Groups judge and run; they do not construct.
- **The module's single index and single problem domain,** third consecutive day.
- **No formal complexity classes,** which removes an entire vocabulary from the day.
- **The evaluator is used and not explained.** Groups read one number from `document_retrieval` — the count of unjudged retrieved documents — and its four companion metrics are named and deferred to S025. Introducing a composite evaluator in full on the module's highest-load day would be indefensible.

### 3.2 Desirable difficulties selected, and those deliberately not

**Selected.**

- *Both directions presented in the pretest,* so the error is exposed as a direction error rather than as ignorance.
- *Reduction 4's special-case failure,* phase 3's payload, now with its generality gap counted rather than argued.
- *Reduction 3's incomparable scales,* which punishes the module's most available shortcut — comparing whatever number the interface returns.
- *Separate verification of all three requirements, shipped as three separate checks,* step 4.
- *Stating what the result does not license,* step 6, in the specific case rather than generically.
- *Attacking all three requirements in sequence,* phase 7, rather than the most obvious one.
- *The sealed Copilot prediction at item 5,* which asks for a named requirement before the requirements have been named.

**Deliberately not selected.**

- *Choosing the source problem unaided.* Shortlisted.
- *Formal complexity classes.* Deferred to S024.
- *Constructing reductions in phase 3.* Supplied.
- *The evaluator's full metric set.* One metric used, four named, deferred to S025 with a named consumer.
- *Withholding the direction rule.* Point 2 states it flatly. This is the one thing on the day that must not be discovered, because a student who generates it wrongly will hold the wrong version confidently. **The platform's rejection is an instance and not a derivation, and §8 objection 3 is where that distinction is defended.**
- *Assessment stakes on the same day.*

**The platform instance, scoped in the strong form.** The day uses a service's configuration asymmetry as an instance of a reduction's direction, and uses a fused ranking as an instance of a reduction that discards information. Both simplifications must carry their conditions.

*Safe.* Reasoning about why one comparison is available and its mirror is not; about why a transformation that discards magnitude cannot support a claim about magnitude; about why scores produced by different methods are not comparable; and about why a measurement on labelled instances does not license a claim about unlabelled ones. In all four the correspondence is sufficient and correct, and the fourth is the day's payload.

*Unsafe.* Treating the rejected query as **evidence for the direction rule**. The rule is a theorem about what a solver yields; the rejection is a product decision about which structures a service builds at indexing time, and a vendor could implement the missing case in a release note without disturbing a single line of complexity theory. **The correspondence is a correspondence of shape and not of warrant.** Also unsafe: carrying the fusion constant, the score ranges or the execution arithmetic anywhere — the unsafe condition here is, as it nearly always is, any version other than the one in front of you.

The boundary is stated to students at point 10 rather than left to this paper, per the widening recorded on the [Whitepaper Standard](wiki/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md), and **step 6's non-licensed conclusions, stated in the student's specific case, are the artefact element under which a student citing the error message as a proof would fail.**

### 3.3 Engagement and motivational mechanism

The day runs on **an error the room makes in both directions at once**.

Items 1 and 2 present the same reduction described two ways. Most students answer one correctly and one incorrectly, and — the important part — cannot say which. Item 4 asks them to predict whether they got them the right way round, and the resulting distribution of confident-and-wrong is the day's opening.

That construction is more effective than a single item because it removes the possibility of attributing the error to carelessness. A student who is right about one direction and wrong about the other has not been careless; they have applied a rule they do not hold.

Item 3 supplies the professional hook immediately: which of these would you use to argue that a colleague's request is unreasonable. That question makes the direction a matter of consequence within the first twenty-five minutes, and it is the question the module's next two days answer.

**Phase 3's reduction 2 supplies the day's cheapest engagement and it is genuinely new.** A group that has judged the inverted reduction plausible runs it and is refused. There is no arguing with the refusal, nobody in the room designed it, and the design's observation is that students who have produced an error message stop treating the direction rule as a convention about which reasonable people might differ. It costs ninety seconds.

Phase 6's disclosure supplies the day's most distinctive engagement. The cost of a bad hardness argument is work that was not attempted, which is invisible and unrecoverable, and students find this more unsettling than a failure that produced an incident. It is the module's strongest argument for point 6.

Competence is protected by phase 3's structure — reductions 1 and 2 are judged correctly by most groups once the direction is in play, and only 3 and 4 discriminate.

## 4. Phase-by-phase design and its warrant

**Phase 1 (25 min).** Both directions, then the professional question, then the confidence probe. The design's decision to present both directions in one pretest rather than one is what makes the error diagnosable. **Item 5 is the sealed Copilot prediction**, administered here per instrumentation rule 8, and it asks for the name of a requirement before the requirements have been named — which is deliberate: the item's value is in phase 8's comparison, where a student who wrote "something about the data" reads it against their own now-precise vocabulary.

**Phase 2 (20 min).** The nineteen-week bridge to S004. Reframing the direction error as assuming-what-you-set-out-to-prove is the day's main conceptual lever and it is delivered through retrieval rather than exposition, so that the connection is made by the student.

**Phase 3 (45 min).** Four reductions, four verdicts, three distinct failure modes plus one correct case, all executed. Reduction 2 is the day's cheapest moment and reduction 4 remains the payload. **Reduction 3 is new and closes a gap the paper version left open**: with a real interface in front of them, students will compare the most prominent number, and the four incomparable score scales make that failure a result rather than a warning. The closing task — state the three things a reduction must establish — is the abstraction and is generated from the four cases rather than supplied.

**Phase 4 (50 min).** Ten points. Point 1 states the argument form, which is the antidote to the linguistic intuition. Point 2 names the error and explains why the language invites it, which matters: an error with an explanation is easier to guard against than one that feels like personal confusion. Point 5 works a complete hardness argument. Point 6's three prohibitions are the day's most important content. **Point 7 works rank fusion as a second reduction** and is placed here because it is the reduction students will actually meet in production and the one whose discarded information — margin — is most often assumed to survive. Point 8 names the defaults; point 10 carries the strong-form scope statement and guards the day's own analogy against §8 objection 3.

**Phase 5 (90 min).** Seven steps at class 2, the module's most heavily faded. Step 4's separate verification is the specification's weighted element. **Shipping the three verifications as three separate executable checks is the design's principal gain from the platform anchor**, because the failure the day exists to prevent — one check performed three times under three headings — is invisible in prose and unmistakable in a diff. Step 6 requires the prohibitions instantiated in the student's own case, which is materially harder than reciting them, and is the artefact element enforcing §3.2's scope boundary.

**Phase 6 (40 min).** A wrong hardness claim whose cost was work not attempted, then an agent failure. The instructor's own six months is the disclosure, and its choice is deliberate: it is the only failure class in the module with no artefact, no incident and no evidence of having occurred. **The agent case is this day's §1 problem enacted**: asked to compare two configurations, the agent compared the visible quantity, computed it correctly, and produced a finished-looking argument with an unverified premise. It is the same failure as reduction 3 and the same failure as a student who verifies the transformation and stops, arriving from a third direction on the same day.

**Phase 7 (30 min).** Three attacks in sequence, then which came closest, with attacks settled by execution where the partner's step 4 checks can settle them. Sequencing rather than selecting is what surfaces the weakest requirement rather than the most visible one, and running the checks rather than debating them follows instrumentation rule 1 — the adjudicator is in the room and costs nothing.

**Phase 8 (20 min).** The both-right, one-right, neither-right distribution against item 4 confidence. The cohort's most common overreach is named from step 6 responses, which makes the prohibition concrete. Then item 5's sealed predictions, coded for which requirement students named.

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**Students with more relevant prior knowledge.** A student who has seen reduction before is *more* likely to make the direction error, because the mechanics are familiar and the familiarity substitutes for the argument form. Predicted failure: they construct a fluent transformation at step 3 and treat step 4 as a formality, verifying the transformation three times under three headings. Countermeasure: phase 7's sequenced attack is conducted by a partner who has the same three headings and will find the unverified one; and the specification's weighted element is the separateness of the verification, **which is now checkable by inspection because the three checks are three artefacts.**

**Students with less relevant prior knowledge.** The module's hardest day with two new proof techniques already behind it. Countermeasures: shortlisted source problems, a worked transformation, decomposed verification, three named check shapes, and no formal class vocabulary. Residual risk: step 3's transformation may not be constructible within the time, and the fallback is to complete step 4 through step 7 on a supplied transformation, which preserves the day's actual objective since the objective is the argument rather than the construction.

**Phase 3 is too easy.** Reductions 1 and 2 are usually judged correctly, and reduction 2's rejection is legible immediately. Reduction 3's incomparable scales are caught by many groups once they see four different ranges. Reduction 4 is caught by few. Fallback: ask a group that catches all four to state, for reduction 4, the weaker claim that *does* follow — which is the correct professional response and is harder than identifying the flaw.

**Affective failure.** The characteristic risk is **discouragement at the prohibitions**. A student who has constructed a genuine hardness argument, at real difficulty, is then required to enumerate three things it does not establish — which can read as the day taking back what it just gave. Countermeasure: point 9 and step 7 immediately convert the result into something usable, by asking what a requester should do given it. A hardness result that ends in a recommendation is a contribution; one that ends in a prohibition list is a deflation, and the ordering matters. The instructor is briefed to keep step 7 in view during step 6.

**Three checks that are one check.** New with the platform anchor and predicted to be common. A student who ships three scripts that all exercise the transformation has satisfied the letter of step 4 and none of its purpose. Countermeasure: the specification's second failing clause names this outcome explicitly, and phase 7's partner has the same three headings and is looking for it. The design notes that this failure was always possible in prose and was undetectable there; making the checks executable does not prevent it and does make it findable.

## 6. Retention architecture

Retrieved by S024 (intractability and the duty to say so, one day later, which is this argument delivered to a person, and where the same index supplies an impossibility that is a product fact rather than a theorem), S025 (approximation and stated guarantees, where `document_retrieval`'s remaining four metrics are taken up with a named consumer), S031 (concurrency as a specification problem, where impossibility results appear), S034 (consistency models, where the trade-off is a proved impossibility rather than an engineering limitation), S055 (what these systems cannot do) and S087 (the safety case).

**S024 at one day carries a specific dependency this revision creates.** Point 10 tells students that a service's refusal is not a theorem; S024's first two requests are refusals that are not theorems and must be delivered to a requester as such. A student who has confused the two at S023 writes an S024 reply that overclaims, and point 1 of S024 is where that is caught.

S034 at roughly two quarters is the significant long-range one: a student who has not accepted proved impossibility reads a consistency trade-off as a temporary engineering limitation, which is the misreading the module exists to prevent.

S055 at roughly four quarters is where the three prohibitions matter most — claims about what language models cannot do are made constantly and are usually overreaches of exactly the kind point 6 forbids.

Retained content: the direction, the three requirements, the three prohibitions, and that a transformation discards something you must name. Four items, no constants.

Own schedule: next morning, day 3, week 2, week 6.

## 7. Assessment and evidence generated

No entrustment judgement.

One specification item in CF103, **plus the prompt record, the intervention record and the frontier statement**. **A reduction whose three requirements are not separately verified fails, even if the reduction is correct**, and **three checks that are the same check under three headings fail.** This is the same structural clause as S021's unchecked-hypothesis rule and S016's unnamed-punished-pattern rule: the module and the quarter assess whether the reasoning was done, not whether the answer was right.

**An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that the design could have detected an intervention-worthy error — a generality check that fails loudly below a stated coverage level being the accepted form, per the escape [S052](wiki/seminars/S052.md) established and [WP-048](wiki/whitepapers/WP-048.md) §8 corrected.

Programme instrumentation: the phase 1 direction distribution against item 4 confidence is the cohort's confident-error baseline; step 4's per-requirement verification quality identifies which of the three is habitually skipped, **and a cohort in which generality is habitually skipped triggers S025's phase 3 fallback question about approach D as the remediation**, per instrumentation rules 4 and 6; the phase 8 most-common-overreach is tracked across cohorts and fed into S055's design. **Item 5's sealed prediction accuracy is a frontier-calibration datum read by the following year's cohort** through the [Durable and Perishable Register](wiki/program/09-Durable-and-Perishable-Register.md), per instrumentation rule 9.

The day's phase 3 evidence is a sample and the day says so, per instrumentation rule 3: four reductions selected by staff to instantiate three known failure modes, over one corpus and one label set, with the label set itself covering a portion of the corpus that reduction 4 measures and does not choose.

Dispositions: **humility**, at step 6, where the incentive is to state weak prohibitions that leave the result looking stronger.

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: the three-requirement formulation is a simplification and the day presents it as the definition.**

At its strongest: the technical condition on a reduction is more specific than "efficient and general." It involves a resource bound on the transformation relative to the classes being related, and different reduction types — many-one, Turing, approximation-preserving — have different conditions and license different conclusions. The day collapses this into three checkable properties, which is pedagogically convenient and is not what a reduction requires. Students taught the three-requirement version will believe they can verify a reduction and will not be able to.

The rebuttal is that the three requirements are correct as necessary conditions and that their failure modes are the ones students actually produce. Every error in phase 3 is a real error under any formulation; none of the omitted machinery would change a single verdict. And the day says explicitly at point 3 that it is stating requirements rather than a definition.

The concession is that the day does not name what it omits, which is now the sixth instance of this pattern recorded across this design's whitepapers — after WP-012's three terms, WP-014's five question types, WP-016's two hierarchy properties, WP-022's four guarantee forms, and the general finding WP-016 first stated. At six instances across three modules the pattern is not a series of oversights. It is a **design habit**: this programme consistently produces tractable enumerations and consistently omits the sentence that bounds them. The correct response is no longer a per-day fix but a standard — every enumeration in the wiki should carry its scope statement — and that standard does not exist and should be added to the [Whitepaper Standard](wiki/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md) and the seminar template.

**Objection 2: the professional framing is asserted from practice and is not evidenced.**

At its strongest: the day claims that hardness arguments are what you show a requester who wants a guarantee you cannot give, and builds item 3, point 7 and step 7 on that claim. But engineers very rarely construct reductions in professional practice. The realistic response to an apparently intractable request is to recognise the problem as a known-hard one, cite it, and propose an approximation — which requires recognition, not construction. The day spends its hardest ninety minutes teaching construction and justifies it with a professional scenario in which construction almost never occurs.

This objection has considerable force and the design's response is partial. Recognition of a known-hard problem is genuinely the common case, and the day supports it: point 5's worked argument and step 2's shortlist both operate by recognition against a reference set. What construction adds is the ability to handle the case where the problem is *not* on the reference list, which is the case where the recognition strategy silently fails — a student who cannot construct will conclude that an unrecognised problem is tractable.

What is conceded is that the day's own materials do not evidence the professional scenario. The [research/05-fde-craft/](research/05-fde-craft/collected-materials.md) material supports the general proposition that engineers must decline infeasible requests with reasons; it does not establish that reductions are the form those reasons take. Phase 6's disclosure is a single instructor case. So point 9's framing is a plausible reconstruction of professional practice presented as a description of it, and the honest version would say that the *stance* — you owe a requester an argument, not a refusal — is the durable content, and that the reduction is one form it can take.

**Objection 3: the day's most persuasive moment is a product decision wearing the clothes of a theorem.**

This is the objection that bites on the practical turn and the design finds it the most uncomfortable of the three.

At its strongest: phase 3's reduction 2 is the moment the room stops arguing. A group sets the comparison up the wrong way round, runs it, and is refused — and the refusal is treated, by the students and by the day's own framing, as the direction rule enacted. **It is nothing of the kind.** A field configured for exhaustive search rejects an approximate query because the vendor's indexer does not build a graph for that field, which is an implementation decision made for storage and cost reasons and which could be reversed in a release note by building the graph lazily on first approximate query. The theorem — that a solver for the richer problem yields the poorer one and not the reverse — is true independently of any product and is not what the students watched. **The day has therefore taken its most memorable ninety seconds and pointed them at a claim they do not support**, and the persuasion runs in the dangerous direction: a student who acquired the direction rule from an error message will reach for error messages as evidence about theorems, and will conclude from the next service that permits the reverse that the direction rule has an exception. That is precisely the reasoning failure the module exists to prevent, produced by the module's own demonstration.

The objection is conceded. The rebuttal available — that the day never claims the rejection is a proof — is true of this paper and was not true of the day as first drafted, where the rejection was introduced as "the direction rule with an error message attached" and nothing marked the gap.

**The cheap fix is one clause at point 10 and it is made.** Point 10 now states that the correspondence between the index's asymmetry and a reduction's direction is safe for reasoning about which comparison is available, and **unsafe as evidence that the direction rule is true — the rule is a theorem and the service is an illustration** — and that a student who cites the error message as a proof has mistaken an instance for an argument. Step 6's non-licensed conclusions are where that would be caught in the artefact.

What remains unfixed is recorded rather than repaired, and it is the general form. This is the third quarter-level instance of the same shape: a platform demonstration whose rhetorical force exceeds its evidential force, after [WP-016](wiki/whitepapers/WP-016.md) §8 on the prompt cache and [WP-021](wiki/whitepapers/WP-021.md) §8 on the paradigm taxonomy's coverage. **Three instances in two quarters is a property of the platform turn rather than three local lapses**, and the design's response so far has been a per-day sentence at the last phase 4 point. That is the same per-day-fix pattern the [Whitepaper Standard](wiki/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md)'s scope rule was created to replace, one register up, and the standard-level response — a rule requiring every platform demonstration to state what it is and is not evidence for — does not exist and is not proposed here, because proposing a rule from a whitepaper is how this design accumulated nine instrumentation rules and one collision it had to record rather than tidy.

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** A majority will answer exactly one of items 1 and 2 correctly, and among those, a majority will express confidence at item 4 that they got both right. *Instrument:* paired item accuracy against item 4 responses.

**Prediction 2.** Students with prior reduction exposure will make the direction error at a rate no lower than students without. *Instrument:* item accuracy split by the admission background survey. **A substantially lower rate would mean the error is a knowledge gap rather than a linguistic intuition, and the day's central premise is wrong.**

**Prediction 3.** Generality will be the requirement most often skipped at step 4, and phase 7's closest attack will most often be the generality one. *Instrument:* per-requirement verification quality and phase 7 closest-attack coding.

**Prediction 4.** At S034, students will describe a consistency trade-off as a proved impossibility rather than as an engineering limitation, at a higher rate than a comparison cohort. *Instrument:* an S034 protocol coded for impossibility-versus-limitation framing. Untestable until a variant delivery exists.

**Prediction 5.** A majority of item 5 sealed predictions will describe the comparison the agent builds in general terms, and fewer than a quarter will name a specific requirement it fails to verify. *Instrument:* item 5 responses coded for generic-versus-specific identification against what each student's agent produced. **The measure of interest is not the rate but the movement: the same students' phase 8 restatements are coded on the same scheme, and a cohort whose restatements are no more specific than their predictions has not acquired the vocabulary the day exists to install.**

**Prediction 6.** In step 4, more than a third of first submissions will ship three checks that all exercise the transformation. *Instrument:* blind inspection of the three checks for what each actually varies. **This is the §5 three-checks-that-are-one prediction. A low rate would mean the executable form does the work the prose form could not, which is the platform anchor's strongest claim on this day and the one least supported at present.**

## Evidence status

**Verified in this repository.** The professional obligation to decline infeasible requests with stated reasons is grounded in [research/05-fde-craft/](research/05-fde-craft/collected-materials.md), though the specific claim that reductions are the form this takes is not — see §8. Algorithmic and complexity material follows [research/02-technical-foundations/](research/02-technical-foundations/collected-materials.md). The platform layering and the standing prohibitions follow [research/06-microsoft-platform/](research/06-microsoft-platform/collected-materials.md). Module placement follows [research/01-course-structure/10-gap-analysis-and-course-architecture.md](research/01-course-structure/10-gap-analysis-and-course-architecture.md).

**Grounded in vendor documentation, with its version and its silence recorded.** Phase 3's four reductions and points 7 to 10 are taken from Microsoft Learn's Azure AI Search vector-ranking and hybrid-ranking pages and the Foundry RAG-evaluators page, read 2026-07-26. What the documentation states: a field configured for the approximate algorithm supports an exhaustive query through a query-time parameter, and **"fields indexed for `exhaustiveKnn` don't support HNSW queries because the extra data structures that enable efficient search don't exist"**; rank fusion is applied whenever two or more queries execute in parallel; each document's contribution is the reciprocal of its rank position plus a constant, and "experiments show the algorithm performs best when you set `k` to a small value, such as 60"; the fused score's upper bound is bounded by the number of queries being fused, so "merging three queries produces higher RRF scores than if only two search results are merged"; a text query plus one vector query targeting two vector fields is three query executions; full-text scores have no upper limit, vector scores fall in a bounded band, and the semantic reranking score is reported in a separate field on a nought-to-four scale; and the `document_retrieval` evaluator returns, alongside its ranking metrics, a **Holes** metric defined as the "number of documents with missing query relevance judgments, or ground truth," categorised by the documentation as label sanity.

**Three silences are recorded and one is taught.** The experiments said to establish the fusion constant are **not cited, described or dated**, and no benchmark, metric or interval accompanies the recommendation — which makes it a claim of exactly the form S025 phase 1 teaches students to identify as guaranteeing nothing, appearing in the documentation of the mechanism this day analyses; the day does not repeat it as fact and point 8 names the silence to students. The documentation states no guidance on **how a threshold should be re-derived when the number of fused queries changes**, which is the practical consequence of the score-ceiling behaviour it does document, and the gap is the reason point 8 states the consequence explicitly. And the `document_retrieval` evaluator's documentation states its metric definitions and **no agreement between any of its judged companions and human judgement**, consistent with the folder-wide asymmetry recorded in [research/06-microsoft-platform/](research/06-microsoft-platform/collected-materials.md); this day uses only the deterministic Holes count and defers the judged metrics to S025, where the uncalibrated status is taught in terms.
