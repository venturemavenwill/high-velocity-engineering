# WP-041 — The Minimal Valuable Slice

**Seminar day:** [S041](/wiki/seminars/S041.md) · **Module:** [M09](/wiki/modules/M09.md) · **Quarter:** [Q5](/wiki/quarters/Q5.md)
**Strands:** PE · ME · **Complexity class:** 3
**Platform anchor:** Microsoft Foundry prompt agents — the smallest deployable unit the platform offers, and an explicit account of what it excludes

## In plain terms

Stage 4 ended with somebody funding a proof. Stage 5 begins by spending the money, and the first decision is what to build first.

**The answer that arrives naturally is the part that is easiest to start**, which is almost never the part that is most worth having. Foundations, scaffolding, a data layer, an authentication flow: all necessary, none of them receivable by anybody outside the delivery team, and therefore incapable of changing what anybody believes.

The session's move is to make the **exclusion list** the artefact rather than the increment. Anybody can describe what they are going to build. The professional act is saying what they are not going to build, to whom, and why — and then holding it while a fortnight of ordinary pressure argues the other way.

Two things make the session harder than it sounds.

**The recipient has to be able to refuse.** An increment received only by the team that made it is activity with better packaging, and the corpus this stage rests on is unusually good on exactly this point: everything short of realised value is activity, including the architecture, the fundamentals and the deployment.

**The freeze has to be tested.** Constraints in a customer estate come in two kinds — decisions somebody made, and assumptions everybody inherited — and they are indistinguishable until somebody asks who would have to agree to change one. **An untested freeze is a rumour**, and a slice shaped by a rumour is smaller than it needed to be for no reason anybody can name.

## Abstract

This paper defends opening the build stage with a slice definition whose assessed content is its exclusion list. The argument has three parts: that an increment nobody outside the delivery team can receive cannot change a belief and therefore cannot test anything; that the natural first increment is selected for ease of starting rather than for value, and that the selection is invisible unless it is asked about before instruction; and that the constraints shaping a slice are a mixture of decisions and inherited assumptions which must be separated by asking who would have to agree to move them. The session installs a one-page slice definition with a named recipient, a dated exclusion list, a frozen-and-fluid separation and a residual-value statement. The paper concedes that no source establishes that small increments outperform large ones, that the practice corpus behind the stage reports no failed delivery of any size, and that the exclusion-list-as-artefact claim is this design's own and appears in no source.

## 1. The problem this session must solve

A commitment has been obtained and the engagement now has a burn rate. Every incentive present favours starting on whatever can be started.

**Three of those incentives are individually reasonable.** Foundational work genuinely is required eventually. Beginning where the ground is firm genuinely does reduce early risk. And a team that produces nothing visible in the first fortnight genuinely does lose credibility. The composition of the three produces a first increment that is defensible in every particular and receivable by nobody.

There is a second problem, which the first conceals. **The constraint environment is treated as given.** A forward-deployed engineer arrives into an estate with an architecture, a release process, a security posture and a set of platform choices they were not consulted about, and the professional default is to design within all of them. The corpus's frozen-and-fluid classification exists precisely because that default is wrong often enough to matter: even in an almost entirely frozen space there is something that can be changed, and **constraint is not an excuse for doing nothing.**

The session's response is to require a named recipient who could refuse, an exclusion list with reasons, one tested freeze, and a statement of what survives if the engagement stops.

## 2. Entry state and prior knowledge

**Entry state: either.** The concept is enterable from ordinary professional experience — most engineers have scoped a first release — and the substrate is the programme's own. A learner arriving with experience of release scoping will recognise the activity and will not recognise the exclusion list, which is the part this session adds.

It assumes [S021](/wiki/seminars/S021.md)'s requirement-as-business-statement, because a slice is cut against a requirement that names an experience rather than a technology. It assumes [S026](/wiki/seminars/S026.md)'s frozen-and-fluid classification, which is the session's main inherited instrument. And it assumes the commitment from [S039](/wiki/seminars/S039.md) and the load-bearing-evidence identification from [S040](/wiki/seminars/S040.md), because *minimal* is meaningless without something to be minimal against.

The prior the session works against is that the first increment is a planning problem. Learners arrive expecting to sequence work. **They are asked instead to refuse work**, which is a different act with a different failure mode.

## 3. Theoretical basis

**3.1 Load profile.** Intrinsic load is low. The surplus is spent on the affective work of writing down something you wanted to build and are not going to, which is the session's actual difficulty and is why the artefact is one page.

**3.2 Why the exclusion list rather than the increment.** A description of what will be built is compatible with almost any subsequent behaviour, because anything additional can be presented as part of the plan all along. **A written exclusion converts a later addition into a visible departure.** This is the same mechanism [S042](/wiki/seminars/S042.md) applies to scope and [S045](/wiki/seminars/S045.md) assesses as dated departures.

**3.3 Why the recipient must be able to refuse.** An artefact whose audience cannot decline it produces no information. This is the argument [S038](/wiki/seminars/S038.md) made about prototype testing populations, arriving here about delivery, and it is the same claim: **a test that could not have failed was not a test.**

**3.4 Why a freeze must be tested by naming who could move it.** The distinction between a decision and an inherited assumption is not visible in the constraint's content — both look like *we cannot do that here.* It is visible in whether anybody can name the authority. **The question is diagnostic rather than confrontational**, and it is asked of colleagues rather than of the customer.

**3.5 Why residual value is asked in the negative.** *What would remain if we stopped* is answerable; *what value will this create* invites forecasting, and the corpus behind this stage demonstrates at length what happens to a practice that forecasts value and never measures it.

**3.6 Why the generation phase precedes instruction, and why exercise 3 is a comparison rather than a re-reading.**

The five opening items are not a warm-up. They are a generation phase in the productive-failure sense: learners attempt a problem they cannot yet solve well, and the attempt is what makes the subsequent instruction discriminable. The mechanism the design depends on is narrower than the popular version of it, and the narrowness is the whole reason exercise 3 is written the way it is. **Presenting a canonical answer beside a learner's own wrong one produced no benefit; only the group instructed to compare the two learned more** ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). The design therefore never asks a learner to read a criterion. It asks them to name the difference between the criterion and what they wrote.

The design also uses this to protect one instrument that cannot be recovered later. **Opening item 3 — *what were you about to build first because it was easy to begin* — is unavailable after instruction**, because once the session has named the failure nobody reports having been about to commit it. It is asked first and submitted, and §9's first prediction is about it.

**Limits:** school, university and laboratory evidence, with no adult-professional study in its reference list. This design's transfer of the mechanism to working engineers is an assumption the programme makes repeatedly and has never tested.

**3.7 Why the value test is a language test, and why the source's hedges are carried rather than dropped.**

The session needs a check on *valuable* that a learner can apply without the customer in the room, on the day the slice is cut. The design literature supplies one, and it is a language test: build the model from the domain experts' own jargon **"cleaned up," to have sharper, narrower definitions**, with the experts holding a veto if the cleaning diverges from accepted field meaning; and then **"If sophisticated domain experts don't understand the model, there is something wrong with the model"** ([research/05-fde-craft/evans-domain-driven-design.md](/research/05-fde-craft/evans-domain-driven-design.md)).

Both hedges in that sentence are load-bearing and both are routinely dropped when it is quoted. The test is against *sophisticated* experts, not any expert; and technical components of the design are explicitly exempt. **A design that dropped either hedge would be requiring every implementation detail to be legible to a non-engineer, which the source does not ask and this session does not either.**

The same book supplies the worked failure the domain sentence exists to catch. A project organised its design around a term that "was not a banking term", which the business experts said several times they did not understand and accepted anyway because "they had deferred to our software knowledge". **The experts noticed, said so, and were overruled by politeness** — which is a social failure with an architectural consequence, and it is exactly the failure a one-sentence artefact tested on one non-engineer will surface in a minute.

**Limits:** a professional pattern book rather than a study. It **declares its own prerequisites** — iterative development and a close developer-expert relationship — and makes no claim without them; its publisher and date were inherited from this repository's reading register and not verified, because the copy read carries no package metadata; and its own conclusion follows up five of the author's projects and reports one as a partial failure with a named cause. **It offers no comparative evidence of any kind.**

**3.8 Why *how will we know* is asked at the slice and not at the specification.**

Deferring the evaluation question to [S043](/wiki/seminars/S043.md) would be tidier and would be wrong, because by then the slice is chosen and the question can only ratify it. The practitioner literature states the discipline directly — **"Before investing time, money, and resources into building an application, it's important to understand how this application will be evaluated"**, named *evaluation-driven development* by analogy with writing tests before code ([research/03-measurement-evaluation/huyen-ai-engineering.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/huyen-ai-engineering.md)).

The design takes the idea and its author's own objection together, and the objection is the reason the slice definition does not contain a measurement plan. Focusing only on applications whose outcomes can be measured **"is similar to looking for the lost key under the lamppost (at night)"**. **A slice chosen because it is measurable is the same failure as a slice chosen because it is startable**, so the artefact asks only for an honest statement of how much of the value will be visible — not for a metric, which would convert the heuristic into a filter.

**Limits:** a practitioner book, first edition December 2024. Its author states the strong form of her evaluation claim explicitly as a belief rather than a finding, and this paper does not upgrade the hedge.

**3.9 Why the ordering claim is taught as a diagnosis rather than as a process.**

The internal delivery standard behind this stage sequences an engagement so that understanding precedes configuration, configuration precedes requirements, and the technical specification is **derived** from the business one rather than authored beside it ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-fde-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-fde-delivery-practice.md)). The design does not adopt the sequence, because the sequence is one organisation's intention and nothing establishes that it is right.

What the design adopts is the diagnosis attached to it: **a crew that writes the technical specification first has committed to an architecture before it understood the problem**, and the ordering rule exists to make the commitment visible rather than arguable. That converts cleanly into a checkable property of a learner's own repository — which artefact was committed first — and the workflow's opening step exists to create that evidence.

**Limits:** internal, paraphrased here and never linked or quoted by label. **It is a plan for how work will be done, reporting no outcome, no evaluation and no comparison against any other way of working**; its phase durations are planning figures and its automation shares are assertions.

**3.10 Why a delivery corpus with no failures is still usable, and where its usability stops.**

The organising thesis this session opens on — that everything short of realised value is activity, including the architecture and the deployment — comes from a corpus that **reports no failed engagement, no cost side and no post-deployment record of any kind** ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-accelerated-delivery-practice.md)). A body of work whose central claim is that only measurable benefit counts supports itself almost entirely with measures of activity.

The design uses it anyway, for one reason and within one boundary. **The thesis is closer to a definition than to a finding**, and definitions do not need outcome data: an increment nobody outside the delivery team can receive cannot change a belief, and an increment that changes nobody's belief has tested nothing. That argument stands on its own. **What the corpus cannot support is any claim that slicing this way produces better deliveries**, and neither this paper nor the session makes one.

## 4. Session design and its warrant

The seven movements run situation, artefact, standard, failure modes, work, review, limits.

**Opening item 3 is the instrument.** *What were you about to build first because it was easy to begin?* is asked before instruction, and the answer is unavailable afterwards — once the session has named the failure, nobody will report having been about to commit it. **Item 5 is the second instrument** and the design's expectation is that most learners answer it with things that would have been learned, which is a proof answer arriving one stage late.

**The exclusion list is required to be longer than the inclusion list, with an escape clause.** The comparison is a heuristic rather than a rule and the escape clause concedes it, in the same form [S040](/wiki/seminars/S040.md) uses for its unproven list.

**One exclusion must be painful.** Without that clause the list fills with things nobody wanted, and a list of unwanted omissions is a description of the increment written backwards.

**The freeze test is bounded to one constraint.** Testing every constraint is a discovery activity and Stage 2 is over; testing one is a habit.

**The agent is used adversarially and then instrumentally in the same movement**, which is the first time the programme does both in one session. It argues against the slice, and then it is asked the corpus's own better question — where does the recipient's system lose time — rather than the question the corpus warns against, which is how to produce more code. **The pairing is deliberate preparation for [M10](/wiki/modules/M10.md)**, where the two uses have to coexist.

**4.1 What the platform workflow is for, and why the smallest deployable unit is the right instrument.**

Not build-path knowledge, which will be stale within the year. The workflow exists to make one abstract claim physically true in an afternoon: **that the boundary of a slice is a real boundary and not a rhetorical one.**

The platform publishes two agent shapes and the line between them is a compute line rather than a capability line. Prompt agents use "declarative config in portal or code" and are "managed by Foundry with **no agent compute**"; hosted agents run "your code (containerized)" on managed container compute ([research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/microsoft-foundry-and-copilot-studio-docs.md)). **That published limit is the pedagogical asset.** A slice that needs logic outside configuration does not degrade on this platform; it stops, and the stop is observable rather than arguable. A learner who has met the wall once will recognise a hosting decision as an architecture decision for the rest of their career.

Two further published facts were chosen deliberately because they belong on an exclusion list. The **basic setup** is documented as being for rapid prototyping and, in the vendor's own words, **"lacks network isolation"**; the standard setup provides "fine-grained control over data, security, and networking". **The design's use of that is not *always choose standard*** — it is that prototyping topology and production topology are different things and the transition between them is a deliberate act, which is exactly the class of decision the exclusion list was invented to hold. And the vendor states the options are **"not mutually exclusive"**, which frequently converts *which platform for this system* into *which shape for this component* — a smaller and more honest question.

**The anchor's second purpose is to make an exclusion cost something.** A slice definition written on paper can exclude anything. A slice definition that has been deployed has already discovered which of its exclusions were real, because the platform enforced some of them without being asked.

**The limits are heavy and travel with every use.** First-party selection guidance whose authority is definitional; **no adoption data, no failure-rate data, no cost comparison and no evidence that its recommended mappings produce better outcomes**; single-vendor by construction; **read through search excerpts rather than full page fetches, with several comparison tables truncated**; and highly perishable, with product names and capability boundaries actively changing. **The workflow is assessed on the reasoning it produces and never on the configuration.**

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**The foundation slice.** The likeliest outcome. *Countermeasure:* the named recipient, plus the standard's warning sign — your recipient is a future version of yourself.

**The whole-thing slice.** The learner concludes that nothing smaller than the system is valuable. *Countermeasure:* the residual-value statement, which forces the question *what would remain* rather than *what would work*, and usually produces something smaller than the system.

**The easy slice defended afterwards.** *Countermeasure:* item 3, asked before instruction and submitted, which makes the retrospective defence visible against the learner's own earlier answer.

**The frozen-space surrender.** The learner produces a trivial slice and attributes it to constraint. *Countermeasure:* the tested freeze, and the standard's requirement that the definition propose more than it explains.

**The exclusion list of unwanted things.** *Countermeasure:* the painful-exclusion clause. **This is the design's weakest countermeasure**, because *painful* is self-reported and unverifiable, and §8 concedes it.

**The demonstration slice.** *Countermeasure:* the requirement that the recipient be able to refuse, which a demonstration cannot satisfy because nobody refuses a demonstration.

## 7. Assessment and evidence generated

No entrustment evidence. The slice definition is submitted at [S045](/wiki/seminars/S045.md) as a constituent of the increment pack, where three of its properties are assessed: that departures from it are marked and dated, that its recipient was in a position to refuse, and that its exclusions carry a current status.

**The definition itself is not marked at this session.** Whether the slice was the right slice is not marked anywhere in the stage, and [WP-045](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-045.md) §8 defends that exclusion.

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: requiring a receivable increment first is a luxury of demonstration projects, and in real estates the first four weeks are unavoidably infrastructure.**

At its strongest: in a regulated environment, a first increment cannot reach a user until identity, network placement, data residency and change approval are all resolved, none of which is negotiable and none of which produces anything anybody can receive. A session that treats *your recipient is a future version of yourself* as a warning sign is teaching graduates to feel bad about the correct behaviour, and the first time they act on it they will ship something into an estate that was not ready for it.

**Substantially conceded, and it is the sharpest objection to this session.** Two partial answers. The frozen-and-fluid instrument exists exactly for this case, and the honest output of applying it to a hard estate is a slice definition whose fluid column is nearly empty and whose residual-value statement is small and true. **That is a passing artefact**, and the session says so. And the recipient requirement is satisfiable by any person outside the delivery team who can decline, including an operator or a security reviewer, not only an end user. **The declined fix is to add an exemption for constrained estates.** It is declined because an exemption becomes the default explanation within one cohort, and the objection's real content — that some first increments genuinely cannot reach a user — is better served by requiring the learner to state it than by pre-authorising it.

**Objection 2: the exclusion list is unfalsifiable as an assessed artefact, because a learner can exclude things nobody was going to build.**

At its strongest: the requirement that one exclusion be *painful* is self-reported. Nothing distinguishes a genuinely relinquished capability from a plausible-sounding omission, and the review movement asks a peer for expectations rather than for verification. The element rewards the learner who can imagine convincingly.

**Conceded.** The mitigation is narrow: [S045](/wiki/seminars/S045.md) assesses the exclusion list's **current status** rather than its content, so an exclusion that was never going to be built and was never built is indistinguishable from a real one at this session and becomes visible across the stage only if it is later built without being marked as a departure. **The declined fix is to require the exclusion list to be countersigned by the customer.** It is declined because it converts a working artefact into a contractual one, and a countersigned exclusion list will contain only what the customer had already agreed to lose, which is the opposite of the instrument's purpose.

**Objection 3: anchoring the session to one vendor's smallest deployable unit teaches a product boundary and calls it an engineering principle.**

At its strongest: the claim the workflow is supposed to make physically true — that a slice has a real boundary — is demonstrated by a limit that exists because a commercial platform drew a billing line between compute and no compute. A different vendor would draw it elsewhere; the same vendor may draw it elsewhere next year, and the documentation this paper cites was **read through search excerpts with several comparison tables truncated** and is described in this repository's own reading as the most perishable material in the research set. **A learner who takes the lesson will have learned that prompt agents cannot run code, which is a fact with a shelf life, and may not have learned anything about boundaries at all.** Worse, the design uses the vendor's published limit as evidence for a pedagogical claim while conceding elsewhere that the same documentation publishes no outcome data of any kind — so the anchor is authoritative exactly where it is definitional and silent exactly where it would matter.

**Partly conceded, and the perishability half is conceded entirely.** The rebuttal is that the design does not use the platform as evidence for anything. It uses it as an **occasion**: the boundary is real for the duration of the afternoon, and what the learner is assessed on is the exclusion list they wrote when they met it, not the reason the boundary was there. That is why the session's perishability register puts the compute line in the right-hand column and the durable claim — a hosting choice is an architecture decision taken by whoever provisioned first — in the left. **The declined fix is to drop the platform anchor and run the session on paper**, which is what the previous version of this design did. It is declined because a paper exclusion list costs nothing to write and therefore excludes nothing, and the whole session turns on an exclusion that hurts. **The cost is real: some learners will remember the product boundary and not the principle, and prediction 6 is written to detect exactly that.**

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** Answers to opening item 3 will be blank or generic for most learners before instruction, and specific for a minority who have shipped under constraint before. *Instrument:* the written answers, coded *specific* / *generic* / *blank* by a second reader, against declared prior experience.

**Prediction 2.** Most first slice definitions will name a recipient inside the delivery team or a future user with no name. *Instrument:* the recipient field, coded *external and named* / *external and generic* / *internal*, by a second reader.

**Prediction 3.** Learners who test a freeze will produce larger slices than those who do not. *Instrument:* the presence of a completed freeze test against the slice's inclusion count, paired per learner. **If this fails, the freeze test is a ceremony** and the movement should be reduced to a written question.

**Prediction 4.** Exclusion lists will shorten between this session and [S045](/wiki/seminars/S045.md), through items being built rather than through items being promoted to the backlog. *Instrument:* the list at both points, with each departure classified *built* / *promoted* / *still excluded* by a second reader.

**Prediction 5.** Learners who deploy the prompt agent and meet the compute boundary will produce exclusion lists containing at least one platform-derived entry, and learners who complete the session on paper will not. *Instrument:* the exclusion lists, split on whether a deployment was completed, coded for the presence of an entry naming a capability the platform declined to provide. **If this fails, the workflow is decoration** and the session should be run on paper as its previous version was.

**Prediction 6.** A minority of learners will be able to restate the durable claim — that a hosting choice is an architecture decision taken by whoever provisioned first — at [S045](/wiki/seminars/S045.md), while a majority will restate the perishable product fact that prompt agents cannot run code. *Instrument:* a single free-text question at the assessment occasion, coded *durable* / *perishable* / *both* by a second reader. **This predicts the failure §8's third objection names**, and it is included because a countermeasure whose failure rate is unmeasured is an assumption.

**Prediction 7.** Slice definitions whose domain sentence was tested on a non-engineer will contain fewer invented terms at [S045](/wiki/seminars/S045.md) than those whose sentence was not tested. *Instrument:* the domain sentence at both points, with invented terms marked by a second reader, paired per learner. **A null result would mean the test is ceremony** and the artefact should lose the field rather than keep it unexamined.

## What a learner should take from this paper

**What the design claims.** That an increment nobody outside the team can receive cannot change a belief; that the natural first increment is chosen for ease of starting; and that an untested constraint is a rumour.

**What the evidence licenses.** Nothing about outcomes. No source behind this stage compares small increments with large ones, and the practice corpus reports no failed delivery at any size against which a slicing discipline could be evaluated.

**The concession worth remembering.** In a genuinely constrained estate the correct first increment may be one nobody outside the team can receive, and this session's warning signs will fire on the right answer. Hold the instrument, not the verdict.
