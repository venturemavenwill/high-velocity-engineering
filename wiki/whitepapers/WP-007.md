## In plain terms

**What this paper argues, before the formal version.**

Everything students are told about splitting up a program is a matter of taste. Keep functions short. Separate concerns. Do not repeat yourself. Give things good names. All four are reasonable and none of them settles anything, because you can hand somebody two completely different designs that both satisfy all four. So people choose by instinct, defend by instinct, and — the part that matters — **can never be shown to have been wrong**, because taste is not the sort of thing evidence touches.

This day replaces the taste with a claim about the future: *these things will change together, those will not.* That converts a design into a bet. A bet can be wrong, and something that can be wrong is something you can get better at.

Then it settles the bet in front of you, using a change that actually happened to the programme's own system rather than one an instructor invented — because a change chosen by somebody who has already seen your three options is a rigged experiment and students are right to discount it. You commit a prediction, the change arrives, you count what you had to touch, and you say where your reasoning was wrong.

The same argument is then run on the repository's own configuration files, where the count is exact and the loop is short. **The better of the two numbers is not how many files you touched but how many conventions you had to repeat in the prompt because no file was carrying them** — because that counts what the design failed to hold, which a file count cannot see.

**What this paper concedes, in the same plain terms.** The configuration surface has no defined composition. Multiple instruction files are combined in an order the documentation explicitly declines to guarantee, and a tool named but unavailable is ignored without saying so. Under those conditions, splitting the surface does not obviously reduce anything — it multiplies the places where an undefined interaction can happen — and **counting files touched is exactly blind to that, because an undefined interaction touches nothing.** The paper adds one artefact requirement in response and states plainly that it does not repair the adjudicator: within one session the day cannot show you that a twelve-file surface is worse than a two-file one, because within one session it is not.

## Abstract

This paper defends replacing aesthetic decomposition criteria with a single cost model — a seam's cost is the cost of the changes that cross it — and defends the commit-then-adjudicate structure that appears three times in the day, once at phase 3's written contrast, once at phase 3's executed contrast, and once across phase 5. The argument for the cost model is that aesthetic criteria are jointly satisfiable by decompositions with radically different change costs, which makes them unable to adjudicate the decisions students actually face; the argument for commit-then-adjudicate is that a prediction recorded before evidence arrives converts an opinion into a testable claim, and that the reconciliation step is where the criterion is acquired rather than merely heard. The paper further defends using the substrate's real change history as the adjudicator, on the grounds that a designer-chosen change request is a rigged experiment which students correctly discount, and defends extending the same reasoning to the repository's own customization surface, where the adjudication is mechanical and the touch count is exact. It concedes that the day risks teaching an availability heuristic in place of a cost model, that its central reconciliation step is contaminated by hindsight in a way the design has not solved, and — the objection that bites hardest on the practical turn — that the surface it now asks students to decompose has no defined composition, so the cost model is being applied to a substrate that does not support the modular reasoning the model assumes.

## 1. The instructional problem this day must solve

Decomposition instruction is almost entirely aesthetic. Students are told to keep functions short, to separate concerns, to avoid repeating themselves, and to give things good names. Every one of these is defensible and none of them adjudicates. Presented with two decompositions of the same program, both of which have short functions, separated concerns and no repetition, the student has no ground on which to prefer one. So they choose by taste, defend by taste, and — critically — cannot be shown to have been wrong, because taste is not the kind of claim that admits evidence.

The consequence is not that students decompose badly. Many decompose well by instinct. The consequence is that they cannot *reason* about it, which means they cannot improve, cannot justify a choice to a colleague who prefers a different one, and cannot recognise when a previously good seam has become bad.

The cost model fixes this by making decomposition a claim about the future: *these things will change together, those will not*. That claim is wrong sometimes, which is the point — it is the kind of thing that can be wrong, and therefore the kind of thing that can be learned from.

The day's second problem is structural and belongs to the programme rather than to the topic. From S026 onward, every measurement day has the same shape: commit a prediction, obtain evidence, reconcile the difference. That shape is unfamiliar and uncomfortable, because it produces documented instances of the student having been wrong. Introducing it in week six on a topic with no numerical content and no assessment stake means that by S026 the shape is a routine rather than a novelty, and the student's capacity can go to the measurement rather than to the discomfort.

The third problem is one the module needs solved before S009. Testing as hypothesis requires a claim to test. A student who cannot state what their design predicts has nothing for a test to falsify, and S009 will collapse into coverage counting. Phase 5 step 3 — state the bet — is the first time students are required to name what their own work commits them to.

The fourth problem is practical and is why the day now runs against the repository's own configuration. A cost model whose evidence arrives once, at the end of a ninety-minute task, on a component the student will not touch again, is a cost model students will believe and not use. **The customization surface supplies the same cost model with a shorter loop and an exact adjudicator:** how many files did the convention change touch, and how many conventions did you have to restate in the prompt because no file carried them. The second number is the day's new instrument and it is the better of the two, because it counts what the decomposition *failed* to hold rather than what it happened to contain — which is the thing a touch count in code cannot see.

- **A working invariant vocabulary** from S006, two days old, with the interface location available. Phase 4 point 1 depends on this and does not re-teach it.
- **Universal decomposition instinct, near-universal absence of criterion.** Every student decomposes; almost none can say what a decomposition costs.
- **The S003 cost habit,** now on its fourth appearance and expected without prompting.
- **The S004 rejection experience,** which is why phase 7's adversarial construction needs no framing.
- **No experience of recording a prediction that will be adjudicated.** This is the day's genuinely new structural demand and it is scaffolded by appearing twice, first briefly in phase 3 and then at length in phase 5.
- **Substantial variance in codebase-size experience.** Students who have only written programs they finished in a week have never experienced a seam becoming wrong.

## 3. Theoretical basis for the design

### 3.1 Load profile and element interactivity

**Moderate, and deliberately lower than S006's.** This is a considered placement. S006 and S008 are both high-interactivity invariant-reasoning days, and putting S007 between them gives the module a load trough at its midpoint. The day's individual elements — a seam, a change, a touch count — are simple and can be considered independently; the interactivity lives only in the relation between the decomposition and the change set, and that relation is made concrete rather than hypothetical by supplying actual changes.

Load management is nonetheless active in three places.

- **Phase 3 supplies all three decompositions.** Groups compare and rank; they do not construct. Construction is deferred to phase 5, after the criterion exists.
- **The change request is a single change,** not a set. Reasoning about a distribution of possible futures is class-3 work and appears at S039.
- **The phase 5 component is small and the change history is supplied in summary,** with implementations withheld. Students read descriptions and dates, not diffs, which removes a large quantity of comprehension load while preserving the predictive task.

The extraneous-load risk the design accepts is that step 5's implementation may consume more of the ninety minutes than intended, leaving step 6 rushed. Step 6 is the phase's payload, so the instructor is briefed to time-box step 4 hard and to accept incomplete implementations.

### 3.2 Desirable difficulties selected, and those deliberately not

**Selected.**

- *Committing a ranking before the adjudicator arrives,* phase 3. The day's core mechanism in miniature.
- *Stating the bet,* phase 5 step 3 — naming what you have made expensive. Substantially harder than naming what you made cheap, and the step students would omit.
- *The counterfactual reconciliation,* step 6. Where would the seam have had to be? This is harder than judging whether the bet was right.
- *The partner's exploiting change,* phase 7, followed by a decision the student must defend.
- *Item 3 in the pretest,* asked before instruction and answerable by almost nobody.

**Deliberately not selected.**

- *Constructing decompositions in phase 3.* Refused; comparison precedes construction.
- *Withholding the cost sentence.* Phase 4 point 2 states it plainly. It is a criterion, and a criterion withheld is a criterion not used.
- *A distribution of future changes.* Class-3 work, refused here.
- *Large-scale code.* The component is small enough that touch-counting is exact rather than approximate, because an approximate adjudicator would let students argue with the evidence.
- *Enabling the settings that would make cases B and D behave as expected.* Refused. Turning on nested `AGENTS.md` discovery before phase 3 would remove the day's sharpest finding, which is that a decomposition can be inert.
- *Prompt files and custom agents as objects to decompose.* Named at phase 4 point 8 and not built. They are seams on a different axis — procedure and permission rather than convention — and building all four surfaces in one day would move the difficulty into configuration.
- *Assessment stakes on the same day.*

**The strong-form scope statement, and where it binds.** *A seam's cost is the cost of the changes that cross it* is safe wherever the crossing can be counted — source files, configuration files, instructions files — and it is the criterion students should use for every decomposition decision this quarter. **It is unsafe in two conditions.** First, where the seam is a trust boundary: a custom agent's tool list is a seam defined by what may be done, and moving it to reduce change cost widens what the agent may do, which is a cost the touch count does not contain. Second, where the seam's cost falls on someone who is not editing — an operator, a reviewer, an on-call engineer. Both are taken up at [S073](/wiki/seminars/S073.md) and [S089](/wiki/seminars/S089.md) and neither is in scope today. The boundary is bound to the artefact rather than left in this paper: **a student who moves a tool-list seam on change-cost grounds alone fails the specification**, which is the widened form of the scope rule recorded in the [Whitepaper Standard](/wiki/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md).

### 3.3 Engagement and motivational mechanism

The day runs on **a bet with a resolution inside the same session**, and that is unusual enough in a curriculum to be a hook by itself.

Students commit a prediction in phase 3 and find out within forty-five minutes. They commit a larger one in phase 5 step 1 and find out within ninety. The interval between commitment and resolution is short enough to sustain attention and long enough that the commitment is genuine. Very little else in an undergraduate programme has this structure, and the reported affect is competitive in a mild and productive way.

The design's most important motivational decision is that **the adjudicating change is real**. Students discount a designer-chosen change request, correctly, because the designer chose it knowing the three decompositions. Drawing step 5's change from the substrate's actual subsequent history removes that objection entirely, and students notice. The half-predicted-it, half-did-not calibration is deliberate: a change nobody predicted teaches that prediction is futile, and a change everybody predicted teaches nothing.

Competence is protected by phase 6's disclosure, which establishes that a seam can be correct when drawn and wrong later. Without that, step 6's honest reconciliation is an admission of incompetence and students will not make it.

Autonomy sits at phase 8, where the student names a seam in their own work and decides whether to move it — and *not moving it* is an acceptable answer if the cost is stated.

## 4. Phase-by-phase design and its warrant

**Phase 1 (25 min).** Five items on an eighty-line function. Item 3 is the day's thesis in interrogative form and is asked before instruction precisely so that its unanswerability is the students' finding. Item 4 makes item 3's outcome a calibration event rather than a failure.

**Phase 2 (20 min).** Free recall with a deliberately *answerable* bridging item, in contrast to S006's deliberately unanswerable one. Two consecutive impossible bridges would teach the cohort that the final item is not worth attempting, which would destroy an instrument the programme uses for three years.

**Phase 3 (45 min).** Three decompositions, one problem, a committed ranking, then a delayed adjudicator; then four decompositions of the customization surface, a committed touch-count prediction, and an adjudicator that is a running system rather than an argument. The mechanism is contrasting cases plus prediction-evidence-reconciliation, and the reconciliation question — *what did your criterion actually predict, and what did it miss* — is the phase's content. Decomposition C, split by expected change, usually wins the written half, and the design is careful that it does not always win, because a phase where the principled option always wins teaches a rule rather than a method. **The executed half's payload is case D**, which is the same lesson delivered by a mechanism no ranking could have reached: the split-by-folder configuration touches one file, looks best on every criterion the room has, and is not running. Case B supplies the second: general-to-specific layering has no guaranteed order, so the refinement wins or loses without a rule. All four cases are operated; the prerequisite is a Copilot subscription and a workspace folder.

**Phase 4 (50 min).** Ten points. Point 1 makes the day continuous with S006 rather than adjacent to it. Point 2 is the single retained sentence. Point 5's claim that the correct response to a wrong bet is to move the seam is what licenses step 6's honesty. Point 6 introduces the one asymmetry — some seams cannot be moved — which is the day's only conservative advice and is deliberately narrow. Points 8 and 9 consolidate the executed half rather than asserting it, per instrumentation rule 7, and point 9's parent-repository default is the day's subject stated by the vendor: a seam that was correct when drawn and became wrong when the working directory changed. Point 10 is the strong-form scope statement.

**Phase 5 (90 min).** Six steps with real change history, now spanning both the component and its customization surface. Steps 1 to 3 are the commitment, step 5 is the evidence, step 6 is the reconciliation. Step 3 is the hardest and is the one that S009 depends on. The withheld implementations in step 1 are a deliberate load decision and also a validity one: a student who reads the diffs is not predicting. **The second touch count at step 5 — conventions restated in the prompt because no file carried them — is the day's most defensible instrument**, because it is exact, it is produced as a by-product of doing the work, and unlike step 6 it is immune to the narration problem at §8 objection 2.

**Phase 6 (40 min).** The instructor's seam that was right for eight months. The disclosure's specific content — the original decision was correct when it was made — is what makes step 6 safe, and it is stated before phase 5 begins as well as during phase 6. The second, agent case is the same classification with a client default rather than a product change as the cause, and it is chosen because two reviewers approved the result: the failure is legible only from outside the diff.

**Phase 7 (30 min).** Adversarial construction against the student's stated bet, plus a decision. The decision half is the day's only moment where the student chooses under a cost they have named, which is a preview of class 2 and is deliberately kept off the artefact so that it stays a conversation.

**Phase 8 (20 min).** Items 3 and 4 resolved together. The unattributed touch-count distribution is shown because the spread is the point: identical instructions, identical component, very different outcomes, and the difference is the bet.

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**Students with more relevant prior knowledge.** A student who has maintained a codebase over years will have an implicit cost model already and may find phase 3 obvious. The predicted failure is that they win the exercise and learn nothing, because their existing intuition suffices and is never made explicit. Countermeasure: phase 5 step 3 — name what you made expensive — is not served by intuition, and experienced students fail it at similar rates to inexperienced ones, because intuition optimises without articulating. Phase 7's partner is briefed to press hardest on students whose bets are vague.

**Students with less relevant prior knowledge.** A student who has never seen a seam become wrong is being asked to predict change in a system they have known for six weeks. Countermeasures: the change history is supplied with dates and descriptions, so prediction is inference from evidence rather than from experience; and phase 3's ranking task is comparative, which is achievable without a cost model. Residual risk: their step 1 predictions will be poorer, their step 5 touch counts higher, and the phase 8 distribution will make this visible. The distribution is unattributed for this reason, and the instructor states that the spread reflects codebase experience rather than ability.

**Phase 3 is too easy.** Groups frequently rank decomposition C first for the right reason, which looks like success. It usually is not: they rank it first because "split by what changes" sounds principled, not because they can say what the other two cost. Countermeasure: the reconciliation question asks what their criterion *missed*, not whether their ranking was right, and a group that ranked correctly for a bad reason is exposed by it. Fallback for a group that genuinely resolves everything: give them the second change request, which favours decomposition A, and ask them to explain why C is still preferable — or concede that it is not.

**Affective failure.** The characteristic risk is **fatalism**: a student concludes that since the future is unknowable, decomposition cannot be reasoned about, and that the day has proved its own topic futile. This is a live risk with any prediction-under-uncertainty exercise and it is the exact opposite of the intended lesson. Countermeasures: phase 4 point 5 frames wrongness as an expected outcome with a defined response; phase 6's disclosure demonstrates a senior engineer treating a wrong seam as ordinary; and step 6's counterfactual gives the wrong bet a productive output. The instructor is briefed to name fatalism if it surfaces, since the unspoken version curdles into cynicism by S039.

**The executed half does not run.** A network failure or a client update that moves the References list removes phase 3's payload and step 4's evidence. Countermeasure: the four configurations are recorded once, with the References list visible, and the recording is held by the instructor. If it is used, **the day states that cases A to D were demonstrated rather than operated, and the specification does not assess elements 4, 6 or 8**; the second touch count falls back to a stated prediction with no adjudicator, which is a materially weaker day and is described as one.

## 7. Assessment and evidence generated

No entrustment evidence.

One specification item in CF101, requiring the full commit-evidence-reconcile chain with both touch counts and the counterfactual seam placement. Eleven elements are checked, each binary, and the list ends *nothing else is assessed*. The treatment of that sentence on a pre-[S041](/wiki/seminars/S041.md) day is argued at [WP-006](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-006.md) §7 and is not re-argued here.

The specification's most unusual clause is that **a reconciliation concluding the bet was right, without evidence from step 5, fails; a reconciliation concluding the bet was wrong, with a counterfactual seam, passes.** This is deliberate and is stated in advance. The programme's assessment architecture depends on students reporting outcomes accurately for three years, and the cheapest possible place to establish that accurate self-report is rewarded and self-serving report is not is a week-six artefact with tokens available and no entrustment consequence.

Element 8 is new and is the artefact binding for §8 objection 3: one named interaction between two of the student's own customization files that the platform does not define, and what they did about it. It is one sentence and it is the cheapest available way to stop the day teaching that a split surface composes.

The three added artefact elements do the work described at [WP-006](/wiki/whitepapers/WP-006.md) §7, with one difference specific to this day. **The prompt record acquires an evidential role here**: a convention that appears in the prompt record and in no file is precisely the seam step 6 must account for, so the record is not only a provenance document but an input to the reconciliation. That is the first time in the programme an artefact element added for one purpose turns out to measure something else, and it is recorded because it is the kind of coincidence that later looks like design.

Programme instrumentation: the phase 1 item 3 answerable rate is the cohort's baseline cost-model presence; the step 3 specification failure rate measures whether students can name what their own work commits them to, and is compared against the equivalent at S009 and S029; the phase 5 source touch-count spread is the codebase-experience proxy used to interpret later results; **the restated-convention count is the customization-surface analogue and is compared against itself at [S045](/wiki/seminars/S045.md)**, which is where a student next decomposes a configuration under assessment. Per instrumentation rule 6, that is the named later occasion and S045's artefact carries the element that consumes the result.

Dispositions: **integrity** is the primary observation, in step 6.

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: the day teaches an availability heuristic, not a cost model.**

At its strongest: students predict future changes by looking at past changes, which works only when the future resembles the past. The substrate's change history is supplied, so students will decompose to make *the changes that already happened* cheap — and step 5 draws its adjudicating change from the same history, which means the exercise rewards exactly the heuristic it should be testing. A student who decomposes purely by pattern-matching recent commits will do well, and will have learned that recent history predicts the future, which is precisely the belief that produces architectures unable to accommodate anything genuinely new.

The rebuttal is partial. It is true that the day rewards history-based prediction, and this is intentional to a point: in most engineering settings, recent change history *is* the best available evidence about future change, and a student who ignores it in favour of speculation is worse off, not better. The design's protection against the pathological version is phase 5 step 3, which requires naming what has been made expensive — a step that pattern-matching does not supply and that forces consideration of changes outside the observed set. Phase 7's partner then constructs a change specifically to exploit the bet, which is an adversarial sample from outside the history.

The concession is that neither protection is strong. Both operate after the decomposition is committed, so the day genuinely does train history-based prediction as its primary move, with the limits noted afterwards. A structurally better design would draw step 5's change from a *different* period than the one supplied in step 1, breaking the correlation. That is a straightforward improvement and it is not currently in the design.

**Objection 2: step 6's reconciliation is contaminated by hindsight and cannot be assessed honestly.**

At its strongest: after seeing the change and counting what they touched, a student can construct a reconciliation that makes their original bet look more considered than it was, or a counterfactual seam that is obvious only because the change is now known. Hindsight bias is robust, it operates below awareness, and it is not something a student can suppress by being asked to. So the artefact records not what the student predicted but what they can now narrate about what they predicted — and the specification clause rewarding honest wrongness may simply reward better narration of wrongness.

This objection the design does not fully answer. The written commitment at steps 1 to 3 is the only real protection: the bet is on paper before the evidence arrives, so the reconciliation can be checked against it rather than taken on trust. That handles fabricated prediction but not the deeper problem, which is the counterfactual — *where would the seam have had to be* is a judgement made entirely in hindsight, with no committed prior, and it is the step the specification weights most heavily. There is no version of that question that is not hindsight-contaminated, because it is a question about a counterfactual past.

The honest position is that step 6 is a **reasoning exercise, not a measurement**. Its value is that constructing the counterfactual requires applying the cost model, and that application is worth doing regardless of whether the resulting judgement is reliable. But the specification currently reads as though step 6 were evidence about the student's predictive quality, and it is not. That framing should be corrected in the CF101 specification text, and it has not been.

**Objection 3: the customization surface has no defined composition, so the cost model is being applied to a substrate that cannot support it.**

This is the objection that bites on the practical turn and it is the sharpest of the three. Decomposition in code is worth teaching because composition is defined: you can reason about the behaviour of the whole from the behaviour of the parts and the way they are joined. The customization surface has no such property. Multiple instruction files are combined in an order the documentation explicitly declines to guarantee. Precedence exists across types and not within them. A tool named in a prompt file and not available is silently ignored rather than reported. Nested discovery depends on a setting. **Under those conditions, splitting the surface does not reduce the cost of a convention change; it multiplies the number of places where an undefined interaction can occur**, and the day's chosen adjudicator — counting files touched — is precisely blind to that cost, because an undefined interaction touches nothing. So the day runs a demonstration in which the split configurations win, counts the wrong quantity, and calls the result a cost model. A student who takes the lesson literally will decompose a real repository's conventions into a dozen files and discover, months later, that the surface is now unreasonable-about in exactly the way a monolith is not.

**The design concedes this, and the concession is larger than the fix.** The premise is correct: composition is undefined, the touch count does not see it, and the day's executed half does structurally favour the split configurations for a reason that is partly an artefact of what is being counted. Phase 4 point 9 names three of the undefined behaviours, which is necessary and is not sufficient, because a phase 4 point is a thing students heard.

**The cheap fix is named and is now in the specification.** Element 8 requires the artefact to name one interaction between two of the student's own customization files that the platform does not define, and to say what they did about it. It costs one sentence, it cannot be produced without looking, and it converts an undefined interaction from something the day mentioned into something the student found in their own work. A decomposition that reports none fails, on the same logic as the intervention record.

What the fix does not do is repair the adjudicator. The touch count still cannot price an undefined interaction, and the day still cannot show a student that their twelve-file surface is worse than a two-file one, because within one session it is not. The honest scope is therefore narrower than the day's phase 4 point 8 implies, and is stated at point 10: **the cost model applies to what crosses a seam, and the customization surface has costs that cross nothing.** Prediction 5 in §9 is the only instrument the design has for whether students leave with the narrower reading or the wider one.

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** Fewer than half the cohort will produce a substantive answer to phase 1 item 3, and the rate will not differ materially by programming experience. *Instrument:* item 3 responses coded as substantive / vacuous / absent, against the admission background survey. **If experienced students answer substantively at a much higher rate, the cost model is acquired by exposure and the day's necessity is weaker than claimed.**

**Prediction 2.** Phase 5 step 3 — naming what was made expensive — will have a higher specification failure rate than step 6, despite being the earlier and apparently simpler step. *Instrument:* per-step specification failure counts.

**Prediction 3.** Step 5 touch counts will correlate with the quality of the step 1 change prediction, not with the quality of the decomposition considered independently. *Instrument:* touch counts against independently-rated prediction quality and independently-rated decomposition quality. **This is the availability-heuristic test from §8 objection 1: if prediction quality dominates entirely, the day is rewarding history-matching rather than design.**

**Prediction 4.** At S026, the commit-predict-reconcile structure will require no procedural explanation, and time-to-first-commitment will be shorter than at S007. *Instrument:* observed phase timings and instructor procedural interventions at S007, S020 and S026.

**Prediction 5.** The interactions students name at element 8 will cluster on the two behaviours phase 4 point 9 named and will rarely include an interaction the student discovered themselves. *Instrument:* element 8 responses coded as *restated from phase 4* or *found in own surface*. **This is the §8 objection 3 test.** A heavy majority of restatements means element 8 is a recall item rather than an inspection, the fix is not working, and the correct response is to withhold point 9's specifics until after phase 5 — which costs the phase 3 consolidation and is the trade the design would then have to make.

**Prediction 6.** The restated-convention count at step 5 will correlate with the number of customization files in the student's decomposition, in the positive direction, and will do so more strongly than the source touch count correlates with the number of source files. *Instrument:* both counts against both file counts. **If it holds, splitting the surface measurably increases what the surface fails to hold**, which is objection 3's mechanism showing up in the day's own data and is the first evidence the design would have that the executed half favours splitting for the wrong reason.

## What a student should take from this paper

**What the design is claiming.** That aesthetic criteria — short functions, separated concerns, no repetition — cannot adjudicate between two decompositions that both satisfy them, and that a seam's cost is the cost of the changes that cross it. Stated that way, a decomposition is a bet about the future, and a bet is the kind of thing that can be wrong and therefore learned from.

**What the evidence licenses.** The change-cost framing of boundaries and the immovable-seam category are **class 1**. Contrasting cases, prediction before adjudication and hindsight bias as a robust and largely uncorrectable distortion are **class 2** — direction and mechanism only, no magnitude. The customization surface's undefined composition is **class 4**, taken from the vendor's own text: instruction files are combined and **no specific order is guaranteed**, and a named-but-absent tool is silently ignored. That the cost model applies to that surface at all is **class 3**, and §8 says so.

**What to do with it.**

- **Name what you made expensive,** not only what you made cheap. That is the step intuition does not supply and the one S009 depends on.
- **Count what the decomposition failed to hold.** Conventions you had to restate because no file carried them are the better instrument, because a touch count cannot see them.
- **Never move a trust boundary on change-cost grounds.** A tool list is a seam defined by what may be done, and the touch count does not price it. That is a fail condition, not advice.
- **A control is not evidence.** A convention written into a file is a configured control; that the surface composes is a separate claim the platform explicitly declines to make. Use the five classes on the [Agent Configuration Standard](/wiki/program/13-Agent-Configuration-Standard.md).

**The concession worth remembering.** The paper concedes that the customization surface has no defined composition, that counting files touched is precisely blind to an undefined interaction because an undefined interaction touches nothing, and that the added artefact element does not repair the adjudicator. **Within one session the day cannot show you that a twelve-file surface is worse than a two-file one, because within one session it is not.** It also concedes that step 6's counterfactual is a reasoning exercise and not a measurement, and that the specification text still says otherwise.
