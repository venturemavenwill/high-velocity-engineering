# WP-069 — Governing the Release Decision

**Seminar day:** [S069](wiki/seminars/S069.md) · **Module:** [M14](wiki/modules/M14.md) · **Quarter:** [Q7](wiki/quarters/Q7.md)
**Strands:** PE · ME · SG · **Complexity class:** 5
**Platform anchor:** Foundry role-based access control as the release authority — the project managed identity, the roles the evaluation and trace paths require, and the tenant, subscription and project rate limits · **MCP servers:** Azure MCP Server · Foundry MCP Server (preview) · Microsoft Learn MCP Server

## Abstract

This paper defends treating a release decision as an object with four named parts — owner, authority, record and reversal path — and defends the claim that an arrangement missing any of them has delegated the decision to whoever is most confident in the room. Its structural argument is produced by a two-minute bridge: the gate's two error rates fall on different parties, and only one of those parties is present when the threshold is set, so an arrangement optimised against one error is a political object rather than a technical one. It defends a phase 3 payload in which the least distributed arrangement produced the best record, and defends locating the mechanism in a requirement that the engineer argue against their own change rather than in the decider's seniority. **It defends the day's practical turn, which is unusual in this module because the day's four cases cannot be operated at all: an authority structure is not a service call. What is operated instead is the arrangement the students are already living under — the actual role assignments on their own project and its monitoring resources — and phase 5 now requires one element of the designed arrangement to become a property of the system rather than of the document.** The day answers the escalation question S024 left open twenty-two months earlier, and discharges the rule-6 obligations placed on it by S066 and S068. It concedes that the day teaches two quarters of professional-formation content with no concurrent course to carry any of it, that its escalation answer is unusable in the case its students will meet first, **and that the enforcement it now demands is demonstrated on a resource where the student is already the owner.**

## 1. The instructional problem this day must solve

Three days have produced artefacts that each end in a question the student cannot answer alone, and the students have not noticed.

[S066](wiki/seminars/S066.md) established that a gate's threshold is a decision about which of two errors you prefer, and stated in one sentence that it does not belong to the engineer holding the number. [S068](wiki/seminars/S068.md) produced, for every student, an explicit list of failures they have decided not to detect — a list nobody has accepted, which means nobody has refused it either. [S067](wiki/seminars/S067.md) established that concurrent releases make attribution impossible and that the remedy is a schedule, which no engineer controls.

The difficulty is not that governance is complicated. It is that **engineers experience governance as an obstacle placed in front of their work, and this cohort has been trained for two years to reason from evidence, which makes the obstacle feel epistemically illegitimate as well as practically annoying.** A student who holds that view will design an arrangement that maximises their own discretion, defend it with the programme's own vocabulary about evidence and traceability, and be wrong.

Three specific difficulties sit underneath.

**The two-cost problem.** [S046](wiki/seminars/S046.md) point 3 gave the adjudicating question: whose view counts is decided by who bears the cost of a wrong output. Applied to a release gate it produces an answer the students have not seen, because there are two wrong outputs. **A wrong pass is paid for by users and by whoever is on call. A wrong block is paid for by the engineer whose change did not ship, and by everyone who needed it and does not know they were prevented.** Those are different parties, only one is represented when a threshold is set, and [S066](wiki/seminars/S066.md) established that only one of the two errors leaves any record at all. An arrangement is therefore never neutral between them, and the students will design arrangements that are silently calibrated against whichever error their own experience contains.

**The authority problem.** The person with the evidence is usually not the person entitled to decide. Conflating them produces two symmetric errors and the day must teach both, because a cohort taught only the first — engineers deciding things that were not theirs — becomes a cohort that escalates everything, which discharges nothing and is worse for being defensible.

**The escalation problem.** [S024](wiki/seminars/S024.md) point 7 asked when a finding stops being yours to hold, twenty-two months ago, and [WP-024](wiki/whitepapers/WP-024.md) §4 recorded that it was answered informally there and would be answered properly here. The answer has three conditions and all three are required, and the third is [S050](wiki/seminars/S050.md)'s standard rather than a lower one: **telling one person does not discharge the duty; reaching someone who can act does.** §8 objection 2 argues that this answer is correct and is unavailable in the situation these students will actually meet.

## 3. Theoretical basis for the design

### 3.1 Load profile and element interactivity

**Low technical, high dispositional, and unevenly distributed.** Phases 1 to 4 are light. Phase 5 is heavy for one reason: step 7 is a live negotiation with a party who may refuse, and the six minutes each student gets are the only six minutes of the module in which a real person with real authority evaluates the student's arrangement.

The interactivity is between steps 2, 3 and 6. The owner determines whose costs the authority is calibrated against; the authority determines which not-detected entries that person may accept; and step 6's assignments frequently reveal that the owner named at step 2 lacks the authority to accept what has been assigned to them. That contradiction is the day's most productive failure and it is why step 2 is unsupported.

Management:

- **All four arrangements come with the release record of their period.**
- **Phase 1's case is supplied with the deciders' own information set**, so no reconstruction is needed.
- **Step 4's record form is supplied** and step 7's trade preparation reuses S039's supplied form.
- **The accountable staff member is scheduled** and briefed.
- **Nothing is built.** Every artefact the day operates on was produced on days S066 to S068.

**Step 2 is unsupported.** Expertise reversal applies for a student with organisational experience: points 1, 3 and 5 will be familiar, and the day's content for them is points 4, 7 and 8 — escalation, gate retirement, and the ownerless entry — of which the last is the one no organisation the student has worked in will have handled.

### 3.2 Desirable difficulties selected, and those deliberately not

**Selected.**

- *Naming who should have decided, then reading who did,* items 1 and 2.
- *Answering the escalation question cold, before any framework,* item 3.
- *Ranking four arrangements before the release records arrive,* phase 3.
- *A task-after whose third question is unanswerable for three of the four cases,* phase 3.
- *Naming an owner and evidencing that they bear both costs,* step 2.
- *Assigning an owner to every failure you cannot detect, or recording that there is none,* step 6.
- *Negotiating with someone entitled to say no,* step 7.
- *An absent party with exactly one question,* phase 7.

**Deliberately not selected.**

- *Organisational theory.* Structures, reporting lines and decision-rights frameworks are not taught; the day works from four concrete arrangements and their records.
- *Regulatory and compliance regimes.* Named at [S066](wiki/seminars/S066.md) point 5's scope statement as the imposed-gate case and not developed.
- *The escalation path that terminates badly.* Named in point 4's scope statement and given to [S082](wiki/seminars/S082.md). §8 objection 2 argues this deferral is in the wrong order.
- *Incident command and on-call structure.* [S080](wiki/seminars/S080.md)'s and the substrate's.
- *Concurrent-release scheduling mechanics.* Named as the governance answer to [S067](wiki/seminars/S067.md)'s interference problem, and treated as a decision with an owner rather than as a scheduling technique.

**Scope, per the [Whitepaper Standard](wiki/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md), strong form.** Point 4's three escalation conditions are **safe wherever an escalation path exists and functions** — where there is somebody with the authority to act, they are reachable in time, and the finding can be put in terms they can act on. They are **unsafe where the path terminates in the person whose decision the finding contradicts.** In that case the three conditions are formally satisfiable and materially useless: the person with the authority is the person who does not want the finding, the duty does not lift, and what remains is not a procedural question but a question about what the engineer is prepared to do and at what cost to themselves. The day says so, names [S082](wiki/seminars/S082.md), and does not answer it.

**The platform instance, scoped in the strong form.** The day reads a permission map as the arrangement the students are actually operating under, and point 11 carries the conditions to students.

*Safe.* Reasoning about any system whose permissions the student can read: who can change this, who can stop it, who granted that, and does the answer match the arrangement anybody wrote down. The gap between those two is real, is cheap to measure, and is the day's opening finding.

*Unsafe.* Two conditions and the second is the dangerous one. **Unsafe as a description of what these roles and limits will be next term** — the role names have already changed once inside the documentation the students are reading, which is why the row is taught rather than skipped. And **unsafe as a model of authority in an organisation whose real decisions are made outside any system the student can read**, which is the ordinary case: a permission map will answer confidently, and it will be answering a question about capability rather than about accountability. That is point 3's conflation given a tool that makes it frictionless, it is the phase 6 agent case, and it is why point 10 is placed after point 8 rather than inside it. **Step 2 is where a student applying the simplification outside its range would fail**: an owner evidenced by a role assignment rather than by a borne cost does not satisfy the element.

### 3.3 Engagement and motivational mechanism

The day runs on **A4**.

Four arrangements are ranked. A4 concentrates the decision in one accountable person, is the slowest, and offends every instinct the programme has installed about distributed judgement and traceable criteria. Every group ranks it last or near it. The release records then show that it produced the fewest reversals and **the only recorded refusals to ship** — the only arrangement, of four, under which anyone ever said no.

The construction is what makes it teach. A4's advantage is not the seniority of the decider and the day does not let the room settle on that reading. **It is the requirement that the engineer state the case against their own change**, which is the only element in the four that changes what the decider is told rather than who they are. That relocates the finding from *concentrate authority* — which would be a bad lesson and a dangerous one — to *change the information the decision is made on*, which is available under any of the four arrangements and costs nothing.

The second mechanism is the task-after's third question: who has the authority to accept a failure nobody can detect. It is answerable for A4 and unanswerable for A1, A2 and A3, and the room discovers this by trying. Every student has a not-detected list in their bag from yesterday.

Phase 6 supplies the affective content and is the false-block error given a face: eleven weeks blocked by a gate whose owner had left, everyone agreeing it was wrong, nobody with the authority to retire it, resolved by adding a second gate alongside the first. **The eleven weeks appear in no incident record, no post-mortem and no metric.** Nobody in the case did anything wrong, which is what makes it a lesson about ownership rather than about competence.

Autonomy is supported by step 2, where the student names an owner and is not told whether they are right. Competence is supported by the four-part form, which is short and checkable. Relatedness is supported by step 7's accountable staff member, who is a colleague being asked to accept a real risk on a real service, and by phase 7's absent party, who is a stand-in for the substrate's actual users.

The competence risk is at step 7, where a substantial share of arrangements will be declined. **The specification states before phase 5 that a recorded refusal with its grounds is a pass**, and the instructor states that an arrangement nobody with authority would accept is a finding about the arrangement rather than about the student, and is exactly what the phase is for.

## 4. Phase-by-phase design and its warrant

**Phase 1 (25 min).** Five sealed items on a real decision with the real record available. Items 1 and 2 diverge by construction and the divergence needs no framework. **Item 5 is the sealed Copilot prediction** and is the same conflation as items 1 and 2 in a form the student can check mechanically twenty minutes later.

**Phase 2 (20 min).** Four retrievals and a bridge that turns [S066](wiki/seminars/S066.md)'s two error rates from a measurement property into a political one.

**Phase 3 (45 min).** **An executed opening — read the role assignments on your own project and its monitoring resources, and say who can change the gate and who can stop it — followed by four arrangements that are demonstrated rather than operated**, with real release records, **selected against the S066 error the cohort proved blind to**, which is the rule-6 element. A4 is the payload and its mechanism is relocated deliberately. The executed opening exists because the four cases cannot be run and the day still owes the room something real; the permission map is real, is readable in minutes, and diverges from the written arrangement in the same way items 1 and 2 diverge in the substrate's history. **Nothing depending on operating A1 to A4 is assessed.**

**Phase 4 (50 min).** Eleven points, unusually weighted toward professional content for a class-5 technical module, for the reason §8 objection 1 gives. Point 4 discharges S024 and carries the strong-form scope statement. Point 8 is what S070 consumes. **Points 9 and 10 are the platform points the [Copilot Practice Standard](wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md) requires — three inherited authority defaults of which one is a rate limit, and the two ends of gate retirement given addresses — and point 11 delivers the perishability boundary, with the same unsafe condition point 4 names.**

**Phase 5 (90 min).** Eight steps, ending in a live negotiation with a party entitled to refuse and then in one enforced element. **Step 6 is the S068 rule-6 element and its number is fixed by that reference.** **Step 8 is new with the practical turn**: one element made a property of the system, paired so that the constraint binds a partner rather than its author, and three named parts that remain enforced by nothing but agreement. The artefact carries the prompt record, the intervention record and the frontier statement.

**Phase 6 (40 min).** Eleven weeks that appear in no record. **Then the agent case: an accurate permission enumeration under a false heading.**

**Phase 7 (30 min).** The absent party and one question, reusing [S039](wiki/seminars/S039.md)'s structure contingently under the amended rule 5.

**Phase 8 (20 min).** The should-versus-did divergence; item 3 against the three conditions; the cohort's unowned-entry total; the S066 instrument resolved; the refusal rate and grounds; **item 5's frontier hit rate; and the cohort's total count of arrangement parts enforced by nothing but agreement.**

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**Students with more relevant prior knowledge.** A student who has worked in a governed organisation will produce a competent arrangement quickly and will name an owner who is plausible. Predicted failure: they name a *function* rather than a person — "the platform team", "product", "on-call" — because that is how their previous employer wrote it, and a function cannot bear a cost or answer a question. Countermeasure: a function-named owner is an explicit fail condition, and phase 7's absent party asks *who tells me, and when*, which a function cannot answer.

**Students with less relevant prior knowledge.** The risk is step 7, where a student with no negotiation experience meets a staff member entitled to refuse. Countermeasures: S039's trade form is supplied; six minutes is short enough to prepare for and long enough to say something; the refusal is pre-declared a pass; and phase 3's four arrangements have already shown the room what an acceptable and an unacceptable proposal look like from the other side.

**Phase 3 is too easy.** A group that has read about accountability will rank A4 highly and reach the payload early. The difficulty then moves to the task-after's third question, which no reading answers. Fallback: ask the group which arrangement they would adopt for the substrate **if the accountable staff member in A4 were replaced monthly**, which removes the element the group has just credited and forces them to say whether the mechanism was the person or the requirement to argue against oneself. There is no supplied answer and it is the phase's real question.

**Affective failure.** The characteristic risk is **resentment**. A day whose content is that the student does not get to decide, delivered to a cohort that has spent three days building the evidence, invites the reading that governance exists to take engineers' judgement away from them. That would be a serious misreading — the day's actual claim is that the engineer's duty is larger, not smaller, since point 4 says the finding remains theirs until it reaches someone who can act — and the design creates the conditions for it.

Countermeasures: point 3's two symmetric errors are stated together, so the day is visibly not arguing that engineers should decide less; A4's mechanism is the engineer's own argument rather than the decider's authority, which makes the best-performing arrangement the one that depends most on the engineer; phase 6's case is one in which the *organisation* failed the engineer, not the reverse; step 7 gives the student a negotiation rather than a submission; and the instructor states once that an engineer who has named an owner has not given away a decision, they have found the person who has to listen to them.

## 6. Retention architecture

Retrieved by [S070](wiki/seminars/S070.md) (where the arrangement governs a decision the student is answerable for), [S081](wiki/seminars/S081.md) (where the organisation stops being a constraint and becomes part of the system), [S082](wiki/seminars/S082.md) (where the escalation path terminates badly), [S085](wiki/seminars/S085.md) (scope negotiation at class 6), [S087](wiki/seminars/S087.md) (the safety case, whose claims require owners) and [S089](wiki/seminars/S089.md) (the handover artefact, where every arrangement designed here must transfer to people who did not design it).

**S070 at one week is the immediate consumer** and the link is instrumented in both directions: the unowned-entry count responds at S070's decision artefact, and the refusal rate responds at S070's defence.

**S089 at roughly two quarters is the demanding one.** An arrangement whose owner is a named person is exactly what does not survive a handover, and the day that teaches "name a person, not a function" is setting up a contradiction S089 has to resolve. The design regards that as correct — the contradiction is real and the resolution is that a handover must transfer the ownership explicitly rather than that the ownership should have been anonymous — but this day does not flag it, and S089 must.

Retained content: the four parts of a decision; the owner as cost-bearer with two costs on two parties; authority as distinct from expertise with its two symmetric errors; the three escalation conditions; the record written at the moment of decision; the reversal path as a term; retiring a gate; and the ownerless entry as an unmade decision. Eight items, above the ceiling, and the design accepts it because points 1, 2 and 5 are a single form the student writes once.

Own schedule: next morning, day 3, week 1, week 4.

## 7. Assessment and evidence generated

No entrustment judgement. The module's is at [S070](wiki/seminars/S070.md).

One specification item in ME301 with SG301. Per rule 1, the checked elements are the release decision, the named owner with evidence of both costs, the authority with its calibration, the record's form and author and location **committed where the gate lives**, the reversal path with its cost, an owner or an explicit none for every not-detected entry with a named person for the user-visible-only class, the negotiated agreement or refusal in three-part form, **the one enforced element and the statement of which parts remain enforced by agreement alone with what would make each fail silently**, **the prompt record, the intervention record and the frontier statement**, and the phase 7 outcome. **Nothing else is assessed.**

Three hard requirements: **an arrangement missing any of the four parts fails**, **an owner named as a function rather than a person fails**, and **an arrangement claiming to be enforced where it is not fails.** The third is new with the practical turn and is argued at §8 objection 3. **An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the arrangement demonstrates it could have detected an intervention-worthy error — a review requirement that would have blocked an unauthorised threshold change is the qualifying artefact.

**Nothing that depends on operating the four arrangements is assessed**, and nothing that depends on a permission change the substrate's tenant does not allow; both are stated to the room as demonstrated rather than operated.

**The owner requirement is scoped to slot and occupant**, per the correction [WP-089](wiki/whitepapers/WP-089.md) §8 required and annotated at the point of change in the day. The artefact must name a slot, which is the transferable object [S089](wiki/seminars/S089.md) hands over, and the person occupying it on the date of the decision, who bears the cost and can be asked a question. A slot with no named occupant fails, and an occupant with no slot fails.

**A recorded refusal is a pass.** This is stated in the specification rather than left to practice, for the same reason [S050](wiki/seminars/S050.md) wrote its decoupling down: an outcome that is nominally acceptable and practically penalised produces students who negotiate for agreement rather than for a defensible arrangement.

**Rule-6 discharge.** Two obligations land here and **both elements exist.**

- **[WP-066](wiki/whitepapers/WP-066.md) §7** named this day as the response occasion for the item 2 and step 3 error-rate results, specifying that phase 3's arrangements be selected against whichever error the cohort proved blind to, and that the cost-bearer of each be named. **Phase 3's case selection is a function of the S066 result rather than a fixed asset, and the task-after requires the cost-bearer of both errors and the authority to accept an undetectable failure, for all four arrangements.** The obligation is discharged.
- **[WP-068](wiki/whitepapers/WP-068.md) §7** named this day as the response occasion for the pooled not-detected list, specifying that every entry be assigned an owner or recorded as having none. **That is phase 5 step 6, it is a checked element, and it carries a named-person requirement for the user-visible-only class** — which is the class [S068](wiki/seminars/S068.md)'s scope statement bound in its artefact and which would otherwise have had nowhere to go. The obligation is discharged.

**A new countermeasure is recorded.** A4's mechanism — **requiring the person who wants the change to state the case against it** — is a structure this design did not previously hold. Per rule 5 as amended at [WP-054](wiki/whitepapers/WP-054.md) §8 it enters the repertoire as **proposed, not validated**; its first use is at [S070](wiki/seminars/S070.md) phase 3, whose decision artefact requires the strongest case against the student's own decision; and any later reuse is contingent on S070's reported outcome.

**No cross-course deferral is available.** [Q7](wiki/quarters/Q7.md) has no PE course. The consequence is argued in §8 objection 1.

**Instrumentation under rule 6.** The unowned-entry count responds at [S070](wiki/seminars/S070.md) phase 3 item 5 and is reported at S070 phase 8; the step 7 refusal rate and grounds respond at [S070](wiki/seminars/S070.md) phase 4, where a student whose arrangement was refused is asked what they changed. **WP-070 §7 must report whether both elements exist.**

**A third instrument, under rule 9.** Item 5's sealed prediction accuracy and the cohort's step 8 unenforced-part counts are this day's frontier and governance data. Their named reader is the following year's cohort, through the [Durable and Perishable Register](wiki/program/09-Durable-and-Perishable-Register.md), which carries the re-verification of the role names and rate-limit levels at point 9. **Per instrumentation rule 3 the sampling is disclosed to students**: one cohort, one tenant, one project's permission map, and four arrangements selected against the S066 result rather than at random.

Dispositions: **integrity**, at step 6, where recording an entry as having no owner is the honest and uncomfortable answer and inventing one is the available shortcut; **humility**, at step 7, where the arrangement may be refused.

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: the day teaches two quarters of professional-formation content with no course behind it.**

At its strongest: this day's subject is governance, authority, escalation and negotiation. Those are PE material. [Q7](wiki/quarters/Q7.md)'s four courses are IS301, SY301, ME301 and SG301, and the nearest PE courses are PE202, two quarters behind, and PE301, one quarter ahead — where change management, stakeholder analysis and risk communication are actually taught. **So the seminar day that most needs a concurrent professional-formation course is in the only year-three quarter without one.**

The consequence is visible in the design. Phase 4 carries eleven points of which five are professional rather than technical, which is unusual for a class-5 module and is a symptom rather than a choice. The [Module Map](wiki/program/07-Module-Map.md) lists PE among M14's strands, and the strand is recruited without a course, which means the recruitment is a claim the timetable does not support. And the cross-course deferral rule — added after [WP-041](wiki/whitepapers/WP-041.md) §8 found a day deferring its most common failure class to a concurrent course with no join — is not merely violated here but **inapplicable**, since there is no course to defer to.

The rebuttal is thin. The day does carry the material itself rather than deferring it, which is the honest response to having nowhere to defer to, and phase 4's weighting is a consequence of that decision rather than an oversight.

**Two fixes exist and the design takes neither.** The structural one — a PE course in Q7, or moving M14 to Q8 where PE301 runs — is not cheap, is not this paper's to make, and must be argued at the [Course Catalogue](wiki/program/06-Course-Catalogue.md); M14 cannot move without breaking [M15](wiki/modules/M15.md)'s dependence on it. The cheap one is available and is declined: **the cross-course artefact-element form works for a prior course as well as a concurrent one**, and step 7 could require the student to state which PE202 material they applied in the negotiation, which costs a clause and creates the join the rule was written to require. That fix does not answer the objection — a two-quarter-old course cannot carry new material, and PE202 is about psychological safety and error management rather than about authority — but it converts an unverifiable strand claim into a checkable one. **This objection is not fully answered**, and the honest summary is that the module's PE strand is under-resourced by the quarter's course allocation and the seminar is absorbing the shortfall.

**Objection 2: the day's escalation answer is unavailable in the case its students will meet first.**

At its strongest: point 4 gives three conditions and all three are required, and the scope statement concedes that they are useless where the path terminates in the person whose decision the finding contradicts. That concession is correct and it is placed wrongly. **A graduate two years into a career does not escalate through a functioning multi-level path; they tell their manager, and their manager is very often the person whose decision the finding contradicts.** The terminal case is not an edge case for these students. It is the normal case, and the day supplies a procedure for the other one.

The deferral goes to [S082](wiki/seminars/S082.md), fourteen weeks later, in a module about deployment engagement rather than about release. Per the [Whitepaper Standard](wiki/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md)'s fourth register, a deferral must name the instrument by which the receiving day shows it was discharged and must check the day can carry it. S082's subject is stakeholders, power and the person not in the room, which is the right neighbourhood, and **this paper cannot check that S082 carries an artefact element for it because S082 is not written.** That is exactly the failure the register was widened to catch, and it is recorded rather than concealed.

The fix is cheap and the design declines it. **A fifth arrangement — one in which the escalation path terminates in the decision's owner — costs one row in a table that already has four**, and the substrate's history contains at least one. It would put the terminal case in front of the room, in the phase where the room reasons rather than in the phase where it is told, and it would let the day say honestly that the arrangement has no procedural remedy while showing why. The reason it is not in the design is that the four arrangements are already selected against the [S066](wiki/seminars/S066.md) error-rate instrument, and adding a fifth constrained case would over-determine the phase. **That is a real constraint and it is not a good enough one**, because the payload A4 delivers would survive a fifth case and the terminal case would not survive being cut.

**Objection 3: the enforcement the day demands is demonstrated where enforcement is easiest and least meaningful.**

This is the objection that bites on the practical turn and it is conceded.

At its strongest: step 8 requires the student to make one element of their arrangement a property of the system rather than of the document, and it is the day's whole answer to the charge that a governance seminar produces paper. **The resource on which they can actually do it is the Foundry project they own.** A student who assigns themselves a role, or adds a review requirement to a file only they touch, has enforced a rule against the person who wrote it — which is not a governance arrangement, it is a note with a permission attached. Meanwhile the arrangement that matters, the one governing the substrate that has real users, is exactly the one the tenant will not let them change, so for that arrangement the build reverts to a proposal. **The day therefore operates the case where enforcement is trivial and proposes the case where it is not**, which is the inverse of the module's usual discipline.

**The fix is one clause and it is made.** Students are paired for step 8, and **the enforced element must be one the partner has to act on** — the partner holds the approval, receives the alert, or occupies the slot. The constraint now binds somebody other than its author, which is the minimum condition for the word *enforcement* to mean anything, and it costs a pairing that the phase already has time for.

**What the fix does not reach is recorded.** The partner has no stake in the student's users. They will approve, because approving costs them nothing and refusing costs them a conversation — which is [S038](wiki/seminars/S038.md)'s expert politeness in a new place, and the day does not apply S038's countermeasure here because a narration protocol for a two-minute approval would cost more than the element is worth. **So the enforcement is structurally real and substantively procedural, and the day says so at step 8 rather than claiming more.** The version that would fix it is one in which the partner bears some consequence of a bad approval, and nothing in a seminar can supply that. It is recorded as a limit of the setting rather than as a defect of the design.

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** At items 1 and 2, more than three-quarters of students will name a different party than the record shows decided, and the modal record answer will be *whoever was available* rather than either the evidence-holder or the authority-holder. *Instrument:* items 1 and 2 coded against the record, with the record's decider classified by role.

**Prediction 2.** At item 3, a majority of students will discharge the escalation duty by telling one person, and the proportion will fall below a quarter when the same question is asked at the next morning's retrieval. *Instrument:* item 3 answers coded against point 4's three conditions, paired with the day-1 retrieval response. **If the next-morning figure does not move, point 4 is being learned as a list rather than as a standard.**

**Prediction 3.** At step 6, more than a third of not-detected entries across the cohort will be recorded as having no owner, and the unowned proportion will be highest for entries in the user-visible-only class. *Instrument:* step 6 assignments coded for owner presence and entry class. **If the unowned proportion is near zero, students are inventing owners** and the function-name fail condition is not catching it.

**Prediction 4.** More than a third of step 7 arrangements will be declined by the accountable staff member, and the modal ground will be the reversal path's cost rather than the owner or the authority. *Instrument:* step 7 outcomes and grounds, coded. **The modal ground is the claim**: the design predicts that students specify ownership carefully and reversal carelessly, because ownership is what the day is visibly about.

**Prediction 5.** At phase 3's executed opening, more than three-quarters of groups will find at least one identity holding a gate-changing permission that no member of the group can name, and more than half will find that no member of the group can say who granted any assignment. *Instrument:* the groups' recorded answers against the actual assignment list. **If the unnameable-identity rate is low, the substrate's project has been provisioned more carefully than a real one** and the opening's finding will not transfer; the fallback is to run it against a second, older project.

**Prediction 6.** At step 8, the median number of arrangement parts a student records as enforced by nothing but agreement will be three or more, and the part most often recorded as unenforced will be the reversal path. *Instrument:* step 8 statements coded by part. **The modal unenforced part is the claim** and it is the same claim as prediction 4 from the other side: if students both specify reversal carelessly and leave it unenforced, the day's four parts are not equally weighted in practice and point 6 needs the emphasis point 2 currently has.
