# WP-042 — Model Selection Without Fooling Yourself

**Seminar day:** [S042](wiki/seminars/S042.md) · **Module:** [M09](wiki/modules/M09.md) · **Quarter:** [Q5](wiki/quarters/Q5.md)
**Strands:** IS · ME · MS · **Complexity class:** 3
**Platform anchor:** Foundry cloud evaluation — versioned datasets, run history, and the conversation split · **MCP servers:** Foundry MCP Server (preview) · Microsoft Learn MCP Server

## Abstract

This paper defends requiring a timestamped selection protocol to be submitted before any model is trained, and defends requiring students to decide their response to a bad result while the result does not yet exist. The first argument is that selection is search, that the best of many figures on a finite sample is a maximum rather than an estimate, and that the resulting optimism is invisible in the artefact — no code is wrong, no leakage occurred, and a build produced under a protocol written afterwards is identical to one produced under a protocol written first. The second argument is that the decision about what to do when a test figure disappoints is systematically corrupted by knowing the figure, so making it in advance is the only version that is not motivated. The paper additionally defends constructing the day's contrasting cases so that two consultations of a test set produce nearly the damage of unlimited ones, and defends running them as real evaluations against versioned datasets so that the day can close with the one thing it previously lacked: **the platform's own run history, which records every consultation the discipline is about and which nobody reads.** That record is the repair to this paper's own first objection, and it converts the day's central claim from an assertion about honesty into a claim about a document. It concedes that the day's central rule is stricter than the practice its students will enter, that its scope boundary is drawn in the wrong place, and that coupling the discipline to a server-side record may teach students that the record does the work.

## 1. The instructional problem this day must solve

S041 established what a held-out estimate estimates, for one model. The professional situation involves many — a hyperparameter sweep, several architectures, a dozen feature sets — and the number that gets reported is the best of them.

That number is a maximum. Evaluate forty models on one finite sample and the highest score reflects both the model's quality and the sample's particular composition, and the second component does not generalise. Report the maximum as an estimate and it is optimistic in proportion to how hard you searched — which means that **a more thorough search produces a better model and a worse estimate**, and separating those two facts is the day's content. Items 1 and 2 in the pretest are constructed to produce exactly this reversal, and almost nobody states it.

The day's second problem is that the failure is invisible. This is worth stating precisely, because it is what makes the day's assessment design unusual. There is no leak — the test set was never trained on. There is no error — every evaluation was correctly executed. And the artefact produced by a student who wrote their protocol first is byte-identical to one produced by a student who wrote it after seeing which model won. Point 7 names this and it is the reason step 1 requires a timestamp.

The third problem is the test set's consumption. Students hold a proportional model: one extra look is a small sin, ten looks are a large one. Protocol D in phase 3 shows this is wrong — a test set consulted twice, with a change in between, produces a figure nearly as optimistic as one used for selection throughout. The damage is not proportional to the number of looks because the *first* look is what converts the set from an estimator into a selector.

The fourth problem is anticipatory honesty. A student who sees a test figure much worse than their validation figure faces a decision — accept it, investigate, or try something else — under conditions that make the self-serving choice feel like diligence. Step 7 requires the decision to be made before the figure exists, which is the only point at which it is uncorrupted.

## 3. Theoretical basis for the design

### 3.1 Load profile and element interactivity

**The module's peak.** The day's content is a discipline rather than a technique, which makes it harder to hold: there is no procedure to execute, only a set of commitments to honour, and the reasons for them are statistical rather than mechanical.

The genuine interactivity is at step 6 — estimating the residual optimism — which requires holding the search's extent, the models' similarity, and the sample's size together, and has no closed form the day supplies.

Management:

- **The protocol form is supplied** at step 1, so the commitment is a filling-in rather than a composition.
- **The search machinery is supplied.** Students configure and run; IS201 owns the algorithms.
- **Four protocols supplied at phase 3** with a fresh-set adjudicator, so groups reason rather than construct.
- **Step 6's estimation is worked with the room** on phase 3's own numbers, which gives a reference magnitude rather than a formula.
- **One dataset and task across the module,** third consecutive day.

### 3.2 Desirable difficulties selected, and those deliberately not

**Selected.**

- *Items 1 and 2's reversal,* where more search yields a worse estimate.
- *Protocol D,* where two consultations approach unlimited damage.
- *Committing the protocol before training,* step 1, with a timestamp.
- *Stating what the protocol forbids,* step 2 — harder than stating what it permits.
- *Pre-deciding the bad-result response,* step 7.
- *Reading a protocol against its own report,* phase 7.

**Deliberately not selected.**

- *The statistics of the maximum.* Named at point 2 and worked numerically on phase 3's figures; the distributional treatment is ME201's.
- *Nested cross-validation.* Genuinely the right answer for small data and omitted, because the day's discipline is about commitment rather than resampling and nesting would let the machinery carry the argument.
- *Multiple-comparison corrections.* S049's material.
- *Adaptive data analysis and its remedies.* Named at point 6's honest-move discussion and not taught.

**Scope, per the [Whitepaper Standard](wiki/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md), strong form.** Point 4's consumption rule — a test set is consumed by use — is stricter than practice generally observes. **Safe** as a discipline for a project with a fixed deliverable and a reportable figure, which is this day's situation and most engineering situations. **Unsafe** as a description of how research proceeds, where re-use of a benchmark across many papers is unavoidable and is managed by holdouts, replication and adaptive-analysis methods the day does not teach. A student applying the rule literally in a research context will be unable to work; a student ignoring it in an engineering context will report a maximum.

**The platform instance, scoped in the strong form.** The day now runs its protocols as real cloud evaluations against versioned datasets, closes on the run history, and teaches the conversation split as a selection decision. Each carries conditions.

*Safe.* Reasoning about how many times a reporting set has been consulted, and by whom; about why a retry, a resubmission and a re-run after a mapping fix are all consultations regardless of intent; about why a split strategy chosen at export time determines what a figure means; and about why a protocol that does not define a consultation cannot control one. In every case the platform supplies a fact the reasoning needs and the reasoning is the student's.

*Unsafe* in three directions. **First, as a compliance mechanism.** The run history is a record and not a control; nothing prevents a second project, a second workspace or a local script, and a student who concludes that the platform enforces the discipline has learned the opposite of the day's content. **Second, as a portable instrument.** Most teams these graduates join will evaluate with local scripts and will have no comparable record, so a discipline that leans on the history is a discipline that does not travel — which is §8's third objection and is conceded there. **Third, as a comparison across runs.** Evaluation runs are documented as repeatable specifically because behaviour may be non-deterministic, and no expected variation is published, so **two runs differing is not evidence of a change** and a student treating a delta between runs as a result has made this day's error inside a single consultation.

The boundary is delivered to students at points 7, 8 and 9 rather than left to this paper, per the widening recorded on the [Whitepaper Standard](wiki/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md). **The artefact element under which a student applying it outside its safe range would fail is step 8's reconciliation**, which requires the count to be stated and accounted for rather than asserted to be one.

### 3.3 Engagement and motivational mechanism

The day runs on **two consultations being almost as bad as unlimited**.

Protocol D is the phase 3 payload and its effect depends on students holding a proportional model, which they do. A test set used for selection throughout is obviously compromised; a test set consulted once, adjusted against, and consulted again feels like ordinary careful practice — because it *is* ordinary practice — and its figure is nearly as bad. Groups reliably rank D second or third and it lands fourth.

Items 1 and 2 supply the opening reversal. Four hundred models with a higher best score is intuitively stronger evidence and is weaker, and item 3's disagreement is genuine and stable until point 1 resolves it.

Step 7 is the day's most distinctive demand and students find it uncomfortable in a specific and useful way: deciding now what to do if the result disappoints exposes that they were planning to decide later, in the light of the result, which is the thing the step prevents.

The competence risk is that step 1's commitment locks a student into a protocol they later regret. Countermeasure: the specification assesses whether the search stayed within the protocol, not whether the protocol was optimal — a student who commits a narrow protocol and honours it passes, and their step 6 estimate will be smaller for it.

## 4. Phase-by-phase design and its warrant

**Phase 1 (25 min).** Two search sizes, one reversal. Item 3's disagreement is real and is left unresolved until phase 4 point 1, which is the design's standard treatment for a genuine division. **Item 5 is the sealed Copilot prediction**, administered here per instrumentation rule 8, and its prompt — *find me the best-performing configuration for this task* — contains no instruction to stop, which is the condition under which the agent's speed becomes the day's hazard.

**Phase 2 (20 min).** The bridge asks which S041 leak class repeated evaluation belongs to and why it was not listed. The answer — a fifth class, deferred, and the deferral was stated — is the design demonstrating that its stated deferrals are kept, which by Q5 is a recognised convention.

**Phase 3 (45 min).** Four protocols, one dataset, a fresh-set adjudicator, **executed end to end against versioned datasets rather than supplied as four figures**. Protocol D is the payload. The phase's critical dependency is that D's two consultations must be individually reasonable — a look, a plausible change, another look — or the case teaches carelessness rather than structure. **The phase's second payload is the run history, opened at the end for all four protocols at once.** Protocol D is legible in it, in one line, and no group looked. That is the day's strongest single moment and it did not exist in the handout version: the design previously had to assert that this failure is detectable in principle, and it can now show that it is detectable in fact and was not detected in the room.

**Phase 4 (50 min).** Ten points. Point 1 is the retained sentence. Point 3's three-sets framing is the operational rule. Point 4 carries the scope statement. Point 6 — what you may claim when you have already looked — is included because the honest move exists, is rarely made, and a day that only prohibits produces students who conceal. **Point 7 now carries the day's professional core in a sharper form**: the failure is invisible in the artefact and visible in the platform, the record is generated automatically, and it is consulted by nobody. **Point 8 is ME201's anchor stated in the seminar** — how you split determines what you are evaluating, and last turn is the default — which places a selection decision at the moment of measurement and is the reason the material belongs here rather than on an evaluation-tooling day. **Point 9 names three defaults the platform chose**, per the [Copilot Practice Standard](wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md), of which the file-format one is the most instructive: exporting to CSV settles the split question, because CSV is turn-level only, and the methodological decision was made by whoever wrote the export.

**Phase 5 (90 min).** Eight steps with a pre-committed protocol. Step 2's forbidden-actions statement is materially harder than step 1's permissions and is where students discover their protocol is vague; it now also requires stating what the protocol forbids the *agent*, which is a different and harder sentence. Step 7 is the discriminator. **Step 8 is new and is this paper's own objection-1 repair**: the student reads their run count off the platform and reconciles it against their protocol. The design expects most counts to exceed one and expects most of the excess to be agent retries and resubmissions, none of them dishonest and all of them consultations. **The specification does not require the count to be one**; requiring that would punish the honest reading and produce students who do not look.

**Phase 6 (40 min).** An unreproducible figure produced by a followed protocol and several consultations, none unreasonable, then an agent failure. The design names the structural parallel to S024's deferral chain explicitly, because by Q5 the cohort has the category and naming it converts a story into an instance. **The agent case is nineteen evaluations in two hours, each reported with a significance comparison against baseline**, concluding correctly about the last comparison and wrongly about the model. It is chosen because the agent made no error: it did what it was asked, at a speed that made the day's failure reachable in an afternoon rather than a quarter, and the significance test made the maximum look more trustworthy rather than less.

**Phase 7 (30 min).** Protocol, report and **run count**, without the search log. Reading permissions rather than actions is what makes the phase about the protocol's adequacy rather than about the student's compliance; the run count is the third document this paper's §8 previously found to be missing, and it is a better one than the search log because it is not the student's own account.

**Phase 8 (20 min).** The item 2 reversal rate, the cohort's optimism estimates against a reference, the protocol-versus-selection comparison, **the distribution of run counts shown unattributed**, and **item 5's sealed prediction opened**.

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**Students with more relevant prior knowledge.** A student with competition or applied-modelling experience will search fluently and will hold strong intuitions about validation practice. Predicted failure: they write a protocol permitting a broad search because that is how they work, honour it, and produce a large residual optimism they then under-estimate at step 6 — because practitioners rarely quantify it. Countermeasure: step 6 is worked against phase 3's measured gaps, which supplies a reference their intuition lacks; and phase 7's partner reads the protocol's permissions, which are unusually broad in their case.

**Students with less relevant prior knowledge.** The day requires no modelling skill and its discipline is stateable. The hard step is 6. Countermeasures: the worked reference, and a required *source* rather than a magnitude — a student who names the search's extent as the source and gives a rough direction passes.

**Phase 3 is too easy.** Protocols A and C are ranked correctly by most groups. B and D are the difficulty and D is the payload. Fallback if a group ranks D correctly: ask what protocol B's figure would be worth after six months of a team consulting the test set on unrelated projects, which is the research case the scope statement names and has no clean answer.

**Affective failure.** The characteristic risk is **the discipline experienced as an obstacle to doing good work**. A student who believes a better model is available beyond their committed protocol, and may not go and get it, is being asked to accept a worse outcome for an epistemic reason — and the reason is real but the loss is immediate and the benefit is abstract.

Countermeasures: point 6's honest move, which shows that exceeding a protocol is survivable if disclosed; the specification's indifference to the protocol's breadth, so a student may commit a broad protocol and honour it; and the explicit statement that the rule protects the *estimate* rather than the model. Whether that lands is the day's open question and Prediction 3 in §9 is the instrument.

## 6. Retention architecture

Retrieved by S043 (capacity, the next day, where the selection protocol governs the regularisation search), S045 (the deployment), S047 (criteria drift and validating the validator — where a criterion tuned against outcomes is this day's failure with a rubric as the model), S049 (clustering, power and the interval you owe the reader), S059 (the benchmark trap, which is protocol A at community scale) and S066 (the release gate as an evaluation artefact).

S059 at roughly two quarters is the principal target and the correspondence is the day's most consequential: a public benchmark is a test set consulted by everyone, thousands of times, and every reported figure on it is a maximum over a search nobody logged.

S047's retrieval is the subtler one — a rubric adjusted until its scores look right is a selection protocol violated, and students who hold this day recognise it.

Retained content: selection-as-search, the three sets, the consumption rule, the protocol's required contents, and the invisibility of the failure. Five items.

Own schedule: next morning, day 3, week 2, week 6.

## 7. Assessment and evidence generated

No entrustment judgement.

One specification item, jointly in IS201 and ME201. Per rule 1, the checked elements are: a timestamped protocol predating training, a forbidden-actions statement covering what the agent may not do, a stated split strategy and repetition count, evidence the search stayed within the protocol, a single test consultation, a licensing sentence with the uncalibrated clause where applicable, a residual-optimism estimate with its source, the pre-committed bad-result response, **a reported run count reconciled against the protocol**, the phase 7 outcome, **and the prompt record, the intervention record and the frontier statement**. **Nothing else is assessed, including the model's performance.**

**A protocol submitted after the first model was trained fails, whatever it says.**

**A run count that is not reconciled fails. A run count greater than one does not.** This asymmetry is deliberate and is the day's most consequential specification decision: a rule that failed honest counts would produce students who stop reading the record, which is the behaviour the day exists to prevent, and it would do so at exactly the moment the record became available.

**The prompt record is evidence on this day and not only documentation.** A prompt reading *keep trying until the score improves* is a protocol violation in the student's own hand, and the design says so on the seminar page rather than treating the record as a compliance artefact whose contents nobody reads.

Programme instrumentation: the item 2 reversal rate is the cohort's search-optimism baseline; the protocol-versus-selection comparison at phase 8 is the protocol-adequacy measure; **the step 6 optimism estimates are compared across cohorts against staff-computed gaps** on the same task, which is the only calibration available for a quantity students cannot compute; **the run-count distribution is new and is the first direct measurement of how far a committed protocol diverges from executed behaviour**, which is the quantity this day has always been about and has never been able to observe; the phase 7 permission-found rate is carried to S059; **item 5's sealed prediction accuracy** feeds the running frontier map per instrumentation rule 9.

Dispositions: **integrity**, at step 5 and now at step 8, where the honest reconciliation is less flattering than the assertion and is the only one that can be checked.

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: the timestamp verifies the artefact's order, not the student's honesty.**

At its strongest: the specification's headline requirement is that the protocol predates training, evidenced by a timestamp. A student may write a protocol, submit it, train a model, discover the protocol was inconvenient, and proceed differently — and the timestamp says nothing. Worse, a student who has done this produces a *better-looking* artefact than one who committed honestly to a narrow protocol and stayed inside it, because their results are stronger and their protocol is on file. The mechanism the design describes as its only available evidence is evidence of one thing that was never in doubt.

The rebuttal is that the timestamp is not the only check. Step 2's forbidden-actions statement, the required evidence that the search stayed within the protocol, and phase 7's reading of permissions against report all operate on the search's content rather than its order. A student whose search exceeded their protocol has a search log that shows it.

The concession was that **the search log was required and not checked against the protocol by anyone**. The specification required "evidence that the search stayed within it"; nothing in the day compared the two, phase 7 was given the protocol and the report and explicitly *not* the log, and no assessor step was specified. So the design had the evidence it needed and had arranged not to look at it — the [Assessment Architecture](wiki/program/03-Assessment-Architecture.md)'s rule 3 failure in a new form, and the first instance since that rule was introduced.

**The repair is made and it is better than the one the objection asked for.** Because every evaluation now runs in the cloud against a versioned dataset, the platform keeps a run history: how many runs were submitted, against which dataset version, when. Step 8 requires the student to read that count and reconcile it against their protocol, and phase 7 receives it as a third document. **It is a stronger repair than handing the partner the student's own search log, because the search log is the student's account of what they did and the run history is not.** The design records that it did not invent this evidence; it noticed that the platform had been generating it since S041 and that nobody in the profession reads it, which is the observation phase 4 point 7 now teaches.

What remains is that the record is bounded by the workspace. A second project, a local script or another account produces consultations the history does not see, and the day says so at point 7 rather than overclaiming.

**Objection 2: the day's central rule is stricter than the practice its students will enter, and the scope statement does not resolve the resulting conflict.**

At its strongest: §3.2 concedes that the consumption rule is unsafe as a description of research practice, where benchmark re-use is unavoidable. But the students are not only entering research — they are entering an industry where a team maintains one evaluation set across years and dozens of decisions, which is exactly the situation the rule forbids and exactly the situation the scope statement assigns to "research". The safe/unsafe boundary is drawn between project and research, and the case that will actually confront these students — a long-lived engineering evaluation set — falls on neither side.

This objection the design does not fully answer. The boundary is drawn in the wrong place: the operative distinction is not project-versus-research but **single-deliverable versus long-lived**, and a long-lived engineering evaluation set has the research problem with none of the research remedies.

What should follow is either a third scope case or a pointer to where the long-lived situation is handled. The programme has a natural home for it — S066's release gate is precisely a long-lived evaluation artefact consulted at every release — and **S066's design does not currently address test-set consumption at all.** So the day defers a case it does not name to a day that does not cover it, which is the deferral failure the standard's fourth register was added to catch, occurring in a paper that cites that register. That is worth recording plainly: the rule caught the general shape and not this instance, because the deferral here is implicit rather than stated.

**Objection 3: coupling the discipline to a server-side record teaches students that the record does the work.**

This is the objection that bites on the practical turn and it is the one the design finds hardest.

At its strongest: the day's durable claim — a test set is consumed by use, and the damage is not proportional to the number of looks — was previously teachable with a pencil. It is now delivered through an instrument. The phase 3 payload is the run history screen; the phase 5 discriminator is a count read off a platform; the phase 7 third document is a server-side artefact. **A student's most vivid memory of this day will be a number they did not have to compute.** Most of these graduates will join teams that evaluate with local scripts, in notebooks, with no versioned datasets and no history, and there the count is not available and never was. A discipline whose most persuasive moment depends on an instrument the graduate will not carry is a discipline that may not survive the first job — and worse, a student who has learned *the platform records this* may reason that where nothing records it, nothing is recorded, which inverts the day's content precisely.

There is a second and sharper form. **The record is a record and not a control**, and the day is careful to say so, but the day also uses it as the emotional climax of phase 3. Students do not weight a caveat and a climax equally. The design has put its rhetorical force behind an artefact whose limits it states in a subordinate clause, which is the same failure mode [WP-016](wiki/whitepapers/WP-016.md) §8 recorded when a vendor's cache appeared to confirm a hardware model.

The objection is conceded.

**The cheap fix is one clause in step 8 and it is made.** The reconciliation must state **how the student would produce the same count without the platform** — what they would have had to log, when, and who would have had to agree to it. It is one sentence, it costs nothing, and it moves the discipline back into the student by requiring them to specify the manual instrument the automatic one replaced. A student who cannot say what they would log has learned the platform and not the practice.

**What remains unfixed is that the design cannot tell whether the fix works**, because the graduate's first job is outside every instrument this programme holds. The only available check is [S066](wiki/seminars/S066.md)'s release gate, where the same discipline must be applied to a long-lived artefact, and §8's objection 2 has already recorded that S066 does not currently address consumption at all. **The two concessions in this paper therefore converge on the same missing day**, which is a stronger finding than either alone and is recorded here rather than repaired: the programme has one natural home for long-lived and un-instrumented evaluation discipline, and it is empty.

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** Fewer than a fifth of students will state at item 2 that the larger search yields a worse estimate. *Instrument:* item 2 responses coded.

**Prediction 2.** Groups will rank protocol D second or third rather than fourth, for a majority. *Instrument:* phase 3 committed rankings.

**Prediction 3.** Fewer than a quarter of students will report in the end-of-module review that the protocol prevented them from doing better work. *Instrument:* the module review's constraint item. **A high proportion indicates the §5 affective failure is active and the discipline is being experienced as an obstacle.**

**Prediction 4.** Step 6 optimism estimates will be systematically low relative to staff-computed gaps, and the underestimate will be larger for students with applied-modelling backgrounds. *Instrument:* estimates against computed gaps, split by the admission background survey.

**Prediction 5.** The modal step 8 run count will be greater than one, and a majority of the excess will be attributable to agent retries, resubmissions and re-runs after a mapping correction rather than to deliberate re-consultation. *Instrument:* run counts against prompt records, coded for the cause of each excess run. **If the modal count is one, either the cohort is under-using the platform or the counts are not being read honestly, and the phase 3 payload has no purchase.**

**Prediction 6.** A large majority of item 5 sealed predictions will say the agent will search broadly and report the best figure, and a small minority will name the specific consequence — that it will consult the reporting set once per candidate without saying so. *Instrument:* item 5 responses coded for generic-versus-specific failure identification against what each student's agent did.

## Evidence status

**Verified in this repository.** Selection bias from repeated evaluation, the three-set discipline, test-set consumption and the unreproducible-figure failure at phase 6 are grounded in [research/03-measurement-evaluation/singh-leaderboard-illusion.md](research/03-measurement-evaluation/singh-leaderboard-illusion.md), [research/03-measurement-evaluation/shankar-evalgen.md](research/03-measurement-evaluation/shankar-evalgen.md) and [research/02-technical-foundations/thakur-beir.md](research/02-technical-foundations/thakur-beir.md). The split-strategy material and the vendor-documentation asymmetry the day relies on are recorded in [research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-framework-and-evaluations-docs.md](research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-framework-and-evaluations-docs.md) and [research/06-microsoft-platform/](research/06-microsoft-platform/collected-materials.md).

**Grounded in vendor documentation, with its version and its silence recorded.** All platform content is taken from Microsoft Learn, read 2026-07-26.

The evaluation mechanics at phases 3, 5 and 7 are from the Foundry SDK cloud-evaluation how-to: an evaluation is created against a data-source configuration and a set of testing criteria and then run, with runs executed against **uploaded, versioned datasets** that the documentation recommends specifically because they support versioning and re-use across multiple runs in production and CI workflows. Data-source types are JSONL, CSV, Foundry response IDs and Application Insights traces; **CSV is turn-level only**, and conversation-level rows require JSONL and are marked preview — which is the point 9 default, since the export format settles the split question. The split strategies at point 8 are from the Agent Framework evaluation documentation: **how you split determines what you're evaluating**, with last turn as the default and full and per-turn as the alternatives, and with **repetitions documented as existing to detect non-deterministic behaviour**. The agent case at phase 6 is built from the Foundry MCP Server tools reference, whose documented example workflow includes *compare my baseline evaluation against the new run* and *show me the comparison results with statistical significance*; the server is in public preview and exposes **38 tools across 10 categories**.

**Three silences are recorded and two are taught.** First: **the documentation states that repetitions exist to detect non-deterministic behaviour and does not state how much variation to expect**, so a student cannot distinguish a real delta from run-to-run noise without measuring it themselves — which is taught at point 9 as the reason a protocol must define what one consultation means. Second: **no retention or completeness guarantee is published for evaluation run history**, so the day teaches the record as a record and not as an audit trail, and point 7 states that it is bounded by the workspace. Third, and consistent with the folder-wide asymmetry recorded in [research/06-microsoft-platform/](research/06-microsoft-platform/collected-materials.md): **no agreement-with-human figure is published for any evaluator**, so every judged score in this day's four protocols is uncalibrated and the S041 clause applies throughout. Nothing in the documentation licenses a claim about the magnitude of selection optimism, and none is made here — **phase 3's figures are measured on the day's own data and are perishable.**

**Cited from general knowledge, not verified here.** Contrasting cases with a fresh-sample adjudicator, prediction before adjudication, retrieval practice at expanding intervals, cognitive load in discipline-based rather than procedural content, motivated reasoning in post-hoc decisions, and error management training are cited for **direction and mechanism only**. No effect size is asserted for any of them, and none is asserted for AI-assisted productivity anywhere in this paper.

**Design reasoning with no external warrant.** The two-search-size pretest reversal; the four-protocol phase 3 and protocol D's construction; the decision to execute the protocols and to close the phase on the run history; the ten points of phase 4 and the honest-move at point 6; the eight-step phase 5 with a timestamped pre-committed protocol; step 7's anticipatory decision; step 8's reconciliation and its deliberate refusal to require a count of one; phase 7's protocol-against-report reading with the run count as a third document; the failure clause on protocol order; and all six §9 predictions are this design's own reasoning, untested. The §8 objection-1 concession is **now repaired**: the search log was required and withheld from the only phase positioned to check it, and the repair supplies a server-side run history that is a better document than the one the objection requested, because it is not the student's own account. The objection-2 concession states that the scope statement's safe/unsafe boundary is drawn between project and research when the operative distinction is single-deliverable versus long-lived, that the long-lived engineering case falls outside both, and that it is implicitly deferred to a day whose design does not address it. **The objection-3 concession states that coupling the discipline to a server-side record risks teaching that the record does the work, names the one-clause fix and records it as made, and records that the design cannot check whether the fix works — and that both of this paper's concessions converge on the same empty day at [S066](wiki/seminars/S066.md).**
