# WP-072 — Runbooks, and the Operator Who Is Not You

**Seminar day:** [S072](/wiki/seminars/S072.md) · **Module:** [M15](/wiki/modules/M15.md) · **Quarter:** [Q8](/wiki/quarters/Q8.md)
**Strands:** PE · ME · **Complexity class:** 5
**Platform anchor:** APIM AI gateway policies and Microsoft Foundry Control Plane lifecycle actions as the runbook's actual subject, alongside the Microsoft Agent 365 role matrix in which no single operator can complete the triage and the Microsoft 365 Agents Toolkit promotion step a stranger can execute — all of it written for someone who was not in the room

> ## Release rule
>
> **§§4, 5 and 9 are post-session reading.** §4 explains why the author is required to stay silent, and the session does not work if the learner knows in advance that the silence is the instrument rather than a courtesy. §5 names the repair the review movement is watching for, and a learner who has read it will not make it in front of the room and will make it afterwards. §§1, 2, 3, 6, 7 and 8 spoil nothing.

> ## Vocabulary used here without definition
>
> | Term | Meaning |
> |---|---|
> | **Entry condition** | The observable state that tells an operator they are in this runbook rather than another one |
> | **Execution record** | What was captured while a non-author ran the document: stops, questions, unintended acts |
> | **Knowledge / ability distinction** | Having the information required to perform an act, versus performing it at the required standard. Attributed and warranted in §3.1 |
> | **Counter key** | The expression a gateway quota is measured against — a subscription, an address, or anything a policy expression can compute. Determines who a noisy neighbour is |
> | **Alertability / actionability** | Whether a metric makes failure unambiguous, and whether it carries enough signal to find a cause. Both are required before an entry condition can be written |
> | **Training–serving skew** | Different preprocessing at training and at serving. Produces no error and no obviously wrong output |
> | **Step kind** | This design's mark on each runbook step: command, judgement or context |

## In plain terms

A runbook is a document written by somebody with full context, for somebody with none, to be read at an hour when the author cannot be asked.

Authors cannot audit their own context. Every step they write is checked against a model of the system they carry in their head, and the model silently supplies what the document omits. The result is a document that is completely correct and partly unperformable, and no amount of careful re-reading by its author will reveal which parts.

There is one cheap instrument that reveals it: give the document to somebody else, watch them run it, and say nothing. The stops are the defects. **The author's silence is not politeness — it is the measurement**, because a spoken sentence substitutes the author's context for the document's and destroys exactly the reading the exercise exists to take.

There is a second problem underneath the first, and it is why this session has a platform anchor where its predecessor argued it needed none. **Most AI-system runbooks are written about the wrong layer.** The application is where the team's attention has been; the gateway is where the failures live, because the gateway is where the multi-tenant concerns are — shared quota, routing between endpoints, breakers, caches — and none of those exists until the second application appears. A runbook written about the application will send an operator to look at a component that is working.

The session then asks for the revision, and watches for the characteristic bad repair: adding a paragraph of background that would have prevented that one executor from asking that one question, rather than fixing the step that could not be performed.

## Abstract

This paper defends teaching runbooks through a single cold execution by a non-author rather than through a documentation standard, and anchoring the exercise to gateway and deployment configuration rather than to the application.

The argument runs in three parts. **First**, the defects that matter in operational documentation are invisible to the author by construction, because the author's own model of the system silently repairs the document as they read it; observation by a non-author is the only instrument available at a session's scale, and the author's silence is what makes it an instrument rather than a rehearsal. **Second**, the runbook's subject is usually misplaced: first-party gateway documentation describes a set of multi-tenant mechanisms — quota keyed on a counter, priority routing to provisioned capacity, breakers whose recovery time comes from the backend, caches that match on vector proximity — whose failures present at the application and are diagnosed at the gateway, and several of which fail silently or expensively without failing loudly. **Third**, silent failure is the characteristic failure mode of data systems rather than an unlucky case, which is why an entry condition can only be written against a surface that is alertable and actionable.

The session produces one runbook for one failure that actually occurred, an execution record with at least one recorded stop, and a revision traced to that stop.

The paper concedes that a single execution by a single peer on a prepared morning is a demonstration and not a measurement; that the peer is a *more* forgiving reader than the real operator, so the exercise is a lower bound; that the step-kind taxonomy is this design's invention with no warrant; and that nothing behind this programme establishes that systems with executed runbooks survive longer than systems without.

## 1. The problem this session must solve

Operational documentation fails in a specific and repetitive way, and the failure is structural rather than a matter of effort.

The author knows which dashboard the entry condition is read from, what *normal* looks like on it, which of two similar-looking failures is the common one, and who actually answers the escalation channel at night. None of that is in the document, and none of it is missing by choice — **it is missing because it never presented itself as information. It presented itself as the situation.**

Three consequences follow, and each has a countermeasure in the session's standard. Entry conditions become comparatives with no reference value, so the operator cannot tell whether they are in the runbook. Branches lose the evidence that selects them, so the operator picks the wrong one confidently. And escalation routes end at people rather than roles, so the document expires when the author's telephone number does.

There is a fourth consequence that the earlier version of this paper missed and that the platform anchor exists to correct. **The author writes about the layer they worked in.** They spent the engagement in the application, so the runbook is about the application, and the failures that will actually wake somebody are in the shared infrastructure the application talks through. An operator following a well-written runbook will inspect a healthy component, find nothing, and be left with no next step — which is worse than having no runbook, because it consumes the time and the confidence.

**The session's job is to make one author see one of these in their own document**, in a way that is not arguable, within a single session.

## 2. Entry state and prior knowledge

**Entry state: this-programme-only.** The session assumes an incident history from Stage 7, an operational readiness argument with its monitoring, and a manifest line from [S071](/wiki/seminars/S071.md) that named runbooks as a transfer object with an owner and an acceptance condition.

The requirement that the runbook address a failure that **actually occurred** is load-bearing and depends on that inheritance. An invented failure produces an invented runbook, whose defects are also invented, and the execution then tests the learner's imagination rather than their document.

The session also assumes the learner can read a gateway policy. It does not assume they have written one, and the workflow's second step exists because most learners will discover at that step that they cannot say what their own counter key is.

## 3. Theoretical basis

**3.1 The knowledge / ability distinction, and its warrant.**

The session rests on the claim that giving a person the information needed to perform an act and that person being able to perform it are different states, that the second requires practice, time and a tolerant environment, and that some people, for some changes, do not reach it.

That distinction comes from a **practitioner change model published by the consultancy that owns its trademark and sells training and certification in it** ([research/05-fde-craft/hiatt-adkar.md](/research/05-fde-craft/hiatt-adkar.md)). Its supporting evidence is that firm's own benchmarking survey work — practitioner self-report, no methodology reproduced in the book, no sampling frame, no response rate, no independent replication — and **no independent evaluation of the model appears anywhere in it.** It licenses **direction and mechanism only**: no ordering strength, no rate, no proportion, no magnitude, no claim that using the model improves outcomes.

**One correction travels with it.** The model is widely summarised as holding that effort past the blocking element is wasted. **The book does not say that**, and this repository withdrew the claim on reading it; the author's own formulations are conditional, with a single absolute confined to instruction in the absence of willingness. The conditional form is the one used here.

**The sentence the session actually turns on is a different one**, and it is why this source is retained despite its provenance: an unopened feedback channel makes a system's fault look like a person's failure. That is an observable claim about a situation rather than a claim about a model, and it describes precisely what happens when an operator stalls on a defective document in an organisation that has no mechanism for noticing that the document was at fault.

**3.2 Why observation rather than review.**

A document review by a second author produces opinions about wording. An execution produces a location: the executor stopped at step four. **The second is a fact about the document and the first is a fact about the reviewer**, and only one of them can be acted on.

Evans reaches an adjacent conclusion from a different direction and supplies a cheaper preliminary instrument. His argument for modelling out loud is that "One of the best ways to refine a model is to explore with speech… Rough edges are easy to hear" ([research/05-fde-craft/evans-domain-driven-design.md](/research/05-fde-craft/evans-domain-driven-design.md)). Reading a runbook aloud catches the audible subset of its defects for free, before anyone else's time is spent. He also supplies the standard the cold execution operationalises: "If the terms explained in a design document don't start showing up in conversations and code, the document is not fulfilling its purpose." **That is a test of a document by what happens to it, which is the session's whole method, stated in a book about domain modelling.**

Evans's limits: a practitioner book with no quantitative claim of any kind, whose evidence is the author's own named projects — several reported as failures, which raises its quality above ordinary consultant anecdote without making it a study.

**3.3 Why the author is silent.**

The instrument measures what the document supplies. Anything the author says is not in the document, and speaking therefore does not help the executor — it destroys the reading and leaves the defect in place for the person who will be alone with it later.

**3.4 The runbook's subject is the shared infrastructure, and the documentation says so more clearly than most teams do.**

The AI gateway documentation frames itself as an extension of an existing API gateway rather than a separate offering, and enumerates five concerns that only exist above one consumer: authenticate and authorise, load balance, monitor and log, manage token usage and quotas across applications, and enable developer self-service ([research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md)).

Four of its mechanisms generate failures a runbook must name and usually does not.

**Quota keyed on a counter.** The documentation's reasoning is stated plainly: with a growing portfolio "you need to make sure that one app doesn't use the whole TPM quota and block other apps from accessing the backends they need." **The victim of a noisy neighbour sees the model as unavailable.** Diagnosis requires a counter key the operator has never heard of, and the runbook's entry condition — errors from the endpoint — is satisfied by a fault whose cause is elsewhere.

**A breaker with a dynamic trip duration**, "applying values from the `Retry-After` header provided by the backend". Better engineering than a fixed timeout, and harder to operate: an operator cannot infer when the breaker will close, because the answer arrived in a header they cannot see.

**Priority routing to provisioned capacity**, recommended "particularly those purchased as PTU instances" so that dedicated capacity is saturated before overflow. **The failure mode is silent and expensive**: overflow works correctly, nothing alerts, and the cost changes.

**Semantic caching on "the vector proximity of the prompt to prior requests".** The documentation states the benefits. It does not state that the similarity threshold is a correctness knob, and that a loose one returns an answer to a question that was not asked.

One caveat on that page pre-empts the commonest wrong repair: gateway scale units do not scale the backends, which "must be deployed in the same regions". **An operator told to scale out will do it, and it will not help.**

**The documentation's limits.** First-party, definitional rather than empirical. Policy names, tier availability and preview status change frequently; several capabilities are preview and the Foundry integration is preview. No failure rates, no latency data, no comparative evidence for any configuration. It recommends logging prompts and completions "for billing and auditing" and does not discuss the privacy consequences — **so a runbook step instructing an operator to read a prompt log is instructing them to read customer content**, and the documentation will not tell them that.

**3.5 Silent failure is the characteristic failure of data systems, which is why entry conditions are hard.**

The production-ML literature names the canonical case: **training–serving skew**, arising from different preprocessing at training and serving, described as common and hard to detect because the model continues to return plausible answers ([research/02-technical-foundations/crowe-machine-learning-production-systems.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/crowe-machine-learning-production-systems.md)). It also supplies the vocabulary that turns a complaint into a locatable defect — drift compares one source across time, skew compares two sources; data drift moves the inputs, concept drift invalidates the mapping — and the two properties a surface must have before an entry condition can be written against it: **alertability**, thresholds that make failure unambiguous, and **actionability**, enough signal to find a cause. Both are borrowed from control theory, where a system can only be controlled to the extent it can be observed.

It warns separately that aggregate metrics conceal subgroup failure and that the choice of slices is a domain question rather than a statistical one. **An entry condition written against an aggregate will not fire for the failure destroying one segment.**

**Its limits:** a practitioner survey from a commercial publisher, not research; no study design, no comparison group, no evidence that its practices outperform alternatives; three of four authors work at the vendor supplying both worked pipelines; every number in it is a property of a particular system and none is reproduced here.

**3.6 Why steps are marked by kind, and the warrant for the taxonomy — which is none.**

An openly published engineering handbook resolves, across its pages, into several distinct genres — normative rules, artefact templates, worked examples, tool-pinned recipes, link lists and stubs — **interleaved with no navigational signal distinguishing them** ([research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md)). Reading it as a uniform standard overstates the depth of close to half of it.

The session generalises that observation down one level: a runbook's steps are also of several kinds — command, judgement, context — and an operator cannot tell which is which, and will execute the judgements as though they were commands. **The observation about the handbook is verified. The taxonomy applied to runbook steps is this design's invention and has no external warrant**, which §8 takes up.

That handbook must be cited by page and by the date that page was read, never as a whole; its footers span years, it contradicts itself in places without noticing, several tools it recommends are archived or deprecated, and it asserts unsupported magnitude claims which this repository records as specimens and **reproduces nowhere**.

**3.7 The pedagogical frame.** The opening items are a generation phase and are not scaffolded; exercise 3 is a comparison instruction rather than an invitation, because side-by-side presentation without an instruction to compare has been shown to achieve nothing ([research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md)). **School, university and laboratory evidence; no adult-professional study in its reference list; no magnitude asserted.**

**3.8 The unperformable step, which is a different defect class from the unreadable one, and the reason the session gained a second pass.**

Everything in §§3.2 to 3.6 concerns comprehension: the operator reads the step and cannot follow it. The cold execution is a good instrument for that and a **useless** instrument for a defect the session had not previously distinguished — the step the operator understands perfectly and cannot carry out.

Three documented platform behaviours establish that this class is real and is not exotic.

**Authority without access.** An enterprise agent control plane publishes a risk surface and, separately, a matrix of which roles may investigate which security products ([research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-365-and-agent-identity.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/agent-365-and-agent-identity.md)). The administrative role holding the authority to approve an agent, assign its owner and consent to its tools **cannot open the threat-detection or compliance evidence behind the risks it is asked to act on**, and one class of risk is investigable by nobody in the published matrix without roles granted inside a different product under a different administrative model. The consequence is deductive rather than empirical and is unusually strong for a claim about a document: **a single-operator triage runbook for that surface cannot be written**, because the required act needs a surface no single role reaches.

**A verb that does not name one operation.** A cloud AI platform's fleet view offers lifecycle control that differs by object class — stop for some, block-only for those whose infrastructure the platform cannot reach, deletion as the only stop for a draft, and stopping a workflow resource stopping every workflow on it — and states that **stopping does not terminate runs already in flight** ([research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-control-plane-and-governance.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/foundry-control-plane-and-governance.md)). The third of these is the dangerous one: the operator performs the step correctly, observes the expected confirmation, and holds a false belief about the incident.

**A command whose precondition is a file on one machine.** An agent development toolchain promotes between environments by a flag rather than an edit, which is the correct shape for a step a stranger must execute, and keeps some environment values in a deliberately uncommitted per-user file ([research/06-microsoft-platform/agents-toolkit-and-sdk-family.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/agents-toolkit-and-sdk-family.md)). **The convention is right and its consequence is that the step works on the author's machine and nowhere else**, failing with an error that names a missing value rather than a missing practice.

**Why this required a design change rather than a paragraph.** The peer executor shares the learner's permissions, so all three defects pass the cold execution silently. The second pass is therefore a paper procedure run before the room, and the taxonomy it installs — lacks the role, operation does not exist, operation does not do what the verb implies — **is this design's invention with no external warrant**, derived from three documented behaviours and evidenced by none of them as a taxonomy. §8 concedes it on the same terms as the step-kind taxonomy in §3.6.

## 4. Session design and its warrant

**4.1 The silence is the design and it is the part most likely to be broken.** Watching somebody fail at something you can fix in a sentence is unpleasant, the social pressure in the room is entirely towards speaking, and the learner has a plausible justification available — that the executor is a peer rather than the real recipient, so the exercise is artificial anyway. The session pre-empts that by making the executor's stop the deliverable: **a run with no stops is reported as a failed instrument, not as a good runbook.**

**4.2 One safety exception is written in rather than left to judgement.** If the executor is about to do something with a consequence outside the exercise, the author stops the run rather than coaching it, and records that they did. The distinction between stopping and coaching is the thing being taught, and it recurs in a sharper form at [S079](/wiki/seminars/S079.md), where the operator is the actual recipient and the intervention condition must be written before the observation begins.

**4.3 The revision is the deliverable and the first draft is the instrument.** This is stated to learners in advance, because a learner who believes the first draft is the assessed object will write defensively and the execution will produce nothing.

**4.4 The platform workflow is placed before the execution, not after.** Steps 2 and 3 send the learner into the gateway configuration before they write anything, and the reason is that a learner who writes first will write about the application and the execution will then test a document about the wrong layer. **What the workflow makes observable is a specific ignorance**: most learners cannot state their own counter key, and discovering that takes minutes and cannot be argued with.

**4.5 The review movement watches for the local repair.** Adding context that answers one executor's one question is the commonest fix and it does not generalise; fixing the unperformable step does. Naming this as the thing the room listens for converts it from a trap into a standard.

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**The rescue.** The author speaks. *Countermeasure:* the pairing is with a learner from a different engagement, so the author has no context to add cheaply, and the execution record has an explicit field for interventions.

**The wrong-layer runbook.** The document is about the application and the failure is in the gateway. *Countermeasure:* workflow step 2 requires the learner to read the policy and record which settings they did not know before they looked.

**The happy-path runbook.** The learner documents the procedure rather than the response, and the executor completes it without difficulty. *Countermeasure:* the standard requires at least one branch with its selecting evidence, and a run with no stops is reported rather than praised.

**The unstartable runbook.** The entry condition is a comparative with no reference — the answers look worse than usual. *Countermeasure:* the executor cannot begin, which makes this the one defect the instrument detects immediately and unarguably.

**The unmarked judgement.** A step requiring a decision is written in the grammar of a command. *Countermeasure:* exercise 4 requires every step to be marked and the judgements counted. **The countermeasure produces a count and not a repair**, and a learner who marks a judgement without stating its alternatives has complied without improving anything.

**The local repair.** *Countermeasure:* the review movement, which asks what changed rather than whether it is better, and exercise 6, which requires both repairs to be written so the difference is visible.

**The single-execution generalisation.** A learner concludes from one peer's difficulty that the recipient will have the same difficulty. *Countermeasure:* stated in the session's limits and tested properly at [S079](/wiki/seminars/S079.md) with the actual recipient. **This countermeasure is a caveat rather than a mechanism**, and caveats lose.

## 7. Assessment and evidence generated

No entrustment evidence. One specification item at [S075](/wiki/seminars/S075.md): at least one runbook with an execution record produced by a non-author, containing at least one recorded stop.

**The runbook's quality is not assessed and the executor's competence is not assessed.** What is checked is that the execution happened and left a record. An assessor marking runbook quality is marking their own model of the system, which is the defect the session exists to expose — and the narrative-assessment literature is direct that assessors reason from prose and confirm with scores rather than the reverse, so an assessor given a quality judgement to make will make it from their own model and then find the criteria that justify it.

**Nothing else is assessed.**

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: one execution by one peer proves nothing, and the session presents it as though it did.**

At its strongest: the executor is a single person with idiosyncratic gaps, drawn from the same cohort with the same training, running the document once in a supportive room on a prepared morning with no time pressure and no consequence. Whatever they stop at may be a fact about them. Two executors would disagree; twenty would produce a distribution; one produces an anecdote, and an anecdote in this shape is exactly the evidence standard this programme criticises its source corpus for accepting.

**This is conceded without qualification.** The rebuttal does not rescue the inference — it changes the claim. The session does not assert that the observed stop is the defect the recipient will hit; it asserts that a document whose author has never watched anyone else run it contains defects the author cannot enumerate, and that one execution moves the count of known defects from zero. **The declined fix was multiple executors per runbook**, rejected on session time, since it costs one execution per learner per peer and the session already runs both directions. The rejection buys coverage of the cohort at the price of the inference, and the objection stands against the price.

**Objection 2: real operational documentation is written for people with organisational context, and stripping it to what a stranger can perform makes it worse — longer, more literal, and unreadable by the people who will actually use it.**

At its strongest: an operations team that has run a platform for years does not want to be told which dashboard to open, and a document that tells them will not be read at all, so the session optimises for a reader who does not exist.

The rebuttal is that the reader does exist in this stage specifically: the transfer case is precisely the case where the operator has no context, and the supplied situation puts the credentials with a managed-service supplier who attended none of the engagement. **The objection is correct about steady-state documentation and wrong about handover documentation**, and the session's scope is handover. **The declined fix was to teach a two-tier runbook** — a terse operator path with an expandable context path — which was declined because writing two tiers requires knowing which steps need which tier, and that is exactly the knowledge the author does not have until after the execution. A team that has run the execution can build the second tier; a team that has not will guess.

**Objection 3: the step-kind taxonomy is invented, and inventing a taxonomy is the failure this programme attacks elsewhere.**

At its strongest: command, judgement and context are three labels with no research behind them, derived by analogy from an observation about a documentation site's genres, and the session asks learners to apply them and count the result as though the count meant something. [S068](/wiki/seminars/S068.md) spends an entire session on the hazard of a taxonomy that produces numbers while asserting nothing, and this paper introduces one two sessions later.

**Conceded, and the parallel is exact.** The mitigation is that the count is never assessed, is never compared between learners, and is used only to make the author notice judgements written as commands — a within-document observation rather than a between-document metric. **The declined fix was to drop the taxonomy and simply instruct learners to find the steps requiring a decision**, and it was declined because an unshaped search for judgements returns few, in the same way an unshaped search for absences does. The trade is a known small hazard against a known small failure, and a reader who prefers the unshaped instruction is making a defensible call.

**Objection 4: anchoring the session to one vendor's gateway teaches a product, and the durable claim does not need it.**

At its strongest: the claim that runbooks are written for the wrong layer is general. Attaching it to a named policy language, named policy elements and a named routing mode makes the session decay on the vendor's release cadence, and a cohort two years from now will spend the session translating.

**Partially conceded.** The perishability is real and the session's own register records every element of it as version-bound. The rebuttal is that the wrong-layer claim is unteachable in the abstract: a learner told that failures live in shared infrastructure will agree, and a learner who cannot state their own counter key has learned something they could not have agreed their way to. **The declined fix was to run the workflow against a diagram of a generic gateway**, and it was declined because a diagram cannot be read for a setting you did not know existed, which is the entire observation step 2 is built to produce.

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** Most first-draft runbooks will contain an entry condition that a non-author cannot evaluate. *Instrument:* the executor's start time and their first question, coded *could start* / *could not start*.

**Prediction 2.** Most learners will be unable to state their own counter key before workflow step 2. *Instrument:* a single written question asked before the step and again after, tallied per learner. **If most learners can state it in advance, the platform anchor is teaching nothing** and the workflow should be shortened to the execution.

**Prediction 3.** Authors will speak during the run more often than they predict they will. *Instrument:* a pre-run written commitment from each author on whether they expect to speak, compared with the observed intervention count in the execution record.

**Prediction 4.** The most common revision will be an addition of background rather than a repair of an unperformable step, **even among learners who completed exercise 6**, which requires both repairs to be written side by side. *Instrument:* the diff between draft and revision, coded *context added* / *step repaired* / *branch added* / *escalation changed* by a second reader, split by exercise-6 completion. **This predicts the failure of the session's own countermeasure**, and it is included because a countermeasure whose failure rate is unmeasured is an assumption.

**Prediction 5.** Runbooks written for a failure that actually occurred will produce more recorded stops than runbooks written for a hypothetical failure. *Instrument:* stop counts, split by whether the learner's chosen failure appears in the inherited incident history. **This is the prediction that matters most for the session's constraints**, because if it fails the requirement for a real failure is unnecessary and should be removed.

**Prediction 6.** Learners will mark fewer steps as judgements than a second reader marks. *Instrument:* the learner's step-kind marking compared against an independent marking of the same runbook, counting disagreements in each direction. **A systematic asymmetry would support §3.6's claim that authors experience their own judgements as commands**; symmetry would mean the taxonomy is capturing reader disagreement rather than author blindness, and §8's third objection wins.

**None of these has been measured.** No cohort has run this session.

## What a learner should take from this paper

**What the design claims.** That an author cannot find the defects in their own operational documentation; that observation is the cheapest instrument that can; that silence is what makes the instrument work; and that the document is probably about the wrong layer.

**What the evidence licenses.** That having information and being able to act are distinct states is direction and mechanism from a commercially interested practitioner source, and nothing more. The gateway mechanisms are first-party documentation at a moment in time, definitional and unmeasured. That silent failure is characteristic of data systems is a practitioner survey's account of its authors' experience. **Everything about the session's method is this design's argument**, including the step-kind taxonomy, which §8 concedes is invented.

**The concession worth remembering.** One executor, once, is an anecdote — and a peer executor is a *friendlier* reader than the person who will be alone with your document at three in the morning. The session's claim is narrower than the exercise feels: not that you have found the defect, but that you now know your document has some.
