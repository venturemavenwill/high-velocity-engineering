## In plain terms

**What this paper argues, before the formal version.**

Yesterday you built a criterion properly — from evidence, traced to named people, with its trade stated. It will stop being right, nobody will notice, and today is about why.

Three things degrade it. **Optimisation:** once anyone is rewarded for the criterion, the cheapest route to satisfying it gets taken, and that route rarely coincides with the thing you meant. **The world moves:** the population changes, the use changes, and the criterion keeps measuring exactly what it was built to measure while the thing that matters becomes something else. And **adjustment accumulates** — a threshold nudged, a category merged, an exclusion added, each defensible on the day it was made, until the criterion measures something nobody chose.

The third is the one the day exists for, because **it has no culprit.** There is no bad decision to find and nobody to blame, which is what makes it both very common and almost never discussed.

The second half is about what a validation actually is. People validate by reviewing: they look at the criterion, consider whether it still seems right, and conclude that it does. That is not a check. **A check is a procedure with a result the criterion might not survive** — and the operative test is simply whether the thing you are about to run could ever come back negative. If it could not, you have written a review and called it a validation, and you can do that annually for years.

**What this paper concedes, and it is a real concession.** The person who knows best where a criterion is weak is the person with the most to lose from finding out — and this design hands them the design of the check, the choice of threshold, and the reporting of the result, on an artefact they built the day before and are graded on. Every countermeasure it offers reduces that incentive without removing it, because the student still chooses the check. **A structural alternative existed: have the partner design the check on your criterion.** That would have removed the incentive rather than narrowed it, at the cost of the student not practising check design on their own work — and the design never weighed the trade anywhere.

## Abstract

This paper defends requiring students to build a check their own criterion could fail, one day after constructing it, and defends failing a check whose stated threshold could not have been reached. The first argument is that a criterion degrades by three mechanisms — optimisation against it, movement in the world, and cumulative adjustment — none of which produces an error, all of which are invisible in the reported number, and the third of which has no culprit; a validation that examines the criterion's current state detects the first two at best. The second argument is that the standard failure of validation is not that it is skipped but that it is designed to pass, and that a pre-committed threshold which the observed range could not have crossed is the operational signature of that failure. The paper additionally defends running the day's four time points as four registered evaluator versions over one response set, on the ground that cumulative drift is a claim until the four columns appear under one name; and it defends using the vendor's own validation advice — align the rubric with your own judgement — as the day's worked instance of a check that cannot fail, because it is well intentioned, widely followed, and exactly wrong in the way the day is about. It concedes that the day's hardest drift mechanism has no student-buildable detector, that it asks students to invalidate work they produced the previous day under conditions that make honesty expensive, and that the platform supplies the day's evidence and its counter-evidence from the same source.

## 1. The instructional problem this day must solve

Yesterday's criterion was built from evidence, traced to named people, with its trade stated. It will stop being right and nobody will notice, and the day's content is why.

**Optimisation degrades it.** Once anyone is rewarded for the criterion, the cheapest route to satisfying it is taken, and that route rarely coincides with the construct. This is S026 point 4 at a twenty-week gap and students hold it in principle; what they have not seen is it happening to a criterion they built.

**The world moves.** The population changes, the use changes, the stakeholders change. The criterion continues measuring exactly what it was built to measure, correctly, and the construct that matters is now a different one.

**Adjustment accumulates.** This is the mechanism the day exists for. A criterion is tuned repeatedly — a threshold moved, a category merged, an exclusion added — each change defensible on its own occasion, and after eleven of them the criterion measures something nobody chose. Phase 1 item 4 asks which adjustment broke it and the answer is none: **there is no culprit, and this is what makes the mechanism both common and undiscussable.**

The day's second problem is what a validation is. Students validate by reviewing: they look at the criterion, consider whether it still seems right, and conclude that it does. That is not a check. **A check is a procedure with a result the criterion might not survive**, and the distinction at point 3 is the day's operative definition. Phase 6's disclosure is a criterion validated annually, passing every year, measuring the wrong thing throughout — where the validation was real, was run, and was checking something the criterion could not fail.

The third problem is that T4-style drift is not detectable by any check that examines the criterion's state. Detecting it requires the criterion's *history* as data — the adjustment log, read as a sequence — which is a different kind of instrument and which most students cannot build. Point 5 names it and step 7 permits them to record that their check cannot detect it, which is the honest outcome.

## 3. Theoretical basis for the design

### 3.1 Load profile and element interactivity

**High, and unusual in that its difficulty is motivational rather than structural.** The three mechanisms are individually simple. Designing a check is a bounded task with supplied forms. What is hard is doing it honestly to an artefact you produced yesterday and will be assessed on.

Management:

- **The criterion is one day old and the student's own,** so no comprehension load.
- **Four time points supplied at phase 3** with their diagnoses adjudicated, so groups reason rather than construct.
- **Three validation forms supplied** at point 4 and at step 2.
- **The specification is indifferent to whether the criterion passed,** stated in advance, which removes the incentive that would otherwise dominate.
- **Step 7 permits recording an undetectable blind spot,** so a student who cannot build a T4 detector is not forced to pretend.

**Step 3 is unsupported.** Setting a threshold before the result exists is the class-4 demand and cannot be scaffolded without supplying the judgement.

### 3.2 Desirable difficulties selected, and those deliberately not

**Selected.**

- *Item 4's attribution demand with no answer.*
- *T4,* which no state-examining check detects.
- *Designing a check your own criterion could fail,* step 2.
- *Pre-committing the failure threshold,* step 3 — the discriminator.
- *Reporting a failure,* step 5.
- *The partner's defeat-the-check construction,* phase 7.

**Deliberately not selected.**

- *Statistical process control for drift.* Genuinely applicable and omitted; it would supply a machinery answer where the day wants a design judgement, and it belongs with S079.
- *Pipeline-caused drift.* Named at point 1's scope statement and deferred to S068.
- *Automated criterion monitoring.* S068's and S079's.
- *Reconstructing the criterion.* Point 6 names reconstruct, restrict and retire as options and the day does not carry any of them out; that is S050's and S069's territory.

**Scope, per the [Whitepaper Standard](/wiki/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md), strong form.** Point 1's three mechanisms explain why a criterion stops matching its construct. They omit drift caused by the measurement pipeline rather than the criterion — a scoring library change, a sampling change, a data-source substitution. **Safe** as an account of criterion-construct divergence, which is this day's subject. **Unsafe** as an account of why a reported number moved, which is the question an engineer actually faces when a metric shifts and which requires the pipeline as a second candidate. A student applying this day's three mechanisms to a moved number will search for a criterion problem that may not exist. That case is S068's.

**The platform instance, scoped in the strong form.** The day uses a registered evaluator's version history as its worked instance of cumulative drift, and the correspondence must carry its limits.

*Safe.* Reasoning about why a criterion's identity survives arbitrary change to its content; about why every report ever written about the criterion names it identically; about why detecting the third mechanism requires the sequence rather than the state; and about the difference between a stored version history and a usable adjustment log — the platform supplies the first and requires nothing of the second. Each of those is a property of any system that versions a definition under a stable name, which includes almost all of them.

*Unsafe.* Treating the version history as an adjustment log. **It records what changed and not why**, and the day's whole answer to T4 depends on the reason, which no schema field carries. A student who leaves believing their criterion's drift is detectable because the versions are retained has the mechanism exactly backwards. Also unsafe as a claim about this vendor's diligence: the stable-`id` decision is correct engineering — an identifier that changed on every edit would break every reference — and the day is not a criticism of it. **The point is that a well-made decision at one layer creates an undetectable failure at another**, which is [research/06-microsoft-platform/](/research/06-microsoft-platform/collected-materials.md)'s invisible-upward finding in a new place. Point 9 states this boundary to students, and step 6's requirement to name the drift the log would make visible is where a student applying it outside that range would fail.

**Remedy propagation, per the [Seminar Day Design Pattern](/wiki/program/04-Seminar-Day-Design-Pattern.md)'s rule 5.** Phase 7 reuses S029's dual-defeat protocol — construct a failure the check misses, then choose between extending the check and recording the blind spot. Under rule 5 this is an obligation rather than a design choice, and this is the first day written after that rule.

### 3.3 Engagement and motivational mechanism

The day runs on **a failure with no one to blame**.

Phase 1 supplies eleven adjustments and asks which broke the criterion. Students look for the bad one. There isn't one — each adjustment was made for a stated reason by someone acting reasonably, and the criterion is nonetheless unrecognisable. Item 4's prediction makes the search explicit and its failure is the day's opening.

That construction matters beyond this day. It is the fifth no-villain failure the programme has presented, after M07's four, and by Q5 students recognise the shape: **the most expensive failures in engineered systems are usually nobody's fault**, and the response is structural rather than disciplinary.

Phase 3's T4 supplies the day's hardest moment. Groups can name a check for T1 through T3 and cannot name one for T4, because every check they can imagine examines the criterion now. Point 5's answer — examine the history — is genuinely surprising and is the day's one piece of technical novelty.

The competence risk is the day's defining one: a student whose check fails their own criterion has, on the specification's terms, done the day well and, on every instinct they hold, produced a bad result. Phase 8's cohort proportion is the countermeasure — seeing that a substantial share of criteria failed their own checks makes it a property of criteria rather than of the student.

## 4. Phase-by-phase design and its warrant

**Phase 1 (25 min).** A real two-year criterion with its full adjustment history. Item 3 asks what would have detected the break at the time, which is the day's constructive question asked before any apparatus exists.

**Phase 2 (20 min).** The bridge retrieves S026 point 4 at twenty weeks and supplies mechanism one without exposition.

**Phase 3 (45 min).** One criterion, four registered versions, four diagnoses, then a check-design demand for each, **run over one held response set rather than described.** The construction's key property is that T1 through T3 have available checks and T4 does not, which sets up point 5 as a resolution rather than an addition. Running the four is what makes T4 bite: the versions share an identifier, a display name and a catalogue entry, so four columns of numbers that are not measurements of the same thing appear under one heading, and nothing in the output objects — because from the service's point of view nothing is wrong.

**Phase 4 (50 min).** Nine points. Point 1 carries the scope statement. Point 3's check-versus-review distinction is the day's operative definition and is stated as a test — could this procedure produce a result the criterion does not survive. Point 5 supplies history-as-data and names what the platform does and does not retain. Point 7 makes the re-validation schedule part of the criterion's definition rather than a follow-up. **Point 8 supplies a published, well-intentioned, widely followed instance of point 3's failure** — the vendor's own advice to validate a rubric by checking that its scores align with your own judgement — and names three defaults besides. Point 9 marks the perishable layer. Points 8 and 9 are the [Copilot Practice Standard](/wiki/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md)'s two required additions.

**Phase 5 (90 min).** Seven steps at class 4 on the student's own criterion, with step 3 unsupported, **shipping a registered validation run rather than describing one.** Step 5's requirement to report failures is enforced by the specification's indifference to the outcome, stated in advance. **Step 6 now requires the log format to name which T4-style drift it would make visible**, which is this paper's own §8 objection-1 repair, made.

**Phase 6 (40 min).** An annually validated criterion that passed every year and measured the wrong thing, then the agent case. The instructor case's attention to *what the validation was actually checking* is the phase's content: the validation examined internal consistency, which the criterion could not fail. **The agent case is the same failure at one adjustment rather than eleven**: an evaluation run uses the version pinned in its configuration, a locally edited rubric that was never re-registered is not that version, and the reported improvement is a true statement about a criterion nobody had written. It is chosen because it contains no incorrect step and no false sentence.

**Phase 7 (30 min).** Defeat the check, then choose. Rule 5 obligation.

**Phase 8 (20 min).** The attribution finding, and the cohort's own-check failure proportion.

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**Students with more relevant prior knowledge.** A student with measurement or quality-assurance experience will design a competent check. Predicted failure: they set a threshold at step 3 calibrated to what they expect, which is a threshold their criterion will meet — not dishonestly, but because expectation and threshold are formed by the same model. Countermeasure: phase 7's partner constructs a break the check misses, and a threshold set to the expected range is trivially evaded; the pair then faces step 7's forced trade.

**Students with less relevant prior knowledge.** Step 2 is bounded by the supplied forms and step 3 is the hard one. Countermeasures: the forms; the outcome-indifferent specification; the permitted blind-spot record. Residual risk: their threshold may be arbitrary rather than reasoned, which the specification's could-not-have-been-reached clause catches only in the extreme case.

**Phase 3 is too easy.** T1, T2 and T3 are diagnosed correctly by most groups. T4 is not, and no group is expected to produce its check. Fallback if a group does: ask what the adjustment log would have to record for their check to work, which is a genuine design question and previews the format demand at step 6.

**Affective failure.** Two, and the first is the day's defining risk.

**Invalidating your own work is expensive**, and a student who suspects their criterion will fail has an incentive to build a check that will not detect it. This is not usually deliberate; it operates through threshold choice and check selection. Countermeasures: the specification's stated indifference; phase 8's cohort proportion; phase 7's adversarial partner; and the could-not-have-been-reached clause. The design's honest position is that these reduce the incentive and do not remove it, because the student still chooses the check.

The second is **fatalism about criteria**, arriving after S046 established that criteria are decisions and this day establishes that they degrade regardless. Countermeasure: point 7's re-validation schedule is the constructive output — a criterion with a schedule is one somebody has committed to checking, which is a materially different object from one without, and the difference is in the design's control.

## 7. Assessment and evidence generated

No entrustment judgement — the module's is at S050.

One specification item in ME201. Per rule 1, the checked elements are: a stated construct, a check with its form, a pre-committed threshold, the check run, the result including failure, a re-validation schedule and log format naming the T4-style drift it would make visible, a statement of what the check cannot detect, the phase 7 outcome, and the prompt, intervention and frontier records. **Nothing else is assessed, including whether the criterion passed.**

**A check whose stated threshold could not have been reached fails.** This is the operational signature of a validation designed to pass and is checkable against the observed range.

**An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that the design could have detected an intervention-worthy error. The accepted instance here is a check that asserts it is running against the registered version it names and fails loudly if it is not — which is the phase 6 agent failure built into the artefact, and a student who built it has done better than one who caught the error by hand.

Programme instrumentation: the item 4 attribution-attempt pattern is the cohort's no-culprit-failure baseline; **the proportion of criteria failing their own checks is the day's headline figure** and is compared across cohorts as an honesty indicator; the phase 7 defeat rate is the check-quality measure carried to S068; **item 5's sealed prediction accuracy is the day's frontier-calibration datum** and is read by the following year's cohort through the [Durable and Perishable Register](/wiki/program/09-Durable-and-Perishable-Register.md), per instrumentation rule 9.

The frontier statements are a sample and the day says so, per instrumentation rule 3: one cohort, one substrate, one validation task on a criterion one day old, chosen by staff so that a version-pinning failure is available.

Dispositions: **integrity**, at steps 3 and 5, where the incentive to set a permissive threshold and to soften a failure is direct and largely undetectable.

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: the day's hardest mechanism has no student-buildable detector, and the day knows it.**

At its strongest: T4 is presented as the payload — the drift with no culprit, the one that matters most, the one no group can build a check for. Point 5 supplies the answer in principle: examine the adjustment history as a sequence. But no student on this day builds such a detector, step 7 explicitly permits recording that their check cannot detect it, and step 6 asks only for a *log format* rather than for an analysis of one. The day therefore teaches that its most important failure mode is beyond the students' reach and asks them to write it down as a limitation.

The rebuttal is that the log format is the substantive contribution and the day underplayed it. T4 is undetectable *without a log designed for the purpose*, and designing that log — what each adjustment must record, in what form, so that a sequence can later be read — is a real and buildable artefact. A cohort that leaves having specified adjustment logs for their criteria has done the thing that makes T4 detection possible for whoever comes next.

The concession was that the design did not weight it this way, and **the fix it named is now in the design**: step 6 requires the format to state which T4-style drift the log would make visible and what field carries the reason a version was created. What remains conceded is that the specification still checks that statement's presence rather than its adequacy, because adequacy is a judgement the day has no instrument for — and that is a weaker version of the same failing rather than its removal.

**Objection 2: the day asks students to attack work they will be assessed on, and its countermeasures address the incentive rather than removing it.**

At its strongest: the student designs the check, sets the threshold, runs it, and reports the result — all on an artefact they built the previous day and are graded on. Every degree of freedom in that chain can be exercised toward a passing result without any conscious dishonesty. The design's countermeasures are a stated indifference to the outcome, a cohort proportion shown afterwards, an adversarial partner, and a clause catching the extreme case. None of these removes the student's control over the check's design, which is where the incentive operates.

This objection the design does not fully answer, and its structure is worth naming: the day's problem is that the person with the most information about where a criterion is weak is the person with the most to lose from finding out.

Two things narrow it. The specification's checked elements do not include the criterion's survival, and this is stated before step 2 — so the *assessed* incentive is neutral even if the reputational one is not. And phase 7's partner designs the attack, which removes the student's control at the one point where it matters most.

**The available structural fix is to have the partner design the check rather than attack it.** A student validating a peer's criterion has no incentive to be gentle and no information disadvantage that matters, since the criterion's construction is documented. That would remove the incentive rather than reduce it, at the cost of the student not practising check design on their own artefact — a real cost and a smaller one than the objection identifies. The design has not weighed this trade anywhere, which is the concession: it built the version with the incentive problem without recording that an alternative existed.

**Objection 3: the day's most persuasive platform evidence is the vendor's own advice, and using it as the worked bad example is closer to a rhetorical move than to a demonstration.**

This is the objection that bites on the practical turn.

At its strongest: point 8 takes a sentence of published guidance — validate that the rubric's scores align with your own judgement before using it at scale — and presents it to students as a check that cannot fail. The sentence is doing something reasonable in its own context. It is advice to someone building a rubric for the first time, in a documentation page about building rubrics, where the alternative to *compare it against your own judgement* is *ship it unexamined*, and against that alternative the advice is straightforwardly good. **The day strips that context and uses the sentence as an exhibit.** A student who learns to read vendor guidance as a source of exhibits rather than as an artefact with a purpose has acquired a habit this programme should not be installing, and it is the same habit the module spends five days warning about in the other direction.

**The objection is conceded, and the concession is the sharper half of it.** The day gains a great deal from the example being real, published and well intentioned — a constructed bad example teaches that bad validations exist, and a real one teaches that good engineers write them — and that gain is what makes the unfairness worth noticing rather than what excuses it.

**The cheap fix is one sentence and it is made.** Point 8 now states that the advice is sound engineering and the wrong shape for a validation, in that order, so that students see the two claims as compatible. What remains unfixed is that **the day never asks what the documentation should have said instead**, which is a two-minute question with a real answer — validate against a judgement you have not seen, held back before you started — and which would turn the exhibit into an exercise. It is not in the design.

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** A majority will attempt to name a single breaking adjustment at item 2, and item 4 predictions will affirm single-adjustment attribution. *Instrument:* items 2 and 4 coded.

**Prediction 2.** No group will produce a T4 detector before point 5. *Instrument:* phase 3 check designs by time point.

**Prediction 3.** The proportion of criteria failing their own checks will be below a fifth. *Instrument:* step 5 results. **A very low proportion is the §8 objection-2 signature and should be read as evidence about threshold-setting rather than about criterion quality.**

**Prediction 4.** In phase 7, partners will defeat the check for a majority of pairs, and defeat will be more common where the step 3 threshold sits inside the observed range. *Instrument:* phase 7 outcomes against threshold placement.

**Prediction 5.** A majority of item 5 sealed predictions will state that the agent's validation will be flawed, and a minority will name that it validates against the constructor's own judgement. *Instrument:* item 5 responses coded for generic-versus-specific failure identification, against what each student's agent produced. **If the specific rate exceeds the generic rate the item is malformed**, since naming the mechanism entails predicting the fault.

## What a student should take from this paper

**What the design is claiming.** That a criterion you built correctly will stop measuring what you meant, by three routes, and that none of them produces an error anybody can point at. The third route — many small defensible adjustments — has no culprit at all, which is why the day is about designing a check rather than about being careful.

**What the evidence licenses.** Criterion drift, proxy degradation under optimisation and validation practice are **class 1**, grounded in [research/03-measurement-evaluation/shankar-evalgen.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/shankar-evalgen.md) and [research/03-measurement-evaluation/singh-leaderboard-illusion.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/singh-leaderboard-illusion.md). The versioning behaviour and the validation advice examined at point 8 are **class 4**, read on 26 July 2026 and marked preview, with the silence recorded: no schema field carries the reason a version was created. **The three-mechanism account and the check-versus-review definition are class 3.** You may say criteria degrade; you may not say this taxonomy is complete or your own check adequate.

**What to do with it.**

- Ask of any validation: could this procedure produce a result the criterion does not survive? If not, it is a review.
- Set the threshold before the result exists, then check afterwards whether the observed range could ever have crossed it.
- **Provenance is not correctness** — see the [Agent Configuration Standard](/wiki/program/13-Agent-Configuration-Standard.md). A retained version history tells you what changed and never why, and the drift that matters lives in the missing reason.
- Specify the adjustment log you would need, and what each entry must record.

**The concession worth remembering.** The design puts the check's design in the hands of the party with the most to lose from what it finds, and its countermeasures reduce that incentive without removing it. A structural alternative — your partner designs the check on your criterion — would have removed it, and was never weighed anywhere in the design.
