# M13 · Running What You Built

**Quarter:** [Q7](/wiki/quarters/Q7.md) · **Sessions:** [S061](/wiki/seminars/S061.md)–[S065](/wiki/seminars/S065.md) · **Complexity class:** 4
**Strands:** PE · ME
**Platform anchor:** none

---

## The central difficulty

A system in production has a state, and almost nothing about the way delivery organisations work makes that state discoverable.

The dashboard was assembled from whatever the platform offered by default. The security work items were closed in a meeting by somebody who said the fix was in. The alert set grew by accretion and is now filtered into a folder. The running cost is known to a finance system and to nobody on the team. **Each of those is a substitution of an assertion for a state**, and each is invisible until the moment it matters, which is the moment somebody who did not build the system has to operate it.

This module's position is that the fix is not diligence. It is that **every claim about the running system should be re-derivable by somebody who does not trust you.** A signal should name the question it answers. A closure should cite a location. An alert should name the action it expects. A cost should be expressed in a unit the customer recognises and instrumented against telemetry the customer can read.

None of that requires a better tool. All of it requires deciding, in advance and in writing, what would count.

## The best idea available to this programme

**A mitigation's status is not something a person ticks. It is re-derived by searching the workspace, and a claim of implemented must cite a file and a location.**

The mechanism comes from a chained agent pipeline built inside a customer-embedded engineering organisation within a large technology vendor. A stage of that chain searches source, pipeline definitions, container files, infrastructure definitions in several dialects, configuration and documentation for evidence that each mitigation is present. Re-running it after a genuine fix finds the evidence, flips the status, and the downstream stage that generates work items then generates nothing for that step.

**Closure is evidence in the repository, not a person's assertion.** That is the right shape, most organisations do not have it, and it is the strongest single design decision anywhere in this programme's source material.

The instruction that governs the whole stage is the right one and the module adopts it verbatim in substance: **when in doubt, classify as unclear, because a false claim of implementation is worse than a false negative.** The corresponding practitioner rule sits alongside it — **evidence is a file path and a line, never an assertion that something is secure.**

## The three caveats, carried and never dropped

The mechanism is good and it is not clean. [S062](/wiki/seminars/S062.md) teaches all three failures as part of the idea rather than as an appendix to it, because a learner who takes the mechanism without them will trust an output that is silent in exactly the cases that matter.

**Nothing schedules the re-run.** The derivation is only true at the moment it is performed, and the design contains no trigger, no cadence and no owner. An un-run derivation is a stale assertion with better provenance.

**A real fix invisible in the repository never closes.** A portal setting, a tenant policy, a control applied by a platform team, a contractual obligation, a changed manual process — each is a genuine mitigation and none leaves a file to cite. **The item regenerates every run, indefinitely, until somebody starts ignoring the output** — and once they are ignoring one item they are ignoring the list.

**The acceptance criteria were written by the same model that wrote the mitigation.** The criterion a human would close a ticket against, and the mitigation it checks, share an author. That is not fatal and it is not nothing: it means the check inherits whatever the mitigation misunderstood, and the two will agree.

[S062](/wiki/seminars/S062.md) requires the procedure, the schedule with a named owner, and **a register of the fixes the procedure cannot see.** The register is the assessed artefact. A closure procedure whose author cannot name its blind spot has not been read by its author, which is the same discipline [S057](/wiki/seminars/S057.md) installed for scope tests.

## The two rules the module takes from the openly published playbook

**Observability configuration is code, and the reason is peer review.** Dashboards, alerts and log queries held in source control and deployed by infrastructure as code acquire the property that every other engineering artefact has and that click-built configuration does not: somebody else looks at them before they take effect, and a change to them can be diffed, reverted and attributed. That is the argument [S061](/wiki/seminars/S061.md) is built on, and it is a better argument than the reproducibility one because it names a mechanism rather than a benefit.

Paired with it is a standing review rule that is more radical than it sounds: **any data collected but not aggregated or alerted on must be reviewed to establish whether it is still required.** Collect what you need rather than what you can. Most telemetry estates fail that rule immediately, and [S061](/wiki/seminars/S061.md) requires the learner to run it on their own.

**A service level is the minimum viable service quality acceptable to the customer — not what you intend to deliver.** That definition is the module's second borrowed rule and it inverts the way most teams set one. An objective set at what the team hopes to achieve produces an error budget of nothing and turns every ordinary variation into a breach; an objective set at what the customer would actually accept produces a budget that can be spent deliberately.

**Alert fatigue is real**, and the same source says so plainly. [S063](/wiki/seminars/S063.md) treats the two rules as one instrument: the service level determines which alerts are legitimate, and everything that fires without expecting an action is deleted, with the deletion recorded.

**The page carrying that service-level definition is among the oldest on its site**, and the site has no single date. That is not a criticism of the definition; it is the reason every citation from that source in this module is pinned to a page and that page's own footer date, and never to the document as a whole.

## The failure this module can name and the source cannot

**A signal produced by a system that no downstream process consumes is not a signal.**

The pipeline M13 learns from has three auxiliary analysers that produce good standalone reports about code and infrastructure. **None of them emits an identifier the rest of the chain uses, none writes into the plan, and no stage consumes their output.** A real vulnerability found by the code analyser, with no corresponding entry in the threat registry, never becomes a threat, never becomes a work item and never reaches anybody's backlog. The reports are excellent and they are parallel outputs that happen to share a knowledge source.

That is the exact structure of the incident nobody saw, and it is why [S063](/wiki/seminars/S063.md) requires an **unconsumed-signal register**: for every output the system produces, the process that consumes it, or a recorded decision that nothing does. **A finding that reaches no queue was not found by the organisation, only by the instrument.**

## Cost, and the asymmetry that starts here

[S064](/wiki/seminars/S064.md) is the module's bridge into [M14](/wiki/modules/M14.md), and it is placed here rather than there for a reason.

**The cost side of the source material is genuinely well engineered.** Consumption is forecast by engineers, instrumented by engineers, and refined against telemetry from the system itself. That is a real practice with a real method and the module teaches it as content.

**Two things are missing from it and both are assessed.** The first is that no cost side appears anywhere in the corpus's own value claims — staffing, compute, the cost of the discovery activity itself and opportunity cost are all absent, and **every claim offered is a gross benefit.** The second is that the one place the right instinct appears — that the customer should be left holding the cost-modelling capability, because they will need it after the team disengages — sits as a reflection inside a narrative rather than as a step in any procedure. **Nobody is required to hand the model over and nobody is required to check that it runs.**

[S064](/wiki/seminars/S064.md) therefore marks two things the source does not: that the model runs in the customer's hands, and that the delivery cost sits in the same document as the running cost.

## Why the module sits here

**It needs [M11](/wiki/modules/M11.md) and [M12](/wiki/modules/M12.md) in front of it.** Evidence-derived closure operates on a threat registry with numbered flows and an intact unmitigated list; without them there is nothing to close and nothing to fail to close. The work items it re-derives are the ones [S060](/wiki/seminars/S060.md) generated.

**It has to precede [M14](/wiki/modules/M14.md)** because a measurement of value is worthless from a system whose state cannot be discovered. A claim that the system reduced something, made from an estate where nobody can say what is running or what it costs, is a claim about a belief.

## Load profile

The module's intrinsic load is moderate and concentrated in [S062](/wiki/seminars/S062.md), where the learner has to hold a mechanism and its three failure modes simultaneously without discarding either.

**Its real difficulty is elsewhere and it is not technical.** [S063](/wiki/seminars/S063.md) requires a learner to delete alerts, which means accepting that a class of incident will now go undetected and writing down which one. [S064](/wiki/seminars/S064.md) requires putting the delivery cost next to the benefit, in a document somebody commercial will read. [S065](/wiki/seminars/S065.md) requires arguing that a system is ready to be operated by people who did not build it, in front of an assessor who will offer to let the argument off its hardest requirement.
