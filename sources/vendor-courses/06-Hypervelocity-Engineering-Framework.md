## At a glance

- Hypervelocity Engineering (HVE) is not a product or a rigid framework — it is a **holistic way of working** that emerges from Microsoft's most effective teams co-innovating and co-engineering with customers.
- HVE is defined as: expert multidisciplinary teams using AI tools, proven starting points, and design thinking across the entire lifecycle to **accelerate time to business value and maximize ROI**.
- The four pillars are: (1) Tight Multidisciplinary Teams with Deep Domain Expertise, (2) Design Thinking Methods that Create Business Value, (3) Proven Mission-Critical Production Starting Points, and (4) AI Agents and Tools across the Full Lifecycle.
- Microsoft-wide impact metrics: **2–3x velocity increase**, **50%+ team size reduction** (3–4 vs 10+ people), and **30%+ of code written by AI**.
- Forward Deployed Engineering (FDE) is the delivery model that HVE powers — reserved for mission-critical, AI-centric problems with clear, measurable business outcomes and executive sponsorship.
- The **Research-Plan-Implement (RPI) flow** is the core AI-assisted engineering methodology, splitting thinking, planning, doing, and reviewing into distinct agent-driven phases to improve output quality.
- The foundational principle: **"Output quality is bounded by context quality, not model quality."** Better models with bad context still produce bad results.
- Design Thinking in HVE distinguishes between Problem, Solution, and Implementation spaces — engineers must resist sprinting straight to implementation before validating the problem.
- Proven starting points (accelerators such as CAIRA, Edge AI, Intelligent Data, and HVE Core) reduce reinvention, embed security/observability from day one, and let teams focus on differentiated business value.
- Responsible AI and the Secure Future Initiative (SFI) are non-negotiables in every HVE engagement: secure by design, security by default, secure operations.

## 1. Hypervelocity Engineering Framework

### 1a. Hypervelocity Engineering, Forward Deployed Engineers (FDE)

**What it covers** — This lesson establishes what HVE is, where it came from, how it differs from traditional Agile delivery, and what the Forward Deployed Engineering model requires from both Microsoft and customers. It frames the AI Engineering Age and explains the three opportunity areas that make HVE possible and necessary.

**Key concepts**

- **Hypervelocity Engineering (HVE)** — A holistic way of working where expert multidisciplinary teams use AI tools, proven starting points, and design thinking across the entire lifecycle to accelerate time to business value and maximize return on investment.
- **Forward Deployed Engineering (FDE)** — Microsoft's delivery model for mission-critical, AI-centric problems; small teams of domain experts embedded side-by-side with customer product teams; not a handoff model.
- **FDE promise** — "Your IP, our experts, rapid innovation."
- **AI Engineering Age** — The era in which AI has fundamentally changed how fast software can be built, making speed + engineering rigor the winning combination.
- **From Rapid Prototyping to Engineering Rigor** — Lasting impact comes from pairing speed with strong engineering discipline, domain expertise, and quality practices to ensure scalability, security, and sustainability.
- **Speed with Purpose** — Real value comes from solving the right problems, those that drive meaningful business outcomes and customer impact, not from maximizing feature velocity for its own sake.
- **Context-Aware, Trustworthy AI** — AI systems perform best when grounded in trusted data, reliable code, and clear context.
- **Tight Multidisciplinary Teams** — Teams composed of Data Scientists, Software Engineers, Program Managers, Designers, and Subject Matter Experts who bring domain expertise, skills, and experience to create robust holistic solutions.
- **ISE Engagement Flow** — Envisioning, exploration, design, and development become continuous and iterative rather than linear; shorter, smaller iterations in days/weeks rather than weeks/months.
- **Traditional Agile** — Baseline: 2-week sprints, 10+ person teams, months to deliver (Microsoft teams as of 2017).
- **The Catalyst** — A step-change in the performance of AI models approximately one year before the course was created drove the shift to HVE.
- **HVE in Action** — 3–4 experts, AI-powered, continuously delivering in days/weeks.
- **Microsoft-Wide Transformation Impact** — 2–3x velocity increase; 50%+ team reduction; 30%+ code written by AI (always reviewed and signed off by a person).
- **Backlog generation via HVE** — GitHub Copilot + ADO MCP converts meeting notes into an 80% ready structured backlog, requiring significantly less manual data entry.
- **Legacy codebase transformation** — HVE used to reason over existing codebases, automate documentation, enable debugging, and empower developers to build on legacy code faster.
- **Workshop acceleration** — Prototypes built during workshops; features added in as little as 5 minutes; drives real user feedback and executive alignment in real time.
- **Customer examples** — Aker BP (no-code to pro-code Copilots for everyone); Fairlife ($1.5 million savings in 2 months from anomaly detection; concept to production in 2 weeks); Trimble (120,000 hours/year saved, $10 million annually via agents writing daily action reports).
- **140+ depth engagements** — HVE used in over 140 depth engagements with customers over approximately 1.5 years.
- **Value measurements** — Faster time to value, increased ROI, higher quality and reliability, solving the right problems, improved developer experience, faster upskilling and onboarding.
- **Frameworks referenced** — Accelerate, SAFe, SPACE, GitHub Engineering System Success Playbook (ESSP).

**Detailed notes**

HVE originated from Microsoft's ISE (Industry Solutions Engineering) organization and represents ~10 years of evolving delivery practice. In 2017 teams used traditional Agile: 10+ person teams, 2-week sprints, months to deliver. A step-change in AI model performance triggered the shift to HVE, enabling 3–4 expert teams to deliver in days/weeks what used to take 10+ people months.

*Forward Deployed Engineering fit criteria:*
- Mission-critical problem with clear, measurable business outcomes
- AI-centric challenge not solved by existing Microsoft products, standard services, or a typical delivery model
- Executive sponsorship from the accountable business owner
- Customer commitment to the Microsoft technology stack
- Customer dedication of product teams to work shoulder-to-shoulder with FDE crews

*FDE three properties:*
1. Small team of domain experts — genuine co-build, not a handoff
2. Customers control their data and IP — Microsoft secures and protects improvements
3. Flywheel — embedded engineers feed real-world feedback directly to the Microsoft product roadmap

*AI Engineering Age three principles:*
1. Rapid prototyping → engineering rigor (prototype in hours, but lasting value requires discipline)
2. Speed with purpose (be problem-driven and user-driven, not tool-driven)
3. Context-aware, trustworthy AI (trend: prompt engineering → context engineering → harness engineering)

*Traditional engagement phases (pre-HVE) with durations:*
| Phase | Duration |
|---|---|
| Envisioning | Weeks to months |
| Exploration and planning | Weeks to months |
| Architecture and design | Weeks to months |
| Development | 2–6 months |
| Handoff | Weeks or longer |
| **Total** | **~1 year or more** |

*With HVE:* Envisioning, exploration, design, and development are interconnected loops, not sequential stages. Iterations shrink to days-to-weeks. Sprint ceremonies that once justified 2-week cadences can now be done in 2-day cycles. Teams test concepts more easily, develop prototypes during workshops, and use data science dashboards to bring SMEs into the analytics process in real time.

*HVE principles for customer engagements:*
1. Everything done must drive tangible business value — this is the North Star
2. Meet customers where they are — no two engagements look exactly the same
3. Shift away from linear handoffs — envisioning, exploration, design, development are continuous loops
4. Speed matters but not recklessly — smaller, faster iterations; days to weeks instead of weeks to months

*HVE Workshops:* Not PowerPoint presentations, not surface-level AI discussions. Hands-on work grounded in real customer examples. Teams leave with deeper understanding and concrete actions to build upon. Prep work (introducing concepts, aligning stakeholders, scoping a foundational pain point) is key before going on site.

**Exam / assessment pointers**

- Know all three Microsoft-wide impact figures: **2–3x velocity**, **50%+ team reduction**, **30%+ AI code**.

- The Catalyst for change was a **step-change in AI model performance**.
- Know the three customer examples and their specific numbers (Fairlife: $1.5M, 2 months; Trimble: 120K hours/yr, $10M/yr; concept to production in 2 weeks for Fairlife).
- The backlog generation example uses GitHub Copilot + ADO MCP to achieve **80% ready** backlog.
- Legacy codebase: HVE used to document, debug, and enable faster builds on old code.

### 1b. Design Thinking for HVE

**What it covers** — This lesson explains how Design Thinking is embedded into HVE engagements as a continuous practice (not a one-time workshop), covering the three spaces (Problem, Solution, Implementation), key mindsets (iterate in small steps; validate and verify), methods woven into the engagement lifecycle, and the maturity model for Design Thinking adoption.

**Key concepts**

- **Design Thinking** — Mindsets and methods for solving the right problem the right way to create business value.
- **Three Spaces** — Problem space, Solution space, Implementation space.
- **Problem space** — Why something is happening; the root cause or unmet need (e.g., "spending too much money on sampling").
- **Solution space** — What could be built (e.g., "reducing frequency of sampling", "smarter sampling", "reducing ad hoc sampling").
- **Implementation space** — How it is built (e.g., "ML prediction model", "knowledge graph", "AI for insights", "custom phone app against Copilot Studio backend").
- **Mindset: Increase business value** — Every activity should move toward measurable impact, not just activity.
- **Mindset: Iterate in small steps** — Gain confidence through frequent small increments, not long build cycles.
- **Mindset: Validate and verify** — Talk with end users throughout the entire process, not just once; every access to users is a hotline to business value.
- **Methods** — Activities and actions that provide confidence of being on the right path; interwoven throughout the engagement lifecycle, not isolated workshops; small things integrated into day-to-day work (sprints, customer conversations).
- **Journey mapping** — Design thinking method for understanding the full user experience.
- **Problem framing** — Explicitly articulating the problem before jumping to solutions.
- **Hypothesis validation** — Testing assumptions about solutions with users.
- **Activity vs. impact** — Teams can tick all technical boxes yet still fail to create business value if they solve the wrong problem.
- **Factory worker case study** — Real engagement where the team built exactly what was asked but failed: (1) workers had greasy fingers/wore gloves and couldn't use keyboard/touchscreen; (2) agent couldn't reliably answer the questions workers actually asked; (3) "secret" repair manuals were never disclosed; (4) it was still faster/cheaper to do things the old way.
- **Correct approach in the case study** — Visit the factory floor; talk through end-to-end scenarios with workers; examine pilot telemetry; interview maintenance engineers; mock up a prototype to measure speed/costs before full production build.
- **Manufacturing fluid-sampling example** — Problem: "spending too much money on sampling"; Solutions: "reducing frequency", "smarter sampling", "reducing ad hoc sampling"; Implementations: "ML prediction model", "knowledge graph", "AI for insights".
- **Happy story vs. sad story** — Sad: built exactly what customer asked, all boxes checked, deployed to production, failed to create value. Happy: uncovered unmet needs, iterated with design feedback, incrementally validated architecture with accelerators, deployed with confidence, created business value.
- **Design Thinking Maturity — Early** — One-time workshop or ideation exercise; disconnected from engineering delivery; insights rarely influence real product decisions.
- **Design Thinking Maturity — Intermediate** — Intentional use to prioritize high-impact problems; user research integrated into solution framing; customer insights begin shaping what gets built.
- **Design Thinking Maturity — Advanced** — Human-centered design fully embedded throughout lifecycle; journey mapping, problem framing, hypothesis validation are standard practice; continuous testing of assumptions; design as a decision-making discipline.
- **HVE Design Thinking Accelerator** — A specific accelerator to support applying Design Thinking in HVE engagements.
- **Design Thinking Coach (in HVE Core)** — Helps think through questions to ask a customer to get into the three design thinking spaces.

**Detailed notes**

Design Thinking in HVE is described through the lens of two stories about a large manufacturer building an agent for factory workers to do their own equipment maintenance.

*The sad story (technology-only mindset):*
- Perfect team assembled; built exactly what the customer asked in 2 weeks
- Used accelerators, production-ready starting points, engineering foundations
- Checked all success criteria; deployed to production
- Still failed to create business value

*Root causes of failure:*
1. Workers had greasy fingers/wore gloves — keyboard/touchscreen unusable
2. Agent couldn't reliably answer the actual questions workers ask
3. "Secret" repair manuals existed that were never shared
4. It was still faster and cheaper to do things the old way

*The happy story (Design Thinking applied):*
- Uncovered unmet needs before building
- Iterated with users to validate solution direction
- Validated architecture incrementally with proven accelerators
- Deployed with greater confidence; succeeded in creating business value

*Breaking down problem/solution/implementation:*
The distinction is critical. When customers describe their needs, they often mix all three. Engineers (particularly) tend to sprint straight to implementation. Design Thinking demands staying in the problem space longer.

| Customer Statement | Space |
|---|---|
| "Reducing frequency of sampling" | Solution |
| "ML prediction model" | Implementation |
| "Knowledge graph of existing systems" | Implementation |
| "Reducing ad hoc sampling" | Solution |
| "Smarter sampling" | Solution |
| "AI for insights" | Implementation |
| "They spend too much money on sampling" | Problem |

*Mindsets applied across spaces:*
- In the problem space: validate and verify (is this the real problem?)
- In the solution space: iterate in small steps (explore multiple solutions before committing)
- In the implementation space: validate and verify with users; iterate on output quality

*Methods woven into engagements:* Design thinking is not a pause in the engagement — it is woven into sprints, customer conversations, and daily work. The awareness of the full picture is valuable even for those who don't go deep into every method (e.g., a developer focused on sprints may not lead scope conversations, but understanding them helps deliver better outputs).

Key takeaways from the lesson:
1. Do what you can with the options you have — even mostly frozen projects have room for design thinking
2. Think about the end user every step of the way; take every opportunity to validate ideas
3. Use AI tools to make each step better, not to skip steps
4. Do more iterations within each step with available time, rather than fewer, more refined iterations

**Exam / assessment pointers**

- Design Thinking definition: "mindsets and methods for solving the **right problem** the **right way** to create **business value**."
- Know the three spaces in order: **Problem → Solution → Implementation**.
- The key error engineers make: sprinting straight to implementation without staying in the problem space.
- The three mindsets: increase business value; iterate in small steps; validate and verify.
- Methods are woven **throughout the lifecycle** — not a one-time workshop.
- Know the factory worker case study details: four things that went wrong and how they could have been prevented.
- Distinguish between the sad story (activity without impact) and happy story (impact through design thinking).
- Know the manufacturing fluid-sampling exercise: "spending too much money on sampling" is the **Problem**.
- Three maturity levels for Design Thinking: early (workshop, disconnected) → intermediate (intentional prioritization) → advanced (fully embedded as decision-making discipline).
- "AI makes it cheaper and easier to build — but you must make sure you are building the right thing."

*Accelerator flywheel:* Teams use accelerators on engagements → learnings flow back → accelerators are continuously updated → quality and reliability continuously increase. This is an organizational knowledge-compounding effect.

*CAIRA deep dive:*

*HVE Core as the AI engineering accelerator:*
HVE Core is the toolkit that brings the RPI flow (see Section 1d) to GitHub Copilot. It includes:
- Prompts, instructions, agents, skills (proven building blocks from dozens of FDE engagements)
- Coding standards for assorted languages and scripts
- Design Thinking Coach
- GitHub, ADO, Jira management
- Project planning
- Installation: marketplace extension; recommended starter = **HVE Core All**; also available as a CLI plugin
- Teams can add their own instruction files (e.g., custom coding standards) or override defaults; enhancements should be shared back with the team and contributed to HVE Core if applicable.

*Gap being closed by HVE Core:*

| What most teams do | What HVE enables |
|---|---|

| No structure, no verification, no persistence | Context-engineered agents for each development phase |
| Works sometimes but creates large or wrong changes | Persistent state across sessions |
| "LGTM" → tech debt, security gaps, rework | Code and output closer to what you expect |

### 1d. AI Agents and Tools across the Full Lifecycle

**What it covers** — This lesson covers context engineering as the foundational principle of effective AI-assisted development, the five-phase Research-Plan-Implement (RPI) flow that HVE Core implements, the five layers of GitHub Copilot customization, the three built-in Copilot modes, and the maturity model for AI tool adoption.

**Key concepts**

- **Context engineering** — "The delicate art and science of filling the context window with just the right information for the next step." Moving away from stuffing as much as possible into a prompt, toward a structured information architecture where context is written down and targeted to specific tasks.
- **Context window** — A finite engineering resource; every token consumed by instructions, history, or retrieved content is a token unavailable for reasoning; when the window is exceeded, the model must compact/summarize prior context, risking loss of important information.
- **Core principle** — "Output quality is bounded by context quality, not model quality." Better models with bad context still produce bad results. Good context enables even smaller models to perform well.
- **Context engineering progression** — Prompt engineering → context engineering → harness engineering.
- **Information architecture** — Setting up a system where context gets written down (not just stored in memory), enabling targeted context per task and scaling well across teams.
- **Research-Plan-Implement (RPI) flow** — The five-phase structured AI workflow for engineering tasks: Research, Plan, Implement, Review, Discovery.
- **Phase 1: Research** — Investigate codebase, APIs, documentation, and conventions before writing a line of code. Researcher-subagents explore the codebase and run in parallel for different research questions. Output: consolidated research markdown files at `.copilot-tracking/research/{date}/{topic}-research.md`. Decisions grounded in evidence, not training data. The AI discovers constraints the prompt didn't mention (API rate limits, existing conventions, related code).
- **Phase 2: Plan** — Transform knowledge into actionable implementation strategy; the plan becomes a contract. Output: coordinated plan files with checkboxes for each step, line references to code, parallel execution markers (`<!-- parallelizable: true/false -->`). Plan-validator subagent checks the plan against research before any code is written. Discrepancy tracking: planning log records gaps between research findings and what the plan addresses. Plans reveal missing requirements, impossible constraints, and better alternatives.
- **Phase 3: Implement** — Each implementation phase is executed by a sub-agent that receives only the context it needs for that specific phase. Change logs document every file modification for review. The plan is a contract — the agent will not expand scope or introduce unexpected changes. This phase produces output assets such as code and markdown.
- **Phase 4: Review** — RPI validators check each phase's changes against the plan; assess overall code quality; run linting, build, and test commands; produce a severity-graded findings list (some to implement now, some to skip, some to loop back through RPI).
- **Phase 5: Discovery** — Identify follow-up work from findings or move to the next task.
- **Sub-agents** — Specialized agents with a sole purpose; an agent with one purpose does a better job at that purpose than an agent trying to do everything at once.
- **Researcher-subagent** — Reads the actual codebase (not model training data); discovers real patterns, dependencies, and constraints; multiple instances run in parallel.
- **Plan-validator subagent** — Checks the plan against research to catch design issues before any code is written.
- **Persistent state** — HVE Core context is written to the `.copilot-tracking` folder, enabling context to persist across sessions and team members.
- **GitHub Copilot modes** — Agent (search, editing, terminal, tool use, command response loops); Ask (read-only for explanations and suggestions, no changes); Plan (structured plans that stay in conversation context). Agents can run locally, in the background, or on cloud.
- **Five layers of GitHub Copilot customization** — Hooks, Skills, Agents, Prompts, Instructions.
- **Hooks** — Enforce guardrails at specific points in workflows.
- **Skills** — Encapsulate domain expertise that can be loaded on demand; GitHub Copilot looks at skill names and descriptions and loads the full skill file only when the task requires it (smart/lazy loading); includes any instructions, scripts, and references needed.
- **Agents** — Built-in or custom; sole-purpose agents perform better.
- **Prompts** — Reusable workflows.
- **Instructions** — Baseline coding standards injected into Copilot sessions depending on file type or editing context.
- **AI Tools Maturity — Early** — Sporadic or single-area use (e.g., code assistance only).
- **AI Tools Maturity — Intermediate** — AI agents used for multiple stages (research, prototyping, testing).
- **AI Tools Maturity — Advanced** — AI embedded end-to-end; a full system used together by the whole team.
- **HVE Core tracking folder** — `.copilot-tracking/` — the directory where all context and artifacts created by HVE Core are stored.

**Detailed notes**

*Why RPI was built:*
Without RPI, prompting a Copilot agent with a task causes it to jump straight to implementation without thorough research or planning. Every phase (thinking, planning, doing) happens in one agent request, which produces output that may read well or even run but is unlikely to be production-ready. Splitting phases into specialized agents, each with a sole purpose, dramatically improves output quality.

*RPI flow in detail:*

| Phase | Agent(s) | Input | Output | Location |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Research | Researcher-subagents (parallel) | Codebase, docs, APIs | Research markdown with evidence, sources, line refs, one recommended approach per question | `.copilot-tracking/research/{date}/{topic}-research.md` |
| 2. Plan | Planner + Plan-validator | Research documents | Plan file with checkboxes, line refs, parallelizable markers; planning log for gaps | `.copilot-tracking/` |
| 3. Implement | Implementation sub-agents (context-scoped) | Plan (specific phase only) | Code, markdown; change logs documenting every file modification | Project files |
| 4. Review | RPI validators | Plan + code changes | Severity-graded findings list; linting/build/test results | `.copilot-tracking/` |
| 5. Discovery | — | Review findings | Follow-up work items or move to next task | — |

*Context window management:*
Each model has a context window (e.g., 200,000 tokens or more). When the window fills, the model must compact/summarize prior context — a process that can lose critical information. Context engineering prevents this by:
- Writing context to disk (not relying on in-memory conversation history)
- Targeting just the right context to just the right task
- Enabling each sub-agent to receive only the context relevant to its phase

*Five-layer customization stack (top to bottom enforcement):*
1. Hooks — workflow guardrails
2. Skills — on-demand domain expertise (smart loading prevents unnecessary context bloat)
3. Agents — purpose-built executors
4. Prompts — repeatable workflow definitions
5. Instructions — always-on coding standards

*Skills vs. Instructions:* Instructions are always injected (baseline standards). Skills are intelligently loaded only when Copilot determines the task requires them, preserving context window space.

**Exam / assessment pointers**

- Know all five RPI phases in order: **Research → Plan → Implement → Review → Discovery**.
- Research output path: `.copilot-tracking/research/{date}/{topic}-research.md`.
- Research phase: multiple subagents run **in parallel**; output is ONE recommended approach per question.
- Plan phase output features: **checkboxes**, **line references**, **parallelizable markers** (`<!-- parallelizable: true/false -->`).
- Plan is a **contract** — the implementation agent will not expand scope or add unexpected changes.
- Plan-validator runs **before** any code is written.
- Review phase: runs **linting, build, and test** commands; produces a **severity-graded** findings list.
- The core principle verbatim: **"Output quality is bounded by context quality, not model quality."**
- Context engineering is about writing context **to disk**, not storing it in memory.
- Five customization layers: **Hooks, Skills, Agents, Prompts, Instructions** (know all five).
- Skills use **smart/lazy loading** — Copilot only loads the full skill when the task requires it.
- Three Copilot modes: **Agent** (full tool use, editing), **Ask** (read-only), **Plan** (structured plans).
- AI tool maturity levels: sporadic → multi-stage agents → end-to-end team system.
- HVE Core install: marketplace extension; recommended = **HVE Core All**; also available as CLI plugin.

### 1e. Multidisciplinary Teams, Responsible AI, and Security

**What it covers** — This lesson covers the multidisciplinary team composition under the FDE model, core engineering fundamentals that are non-negotiables for HVE, Responsible AI principles, the Secure Future Initiative (SFI) with its three core security principles, and how all four HVE pillars together deliver measurable value through recognized engineering frameworks.

**Key concepts**

- **Multidisciplinary team roles** — Data Scientists, Software Engineers, Program Managers, Designers, Subject Matter Experts.
- **Domain excellence** — In order to get the best results from AI tooling, domain expertise is required to review and validate what AI generates; applies to every discipline.
- **Core engineering fundamentals / non-negotiables** — CI/CD, review processes, security, observability, Responsible AI; these enable HVE success and define the quality bar.
- **Multidisciplinary Teams Maturity — Early** — Cross-functional but still operating in silos.
- **Multidisciplinary Teams Maturity — Intermediate** — Real-time collaboration replaces handoffs; working across disciplines.
- **Multidisciplinary Teams Maturity — Advanced** — Fully integrated; leveraging domain expertise, AI tools, and other pillars together to act swiftly and deliver high-quality results.
- **Responsible AI (RAI)** — A non-negotiable part of every AI-focused engagement.
- **RAI principle 1** — Be intentional about how AI is used: define where AI adds value, where human judgment is required, where automation is not appropriate.
- **RAI principle 2** — Design for transparency and explainability from day one: ensure recommendations and actions can be understood and explained, not treated as black boxes.
- **RAI principle 3** — Actively manage risk up front: address bias, hallucinations, data leakage, misuse, and failure modes with guardrails, monitoring, and human-in-the-loop.
- **RAI principle 4** — Optimize for quality of outcomes, not novelty: prioritize trust, clarity, and business impact over shipping AI features for speed or spectacle.
- **RAI principle 5** — Build AI for production, not experimentation alone: ensure systems are secure, observable, auditable, and designed to evolve over time.
- **Human in the loop** — Engineers are the human in the loop when using AI tools; the engineer's role is to review, validate, and approve AI-generated output.
- **Secure Future Initiative (SFI)** — Microsoft's overarching security framework; not a single control or checklist; how Microsoft thinks about security across the entire lifecycle.
- **SFI Principle 1: Secure by design** — Security is built in from the very beginning: architecture, threat modeling, identity boundaries, network assumptions. Waiting to add security later loses leverage.
- **SFI Principle 2: Security by default** — The safest configuration is the easiest path; least privilege, isolation, and guardrails are on automatically, not something teams must remember to turn on.
- **SFI Principle 3: Secure operations** — Shipping secure software is not enough; operate it securely over time: monitoring, responding, learning, continuously reducing risk as the system evolves.
- **Engineering frameworks** — Accelerate, SAFe, SPACE, GitHub Engineering System Success Playbook (ESSP).
- **Accelerate** — Framework rooted in the book "Accelerate"; identifies key capabilities (continuous delivery, lean management) that drive software delivery performance and organizational outcomes.
- **SAFe** — Scaled Agile Framework; helps large organizations scale agile across teams and portfolios; emphasizes alignment, collaboration, and delivery of value at scale.
- **SPACE** — Modern framework for measuring developer productivity; five dimensions: Satisfaction & well-being, Performance, Activity, Communication & Collaboration, Efficiency & Flow.
- **ESSP** — GitHub's Engineering System Success Playbook; builds upon SPACE; focused on developer happiness, quality, and velocity.
- **HVE value through frameworks** — Faster time to value, increased ROI, higher quality and reliability, solving the right problems, improved developer experience, faster upskilling and onboarding.
- **Additional metrics** — Mean time to recovery, throughput, engineering cost per feature (standard engineering metrics still relevant in HVE).
- **HVE success measured by two things** — Increased speed (working faster?) and improved quality (outputs more secure, documented, validated?).
- **TPM backlog example** — A Technical Program Manager uses AI tools to generate a backlog from a Teams call transcription, pushes it to Azure DevOps via Azure MCP server, then brings domain expertise to validate the output, tweak Copilot mistakes, and verify structure — combining speed with quality through human judgment.

**Detailed notes**

*Team composition under FDE:*
HVE engagements bring together individuals from across a number of teams and disciplines. The key principle is that AI tools do not replace domain expertise — they amplify it. The same AI-generated output requires domain expertise to validate whether it is correct, appropriate, and production-ready. This applies equally to code (Software Engineer), data models (Data Scientist), project plans (Program Manager), and user interfaces (Designer).

*Core engineering non-negotiables:*
These are the quality bar that all HVE teams agree on, making them more efficient and ensuring consistent quality delivery with customers. While AI tools might enable faster work, this is a reason for quality to go **up**, not down.
- CI/CD (Continuous Integration/Continuous Deployment)
- Review processes
- Security
- Observability
- Responsible AI

*RAI as a part of craft:*
RAI is not a compliance checkbox — it is part of how HVE is practiced. The engineer is the human in the loop when using AI tools. The five RAI principles map directly to HVE workflow stages: intentionality at design time, explainability at build time, risk management at deployment time, quality optimization at all times, production-grade systems at release time.

*SFI and HVE:*
Security in HVE is anchored to the Secure Future Initiative. The three principles apply at different stages:
- Secure by design → applied during research and planning (architecture, threat modeling)
- Security by default → applied in accelerators (CAIRA bakes in security; least privilege automatically on)
- Secure operations → applied post-deployment (monitoring, response, continuous improvement)

*Measuring HVE success:*
HVE success is ultimately measured against two dimensions:

| Dimension | Questions | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | Did we work faster? Did we accomplish more in the same time? | Faster time to prototype; less time writing code; AI speeds up right parts |
| Quality | Are we creating higher-quality outputs? | More user validation cycles; more secure/reliable outputs from trusted starting points; domain expertise validates AI outputs |

*TPM backlog example (combining speed and quality):*
- Speed: TPM uses AI to generate backlog from Teams transcript + Azure MCP to push to Azure DevOps
- Quality: TPM applies domain expertise to validate output, fix Copilot mistakes, verify structure
- Result: both speed AND quality, because human knowledge guides AI tooling

**Exam / assessment pointers**

- Know all five Responsible AI principles (intentionality, transparency/explainability, proactive risk management, quality-over-novelty, production-ready systems).
- Know all three SFI principles in order: **secure by design, security by default, secure operations**.
- SFI is **not** a single control or checklist — it is how Microsoft thinks about security **across the entire lifecycle**.
- "Least privilege, isolation, and guardrails are on automatically" → **Security by default**.
- "Shipping secure software isn't enough; operate it securely over time" → **Secure operations**.
- SPACE has **five** dimensions: Satisfaction & well-being, Performance, Activity, Communication & Collaboration, Efficiency & Flow.
- Know the four frameworks: Accelerate (book-based, continuous delivery/lean), SAFe (large-scale agile), SPACE (developer productivity, 5 dimensions), ESSP (developer happiness, quality, velocity).
- HVE success measured by **two things**: increased speed and improved quality.
- "AI might enable us to do things faster — it is not a reason for quality to go down, but up."
- Non-negotiables: CI/CD, review processes, security, observability, Responsible AI.
- The engineer is always **the human in the loop** when using AI tools.
- Multidisciplinary maturity: silos → real-time collaboration → fully integrated.

## Common pitfalls and gotchas

- **Sprinting at the wrong problem.** The most costly HVE failure mode is building the right thing incorrectly implemented — but the even more common failure is building the wrong thing correctly. Always stay in the problem space before moving to solution or implementation.

- **Confusing solution and problem statements.** When customers describe their needs, most statements are actually solutions or implementations masquerading as problems. "We need to reduce sampling frequency" is a solution; "we spend too much money on sampling" is the problem. Know how to classify statements.

- **Mistaking HVE for a methodology or framework.** HVE is explicitly described as "not a product or framework" but a "holistic way of working." Exam questions may try to characterize it as a methodology — the correct term is "way of working."

- **Assuming Design Thinking is a one-time workshop.** Design thinking methods are woven throughout the entire engagement lifecycle — in sprints, in daily customer conversations. The early maturity mistake is treating it as a single ideation session.

- **Context vs. model quality confusion.** A common trap is thinking that upgrading to a more powerful model will fix bad outputs. The course is explicit: "Output quality is bounded by context quality, not model quality." Poor context will cause even the best model to produce poor results.

- **Ignoring the plan-as-contract principle.** In the RPI flow, the plan is a contract. Implementation agents will not expand scope or add unexpected changes beyond the plan. If something is not in the plan, it will not be in the implementation — this is a feature, not a bug.

- **Forgetting that Review runs tests.** The Review phase is not just a human code review — it actively runs linting, build, and test commands to produce a severity-graded findings list.

- **Conflating Instructions and Skills in Copilot customization.** Instructions are always injected (baseline standards, always-on). Skills are loaded on demand via smart/lazy loading only when Copilot determines the task requires them — this distinction is likely to appear on the assessment.

- **Missing the FDE fit criteria.** FDE is not for every project. Students must know: mission-critical + AI-centric + measurable business outcomes + executive sponsorship + Microsoft stack commitment + dedicated product team. If any of these are missing, FDE is not the right model.

- **Underestimating the security requirements.** SFI three principles must be known precisely — "secure by design," "security by default," "secure operations" — and students must be able to match descriptions to the correct principle (e.g., "safest configuration is the easiest path" = security by default, not secure by design).

- **Thinking AI replaces domain expertise.** The course is emphatic: AI tools amplify domain expertise; they do not replace it. Every AI-generated output requires domain expert review, regardless of discipline.

- **Assuming a one-size-fits-all HVE process.** The course explicitly states "there isn't a fully defined one-size-fits-all process here." HVE principles are consistent but execution varies across studios and customers.

- **Overlooking the backlog generation specifics.** The backlog example specifically uses GitHub Copilot + ADO MCP (not just Copilot alone) and achieves "80% ready" (not 100%) — the human still validates and refines.
