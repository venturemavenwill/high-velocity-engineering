# S018 · Virtual Memory and Paging as an Allocation Policy

**Module:** [M04](/archive/bsc-programme/modules/M04.md) · **Quarter:** [Q2](/archive/bsc-programme/quarters/Q2.md) · **Complexity class:** 2
**Strands:** SY · CF
**Whitepaper:** [WP-018](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-018.md)
**Depends on:** S013, S016, S017
**Platform anchor:** Foundry rate-limit budget as the allocated resource; `max_tokens` as allocation; prompt-cache retention as eviction · **MCP servers:** Azure MCP Server (read) · Microsoft Learn MCP Server

---

## What this day is for

Virtual memory is usually taught as a mechanism: address translation, page tables, faults. The mechanism is worth an hour. The interesting part is that **paging is the same allocation problem as scheduling, with memory as the resource and eviction as the deprioritisation** — and that it has a property scheduling does not, which is a cliff.

Almost every allocation policy degrades gracefully as pressure rises. Paging does not. Below a threshold, performance is fine; above it, the system does not slow down proportionally, it collapses. The threshold is a property of the working set, not of the policy, and the collapse is the most important non-linear behaviour students meet before S019.

The target: **the same policy can be the best and the worst available, determined by one number you did not choose.**

The platform supplies the cliff in a form that is both documented and billed. A Foundry deployment holds a token budget per minute; the budget is charged against an **estimate made when the request arrives**, which includes the `max_tokens` you asked for and not the tokens you were sent; and when the budget is gone the deployment returns 429. That much is a boundary, not a cliff. The cliff is the next sentence in the documentation: **unsuccessful requests still count toward the per-minute rate limit.** A client that retries immediately spends budget on failures, which produces more failures, which produce more retries. The feedback is positive and it is the same shape as thrashing, from a completely different mechanism.

## Phase 1 · Pretest and prediction (25 min)

Five items.

1. What does paging most resemble, among things you have seen in this module? Say why.
2. A client's demand grows steadily from half of its allocated token budget to double it. Sketch the curve of successful requests per minute against demand.
3. At what point does your curve change shape, and what changes it?
4. Predict: is your curve's shape closer to the room's median, or to the measurement?
5. **Copilot prediction, sealed.** You will ask the agent to make a throttled client reliable. Write down what it will produce, and **name the one thing it will get wrong**. Opened at phase 8.

Item 1 is the transfer probe — the answer is S016's hierarchy at a different scale — and is asked before any instruction.

**Warrant.** Pretesting with a transfer probe and a sketch demand. Item 2's sketch is the day's central misconception in a visible form: most students draw a smooth degradation, and a plurality draw a curve that flattens rather than one that falls. Item 5 is sealed at phase 1 per instrumentation rule 8, and its expected answer — that the agent will write a retry loop that makes throttling worse — is the day's most useful frontier datum.

## Phase 3 · Contrasting cases and generation (45 min)

**Executed, not read.** Groups have a Foundry deployment with a small, known token allocation, and a load generator. They run **four client configurations**, each swept across **five levels of demand** relative to the allocation: well under, under, at, over, well over.

| Case | Client configuration |
|---|---|
| A | `max_tokens` set generously, far above the response length actually produced |
| B | `max_tokens` set to the expected response size |
| C | As B, retrying immediately on 429 with no delay |
| D | As B, with exponential backoff, jitter, and the `retry-after-ms` header honoured |

They predict, for each case at each level, the successful-request rate and the 429 rate, and commit.

Then the runs happen. The curve for B is flat, flat, slightly worse, far worse, catastrophic. **Case A produces the same output as B, is billed for the same tokens, and collapses at a substantially lower demand.** Case C is the best of the four at low demand — it wastes no time waiting — and is the worst by a wide margin at high demand, and it is worst precisely where it matters.

Task after: **identify the number that determines when each configuration collapses**, and say whether you control it.

**Warrant.** Contrasting cases along one continuous dimension with a non-linear adjudicator, run against a metered service. The five-point sweep is deliberately coarse so that the cliff falls between two adjacent samples and its location must be inferred rather than read off. **Case A is the phase's payload**: it defeats expectation completely, because nothing about the request that the student can see is different, and the billed token count — the number they would have reasoned from — is identical. Case C preserves the second-policy structure the design has always used: better at three levels, worse at two, worse where it matters.

## Phase 4 · Consolidating instruction (50 min)

1. **The mechanism,** briefly. Translation, pages, faults. Twelve minutes, and marked as the least important part of the day.
2. **Paging is S016's hierarchy one level down** — the answer to item 1, and the same two properties at a different scale and a much larger cost ratio. The platform states this about itself without being asked: extended prompt-cache retention is documented as working by **offloading key/value tensors to GPU-local storage when memory is full**, which is paging, described by a vendor, in a product note.
3. **The working set,** defined as what a program actually touches in a window, not what it allocates. Nearly all capacity errors are a confusion between these. **Case A is that confusion with a price on it:** the rate limit is charged against an estimate formed when the request arrives, which counts the `max_tokens` you reserved, while the bill counts the tokens you were sent. Reserve four thousand and use two hundred, and you have paid rate-limit budget for thirty-eight hundred tokens that never existed. **And note what the limit is.** It is an allocation, not a capability — it can be raised by asking, which no amount of reasoning about feedback loops will suggest. The documentation lists four distinct reasons a request can be refused and only two of them respond to a larger allocation. **The shape of a failure tells you nothing about its remedy.**
4. **The cliff.** Why performance collapses rather than degrading: each failure costs budget, failed requests still count against the limit, failures beget retries and retries beget failures, and the feedback is positive. This is the day's central explanation and case C is its demonstration.
5. **Eviction policies** as S017's allocation problem — what each optimises, what each starves, and the workload that defeats each. The prompt cache is the instance in the room: entries are typically cleared within five to ten minutes of inactivity and are always removed within an hour of last use, so the policy is partly time-based and partly not, and the documentation gives a bound for one and a tendency for the other.
6. **Why the cliff is dangerous specifically**: it is invisible in testing, because tests are run at demand levels that fit, and the threshold is crossed by growth nobody scheduled. On this platform there is a second reason, and it is worse. **The metric does not show it.** Azure Monitor's token metrics report billed tokens from requests that succeeded, so a deployment throttling steadily can present as a deployment with comfortable headroom, and the vendor documents this in those terms.
7. **Overcommitment as a deliberate bet,** and what makes it a defensible one — S007's framing applied to memory, and to a token allocation shared by more than one caller.
8. **The default the platform chose for you.** `max_tokens` has a default, the SDK has a **built-in retry that is on before you write any code** — two attempts in the Python client — and a hand-written retry layer stacked on top of it multiplies rather than replaces it, which the documentation warns about explicitly. Two retry policies you did not both choose are a scheduling decision made by accident.

**Warrant.** Consolidation with the mechanism deliberately demoted and the cliff given the central position. Point 6 is the day's professional content and is the reason the cliff matters more than the policy; its second half is the day's perishable platform-specific point and the strongest evidence the module has for its own claim that a layer's failures are invisible from the layer above. Point 8 names the default, per the [Copilot Practice Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md).

## Phase 5 · Whole-task learning task (90 min)

**Class 2. The policy and the provisioning are not given. This phase produces a build.**

Students use Copilot in agent mode, through the Azure MCP Server in read configuration, to ship a client that survives its own allocation. They:

1. **Measure its working set,** distinguishing it from its allocation — tokens actually consumed against tokens reserved.
2. **Locate the cliff** — the demand level at which the current configuration collapses. Predict first, then find it by bisection.
3. **Choose a policy and a provision.** Concurrency bound, retry discipline, `max_tokens` setting, allocation request. Any.
4. **State the bet.** How much growth headroom does your choice buy, and what growth rate would consume it?
5. **State what you gave up** to buy that headroom — cost, or performance below the cliff, or someone else's share, or something else.
6. **Build and measure,** running alone and again while the room is running theirs.
7. **State the signal** that would tell you the cliff was approaching, before it was crossed.

Step 7 is the day's payload and is the programme's first detectability requirement. The platform offers two candidate signals with different lead times: the `x-ratelimit-remaining-tokens` and `x-ratelimit-remaining-requests` headers returned on every call, and the platform metrics in Azure Monitor, retrieved through the Azure MCP Server. Students may use either or neither, and the choice is theirs to defend.

**The artefact carries three further elements**, per the Copilot Practice Standard.

- **Prompt record.** The instructions you gave, in order, including the ones you abandoned.
- **Intervention record.** Every correction you made. **An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that the design could have detected an intervention-worthy error — a retry layer that refuses to stack on the SDK's own is the obvious example and is accepted.
- **Frontier statement.** One thing the agent did faster than you could have, and one thing it did badly that you had to catch.

**Fading:** step 1's measurement is worked with the room; step 2's bisection procedure is supplied.

**Warrant.** Whole-task at class 2, now shipping rather than describing. Step 2's predict-then-bisect is the module's commit-then-adjudicate structure applied to locating a threshold. Step 7 makes the day's danger actionable rather than merely known, and offering two candidate signals rather than one is the improvement [WP-018](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-018.md) §8 identified and [S019](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S019.md) formalises.

## Phase 6 · Error clinic (40 min)

Instructor first: a substrate memory incident where the system ran correctly for months and collapsed within minutes — with attention to what the monitoring showed in the hour before, and why nobody read it as a warning.

Then the agent case: a Copilot-generated retry layer that was **confidently wrong and passed review**. Asked to make a throttled client reliable, it produced a textbook exponential backoff with jitter, correct in every detail, wrapped around a client whose SDK already retried twice per attempt — so each logical call became several, and the client's response to being throttled was to consume more of the budget it had run out of. It reviewed well because every line of it was right. Attention goes to why review did not catch it: **the defect was not in the code, it was in the composition**, and nobody had read the two layers together.

Then students: a resource limit you did not know you were near, or a place today where the agent's answer was correct and its interaction with something else was not.

**Warrant.** Error management. The instructor disclosure is the module's fourth with a monitoring dimension and is the most direct: the signal existed. That is exactly what step 7 exists to prevent and phase 7 shows is hard to prevent. The agent case is chosen because it is the day's own sealed phase 1 prediction resolved as an artefact, and because composition failures are the class of agent error that review is structurally worst at.

## Phase 7 · Peer articulation (30 min)

Pairs. Each student presents their step 7 signal, and the partner's task is to **construct a scenario in which the signal fires and nothing is wrong**, and a second in which the cliff is crossed and the signal does not fire.

Both are usually constructible, and on this platform both have documented instances the partner may use. A single request reserving a large `max_tokens` drops the remaining-token header sharply and then returns almost nothing, which fires a header-based signal for no reason. A burst inside a one-second window throttles a client whose per-minute counter never came close, which a header-based signal will not see. And a metric-based signal misses throttling entirely, because the metric counts successful requests.

**Warrant.** Interactive-tier articulation with a dual falsification demand. False positives and false negatives are introduced here as a pair, informally, and are the direct precursor of S048's judge-bias material and S068's detectability material. The documented instances are supplied as a floor, not a ceiling: a partner who only reproduces them has done the reading, and a partner who finds a third has done the work.

## When it does not work

These are [WP-018](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-018.md) §5's predicted failure modes, restated as instruction to the person in the room. The whitepaper is the authority; where this section and §5 diverge, §5 is right.

**A student with operations experience answers items 1 and 2 correctly and then produces a signal from standard practice.** They have seen thrashing, so the transfer probe and the curve cost them nothing — and their step 7 signal will be a utilisation threshold, chosen without examining whether it leads or lags. Do not correct it. Utilisation thresholds are among the easiest signals for a partner to defeat in both directions at phase 7, and theirs is the most likely to be broken. Their assignment is to construct a signal that **leads by a stated interval**, which standard practice does not supply.

**A student with no systems background cannot hold point 4's feedback mechanism.** It is the module's hardest single explanation for them, and it is a feedback argument, so it cannot be taken one step at a time. Three things already help: it is given thirty minutes; it is indexed to a curve the student has just drawn wrongly and then seen measured; and the bisection procedure means step 2 does not depend on holding it. *Residual risk the design accepts:* their step 7 signals may be arbitrary, and phase 7's adversarial feedback is the only mitigation.

**Nearly everyone predicts the first three sample points correctly.** That is intended; the fourth and fifth are not. If a group predicts the whole curve including the knee, ask where the knee moves under the second policy.

**A student is defeated at phase 7.** They construct a signal, are pleased with it, and watch a partner break it in both directions inside ten minutes. Repeated across the room this produces the conclusion that monitoring is futile — which is the fatalism risk the module has now met three times in different clothes. State, *before* the phase rather than after it, that every signal in production has both failure modes, that the question is never whether but how often and in which direction, and that a signal whose failure modes are known is far better than one whose are not. *Residual risk the design accepts:* §5 records that this reframing is the day's most important instructor-dependent moment and that it is a single statement, which is thin.

**A signal with no stated false-positive and false-negative case fails the specification.** Phase 7 supplies both; the artefact must record them. **An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that the design could have detected an intervention-worthy error.

## What this day does not establish

**Demonstrated.** That four client configurations swept across five demand levels collapse at different points, measured by the room. That an immediate-retry client is the best of the four at low demand and the worst by a wide margin at high demand. And the sharpest of them: that two clients producing identical output and billed for identical tokens collapse at substantially different demand levels, with nothing visible in the request to distinguish them.

**Read rather than demonstrated.** What the rate-limit estimate is composed of; the retention windows at point 5; the SDK's retry count at point 8; and the fact that the token metrics report only successful requests. The last of these is checkable on the day if the metrics are reachable, and is otherwise an assertion.

**Asserted, and conceded.** The day identifies the paging cliff with the platform's throttle, and [WP-018](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-018.md) concedes that these are two different things run together without being distinguished: a thrashing machine is genuinely collapsing and cannot be talked out of it, while a service returning 429 is a healthy machine declining on purpose because somebody set a number — and that number can very often be raised by asking. Point 3's closing sentence, *the shape of a failure tells you nothing about its remedy*, is the design's whole answer, and it is one sentence. The paper records that a student could pass holding the confusion intact, and that the confusion is wrong in the commonest case they will meet.

**What rests on a `research/` note.** The memory-pressure failure mode, the working-set concept and phase 6's monitoring-signal failure, at [research/02-technical-foundations/kwon-pagedattention-vllm.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/kwon-pagedattention-vllm.md) and [research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md).

**What rests on nothing.** The day's opening move most of all. *Sketching as an externalisation of a misconception* is why item 2 asks for a drawing rather than a sentence, and **this repository holds no note for it** — nor for contrasting cases along a continuous dimension, prediction before adjudication and the pretesting effect, cognitive load and interactive feedback explanations, or expanding-interval retrieval. **One item is worse than the rest:** error management training carries phase 6 and is cited from general knowledge, while [research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md](/research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md) records a complete reading of the article, including the one setting in which the effect does not appear. That matters here specifically, because phase 7 is a phase in which students are deliberately made to fail.

## What to take from this day

**The claim to hold.** **A working set is not an allocation, and saturation collapses rather than degrades.** The number that decides when your system falls over is usually one you did not choose, and the feedback that makes the fall sudden — failures costing budget, budget shortage causing failures — is a property of the arrangement rather than of the policy.

**What the sources license, and what they do not.** The memory-pressure failure mode, the working-set concept and the monitoring-signal failure at phase 6 are verified in this repository, at [research/02-technical-foundations/kwon-pagedattention-vllm.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/kwon-pagedattention-vllm.md) and [research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md). The instructional mechanisms behind the day — pretesting, contrasting cases, error management — are cited for **direction and mechanism only, and no magnitude is asserted for any of them.** The vendor documentation states that a refusal can occur while the metrics look healthy and **does not publish how often**, so nothing today licenses a rate.

**Guidance for your own work.**

- Measure what you consumed, not what you reserved. The bill and the limit count different things.
- Find the cliff by bisection before growth finds it for you, and write down where it was.
- Give every approach signal a false-positive case and a false-negative case, or it is not a signal.
- **A control is not evidence.** A retry policy with backoff is a configured control; that the client survives its allocation is an observed result and needs a measurement. The five classes on the [Agent Configuration Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/13-Agent-Configuration-Standard.md) are where each belongs.

**Where this is easy to get wrong.** Reading the shape of a failure as its remedy. A refusal looks like a wall, and some of its documented causes respond to asking for a larger allocation — so a week spent perfecting backoff can be a week spent not making a request that would have worked.
