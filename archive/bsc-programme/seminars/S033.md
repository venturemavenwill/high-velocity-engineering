# S033 · Partial Failure and the Unreliable Network

**Strands:** SY · MS · CF

**Depends on:** S019
**Re-tests:** S031, S032
**Platform anchor:** API Management backend pools and circuit breaker · Azure MCP Server tool idempotence annotations · Bicep · **MCP servers:** Azure MCP Server (write) · Microsoft Learn MCP Server

---

## What this day is for

In a single process, a call either returns or the process dies. Across a network there is a third outcome, and it is the common one: **you do not know.** The request may have arrived and been processed; it may have arrived and not been processed; it may not have arrived. The response may have been sent and lost. From the caller's side these are indistinguishable, and no amount of engineering makes them distinguishable.

Students meet this as an inconvenience and treat it as one: retry, and move on. The retry is frequently correct and it is a decision with consequences that students do not make deliberately — it can duplicate an effect, it can amplify a failure, and it can turn a slow dependency into an outage.

The target: **decide which failures you handle, which you accept, and what the acceptance costs — with the probabilities estimated rather than assumed.**

**This is the quarter's deploy-and-destroy day.** Students build a real multi-backend gateway with a circuit breaker, from Bicep, with Copilot, break a backend, watch what the gateway does, and delete the whole resource group before the error clinic. Nothing survives the day. What survives is the finding, and the finding is that the resilience you configured is not the resilience you have.

## Phase 1 · Pretest and prediction (25 min)

Five items.

1. A request times out. List everything that might have happened.
2. Which of those does a retry make worse?
3. A component depends on four services, each available 99.9 per cent of the time. What is the component's availability?
4. Predict: is your item 3 answer higher or lower than the truth?
5. **Copilot prediction, sealed.** You will shortly ask the agent to "make this gateway resilient to one of its two model backends going down." Write down the configuration you expect, and **name the one part of it that will not do what its name says.** Sealed with items 1 to 4 and opened at phase 8.

Item 1's lists are consistently incomplete. Item 3's arithmetic is straightforward and almost everyone gets it wrong in the same direction. Item 5's answer is a documented dependency between two features that most engineers configure separately.

**Warrant.** Pretesting on the outcome space and the arithmetic. Item 3's near-universal error is the day's opening finding and it is quantitative, which is unusual for a pretest and is deliberate. Item 5 is sealed at phase 1 per instrumentation rule 8 and is the day's frontier datum; it is written so that a student scores only by naming a specific mechanism, not by predicting that the agent will be imperfect.

## Phase 3 · Contrasting cases and generation (45 min)

**Executed, not read.** Groups have a gateway with two configured backends and the ability to make either one fail, hang or drop responses. They receive **one call and four failure scenarios**, each with the same observable symptom — a timeout — and different underlying events, and they produce each one for real.

- **Scenario A** — the request never arrived.
- **Scenario B** — the request arrived, was processed, and the response was lost.
- **Scenario C** — the request arrived and is still being processed.
- **Scenario D** — the request arrived, failed partway through, and left state behind.

For each, groups state what a retry does, and commit. Then they retry, and see.

Then the same four are re-run **with the call made idempotent**, and the answers change for three of them.

The fourth run is where the room is defeated. Groups are given the Azure MCP Server's published tool annotations and asked to predict, before looking, which operations are marked idempotent. **Nearly everyone predicts that creating a resource is the safe one to retry and deleting a resource is the dangerous one.** The documentation says the reverse: deleting a managed disk is annotated idempotent and returns success whether the disk was removed or did not exist, while creating a managed disk and creating a virtual machine are both annotated **not** idempotent. The room then retries an interrupted create through the agent and counts what it is left holding.

Task after: **state what idempotence bought, what it cost, and which scenario it does not help.**

Scenario D is the payload — a partial effect is the case idempotence does not resolve without additional machinery, and it is now the case the students have produced, retried and paid for.

**Warrant.** Contrasting cases across indistinguishable causes with a fixed symptom, then a single intervention applied across all four. The construction isolates what one design decision changes. The annotation prediction is the executed payload and it defeats expectation in a useful direction: students hold *destructive* and *dangerous to retry* as the same idea, and they are orthogonal. A delete that is safe to repeat and a create that is not is the cleanest available statement of what idempotence actually is.

## Phase 4 · Consolidating instruction (50 min)

1. **The third outcome.** Success, failure, and unknown. Unknown is not a transient state; it is a permanent epistemic condition of the caller.
2. **What a timeout is** — a decision by the caller about how long to wait, not an event at the callee. Choosing it is a choice about which failures you convert into unknowns.
3. **Retry and its four consequences:** duplication, amplification, latency inflation, and masking a real failure until it is worse. **This list omits retry's interaction with backpressure and with client-side queueing, which S062 covers; the four here are the ones visible at a single call site.** Safe to reason with when deciding whether to retry a call; unsafe as a basis for designing a system-wide retry policy, which this day does not do.
4. **Idempotence** as the property that makes retry safe, what it requires, and what it cannot repair — with phase 3's annotation result worked completely. Destructive and non-idempotent are independent properties, and the platform's own annotation table is the demonstration.
5. **Availability arithmetic,** from item 3: dependencies multiply, and a component is less available than any of its dependencies. Worked completely. The independence assumption is named here and is false in practice; it is safe for establishing that availability multiplies and unsafe for any actual estimate, because correlated failure dominates.
6. **Which failures to handle.** Handling costs code, latency and complexity, and the failures you decline to handle should be named and their probability estimated — not left implicit.
7. **Degradation as a design choice:** what the component does when a dependency is unavailable, decided in advance rather than by default.
8. **The circuit breaker, and the sentence nobody reads.** A backend pool supports round-robin, weighted, priority-based and session-aware distribution, and a backend may carry circuit-breaker rules that trip on a count or percentage of failures in an interval across a range of status codes. When a breaker trips the gateway stops forwarding and **returns 503 to the client**, which is a new failure the caller cannot distinguish from the backend having failed. Then the sentence: **the service uses backends in lower priority groups only when all backends in higher priority groups are unavailable *because circuit breaker rules are tripped*.** Priority-based routing is not failover. It is failover only in the presence of a breaker that has tripped. Configure priorities without a breaker and you have configured an ordering, not a fallback — and the deployment succeeds, the configuration reads correctly, and nothing fails over.
9. **Defaults are opinions and thresholds are policy.** Four today. The portal offers a failure interval of one hour and a trip duration of one hour, and almost nobody changes them, so most breakers in most organisations are set to a number a form pre-filled. **You may configure only one circuit-breaker rule per backend**, so the choice of which failure condition to detect is a choice not to detect the others. `acceptRetryAfter` lets the backend name its own recovery time, which is better engineering and means **your trip duration is advisory** — a dependency can tell your breaker to hold off longer than you configured. And the Azure MCP Server's idempotence, destructiveness and secret markings are documented as **hints**, not guarantees: nothing in the documentation obliges a client to honour them, and a `force` parameter exists on at least one delete tool specifically to bypass confirmation.
10. **Everything above is approximate, and the documentation says so.** Backend load balancing is approximate and circuit-breaker tripping rules are approximate, both for the same stated reason: **different gateway instances do not synchronise, and each applies the rules on its own information.** A pool holds at most thirty backends, and the breaker is unavailable in the Consumption tier. **All of that is perishable. What is durable is that a failure detector distributed across independent observers has no consistent view of failure**, which is S031's approximate counter in a second register and is the same result.
11. **What the documentation does not say.** It calls the breaker's tripping approximate and **gives no bound on the approximation** — no figure for how much traffic reaches a failed backend after the condition is met, at any instance count. It gives no availability figure for the gateway itself. Step 2's estimates therefore cannot be sourced from the vendor and must come from your own history, which is what step 2 requires and why it requires provenance.
12. **The caller's view.** Everything above is invisible to your caller unless you tell them, which is S034's subject.

**Warrant.** Consolidation indexed to phase 3. Point 3 carries the scope statement in the strong form required by the [Whitepaper Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md). Point 6 is the day's class-3 demand: the criterion for what to handle is the student's. Points 8 to 11 are the day's platform content and satisfy the [Copilot Practice Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md). Point 8 is placed before phase 5 rather than discovered in it, deliberately: a student who discovers the priority-breaker dependency by having their failover fail has learned it, and a student who discovers it while their gateway is the only route to a running service has learned it expensively.

## Phase 5 · Whole-task learning task (90 min)

**Class 3. The failure-handling criterion is the student's. This phase produces a build, and destroys it.**

Students use Copilot in agent mode to author a Bicep file describing a gateway with two model backends, a load-balanced pool and a circuit breaker, deploy it to a disposable subscription through the Azure MCP Server, and then:

1. **Enumerate the failure modes** of each dependency, including the unknowns.
2. **Estimate each one's probability** from the substrate's actual history, and state your confidence and provenance. **Where a dependency has never been observed to fail, say so and give a bounded estimate**; a zero-observation case is the common one and is not an excuse for omitting the number.
3. **Compute the component's availability** under your estimates.
4. **Decide which failures to handle,** and state the criterion you used.
5. **Name the failures you are not handling,** with their estimated probability, consequence **and the confidence you attached at step 2** — which travels with the number and does not stay behind in the working.
6. **Specify the degradation behaviour** for the largest unhandled failure, and configure it.
7. **Break the primary backend and observe.** Does traffic move? At what delay? What does a caller see in the interval? Record it.
8. **State what your caller will observe** in each unhandled case, and whether they can distinguish it from success.
9. **Destroy the resource group** and confirm it is gone.

**The artefact carries three further elements**, per the Copilot Practice Standard.

- **Prompt record.** The instructions you gave the agent, in order, including the ones you abandoned.
- **Intervention record.** Every point at which you corrected it. **An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that your own design would have caught an intervention-worthy error — a stated expectation at step 6 checked against the observation at step 7 is the accepted form.
- **Frontier statement.** One thing the agent did faster than you could have, and one thing it did badly that you had to catch.

**Fading:** step 2's estimation from history is worked with the room, in both the observed and zero-observation cases; step 3's arithmetic is supplied as a form. Bicep is read before it is written: the file is agent-authored and the student's job is to review it.

**Warrant.** Whole-task at class 3. Step 5 is the day's discriminator — an unhandled failure that is not named is not a decision — and it now carries the confidence alongside the probability, which repairs the defect [WP-033](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-033.md) §8 identified: the day previously required provenance at step 2 and stripped it from the only output anyone else would read. Step 7 converts the whole exercise from a design document into a measurement. Step 9 is not administrative: **a student who cannot destroy what they built does not know what they built**, and the teardown is where an unnoticed resource created by the agent becomes visible.

## Phase 6 · Error clinic (40 min)

Instructor first: a substrate incident in which a retry policy that was correct for one dependency amplified a slow response into an outage — with attention to the fact that the dependency never failed.

Then the agent case. A team asked Copilot to "add failover so that if the primary model endpoint is down we use the secondary." It produced a backend pool with two backends at priorities 1 and 2, in a clean Bicep file, matching the documented shape. It was deployed. It was reviewed by two engineers. Nine weeks later the primary endpoint began returning errors and **every request failed**, because no circuit-breaker rule had ever been configured and the pool therefore never regarded the primary as unavailable.

Attention goes to why review did not catch it. **The reviewers checked that the configuration matched the request, and it did.** What was missing was not in the file; it was a second resource property nobody had asked for, whose absence is legal, silent, and indistinguishable from a working configuration until the day it matters. **The agent answered the question it was asked.** The question was incomplete, and neither the agent nor the review had any means of noticing — there is no test that fails, no linter warning, and no deployment error.

Then students: something you retried without thinking about it, or a place today where the agent's output was correct and insufficient.

**Warrant.** Error management. The instructor disclosure targets amplification specifically, because it is the retry consequence students find least intuitive and because the failure has no faulty component to blame. The agent case is the [Copilot Practice Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md)'s required addition and is the module's clearest instance of the class of agent failure that matters most under write access: **not a wrong answer, but a complete answer to an incomplete question, deployed.**

Pairs. Each student presents their step 5 unhandled failures, and the partner's task is to **construct the sequence of events** in which one of those failures occurs at the worst possible moment — and to state what a user experiences.

**Warrant.** Interactive-tier articulation with a worst-case construction and a user-experience demand. Naming an unhandled failure is abstract; narrating its arrival is not, and it is the earliest form of the incident-narrative work at S080.

## When it does not work

These are [WP-033](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-033.md) §5's predicted failure modes, restated as instruction to the person in the room. The whitepaper is the authority; where this section and §5 diverge, §5 is right. Nothing here is assessed.

**A student with distributed systems experience knows the third outcome and sails through phase 3.** Their step 4 criterion will then be inherited practice — retry idempotent calls, circuit-break on repeated failure — applied with no estimates, because operational convention supplies defaults that work. Step 5's probability requirement is not served by convention, and their phase 7 partner constructs the case their inherited default handles worst. **The stronger check is step 7**: an experienced student is the *most* likely to configure a priority pool and assume failover, because the convention they inherited is correct on other platforms, and breaking the backend is what tells them.

**A student without the background will stall at step 2.** Estimating from history is the day's hardest demand for them. Work it with the room in both the observed and the zero-observation cases, supply the arithmetic form, accept a stated low confidence, and keep Bicep read rather than written. **The residual risk §5 accepts is that their availability computation is built on estimates they cannot defend, and step 2's provenance requirement is the only check on it** — there is no second detector.

**Scenarios A to C go too smoothly on the first pass.** Expected. The phase bites at the re-run with idempotence and at D's resistance. If a group resolves D, ask what machinery would resolve it and what that machinery costs. The answer is transactional and previews SY103's material without teaching it.

**The tier does not carry the circuit breaker.** This is the module's only planned demonstrated-rather-than-operated case and it is named at [M07](/archive/bsc-programme/modules/M07.md) so that the exception is visible at module level rather than buried here. Confirm the tier before the offering; where it does not support the breaker, state to the room that the breaker cases are **demonstrated rather than operated**, do not assess step 7 or the breaker element of the specification, and teach the priority-breaker dependency from the documentation.

**The room concludes that distributed systems cannot be built.** This is the day's characteristic affective risk and it is well founded: three outcomes, four retry consequences, multiplying availabilities and an unhandled-failure list is an accumulation of bad news landing on the module's third consecutive day of things being worse than they looked. Four things work against it. Deliver point 7's degradation-as-choice as a design decision rather than as damage control. Show at phase 8 that the substrate's measured availability, lower than the room computed, is adequate for its users — which is evidence that these systems do work. Keep step 4's criterion the student's, because it converts an unbounded problem into a bounded decision. And note that the day now builds something that works before it breaks it. **The design's position is that negativity peaks here and that S034's contract framing is the resolution, which is a deferral rather than an answer** — and §5 names its verification: [S034](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S034.md)'s phase 8 asks what students can now promise a caller, and a cohort that cannot answer indicates this deferral failed.

## Assessment

Specification item in SY201.

Per the [Assessment Architecture](/archive/bsc-programme/program/03-Assessment-Architecture.md)'s rule 1, the checked elements are: presence of enumerated failure modes including the unknowns; probability estimates with stated confidence and provenance; the computed availability; the handling criterion; the named unhandled failures **each carrying probability, consequence and confidence**; the degradation specification; **the recorded observation from step 7**; the caller's observable in each unhandled case; **confirmation of teardown; the prompt record; the intervention record; and the frontier statement.**

**An unhandled failure named without a probability estimate fails.** "Unlikely" is not an estimate. **An unhandled failure carrying a probability without its confidence also fails** — a bare number read by someone else is false precision, and this clause exists because [WP-033](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-033.md) §8 found the day propagating exactly that. **An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that the design could have detected an intervention-worthy error.

Nothing else is assessed. Whether the failover worked, how elegant the Bicep is, and how many prompts the build took are all outside the specification.

## Perishable content in this day

| Durable claim | Platform instance, perishable |
|---|---|
| A caller cannot distinguish the three outcomes | A gateway timeout over two configured backends |
| Idempotence and destructiveness are independent properties | Disk delete annotated idempotent; disk and VM create annotated not |
| A distributed failure detector has no consistent view of failure | "Circuit breaker tripping rules are approximate"; instances do not synchronise |
| A fallback that is not triggered is not a fallback | Priority groups used only when higher groups are breaker-tripped |
| Availability multiplies, and correlated failure dominates | The gap between computed and measured substrate availability |
| A default is an opinion somebody else held | One-hour interval and trip duration; one rule per backend; `acceptRetryAfter` |
| A hint is not a guarantee | Tool annotations documented as hints; `force` bypassing confirmation |

The substrate's dependency set and measured availabilities, every policy and resource property, the thirty-backend pool limit and the tier restrictions are **perishable** and are marked at points 8 to 11. The third outcome, the timeout-as-caller-decision framing, retry's consequences, idempotence and its limit, availability multiplication, and degradation-as-choice are durable and are what the specification assesses.

**Where a case cannot be operated.** The circuit breaker is not available in every API Management tier. Where the programme's instance is on a tier that does not support it, **phase 3's breaker behaviour and step 6's configuration are demonstrated rather than operated, and step 7 and the breaker element of the specification are not assessed** — the priority-failover dependency is then taught from the documentation and the day says so. The tier is confirmed before each offering.

**Scope, strong form.** Treating a gateway backend pool as an instance of partial failure is **safe** for reasoning about the outcome space, about what a retry does, about idempotence, and about what a caller can and cannot distinguish. It is **unsafe** as a model of the failure modes the gateway itself introduces — a 503 from a tripped breaker is a failure the caller sees that no backend produced — and unsafe as a basis for a system-wide retry policy, which is S062's. Step 8's caller-observable is where a student applying it outside that range would fail.

## What this day does not establish

**Demonstrated.** That four scenarios sharing one observable symptom produced different consequences under retry on one gateway; that making the call idempotent changed the answer for three of them and not for the fourth; that retrying an interrupted create through the agent left resources behind; and what the room's own gateway did when a backend was broken, at whatever delay it did it. One configuration, one tier, one afternoon.

**Read rather than demonstrated.** The tool annotation table — which operations are marked idempotent and which are not — and everything at points 8 to 11: the breaker's trip conditions, the 503 returned to the client, the sentence making priority groups conditional on a tripped breaker, the one-rule-per-backend limit, `acceptRetryAfter`, the pool size limit, the tier restrictions, and the vendor's statements that load balancing and breaker tripping are approximate because instances do not synchronise. Documentation, read on the day.

**Not established, deliberately.** Any bound on the approximation. There is **no published figure for how much traffic reaches a failed backend after the trip condition is met, at any instance count**, and no published availability figure for the gateway itself — which is precisely why step 2 requires provenance and takes its estimates from the substrate's own history. Also not established: whether any student's probability estimates are right, and how much correlated failure actually dominates, which point 5 names as the reason the independence assumption is false and does not quantify anywhere.

**Asserted.** That an availability computation is worth teaching in a form the day itself calls false; that teardown teaches something about what was built; and that the module's negativity resolves at S034. The last of these is a deferral with a named verification and the other two are the design's reasoning.

**What rests on a `research/` note.** The gateway layer, at [research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md), which is a complete reading of one overview page and confirms the load-balancer-and-circuit-breaker pairing, the four distribution modes, and the breaker's dynamic trip duration taken from the backend's `Retry-After` header. The note describes itself as definitional rather than empirical — no benchmark, no latency figure, no comparison. The estimation obligation, at [research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md). **No note covers the Azure MCP Server's tool annotations**, so point 4's central demonstration — that destructiveness and non-idempotence are independent properties — rests on a documentation table this repository has never read or recorded.

**What rests on nothing.** The day's technical core. **No note anywhere under [research/](/research/99-source-register/source-register.md) treats distributed systems.** Partial failure, the third outcome, retry's four consequences, idempotence and its limit, and availability composition have no source note here; the closing section's own phrase for this is *held at folder level*, and at folder level there is nothing on any of them. The learning mechanisms are in the same position: **retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving, pretesting and cognitive load have no note anywhere**, so phases 1 and 2 rest on nothing this repository has verified. Error management, which carries phase 6, does have one — [research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md](/research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md) — for direction and mechanism only.

## What to take from this day

**The claim to hold.** **Across a network there is a third outcome — you do not know — and it is permanent.** A timeout is a decision you made about how long to wait, and the failures you decline to handle are a decision too.

**What the sources license, and what they do not.** Partial failure, retry amplification, idempotence and availability composition are held at folder level here and in [research/05-fde-craft/palantir-fde-practitioner-writing.md](/research/05-fde-craft/palantir-fde-practitioner-writing.md); the estimation obligation follows [research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md). **Direction and mechanism only; no magnitude is asserted.** The vendor calls its load balancing and its breaker tripping approximate and publishes no bound on either, so your estimates come from your own history or they are invented.

**Guidance for your own work.**

- **Name every unhandled failure with a probability and its confidence.** "Unlikely" is not an estimate, and a bare number somebody else reads is false precision.
- **Ask what second property a configuration needs before it does what its name says.** Priority ordering is not failover until a breaker trips, and the deployment succeeds either way.
- **Classify what you know** using the [Agent Configuration Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/13-Agent-Configuration-Standard.md). A deployed breaker is a *configured control* and licenses only that you stated the constraint; breaking the backend and watching is what makes it an *observed result*.

**Where this is easy to get wrong.** Expecting the failure to look like a wrong answer. This day's is a complete answer to an incomplete question: the reviewers checked that the configuration matched the request and it did, and the missing property was legal, silent and indistinguishable from a working system.
