**Platform anchor:** API Management policy scope as a per-operation contract; the gateway's caller-visible anomalies · **MCP servers:** Azure MCP Server (write) · Microsoft Learn MCP Server

## What this day is for

Three days have established that correctness is chosen, that evidence is scarce, and that failure is partial. All three are facts about the system. **This day is about what you tell the person using it.**

A consistency model is not an implementation property. It is a **contract**: a statement of what the caller may assume, what anomalies they will observe, and what they must do about them. Written down, it lets a caller build correctly on a system they do not understand. Left unwritten, the caller assumes the strongest model, builds on that assumption, and is wrong in ways neither party can attribute.

The module's negativity resolves here. Everything M07 has taught becomes something you can promise.

And the gateway is where it becomes concrete, because the gateway has spent three days generating anomalies its consumers cannot explain. A charge above a stated limit. A 503 from a component that is healthy. A confident answer to a question nobody asked. **Each of those is a promise somebody failed to write down**, and today they are written.

## Phase 3 · Contrasting cases and generation (45 min)

**Executed, not read.** Groups receive **one gateway and four consumers**, each with a different dependency on its behaviour, and each consumer is a client they can actually run against the deployed instance.

- **Caller A** displays data to a person who will retry if the answer looks wrong.
- **Caller B** makes an irreversible decision on a single response.
- **Caller C** aggregates across many calls for a report and cares about the total charged.
- **Caller D** sends a request and immediately sends a near-identical one, expecting the same answer.

For each, groups state the weakest guarantee that serves them, and commit. Then they run all four against the live gateway with the token limit and the semantic cache both active, and record what each consumer actually observes.

Caller D is the one that defeats the room. The group's prediction is that two near-identical requests are the easy case. What they observe is that **the second request is served from the cache and is therefore consistent, fast and free — and would have been served from the cache even if the second question had meant something different**, which is exactly the property caller B cannot tolerate and caller D was relying on. One mechanism serves one consumer and endangers another, and it is switched on for both.

Then **the cost of each guarantee is supplied** — latency, availability under a failed backend, and operational complexity — and groups re-rank.

Task after: **can one gateway serve all four? If not, what do you do?**

The answer is that it can, by promising differently on different operations, and almost no group proposes this.

**Warrant.** Contrasting cases across callers with a fixed service, then a cost reveal. Running the consumers rather than reasoning about them supplies the payload: the caching decision that resolves caller D is the caching decision that breaks caller B, and no group predicts that a single configuration is simultaneously the best and worst answer in the room. The closing question's answer — a service may offer more than one contract — is the day's most useful and least anticipated idea.

## Phase 4 · Consolidating instruction (50 min)

1. **A consistency model is a contract,** not an implementation detail. It states what the caller may assume.
2. **The four models from S031, restated as promises** and as the anomalies each permits. What the caller *sees* is the operative form.
3. **The cost of each,** in the three terms from phase 3, and why the trade is proved rather than engineering-limited — S023, directly. **This treats the availability-consistency trade as a single named result; it omits the more precise formulations that distinguish latency from availability and that apply during normal operation rather than only under partition. Safe for choosing a contract and explaining it to a caller; unsafe as a basis for arguing about a specific system's published claims, which requires the precise version.**
4. **Read-your-writes, monotonic reads, and the other session guarantees** as contracts that are weaker than the strong models and sufficient for most callers.
5. **Per-operation contracts.** A service may promise differently on different operations, and this is usually the right answer.
6. **Writing the contract:** what you may assume, what you will observe, what you must do about it. Three parts, in the caller's language.
7. **The anomaly catalogue** as the contract's operative half. A caller who knows what they will see can handle it; a caller who knows only the model's name cannot. **The catalogue is bounded by the callers you listed** — it covers the anomalies your consumers can encounter and act on, not the anomalies the model theoretically permits. A catalogue derived from the model is unbounded and useless; one derived from step 1's caller list is finishable.
8. **What happens when the contract is violated** — the notification, the remediation, or the accepted loss. S025's not-met procedure, applied to a consistency contract.
9. **Per-operation contracts, made concrete and marked perishable.** Policy in API Management is applied at a scope, and the scopes are global, workspace, product, API and **operation**. Point 5's idea is therefore not an aspiration on this platform; it is a directory structure. **But the available scopes differ by policy**: the token-limit policy may be applied at all five, and the semantic-cache policies at four — not at workspace — and the cache policies are unsupported on the workspace gateway altogether while the token-limit policy is unsupported on the consumption tier. So the set of contracts you can express is bounded by which mechanism you need and where you are hosting. **The names, tiers and scope lists are perishable. What is durable is that the granularity at which you can promise is a property of your mechanism, and it is smaller than the granularity at which callers differ.**
10. **Defaults are opinions and thresholds are policy — and here they are the contract.** Three of the anomalies your callers see are defaults nobody wrote down. The retry interval arrives in a header named `Retry-After` unless you renamed it, so your contract's timing clause already exists in your consumers' clients. The semantic cache is unpartitioned unless you add `vary-by`, so **the absence of one optional element is a contract term about who may read whose answers**. And a tripped circuit breaker returns **503**, which your caller will read as your service being down when in fact your service is deliberately protecting a dependency — a distinction that exists only if you state it. **The contract is the union of what you promised and what you defaulted**, and a caller cannot tell which is which.
11. **What the documentation does not say, and why it matters to a contract.** The vendor states that its limits are approximate, that its breaker rules are approximate, and that semantic caching may surface responses that are incorrect, outdated or unsafe — **and it publishes no rate, bound or frequency for any of the three.** No accuracy figure appears anywhere in this stack. That is not an oversight to be complained about; it is a fact about your evidence, and it has a contractual consequence: **you cannot promise a caller a bound you have not measured, and nobody upstream is going to measure it for you.** Your catalogue's numbers come from your own traffic or they are invented.

**Warrant.** Consolidation indexed to phase 3. Point 3 carries the strong-form scope statement. Point 7 is the day's most important claim, and its second half repairs the omission [WP-034](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-034.md) §8 identified — the design held that the catalogue is bounded by the caller list and had not written it into the day. Points 9 to 11 are the day's platform content and satisfy the [Copilot Practice Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md). Point 10 is the day's strongest single sentence: on a platform, a default is not a convenience, it is a term.

## Phase 5 · Whole-task learning task (90 min)

**Class 3. The contract is the student's. This phase produces a build.**

Students take the gateway they specified at S031 and, with Copilot in agent mode through the Azure MCP Server:

1. **List its consumers** and what each depends on.
2. **Choose the contract** for each operation — per-operation, per point 5.
3. **Write the anomaly catalogue:** for each contract, what the consumer will actually observe, with a concrete example **taken from an observation, not from the model.** A charge above the stated limit, a 503 with no failed backend, a cached answer to a different question.
4. **State the cost** of each contract in the three terms.
5. **Deploy the contract.** Apply the policies at the scopes that implement what you promised, and no wider. Where a promise cannot be expressed at the scope you need, say so in the contract rather than promising it anyway.
6. **Verify one clause by experiment.** Pick a clause a consumer would rely on, construct the traffic that tests it, and record whether the deployed system honours it.
7. **Write the contract document,** three parts, in the consumers' language, one page.
8. **State the not-met procedure** per point 8.
9. **Compare against S031 step 7:** the gap you named on day one — is it closed, and if not, what remains?

**The artefact carries three further elements**, per the Copilot Practice Standard.

- **Prompt record.** The instructions you gave the agent, in order, including the ones you abandoned.
- **Intervention record.** Every point at which you corrected it. **An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless step 6's experiment demonstrates that your own verification would have detected an intervention-worthy error.
- **Frontier statement.** One thing the agent did faster than you could have, and one thing it did badly that you had to catch. **Contract writing is the day's most likely place for the agent to be fluent and wrong**, and the statement should say whether it was.

**Fading:** step 3's catalogue form is supplied with worked examples for two contracts; step 7's three-part structure is supplied as headings. Policy syntax is agent-authored.

**Warrant.** Whole-task at class 3, closing the module's opening problem. Step 3 is the day's discriminator — a contract without observable anomalies is a name — and requiring the examples to come from observations rather than from the model is what makes the catalogue bounded and checkable. Step 6 is new and is the practical turn's contribution: a contract clause nobody has tested is a claim, and the day now requires one of them to be tested. Step 9 closes the loop S031 deliberately left open.

## Phase 6 · Error clinic (40 min)

Instructor first: a substrate caller that assumed a stronger guarantee than the service provided, built correctly on that assumption, and failed rarely — with attention to the fact that **neither team was wrong** and that the contract had never been written down.

Then the agent case, which is this one in modern dress. A team asked Copilot to generate consumer documentation from their gateway's policy configuration. It produced a clear, accurate, well-organised page, and it contained the sentence *"each subscription is limited to 500 tokens per minute."* That sentence is a faithful description of the configuration. It is not a description of the system. A consumer built a reconciliation job that treated any interval charged above 500 as a billing error, and raised forty-one disputes over two months, every one of which was correct according to the documentation and wrong according to the platform.

Attention goes to why review did not catch it. **The documentation was reviewed against the configuration, which is the natural thing to review it against, and it matched.** Nobody reviewed it against the behaviour, because the behaviour had never been measured — and the agent could not have known, because the overshoot is a property of the platform and not of the file it was given. **The agent was asked to describe a configuration and it described a configuration.** The consumer needed a description of a system.

Then students: something you assumed a system would do, or a claim you made in step 7 that you have not tested.

**Warrant.** Error management. The instructor disclosure's attribution problem is the phase's content and is the module's fourth consecutive no-villain failure, which is now stated to the room explicitly. The agent case is the [Copilot Practice Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md)'s required addition and is placed here because it is the same failure as the instructor's with a new author: **an unwritten contract has become a confidently written wrong one, faster and at scale.** Step 6 exists because of it.

## When it does not work

These are [WP-034](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-034.md) §5's predicted failure modes, restated as instruction to the person in the room. The whitepaper is the authority; where this section and §5 diverge, §5 is right. Nothing here is assessed.

**A student with a distributed systems background names models fluently and writes the contract in model terms.** The result is technically correct with a thin anomaly catalogue, because their own understanding runs on the model's name and they do not need the catalogue to use the system. Three things catch it and none requires your judgement: the specification fails a contract naming a model without its observable anomalies; every anomaly must trace to an observation; and phase 7's partner reads the document alone and cannot supply the derivation that is missing from it.

**A student without the background will stall at step 3's derivation.** Everything available is already given — two worked catalogue examples, phase 3's concrete anomalies, the supplied form, and three days of their own recorded observations to draw from. **The residual risk §5 accepts is that their catalogue restates the guarantee instead of listing observations, and the traceability requirement is the only check on it.**

**Phase 3's four matchings go too smoothly.** Expected — most groups complete them. The cost reveal reshuffles some rankings and the incompatibility question is where the phase bites. If a group proposes per-operation contracts unprompted, ask what happens when a single caller uses two operations with different guarantees. That is a genuine composition problem and it is hard.

**The platform fails on the day.** Lower risk here than at S032 or S033, because the day's core work is writing. Adjudicate phase 3's consumers from the module's recorded observations, declare step 5's deployment and step 6's verification **demonstrated rather than operated**, and do not assess them. The contract, the catalogue and the not-met procedure are unaffected, which is why this is the module's most robust day and why it carries the deferral discharge.

**The module's negativity does not lift.** This day's assigned job is to repair it, and the honest risk is simply that it does not. The countermeasures are structural — a usable artefact, a closed loop back to S031's open gap, a deployed contract, and a public question at phase 8 — and §5 declines to claim they are adequate. **Read phase 8's question as §5 asks you to: a weak instrument with an informative failure mode.** A cohort that answers *what can you now promise a caller* fluently is weak evidence that the deferral discharged; a cohort that cannot answer it is strong evidence that it did not. Record the failure and do not over-read the success.

## Assessment

Specification item in SY201 and PE201 jointly.

Per the [Assessment Architecture](/archive/bsc-programme/program/03-Assessment-Architecture.md)'s rule 1, the checked elements are: presence of per-operation contracts; presence of concrete anomalies for each, **each traceable to an observation rather than to the model**; presence of costs in three terms; **the deployed policy scopes and any promise the available scopes could not express**; **the step 6 verification and its result**; presence of a not-met procedure; the S031 gap comparison; survival of phase 7's document-only test; **the prompt record; the intervention record; and the frontier statement.**

**A contract naming a model without its observable anomalies fails.** **An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless step 6's verification demonstrates that the design could have detected an intervention-worthy error.

Nothing else is assessed. The prose quality of the contract document, the number of anomalies listed and whether the step 6 verification passed are all outside the specification — **a clause that failed its own experiment and says so is a better artefact than one that was never tested.**

## What to take from this day

**The claim to hold.** A consistency model is a **contract**, not an implementation property: what the caller may assume, what they will observe, and what they must do about it. The anomaly catalogue is its operative half, bounded by the callers you listed rather than by what the model permits.

**What the sources license, and what they do not.** Consistency models, session guarantees and the availability-consistency trade are held at folder level and in [research/05-fde-craft/evans-domain-driven-design.md](/research/05-fde-craft/evans-domain-driven-design.md), which carries that discipline. **Direction and mechanism only; no magnitude is asserted.** The vendor warns that its rate limiting is never completely accurate, that breaker tripping is approximate, and that semantic caching can surface incorrect, outdated or unsafe responses — and publishes no rate or bound for any of them ([research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md](/research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md)).

**Guidance for your own work.**

- **List your consumers before you write the catalogue**, and take each anomaly example from an observation rather than from the model's name. One derived from the model is unbounded; one from the caller list is finishable.
- **Test one clause by experiment.** A clause nobody has tested is a claim.
- **Separate what you promised from what you defaulted**, per the [Agent Configuration Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/13-Agent-Configuration-Standard.md). A policy applied at a scope is a *configured control*; only the experiment makes it an *observed result*, and your caller cannot tell them apart.

**Where this is easy to get wrong.** Reviewing consumer documentation against the configuration. It will match — the natural thing to check — and it can still be a false description of the system: the behaviour was never measured, and the file the agent was given does not contain it.
