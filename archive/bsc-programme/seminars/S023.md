# S023 · Reduction as an Argumentative Move

**Platform anchor:** Azure AI Search — the exhaustive-and-approximate asymmetry, and rank fusion · Foundry `document_retrieval` · **MCP servers:** Azure MCP Server (read) · Microsoft Learn MCP Server

## What this day is for

Reduction is the single most useful argumentative move in computer science and the single most reliably confused one. Students learn the mechanics — transform an instance of one problem into an instance of another — and invert the direction, which reverses every conclusion.

The confusion has a cause. In ordinary speech, "reducing A to B" suggests A is being simplified, so students hear it as a claim that A is easier. The technical meaning is the opposite: if you can reduce A to B, then B is at least as hard as A, because a solver for B gives you a solver for A.

The day's target is that **a reduction is an argument with a direction, and the direction determines the conclusion.** It is taught as an S004 argumentative move, not as an algorithmic technique, and this is why the day retrieves from the proof material rather than from the algorithms material.

**The direction is also, today, an error message.** The module's index carries the asymmetry in its own configuration: a field built for approximate search will answer an exhaustive query, and a field built for exhaustive search will not answer an approximate one, **because the structures the approximate query needs do not exist there.** That is a reduction's direction stated by a service rather than by a lecturer, and a student who tries the wrong way round finds out in under a second. Very little else in this programme gives a direction error a stack trace.

## Phase 1 · Pretest and prediction (25 min)

Five items.

1. A reduction from problem A to problem B is described. What does it let you conclude?
2. The same reduction is described in the other direction. Same question.
3. Which of these two would you use to argue that a colleague's request is unreasonable?
4. Predict: did you get items 1 and 2 the right way round?
5. **Copilot prediction, sealed.** You will shortly ask the agent to *show that the approximate retrieval configuration is as good as the exact one.* Write down the comparison it will build, and **name which of a reduction's requirements it will not verify.** Sealed with items 1 to 4 and opened at phase 8.

Most students get one of the two right and cannot say which. Item 5 is asked before the three requirements have been named, and its expected finding is that students can predict *that* something will go unverified and cannot yet say what.

**Warrant.** Pretesting on the exact confusion, with both directions presented so that the error is a direction error rather than a knowledge gap. Item 3 makes the distinction professional immediately. Item 5 is sealed at phase 1 and may not migrate into phase 5, per instrumentation rule 8; it is deliberately asked while the students lack the vocabulary to answer it precisely, so that phase 8's comparison shows the vocabulary arriving.

## Phase 3 · Contrasting cases and generation (45 min)

**Executed, not read.** Groups receive **four reductions**, each with a stated conclusion, and each expressed as a comparison they can run against the module's index. They judge first and commit, then run.

**Reduction 1 — correct, and its conclusion follows.** *To establish that the approximate configuration returns the documents the exact one would, reduce the question to running the same query set exhaustively on the same field and comparing the returned sets.* Direction correct; the transformation is available because an approximate field accepts an exhaustive query; general over the stated query class. It runs, and it answers.

**Reduction 2 — correct, and its conclusion is inverted.** *The same comparison, arranged the other way: build the field for exhaustive search only, then run an approximate query against it to see what the approximation misses.* Groups who accept this run it. **The request is rejected.** The exhaustive field does not carry the structures the approximate query requires, because those structures are built during indexing and were never built. The direction was never a matter of presentation: a solver for the richer-structure problem yields the poorer one and not the reverse.

**Reduction 3 — right direction, transformation not adequate, so nothing follows.** *To establish which of two configurations is better, reduce the question to comparing their scores.* It runs, and produces numbers. The full-text score has no upper bound; the vector score sits in a narrow band and is a transformation of the distance rather than the distance itself; the fused score's ceiling depends on how many queries were fused; the reranked score sits on a different scale again and is reported in a separate field. **Four scales, one comparison, no conclusion.**

**Reduction 4 — right direction, efficient, and it transforms only a special case.** *To establish that retrieval is good on this corpus, reduce the question to measuring it on the queries for which we hold relevance labels.* Groups run the Foundry `document_retrieval` evaluator, which returns its ranking metrics **and a count of how many retrieved documents carry no relevance judgement at all.** The conclusion is weaker than stated, and for once the platform says by how much.

Groups judge each and commit before running. All four sound plausible.

Task after: **state the three things a reduction must establish** for its conclusion to follow.

**Warrant.** Contrasting cases across the three ways a reduction fails, now executed rather than adjudicated by the instructor. Reduction 2 is the day's cheapest and most durable moment: a direction error that returns an error message is a fact rather than a correction, and no student who has produced it argues about the rule afterwards. Reduction 4 remains the phase's payload — the direction and the efficiency are both correct and the argument still does not support the claim — and it is strengthened by having its generality failure counted rather than asserted. Reduction 3 is new and is the phase's warning against the module's most available shortcut, which is to compare whatever number the API puts in front of you.

## Phase 4 · Consolidating instruction (50 min)

1. **What a reduction is,** stated as an argument: if I can solve B, then by this transformation I can solve A; therefore B is at least as hard as A.
2. **The direction, resolved.** Reducing A to B makes a claim about **B**. The common error and why the language invites it — and phase 3's reduction 2, which is the same error with an error message attached.
3. **The three requirements,** from phase 3: correct direction, efficient transformation, and generality over the problem you are claiming about.
4. **Reduction to prove hardness, and reduction to obtain an algorithm** — the same mechanism used for opposite purposes, and how to tell which you are doing.
5. **A worked hardness argument** on a real substrate problem, completely, from a known-hard problem.
6. **What a hardness result licenses you to say** — and the three things it does not: that no algorithm works, that your instances are hard, or that approximation is impossible.
7. **Rank fusion as a reduction, worked.** When the index answers a query two ways, it does not average the scores. It **reduces the fusion problem to rank position**: each document's contribution is the reciprocal of its position in each list, and the original scores are discarded. That is a legitimate reduction and it licenses one thing — a statement about agreement in ordering. **It licenses nothing about margin.** A document that was far ahead and a document that just edged in front contribute identically, because the transformation cannot see the difference. Reduction 3's incomparable scales are the same lesson arriving from the other side.
8. **Defaults are opinions and thresholds are policy.** The fusion's damping constant is a fixed small number the documentation recommends on the strength of experiments it does not cite. **The fused score's ceiling rises with the number of ranked lists being merged**, so a threshold that was calibrated against a text query plus one vector query is wrong the moment somebody adds a second vector field — and the count is not the number of things you typed: one text query plus one vector query targeting two fields is three executions. **A threshold nobody re-derived after a configuration change is a policy nobody owns.**
9. **The professional form.** A hardness argument is what you show a requester who wants a guarantee you cannot give. S024 is about delivering it.
10. **What is perishable and what is not.** The constant, the score ranges, the execution arithmetic and the evaluator's metric names are perishable. The direction, the three requirements, the two purposes and the three prohibitions are not. **The correspondence between the index's asymmetry and a reduction's direction is safe for reasoning about why one comparison is available and its mirror is not, and unsafe as evidence that the direction rule is true** — the rule is a theorem and the service is an illustration, and a student who cites the error message as a proof has mistaken an instance for an argument.

**Warrant.** Consolidation indexed to phase 3's four failures. Point 1 states the argument form, which is the antidote to the linguistic intuition. Point 2 names the error and explains why the language invites it, which matters: an error with an explanation is easier to guard against than one that feels like personal confusion. Point 6's three prohibitions are the day's most important content and prevent the module's characteristic overreach. Point 7 is the day's second reduction worked completely and is placed here because it is the one students will meet in production; point 8 satisfies the [Copilot Practice Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md)'s default-naming requirement, and point 10 carries the strong-form scope statement and guards the day's own analogy.

## Phase 5 · Whole-task learning task (90 min)

**Class 2. The reduction target is not given. This phase produces a build.**

Students take a real substrate problem believed to be hard and:

1. **State the claim** you intend to establish, precisely.
2. **Choose the problem to reduce from,** and say why that one.
3. **Construct the transformation.**
4. **Verify all three requirements** from point 3, separately and explicitly — and **ship the verification as three separate executable checks**, built with Copilot in agent mode through the Azure MCP Server in read configuration:
   - a **direction check** that exhibits the claimed solver on one worked instance, so that the inference is demonstrated rather than described;
   - an **efficiency check** that measures the transformation's cost on inputs of increasing size;
   - a **generality check** that reports what proportion of the claimed domain your instances actually covered, in the shape phase 3's reduction 4 supplied.
5. **State the conclusion** in the form point 1 licenses.
6. **State the three things it does not license,** per point 6, in this specific case.
7. **State what a requester should do** given your result.

**The artefact carries three further elements**, per the [Copilot Practice Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md).

- **Prompt record.** The instructions you gave the agent, in order, including the ones you abandoned.
- **Intervention record.** Every correction, and what it was. **An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that your own design would have caught an intervention-worthy error — a generality check that fails loudly when coverage falls below a stated level is the obvious example and is accepted.
- **Frontier statement.** One thing the agent did faster than you could have, and one thing it did badly that you had to catch.

**Fading:** step 2 supplies a shortlist of three candidate source problems; step 3's transformation is worked with the room for a different problem; step 4's three check shapes are named and their implementations are not.

**Warrant.** Whole-task at class 2, the module's most heavily faded. Step 4's separate verification is the day's discipline and prevents the single most common error, which is verifying the transformation and assuming the rest. **Making the three verifications three separate executable artefacts is the design's principal gain from the platform anchor**, because the failure the day exists to prevent — one check performed three times under three headings — is visible in a diff and invisible in prose.

## Phase 6 · Error clinic (40 min)

Instructor first: a hardness claim made about a substrate problem that was wrong — the reduction was in the right direction and covered only a special case — and the six months of work that were not attempted because of it.

Then the agent case: a Copilot comparison that was **confidently wrong and passed review**. Asked to show that the approximate configuration matched the exact one, the agent produced a clean, well-structured script that ran both, collected the scores, computed the mean difference between them, found it small, and reported agreement. The script was correct. The statistic was correctly computed. **It compared the scores and not the sets**, and because both configurations score with the same distance transformation, the scores agreed closely while the documents differed. Attention goes to why review did not catch it: the score is the field the API returns most prominently, comparing it is the obvious move, and nobody in the review asked what the number was a reduction *of*.

Then students: a time you concluded something was impossible, or a place today where you compared the thing that was easiest to compare.

**Warrant.** Error management, with a disclosure whose cost is work not done rather than work done wrong, which is the characteristic and least visible cost of a bad hardness argument. The agent case is the [Copilot Practice Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md)'s required addition and instantiates this day's §1 problem exactly: the transformation is the visible part of a reduction, the agent verified the visible part, and the artefact looked finished.

---

## Phase 7 · Peer articulation (30 min)

Pairs. Each student states their step 5 conclusion, and the partner's task is to **attack it on all three requirements in turn** — direction, efficiency, generality — and to say which attack came closest. Where an attack can be settled by running the partner's step 4 checks, it is run.

**Warrant.** Interactive-tier articulation with a structured attack. Requiring all three in sequence rather than the strongest one means the weakest link is found rather than the most obvious one. Settling attacks by execution where execution is available follows instrumentation rule 1: the check is present in the room and costs nothing.

---

## Phase 8 · Calibration and commitment (20 min)

- Items 1 to 4 resolved. The proportion who got both directions right, one, and neither — shown against their item 4 confidence.
- The room's step 6 non-licensed conclusions collected; the most common overreach is named.
- **Item 5 opened.** The sealed predictions are read against the comparison the agent actually built, and coded for which requirement students named. The cohort's rate is recorded on both halves — predicting an unverified requirement, and naming the right one.
- Each student names one thing they have described as impossible without an argument.
- Retrieval schedule: next morning, day 3, week 2, week 6.

## When it does not work

These are [WP-023](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-023.md) §5's predicted failure modes, restated as instruction to the person in the room. The whitepaper is the authority; where this section and §5 diverge, §5 is right.

**A student who has seen reduction before is *more* likely to make the direction error, not less.** The mechanics are familiar and the familiarity substitutes for the argument form. Their predicted failure is at step 4: a fluent transformation at step 3, then three checks that all exercise the transformation under three headings. Phase 7's sequenced attack is the countermeasure and the partner has the same three headings, so point them at the *separateness* of the verification rather than at its correctness. The specification's weighted element is now checkable by inspection, because the three checks are three artefacts.

**A student without that background meets the module's hardest day with two new proof techniques already behind them.** Five supports exist — shortlisted source problems, a worked transformation, decomposed verification, three named check shapes, and no formal complexity-class vocabulary — and all five should be in play rather than held in reserve. *Residual risk the design accepts:* step 3's transformation may not be constructible in the time. **The fallback is explicit and should be offered without ceremony:** complete steps 4 to 7 on a supplied transformation. The objective is the argument, not the construction.

**Phase 3 is too easy in its first three cases.** Reductions 1 and 2 are usually judged correctly and reduction 2's rejection is legible on sight; reduction 3 is caught by many groups once they see four different ranges. **Reduction 4 is caught by few, and that is the phase working.** If a group catches all four, ask them to state the weaker claim that *does* follow from reduction 4 — which is the correct professional response and is harder than identifying the flaw.

**Discouragement at the prohibitions.** This is the day's characteristic affective failure and it has a specific shape: a student who has constructed a genuine hardness argument, at real difficulty, is then required to enumerate three things it does not establish, and the day reads as taking back what it just gave. **The ordering is the countermeasure and it is the facilitator's to hold** — keep step 7 in view while the room is working step 6, because a hardness result that ends in a recommendation is a contribution and one that ends in a prohibition list is a deflation, and the difference is which one the student writes last.

**Three checks that are one check**, which §5 predicts will be common with the platform anchor. A student who ships three scripts all exercising the transformation has satisfied the letter of step 4 and none of its purpose. The specification's second failing clause names this outcome explicitly and phase 7's partner is looking for it. **§5's honest note is worth repeating to the room:** this failure was always possible in prose and was undetectable there; making the checks executable does not prevent it, and does make it findable.

## Assessment

Specification item in CF103. Checked elements: the precise claim; the chosen source problem with justification; the transformation; all three requirements verified separately, as three separate executable checks; the licensed conclusion; the three non-licensed conclusions in the specific case; the requester's recommended action; the prompt record; the intervention record; the frontier statement.

**A reduction whose three requirements are not separately verified fails the specification,** even if the reduction is correct. **Three checks that are the same check under three headings fail the specification.** **An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that the design could have detected an intervention-worthy error.

Nothing else is assessed.

## What this day does not establish

**Demonstrated.** That an exhaustive query runs on a field built for approximate search and that the mirror request is refused; that the four scores the service reports sit on four scales that cannot be compared with one another; and that the retrieval evaluator counts how many returned documents carry no relevance judgement. Three observations, all taken in the room, all about this index and this label set.

**Read rather than demonstrated.** The fusion's damping constant and the vendor's recommendation of it; the score ranges; the execution arithmetic behind a fused score's ceiling; and the evaluator's metric names. All documentation, read on the day. **The experiments said to justify the fusion constant are not cited, described or dated anywhere**, so it is a recommendation and not a result, and the day says so.

**Not established here, and the day is emphatic about it.** **That the direction rule is true.** The rule is a theorem and the error message is an instance; an instance is not an argument, and the instance will be different next year. Point 10 states this and *Where this is easy to get wrong* states it again, because it is the single misreading the day's best moment invites.

**Asserted, and flagged by the day itself as unverified.** That **reductions are the form a professional refusal takes.** The obligation to decline infeasible requests with stated reasons is verified; the further claim that a reduction is how that refusal is constructed is **this design's own reconstruction and rests on no source.** It is the bridge into [S024](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S024.md) and it carries the day's professional half, and it is worth knowing that it is the design's argument rather than a finding.

**What rests on a `research/` note.** The professional obligation to decline infeasible requests with stated reasons, at [research/05-fde-craft/collected-materials.md](/research/05-fde-craft/collected-materials.md); the algorithmic material behind the two vector configurations, at [research/02-technical-foundations/malkov-yashunin-hnsw.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/malkov-yashunin-hnsw.md). **What rests on nothing.** Pretesting, expanding-interval retrieval, contrasting cases, worked-example fading and cognitive-load management have **no note anywhere in this repository.** They carry phases 1, 2, 3 and 5's fading schedule, which is most of the day's structure, and the design cites them for direction and mechanism only because there is nothing here to cite for more.

## What to take from this day

**The claim to hold.** **A reduction is an argument with a direction, and the direction decides the conclusion.** Reducing A to B makes a claim about B. Three things must hold before anything follows — correct direction, an efficient transformation, and generality over the domain you are claiming about — and each has to be checked on its own.

**What the sources license, and what they do not.** The professional obligation to decline infeasible requests with stated reasons is verified in this repository at [research/05-fde-craft/collected-materials.md](/research/05-fde-craft/collected-materials.md); the further claim that reductions are the form that refusal takes is **not** verified and is this design's own reconstruction. Algorithmic material follows [research/02-technical-foundations/malkov-yashunin-hnsw.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/malkov-yashunin-hnsw.md). The learning mechanisms are cited for **direction and mechanism only and no magnitude is asserted.** The experiments said to justify the fusion constant are **not cited, described or dated**, so it is a recommendation and not a result.

**Guidance for your own work.**

- Ship the three checks separately. Three checks that all exercise the transformation are one check with three headings.
- Say what your transformation discarded. Rank fusion cannot see margin, and that bounds everything it licenses.
- Re-derive a threshold whenever the number of things being merged changes.
- **Sort what a comparison gives you** into the five classes on the [Agent Configuration Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/13-Agent-Configuration-Standard.md). A correctly computed difference between scores is an observed result about scores, not evidence about the documents.

**Where this is easy to get wrong.** Citing the service's refusal as proof of the direction rule. The rule is a theorem and the error message is an illustration; an instance is not an argument, and the instance will be different next year.
