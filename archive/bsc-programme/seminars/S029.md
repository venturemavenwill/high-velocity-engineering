# S029 · Designing an Instrument for a Question

**Module:** [M06](/archive/bsc-programme/modules/M06.md) · **Quarter:** [Q3](/archive/bsc-programme/quarters/Q3.md) · **Complexity class:** 2
**Strands:** ME · SY · PE
**Whitepaper:** [WP-029](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-029.md)
**Depends on:** S026, S028
**Re-tests:** S009, S015
**Platform anchor:** Microsoft Foundry custom evaluators — prompt-based and code-based — and local evaluators · **MCP servers:** Microsoft Learn MCP Server · Azure MCP Server (read)

---

## What this day is for

Three days of the module have each subtracted something. The instrument may not measure the construct. The error may not be reducible. The number may not describe the population you care about.

**This day is constructive, and it is designed to be.** The module's diagnostic apparatus exists to make instruments better, not to make measurement impossible, and a student who leaves M06 believing nothing can be established has learned the module backwards.

The target: **given a question that matters, build the instrument that answers it — and know, before you build it, what it will not be able to detect.**

The day's second target is the one students will carry furthest. **An instrument that has never failed has never been tested.** That is true of a laboratory apparatus, it is true of the evaluator you write this afternoon, and it is true of the prompt file sitting in your repository that has produced acceptable output every time you have run it and has therefore been validated by nobody. **A prompt file with no review date is an unvalidated instrument in a new costume**, and this is the day the programme says so.

The design's obligation here is explicit. [WP-026](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-026.md), [WP-027](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-027.md) and [WP-028](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-028.md) each recorded that the module's cumulative fatalism risk peaks and "resolves at S029." This day either discharges that or the module has a structural problem.

**Staff preparation.**

- **Read [research/03-measurement-evaluation/huyen-ai-engineering.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/huyen-ai-engineering.md) before teaching point 7.** The three-parameter account of a judged instrument — model, prompt, sampling configuration — and the disclosure rule that follows from it are both quoted from it, and its hedges are load-bearing: what it reports about scale shape is reported as *it's been reported* and *seems to*, and **no magnitude is available or asserted anywhere in this day.**
- **Prepare to run the cheapest check in front of the room:** feed a new evaluator something you know is bad and confirm that it says so. Point 7 names it, phase 6's agent case is what happens without it, and it takes a minute.
- **Decide the phase 5 pairing before the day**, because step 4's second half requires the phase 7 partner to hand-score the same ten cases, and a pairing arranged at phase 7 arrives too late.
- **Prepare the day as constructive and mean it.** §5 says the day's assigned task is to *repair* an affective failure rather than to avoid one, and that the countermeasures are the day's whole structure rather than anything the facilitator says. Do not add reassurance; run the procedure.

**Platform prerequisites.**

- A **Microsoft Foundry project** with custom prompt-based and code-based evaluators creatable, local evaluators runnable, and `Groundedness Pro` reachable.
- A **model deployment** for instrument A, and — for the demonstration only — a continuous-evaluation configuration with telemetry redaction controllable, so that point 6's measurement effect can be shown.
- The **Microsoft Learn MCP Server**, the **Azure MCP Server in read configuration**, and Copilot agent mode.
- **A custom prompt-based evaluator requires a threshold at creation time**, and a code-based evaluator that never calls a model still requires a model deployment to be named. Both are provisioning facts before they are teaching points.

**If a prerequisite is missing.** The general provision is rule 3 of the [Copilot Practice Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md): a case that cannot be run is declared demonstrated rather than operated and is not assessed — which instrument D already is. **If the held-back set cannot be assembled and hand-scored, step 4 collapses and with it the specification's failing clause**, since an instrument with no validation criterion fails and there would be no way to produce one. **If no register exists to receive the adopted instruments, say so to the room rather than implying an adoption that will not happen**; the day's honesty is its instructional mechanism and a hollow adoption undoes it. §5 supplies one genuine fallback: **a designed, costed, unbuilt instrument passes the remaining specification elements**, which is the route for a student who cannot build anything in the time.

## Phase 1 · Pretest and prediction (25 min)

Five items on a new, unanswered substrate question — different from the module's running one.

1. Design an instrument to answer it. Sketch, five minutes.
2. What will your instrument fail to detect?
3. What would you accept as evidence that your instrument is working?
4. Predict: is your instrument's main weakness one of construct, error or sampling?
5. **Copilot prediction, sealed.** You will shortly ask the agent to *write us an evaluator for this*. Write down what it will produce, and **name the one thing it will get wrong**. Opened at phase 8.

Item 3 is the day's move — instruments are themselves subject to validation — and is answered by very few.

Item 5's expectation is that the agent will produce a working, well-formed, plausibly-worded prompt-based evaluator and no way whatever to tell whether it is right. It will not propose a labelled set, it will not ask what a correct score looks like, and it will not suggest a review date, because nothing in the request asked for one and nothing in the platform requires one.

**Warrant.** Pretesting with a design demand. Item 4 requires the student to classify their own weakness using the module's three days, which is the module's integration check placed before instruction. Item 5 is sealed here per instrumentation rule 8, and its expected answer is the same omission item 3 exposes — which is the day's argument in miniature.

## Phase 3 · Contrasting cases and generation (45 min)

**Executed, not read.** Groups receive **one question and four instruments**, all of which were actually built by somebody to answer it. Three are run against the same response set; the fourth is shown running.

| Instrument | What it is | Property |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | A **custom prompt-based evaluator**, ordinal 1–5, written for this exact construct | Measures the construct well; costs a model call per item, so it will not be run often |
| **B** | A **local evaluator** — a deterministic keyword and tool-call check, no model, no API call | Measures a proxy, cheaply, with the relationship stated |
| **C** | **Groundedness Pro**, a binary service-side check needing no model deployment of your own | Measures the construct well under conditions that rarely occur — it requires a correct retrieved context to be present, and when retrieval fails it has nothing to judge against |
| **D** | **Continuous evaluation** running against live traffic | Measures the construct well and changes the system by measuring it |

Groups rank them for adoption and commit. Then the decision context is supplied: how often the question must be answered, by whom, and what will be done with the answer.

Task after: **re-rank, and state which property of the decision context changed your ranking.**

**Instrument D is the payload** and introduces measurement effects, which S026's scope statement explicitly deferred to this day. It consumes the same model capacity as the system it watches, it adds cost per interaction on the busiest paths, and — the part nobody predicts — **its explanations are redacted by default, and turning them on writes the judge's reasoning about the conversation into your telemetry, where it may reproduce whatever sensitive content the conversation contained.** Observing changed what is stored about the observed. **Instrument D is demonstrated rather than operated**: continuous evaluation is a preview capability requiring production traffic and a connected Application Insights resource the programme does not hold for a student project. Staff run it and the room watches. **It is not assessed.**

**Warrant.** Contrasting cases across instruments with a late-revealed decision context. The construction deliberately mirrors S025's, so that the move — a criterion arriving after commitment — is recognised. Instruments A, B and C are executed so that the cost, the latency and the failure of C under retrieval failure are observed rather than asserted.

## Phase 4 · Consolidating instruction (50 min)

1. **Start from the decision, not the question.** What will change depending on the answer determines how good the instrument must be, and an instrument better than the decision requires is waste.
2. **The design sequence:** decision → question → construct → instrument → error budget → sampling scheme → licensing sentence. Six steps, each constrained by the one before.
3. **Designing against the S026 gaps.** Choose the construct explicitly, choose the proxy deliberately, state the relationship in the instrument's own documentation.
4. **Designing against the S027 sources.** Randomise what you cannot control, control what you can, record what you cannot randomise, and instrument the recording so that selection is visible.
5. **Designing the sample before taking it,** so that the population the instrument estimates is the population the decision concerns.
6. **Measurement effects,** from instrument D: when observing changes the observed. What to do — reduce, account for, or accept and state.
7. **Validating the instrument itself:** what would show it is working, what would show it has stopped. **This is item 3, and it is the day's most-skipped step in practice.** For a judged evaluator the honest answer is uncomfortable and concrete: **nobody publishes how well these evaluators agree with human judgement, so the only calibration you will ever have is one you produce.** That means a set of outputs you scored yourself, held back, and re-scored against; a disagreement rate you can quote; and a re-check when the judge model deployment changes. **A validation criterion that cannot fail is not a validation criterion.** An evaluator that has returned `pass` on every input you have ever given it has told you about your inputs, not about itself, and the first thing to do with a new evaluator is to feed it something you know is bad and confirm that it says so.

   **And the instrument is not the model.** A practitioner account of evaluation for foundation-model systems states it in one sentence: *an AI judge is not just a model — it's a system that includes both a model and a prompt*, and *altering the model, the prompt, or the model's sampling parameters results in a different judge.* **Three parameters, not one**, so a held-back set re-scored after a prompt edit is not a re-check of the same instrument, and a validation record that names only the deployment has named a third of what it validated. The same source's rule for reading somebody else's score is a disclosure rule rather than a calibration one — **do not trust any AI judge if you can't see the model and the prompt used for the judge** — which is the form point 7 can actually ship, given that the calibration nobody publishes is not going to arrive. **It also declines to make agreement with humans a precondition of use**: judgements that are not as good as human judgements *might still be good enough to guide an application's development*. That is a permission to begin and not a licence to report, and it belongs beside the uncomfortable answer above rather than in place of it. And it supplies the reason point 8's list is always incomplete, which is a property of the measurement and not of your diligence — **no perfect evaluation method exists**, because *it's impossible to capture the ability of a high-dimensional system using one- or few-dimensional scores*. That is an argument for combining instruments of different kinds, which is what phase 3 hands you four of. See [research/03-measurement-evaluation/huyen-ai-engineering.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/huyen-ai-engineering.md); **direction, mechanism and vocabulary only, and no magnitude is carried from it into this day.**
8. **What an instrument cannot detect,** written down at design time and shipped with it. **This list is always incomplete and must say so** — the honest form is "here is what I checked for and could not detect," not "here is everything it misses."
9. **Your prompt files are instruments and none of them is validated.** A prompt file is a procedure with a variable output, a documented invocation and no review date. It drifts when the model behind it changes, exactly as instrument C drifts when retrieval changes, and nothing tells you. It has never failed in your hands, which is the same evidence you have that an untested backup works. **Everything at point 7 applies to it without modification**: a held-back set of cases you know the right answer to, a disagreement rate, a review date, and a withdrawal condition. This is the [Copilot Practice Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md)'s treatment of instructions files as specifications, arriving from the measurement side, and the two accounts are of the same object.
10. **Defaults are opinions and thresholds are policy.** Three chose for you today. A custom prompt-based evaluator requires you to supply a **threshold at creation time**, which means the pass/fail boundary is fixed before you have seen a single score — the platform makes you commit to the criterion before the evidence, which is the correct order and almost nobody notices it is being enforced. Ordinal and continuous scoring both assume **higher is better**, so an evaluator for a quantity you want to minimise has to be written inside out. And a **code-based** evaluator that never calls a model still requires you to name a model deployment, because the service schema demands one — a requirement with no relationship to what your evaluator does. **A fourth was chosen by you and you may not have noticed choosing it, and it is the shape of the scale.** The practitioner account cited at point 7 reports — in hedges that must be kept, *it's been reported* and *seems to* — that judges do better at classification than at numerical scoring, that discrete scoring appears to do better than continuous, and that wider discrete ranges appear to do worse. **No magnitude is asserted for any of that here and none is available**; the operative point for this day is that **the scale is one of the judge's parameters rather than a neutral container for its output**, and instrument A's one-to-five ordinal is the place to test it on your own construct.
11. **What is perishable here and what is not.** The evaluator types, the required parameters, the preview status of custom evaluators and continuous evaluation, and the current judge families are perishable. **The durable claim is that an instrument ships with a validation criterion and a withdrawal condition or it is not finished**, and that this applies identically to a laboratory apparatus, a cloud evaluator and a prompt file. The safe reading of today's platform instance is that it shows you what the artefacts look like; the unsafe reading is that a platform providing a threshold parameter has thereby validated anything.

**Warrant.** Consolidation organised as a constructive procedure rather than as a set of cautions. Points 3, 4 and 5 map the module's three diagnostic days onto design decisions, which is the module's assembly. Point 7 is the most-skipped step and is given its own segment. Point 8 applies the scope rule reflexively — to the instrument's own limitation statement. **Point 9 is the [Copilot Practice Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md)'s commitment for this day** and is reachable from phase 3's instrument A, which the students have just watched behave like a prompt file because it is one. Points 10 and 11 are the required platform default and perishability content.

## Phase 5 · Whole-task learning task (85 min)

**Class 2. The instrument is the student's, it will be run, and it will be adopted.**

Students take **a real unanswered substrate question that staff want answered**, and — working through Copilot in agent mode, grounded on the Learn MCP Server — build and ship a custom evaluator. They:

1. **State the decision** the answer will inform, and who makes it.
2. **Work the six-step design sequence** from point 2, recording each constraint.
3. **State what the instrument cannot detect,** in the honest form from point 8.
4. **State the validation:** what would show the instrument is working, and what would show it has stopped. **Then produce it.** A held-back set of cases whose correct scores you assigned by hand, the instrument's disagreement rate against them, and a review date. **Then have your phase 7 partner independently hand-score ten of the same cases, and report two numbers: how far the instrument is from you, and how far your partner is from you.** If the second number is as large as the first, you have not found a fault in the instrument — you have found that the construct is harder to judge than either of you assumed, and that is a different finding with a different remedy.
5. **Build it.**
6. **Run it once** and report the result with its licensing sentence.
7. **State the cost** of running it at the frequency the decision requires.

Step 4 is where the day's work concentrates. Hand-scoring even twenty cases is tedious, it is the part every team skips, and it is the only thing that will ever tell the student whether their instrument works — **because nobody else is going to publish it for them.** A student who reports a disagreement rate has done something no page of the vendor's documentation does for any built-in evaluator, and the design says so out loud.

**The artefact carries three further elements**, per the [Copilot Practice Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md).

- **Prompt record.** The instructions you gave the agent, in order, including the ones you abandoned. **If your evaluator is prompt-based, its own prompt is an instrument and is versioned separately from this record.**
- **Intervention record.** Every point at which you corrected it, and what the correction was. **An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that the design could have detected an intervention-worthy error — step 4's held-back set is precisely such a demonstration and is accepted, provided the instrument actually disagreed with the student somewhere.
- **Frontier statement.** One thing the agent did faster than you could have, and one thing it did badly that you had to catch.

**Fading:** step 2 is worked with the room for a different question; step 4's validation forms are supplied as a menu.

**Warrant.** Whole-task at class 2 producing an adopted artefact. Step 4 is the day's discriminator and its second half converts a stated intention into a produced calibration. Step 7 makes the instrument an operational proposal rather than an exercise.

## Phase 6 · Error clinic (40 min)

Instructor first: an instrument built on the substrate that was correct at design time and silently stopped being correct — with attention to whether anything would have caught it, and the fact that nothing did for months.

Then the agent case. A team asked Copilot to *write an evaluator that checks our answers are on-brand*. It produced a prompt-based evaluator with a clear rubric, a 1-to-5 scale and a threshold of 3, and it ran. **It returned `pass` on every input for four months.** Nobody noticed, because a quality check that never fails reads as good news and nothing in the platform flags an evaluator that has never discriminated. The rubric's wording made a failing score reachable only for output the assistant could not physically produce. Attention goes to why review did not catch it: the evaluator was reviewed once, at creation, by someone reading the rubric and agreeing with it — and **reading a rubric and agreeing with it is not a test of anything.** The one cheap check that would have caught it in a minute is the one at point 7: feed it something you know is bad.

Then students: a measurement you set up and never checked, or a prompt file in your own repository you have never tried to make fail.

**Warrant.** Error management targeting point 7 rather than the design itself, because instrument validation is the module's most-skipped step and the failure is invisible. The agent case is the required addition and is chosen because the agent did nothing wrong — it built what was asked — and because an evaluator that never fails is the failure signature students are least likely to read as a failure.

## Phase 7 · Peer articulation (30 min)

Pairs. Each student presents their **step 3 limitation statement and step 4 validation**, and the partner's task is to find a failure that:

- the limitation statement does not cover, **and**
- the validation would not catch.

If they find one, the pair decides whether to extend the validation or to add the failure to the limitation statement — and the choice has a cost either way.

**Warrant.** Interactive-tier articulation combining the two outputs, with a forced trade at the end. This is S025's phase 7 repaired: the break must defeat both the statement and the detection, which calibrates it.

## Phase 8 · Calibration and commitment (20 min)

- Items 2, 3 and 4 resolved: how many predicted their own weakness class correctly, and how many answered item 3 at all.
- **Item 5 opened.** The sealed Copilot predictions are read against what the agent actually produced, in two bands: predicted *some* error, and named *the missing validation* specifically. The design expects the second band to be small, because item 3 has just shown that most of the room would have made the same omission.
- **The module's constructive claim is put to the room directly**: name one thing about this system that you could not have established four days ago and now can. Answers are collected and read out.
- Each student's instrument is submitted for staff use, with their name, their step 3 statement, their disagreement rate and their review date attached.

## When it does not work

These are [WP-029](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-029.md) §5's predicted failure modes, restated as instruction to the person in the room. The whitepaper is the authority; where this section and §5 diverge, §5 is right.

**A student with instrumentation or experimental background designs fluently and builds to a standard the decision does not require.** Their training rewards rigour independent of use, and step 7's operating cost then makes the instrument unaffordable at the frequency the decision needs. Two things catch it: step 1 requires the decision **and its owner**, and step 7 forces the frequency calculation. **An instrument that cannot be run as often as the decision needs has failed a stated requirement rather than an aesthetic one**, and saying that plainly is what stops the correction reading as a preference.

**A student who has only ever used supplied apparatus finds building anything in eighty-five minutes demanding.** Four supports exist: the substrate's plumbing, a worked sequence on a different question, a validation menu, and the stated fallback that **a designed, costed, unbuilt instrument passes the remaining specification elements.** Offer the fallback early rather than at minute seventy. *Residual risk the design accepts:* their instrument may be adopted and be poor — which is a substrate risk rather than a student one, and is handled by staff review before adoption, not by refusing the submission.

**Phase 3's initial ranking is straightforward and the re-ranking is where the difficulty is.** Instrument D reliably survives the first ranking and fails the second for a reason groups struggle to articulate. If a group handles all four, ask which instrument they would choose if the decision were made once a year rather than daily; that usually reverses their answer and makes the frequency dependence explicit.

**The day's affective task is to repair a failure rather than to avoid one, and this is worth stating plainly to yourself before you start.** The module's cumulative fatalism risk has been asserted three times to peak and resolve here. The countermeasures are the day's whole structure — a constructive procedure, four usable instruments, an adopted artefact, and a public claim at phase 8 — and none of them is a thing the facilitator says.

**And the design concedes that it cannot tell whether this works.** Phase 8's question is not a measurement: it is a group of students at the end of a day, asked to say something positive, in a setting where saying nothing is socially awkward. **A room that answers enthusiastically is weak evidence; a room that cannot answer is strong evidence of failure.** The instrument is asymmetric and unsatisfactory, §5 says so, and the useful thing a facilitator can do is record which of the two happened rather than manage the answer.

## Assessment

Specification item in ME101. Checked elements: the decision and its owner; the six-step sequence with recorded constraints; the honest-form limitation statement; the validation criterion **and the two produced disagreement rates — instrument-to-student and partner-to-student — against a held-back hand-scored set**; the built instrument; one run with its licensing sentence; the operating cost; **the prompt record**; **the intervention record**; **the frontier statement**.

**An instrument with no validation criterion fails the specification**, however well designed.

**An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that the design could have detected an intervention-worthy error.

Instrument D at phase 3 is demonstrated rather than operated and is not assessed.

Nothing else is assessed.

| A prompt file is an instrument and drifts when its model changes | `.prompt.md` with no review date |
| An instrument better than the decision requires is waste | A per-item model call for a question answered once a quarter |
| A gate's authority is a property of where it runs, not of what it checks | The same check advisory at the pull-request tier and authoritative in the nightly run |
| A grader tests the shape it was written to test | A regex-graded behavioural check that passes on well-formed wrong output |

The evaluator types, required parameters, preview statuses and judge model families are **perishable** and are marked at point 11. The design sequence, the decision-first principle, measurement effects, instrument validation, the honest-form limitation statement and the prompt-file-as-instrument claim are durable and are what the specification assesses. **The two rows added on 2026-07-30 — the three-parameter account of a judged instrument, and the scale as one of its parameters — are durable and are not assessed.** They change what a competent validation record contains and they do not change the checked elements, which are unaltered.

**Two instruments are supplied as specimens, both from a published configuration corpus and both marked perishable.** The first is a behavioural check graded by pattern match, whose author documents it as testing **output shape and not correctness** — an instrument that states its own validation criterion honestly, and whose criterion is far narrower than its name suggests. The second is the tier distinction: the same check runs advisory at one gate and authoritative at another, which means **an instrument's consequences are configured elsewhere than the instrument.** A student who has validated an instrument and never asked which tier it runs in has validated half of it. Neither specimen is assessed, both are pinned to a commit, and the [Agent Configuration Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/13-Agent-Configuration-Standard.md) carries the manifest.

## What this day does not establish

**Demonstrated.** The cost and the latency of a per-item model call; that a deterministic local check runs without a model call at all; that a service-side groundedness check has nothing to judge against when retrieval fails; and — by staff demonstration rather than by the room — that a judge's explanations are redacted by default and that enabling them writes reasoning about the conversation into telemetry. **The last is a measurement effect the room watches and cannot reproduce.**

**Read rather than demonstrated.** The evaluator types and their required parameters; the threshold-at-creation-time requirement; the higher-is-better assumption in ordinal and continuous scoring; the model-deployment requirement for a code-based evaluator that never calls a model; and the preview status of custom evaluators and continuous evaluation. All documentation, read on the day.

**Not established here, and this is the day's own most important admission.** **Whether the module's fatalism risk actually resolves.** Three whitepapers assert that it peaks at [S028](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S028.md) and resolves here, and [WP-029](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-029.md) §5 states plainly that the design cannot demonstrate it: phase 8's question is not a measurement, the instrument is asymmetric, and the better test is deferred by a quarter. A facilitator reading phase 8's enthusiasm as evidence has read weak evidence as strong.

**Not established anywhere.** **What any built-in evaluator agrees with.** No agreement figure is published for any of them, which is why point 7's honest answer is that the student's own held-back set is the only calibration that will ever exist for their instrument. **And no magnitude is carried from the practitioner account at point 10** — what it reports about classification against numerical scoring, and about discrete against continuous scales, it reports in hedges the day keeps deliberately, and no figure is available or asserted.

**Asserted.** That an instrument ships with a validation criterion and a withdrawal condition or it is not finished, and that this applies identically to a laboratory apparatus, a cloud evaluator and a prompt file. **Point 9's transfer — that your prompt files are instruments and none of them is validated — is the day's most transferable claim and it is an argument by analogy from instrument A**, reachable from the artefact per rule 7 and resting on the design's own reasoning rather than on a source.

**What rests on a `research/` note.** Point 7's three-parameter account of a judged instrument, its disclosure rule, its permission to use an instrument that is not as good as human judgement, and its argument that no perfect evaluation method exists, all at [research/03-measurement-evaluation/huyen-ai-engineering.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/huyen-ai-engineering.md) — **a complete, page-anchored reading of the full book, and the note records that it replaced a flagged placeholder.** Direction, mechanism and vocabulary only. Error management, which carries phase 6, has a complete reading at [research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md](/research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md).

**What rests on nothing.** Pretesting with a design demand, cumulative retrieval, contrasting cases with a late-revealed decision context, and worked-example fading have **no note anywhere in this repository.** The late-revealed criterion is reused from [S025](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S025.md) deliberately so that students recognise the move, and the recognition claim is the design's own.

## What to take from this day

**The claim to hold.** **An instrument ships with a validation criterion and a withdrawal condition, or it is not finished** — and a criterion that cannot fail is not one. That holds without modification for a laboratory apparatus, for the cloud evaluator you built this afternoon, and for the prompt file in your repository that has produced acceptable output every time you have run it and has therefore been validated by nobody.

**What the sources license, and what they do not.** Instrument design, measurement effects, validation and drift are verified in this repository at [research/03-measurement-evaluation/husain-your-ai-product-needs-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/husain-your-ai-product-needs-evals.md), and the finding that model-generated evaluators must themselves be validated against human grades is recorded at abstract-verification depth in the same folder's note on Shankar and is cited for **direction and mechanism only.** No page publishes any figure for how well a built-in evaluator agrees with a competent human, so **the only calibration your instrument will ever have is one you produce.**

**Guidance for your own work.**

- Start from the decision. An instrument better than the decision requires is waste.
- Feed a new instrument something you know is bad and confirm that it says so, before anything else.
- Write what it cannot detect in the honest form: what you checked for and could not detect, not everything it misses.
- **A gate's authority belongs to the tier it runs in, not to the check.** The same check can be advisory at one gate and authoritative at another — see the [Agent Configuration Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/13-Agent-Configuration-Standard.md). Validating an instrument without asking where it runs validates half of it.

**Where this is easy to get wrong.** Reading a rubric, agreeing with it, and calling that a review. An evaluator that has never failed has told you about your inputs, not about itself.
