# S012 · Ordering, Searching and the Cost of Comparison

**Module:** [M03](/archive/bsc-programme/modules/M03.md) · **Quarter:** [Q2](/archive/bsc-programme/quarters/Q2.md) · **Complexity class:** 1
**Strands:** CF · MS · SY

**Depends on:** S008, S011
**Re-tests:** S005
**Platform anchor:** Microsoft Foundry embedding deployment as a comparison that costs a round trip — `POST {endpoint}/openai/v1/embeddings`, batched
**MCP servers:** Microsoft Learn MCP Server (grounding) · Azure MCP Server, Read only = true

---

## What this day is for

Sorting and searching are taught as algorithm families with comparison counts attached. The comparison is treated as the unit — one comparison, one unit of cost — and every analysis is denominated in it.

The unit is a fiction, and the day is about what happens when it stops being a useful one. Comparing two integers is nearly free. Comparing two strings under a locale-aware collation is hundreds of times more expensive. Comparing two records by a field that requires a lookup is a different order of magnitude again. Comparing two records by *meaning* requires a call to a model, over a network, against a quota — and at that point the comparison is not the same kind of object at all.

The fourth case is not decoration. It is the comparison students will write for the rest of their careers, it is the one whose cost they will most consistently under-model, and it is the one where the fix — compute the vectors once, compare them many times — is [S011](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S011.md)'s pair applied to a derived structure.

The target: **the algorithm's comparison count and the comparison's cost are separate terms, and the product is what you pay.** Optimising one while ignoring the other is the most common performance error students make and the easiest to demonstrate.

## Phase 1 · Pretest and prediction (25 min)

Four items on the module's dataset.

1. Two sorting approaches are given with their comparison counts. Which is faster?
2. Now the records are sorted by a locale-aware string field. Same question.
3. Now by a field that requires a lookup in a second structure. Same question.
4. Predict: at which item did your answer change, and did it change for the right reason?

The answer reverses between items 1 and 3 for most sensible pairings.

**Copilot prediction, sealed.** *In phase 5 you will ask Copilot to make an expensive comparison cheaper. Write down now: will it attack the count or the cost per comparison, and which of the three terms will it not mention at all?* Opened at phase 8.

**Warrant.** Pretesting through a single varying dimension. Three items differing only in the comparison's cost is contrasting cases inside a pretest, and the reversal is produced by the student. The Copilot item probes a specific frontier property — the agent optimises what the prompt makes salient — and is administered before instruction, per instrumentation rule 8.

## Phase 3 · Contrasting cases and generation (45 min)

**Executed, not read.** Groups receive **one dataset, one sorting task, four comparison functions** of increasing cost, and run all four.

| Case | Comparison function | Where the cost lives |
|---|---|---|
| A | Integer field | Register |
| B | Short string, ordinal | Memory |
| C | Locale-aware collated string | Normalisation and collation tables |
| D | Semantic similarity via a Foundry embedding deployment | A network round trip, a token budget and a quota |

For each, they rank three algorithms and commit. Then they measure.

The ranking is stable across the first two comparison functions and reverses for the last two — the algorithm with more comparisons but better locality wins when comparisons are cheap, and loses when they are expensive.

**Case D is the payload, and it defeats the room twice.** The first defeat is expected by the design and not by the students: at case D the comparison count stops mattering almost entirely, because one round trip costs more than every comparison in cases A to C put together, and the correct move is not a better algorithm but *not comparing this way at all* — precompute the vectors, then compare in memory.

The second defeat is the one groups walk into. Having discovered batching, they batch harder, and the request fails with **HTTP 400** rather than succeeding slowly. Microsoft's own guidance states it plainly: an embeddings request returns 400 when the **sum** of input tokens exceeds 300,000, *even if every individual input is under the 8,192-token per-input limit*, and the array itself may hold at most 2,048 inputs. A group that has correctly optimised the count has hit a boundary that is a property of the batch and of no item in it. That is the day's whole thesis arriving as an error code.

Task after: **find the comparison cost at which the ranking flips.** Groups estimate; the measured crossover is then supplied. A second crossover is asked for at case D: **at how many comparisons does precomputing the vectors pay for itself?**

Then, of every group: **what did you not know that you could have looked up, and what would it have cost to look it up?** A group that consulted the Learn MCP Server before batching answers this best and is credited for it. The question exists because the alternative design rewards a group for walking into a documented limit, which points the incentive the wrong way in a programme whose claim about agents is that you ground before you act.

**Warrant.** Contrasting cases along a single controlled dimension with a measured adjudicator. The crossover-location demand is the day's generation step and is the first time students are asked for a *threshold* rather than a ranking. Case D makes the dimension continue past the point where the algorithm is the answer, which is the condition under which the three-term model becomes necessary rather than merely correct.

## Phase 4 · Consolidating instruction (50 min)

1. **Total cost is comparison count times comparison cost, plus movement.** The retained sentence, and the third term is the one students omit.
2. **What the model omits.** The three terms are not everything. Branch prediction, allocation and collection behaviour, instruction-level parallelism and contention with other work all contribute and sometimes dominate. They are excluded because they are not separately measurable with the instrumentation you have and are mostly consequences of your decisions rather than decisions themselves. **Safe** for reasoning about a choice you control; **unsafe** as an account of why a program is slow.
3. **What a comparison actually costs**, by kind: primitive, string, collated string, structural, lookup-requiring, remote-model. Orders of magnitude given, marked perishable.
4. **Why comparison-count analysis exists and when it is right** — when comparison cost is uniform and dominant. Named honestly.
5. **Movement cost and locality**, introduced here and explained at S016.
6. **Search: the same decomposition.** Probes times probe cost. A structure with fewer probes each requiring a lookup can lose to one with more probes in contiguous memory.
7. **The crossover as a design object.** Where the answer changes is more useful than which answer is right, because the crossover is what you monitor.
8. **Precomputation as buying comparisons in advance** — sort keys, normalised forms, indices, and embedding vectors — and what each costs to maintain. This is S011's pair applied to a derived structure, and case D is the version of it students will actually ship.
9. **The platform's limits are the third term made visible, and they are perishable.** For the embeddings endpoint today: 8,192 tokens per input, at most 2,048 inputs in one array, and a 300,000-token aggregate per request above which the response is 400 rather than a slower 200. **Safe** to reason with against this endpoint at this API version; **unsafe** at any other version, against any other model family, and as a guide to any other service's batching behaviour. Three numbers that will change; one shape — *the batch has a cost of its own* — that will not.
10. **What the platform chose for you.** `encoding_format` defaults to `float`, so every vector crosses the wire as decimal text unless you ask for `base64`; that is a transport decision made for you, and you can measure it this afternoon. The Azure MCP Server's retry policy defaults to **exponential** backoff with **3** retries, a **2**-second base, a **10**-second cap and a **100**-second network timeout — which is to say that somebody has already decided how long your caller waits before your caller finds out, and it was not you. *Defaults are opinions and thresholds are policy.*

**Warrant.** Consolidation indexed to phase 3's crossover. Point 7 is the day's transferable idea and reappears at S019, S058 and S062. Point 2 is the scope statement the model requires of itself, added because a day that attacks comparison-count analysis for having an unstated scope must state its own. Points 9 and 10 are the day's platform layer and are re-verified before every offering.

## Phase 5 · Whole-task learning task (90 min)

**The output is a build.** Students take a real substrate ordering problem — records ranked for presentation to users, where the ranking criterion is semantic and the comparison therefore costs a call — and ship a working ranker to a disposable environment, directed through Copilot agent mode.

1. **Decompose the current cost** into count, comparison and movement. Measure each separately.
2. **Predict which term dominates.** Commit before measuring.
3. **Propose two improvements**: one that reduces the count, one that reduces the cost per comparison.
4. **Predict the effect of each,** in writing, with a number.
5. **Build and measure both.** Direct the agent; do not accept its first decomposition. Ground every claim it makes about the endpoint's limits with the Learn MCP Server rather than with its own memory.
6. **State the crossover:** at what data size or comparison cost would your preferred improvement stop being preferred?

Step 6 is the day's payload and is the first time a student is asked to state the conditions under which their own recommendation expires.

**Artefact elements.** As at S011, and assessed.

- **Prompt record** — the instructions given, in order, including abandoned ones.
- **Intervention record** — every correction and what it was. **Empty fails**, unless the build demonstrates that an intervention-worthy error was detectable: here the cheap demonstration is a batch sized deliberately past the aggregate token limit, whose 400 the ranker must surface rather than swallow.
- **Frontier statement** — one thing the agent did faster than you could have, one thing it did badly that you caught.

**Faded guidance:** step 1's decomposition is worked with the room for one term; the endpoint, deployment name and credential are supplied.

**Warrant.** Whole-task at class 1. Step 3's paired improvements force engagement with both terms rather than with whichever is more familiar. Step 6 generalises the answer into a condition. The intervention record's demonstration route is specified concretely because a generic "could have detected an error" clause is unassessable.

## Phase 6 · Error clinic (40 min)

Instructor first: a substrate optimisation that reduced comparison count, was measured as an improvement on the development dataset, and was slower in production — with attention to which term changed between the two datasets.

**Then the agent case.** Asked to "make this ranking faster", Copilot produced a correct, well-structured, well-named change that reduced the comparison count and left the per-comparison call in place, then reported a speed-up measured on a fixture whose records were short enough that the call dominated nothing. The change shipped. The reviewer read the diff, agreed the algorithm was better, and did not ask what a comparison cost, because **the prompt had made the count salient and the review inherited the prompt's frame.** Attention on that inheritance: an agent optimises what you made visible, and a reviewer reading the agent's output is reading a document already shaped by your omission.

Then students: an optimisation you made that did not help, and which term you were reasoning about.

**Warrant.** Error management with a disclosure that separates the two terms in a case where the reasoning was correct and the model was incomplete. The agent case is the required sixth element and is chosen so that the failure is in the *prompt* rather than in the generation, which is the failure students are least likely to attribute correctly.

---

## Phase 7 · Peer articulation (30 min)

Pairs. Each student states their step 6 crossover, and the partner's task is to **describe the day on which it will be crossed** — from the substrate's growth data, its user base, or a plausible product change.

If the crossover will never be reached, the pair says so, and the student's recommendation is stronger for it.

**Warrant.** Interactive-tier articulation with a realism demand, parallel to S011 phase 7 and deliberately so. A crossover that cannot be reached is a good answer and students expect it to be a bad one.

## Phase 8 · Calibration and commitment (20 min)

- Item 4 resolved: where answers changed against where they should have.
- Step 4's numeric predictions against measured effects, shown as a distribution of ratios rather than of errors.
- **The Copilot prediction opened.** How many of the room predicted the agent would attack the count rather than the cost, and which term it in fact never mentioned. Recorded as this day's frontier-map entry.
- Each student names one comparison in their own current work whose cost they have never considered.
- Retrieval schedule: next morning, day 3, week 2, week 6.

## When it does not work

These are [WP-012](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-012.md) §5's predicted failure modes, restated as instruction to the person in the room. The whitepaper is the authority; where this section and §5 diverge, §5 is right.

**A student produces strong step 3 improvements and a vague step 6.** They hold the three-term model already, informally, from systems or performance work, and they predicted phase 3's reversals. The gap is not knowledge: practitioners rarely state expiry conditions, so experience does not supply the habit. Step 6 is the specification's weighted element, so the assessment already bites. Brief their phase 7 partner to demand a *date* rather than a condition, which is harder and which their experience does not answer.

**A student's step 1 decomposition does not isolate the three terms.** They have taken exactly one measurement in their life and are now designing three. Check step 1 outputs at the midpoint of the phase rather than at its end. *Residual risk the design accepts:* if a bad decomposition survives to step 2, everything after it rests on the bad decomposition, and §5 concedes that this check is an intervention the design relies on and cannot guarantee at scale.

**A group predicts everything, including the crossover.** The first two comparison functions are expected to be predicted correctly by most groups, so this is a group that has beaten the reversal as well. Ask them to construct a comparison function for which no reversal occurs at any cost, and to say why. The answer requires reasoning about the movement term.

**The room concludes that theory is useless.** This is the day's characteristic affective failure and it is compounded rather than caused here: two consecutive days in which the analytical answer loses to the measured one is a pattern students will generalise, and the generalisation is wrong. Three correctives, in order of strength. Deliver the honest scope statement in phase 4 — comparison-count analysis is right when comparison cost is uniform and dominant, and that is said in the model's favour. Point at phase 3, where the crossover was located *analytically* before it was measured, which is analysis doing work measurement cannot. Then say explicitly that the crossover cannot be found by measurement alone, because measurement gives points and a crossover is a condition. *Residual risk the design accepts:* §5 records that this last and strongest corrective is stated once, and that once is probably not enough.

## Assessment

Specification item in CF102. The checked elements are: the three-term decomposition with separate measurements; the committed dominance prediction; two paired improvements with numeric predictions; measured effects from the built ranker; the stated crossover condition; **the prompt record**; **the intervention record**; and **the frontier statement**.

An improvement proposed without a numeric prediction fails the specification. An intervention record with no entries fails unless the build surfaces the aggregate-limit error rather than swallowing it.

**Nothing else is assessed.** The agent's output quality is not assessed, the phase 1 Copilot prediction is not assessed, and the case D crossover estimate is instruction rather than evidence.

## What to take from this day

**The claim to hold.** **Total cost is comparison count times comparison cost, plus movement**, and the product is what you pay. Optimising one term while ignoring the other is the commonest performance error there is. The **crossover** — the point at which the answer changes — is the design object, because the crossover is the thing you can monitor.

**What the sources license, and what they do not.** The cost-model decomposition, the physical basis of the movement term deferred to [S016](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S016.md), and the treatment of measured constants as perishable are verified here, at [research/02-technical-foundations/pope-scaling-transformer-inference.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/pope-scaling-transformer-inference.md) and [research/02-technical-foundations/subramanya-diskann.md](/research/02-technical-foundations/subramanya-diskann.md). Contrasting cases along a single controlled dimension, prediction before adjudication and worked-example fading are cited for **direction and mechanism only**, with no effect size asserted. The batching limits are one vendor's, at one API version, and nothing about any other service's batching follows from them.

**Guidance for your own work.**

- **Measure the three terms separately** before proposing anything. A single number cannot tell you which one to attack.
- **Attach a number to every proposed improvement, in advance.** A proposal with no prediction cannot be wrong, which is what makes it worthless.
- **Ground the limit rather than remembering it.** A limit you looked up costs a minute; the one you assumed costs a rebuild.
- **The batch has a cost of its own, and so does the loop that calls it.** Pin the version, host and model against which you observed any of these numbers, as the [Agent Configuration Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/13-Agent-Configuration-Standard.md) requires — a figure observed against an unpinned object is an anecdote about an unknown thing.

**Where this is easy to get wrong.** Letting the prompt set the frame. An agent optimises what you made salient, and a reviewer reading its output is reading a document already shaped by your omission.
