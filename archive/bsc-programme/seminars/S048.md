# S048 · The Judge Is Biased: Measuring the Measurer

**Module:** [M10](/archive/bsc-programme/modules/M10.md) · **Quarter:** [Q5](/archive/bsc-programme/quarters/Q5.md) · **Complexity class:** 4
**Strands:** ME · IS · SG
**Whitepaper:** [WP-048](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-048.md)
**Depends on:** S027, S046
**Re-tests:** S047
**Platform anchor:** Foundry LLM-as-judge evaluators and the judge-model choice · GitHub Copilot reviewing its own output · **MCP servers:** Foundry MCP Server (preview) · Microsoft Learn MCP Server

---

## What this day is for

A criterion says what good means. Somebody or something then decides, case by case, whether a given output is good — and that judge has an error structure of its own.

Human judges drift within a session, anchor on the first case they see, and disagree with themselves on re-presentation. Model judges prefer longer outputs, prefer outputs resembling their own generation style, and are sensitive to position and formatting in ways unrelated to quality. Rubric-guided judges of both kinds trade one bias for another.

The target: **the judge is an instrument with an error structure, S027 applies to it, and the systematic component must be identified and its direction stated** — because a systematic judge error does not average out over more cases, and more cases are exactly what a scaled evaluation supplies.

**This is also the day the programme turns the argument on its own primary tool.** Every build in this programme is produced by an agent and reviewed by a human who is frequently helped by the same agent. **A judge that scored its own work would be dismissed in any other setting**, and it is the ordinary condition of agent-assisted engineering. The [Copilot Practice Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md) names this day as the place that commitment is discharged, and it is discharged by measurement rather than by warning: the student characterises the agent as a judge of its own output, on their own artefact, and reports the systematic component they find.

## Phase 1 · Pretest and prediction (25 min)

Five items. Twenty outputs and their scores from a judge are supplied.

1. Which of these scores do you disagree with?
2. Here are the same twenty outputs, reordered, scored again by the same judge. Which scores moved?
3. What would explain the movement?
4. Predict: would a different judge have the same problem?
5. **Copilot prediction, sealed.** You will shortly ask the agent to *review the evaluator you built at S046 and the check you built at S047, and tell you what is wrong with them.* Write down what it will flag, **what it will not flag, and which of those two lists is longer**. Sealed with items 1 to 4 and opened at phase 8.

Item 2's movement is substantial and is not random with respect to position.

Item 5 is the day's contribution to the running frontier map and the sharpest one the programme takes. The agent wrote most of both artefacts. The design's expectation is that it will produce a fluent, correct, useful review of the things it can see — schema, coverage, edge cases — and will not raise the defect it introduced, because the reasoning that produced the defect is the reasoning being asked to find it.

**Warrant.** Pretesting with a re-presentation reveal. Same judge, same outputs, different order, different scores — which is unarguable and requires no framework to see. Item 5 is administered here and may not migrate into phase 5, per instrumentation rule 8, and its two-list form is deliberate: predicting that a self-review misses things is easy, and predicting the ratio is the measure.

## Phase 3 · Contrasting cases and generation (45 min)

**Executed, not read.** Groups take **the same fifty outputs** and have them scored by **four judges**, three of which they run themselves.

- **Judge A** — a human, unguided. Supplied.
- **Judge B** — a human, following the S046 criterion as a rubric. Supplied.
- **Judge C** — a model, unguided: a built-in Foundry quality evaluator with a judge model the group names in `deployment_name`.
- **Judge D** — **the model that produced the outputs, judging them.** Same family, same deployment, now scoring its own generation against the group's S046 rubric.

The four disagree, and their disagreements are structured: each pair disagrees on a different subset, in a consistent direction.

Groups characterise each judge's systematic component and commit.

Then **the outputs' known properties are supplied** — length, format, position in the batch, and whether each output was produced by the judge-D model or by a different one — and the correlations are shown.

Task after: **state which judge you would use, for what, and what you would correct for.**

**Judge D is the payload and the room's expectation about it is wrong in a specific way.** Students predict that a model marking its own work will be generous. What the correlations show is narrower: the effect is concentrated on the outputs the judge's own family produced, and it survives the rubric — Judge B and Judge D are following the same written criterion and disagree on a subset that is identifiable from provenance and not from quality. Microsoft's own AI playbook names the mechanism among the known reliability problems with model judges, alongside positional and verbosity bias, and states plainly that **evaluation by language model has not yet been systematically studied.** The playbook also records, citing external work, that reference-free metrics carry a bias toward their underlying models' outputs **and a bias against higher-quality text** — which is the ranking-inversion case phase 7 asks students to construct, named in the documentation before anyone in the room has built one.

**No magnitude is asserted for any of these effects, here or anywhere in this day.** The direction is what the documentation supports; the magnitude is what the group measures on its own fifty outputs, and it is theirs alone.

**Warrant.** Contrasting cases across judges with a fixed output set and a property-correlation adjudicator, now run rather than supplied. The four-way structured disagreement is the phase's payload: no judge is neutral and the biases are not the same bias. Judge D discharges the [Copilot Practice Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md)'s commitment that this day treat the agent as a reviewer of its own output, and it is placed in phase 3 rather than phase 4 so that the effect is measured by the room before it is named by anyone.

## Phase 4 · Consolidating instruction (50 min)

1. **A judge is an instrument.** S026's construct-instrument distinction and S027's error taxonomy both apply, unchanged. **And the instrument is not the model.** A practitioner account of evaluation for foundation-model systems states it exactly: *an AI judge is not just a model — it's a system that includes both a model and a prompt*, and *altering the model, the prompt, or the model's sampling parameters results in a different judge.* **Three parameters, not one.** Two consequences follow and both are load-bearing today. A characterisation is a characterisation of **all three together**, so a column of scores taken before a prompt edit and a column taken after are columns from two instruments, however identical the name on the report; and the rule the same source gives for reading somebody else's score is a **disclosure** rule rather than a calibration one — **do not trust any AI judge if you can't see the model and the prompt used for the judge.** That is the only rule available while point 8's silence holds, and it is the one to ask of every judged number anyone shows you. **Point 9's `deployment_name` is one of the three, and it is the only one the platform makes you name.** See [research/03-measurement-evaluation/huyen-ai-engineering.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/huyen-ai-engineering.md); direction, mechanism and vocabulary only.
2. **Systematic judge error does not average out,** and a scaled evaluation supplies more cases rather than more judges — which increases confidence in a biased estimate. S027 point 3, at the point where it costs most.
3. **Known human judge effects:** anchoring on early cases, within-session drift, self-disagreement on re-presentation, and sensitivity to order. Directions stated where the repository supports them.
4. **Known model judge effects:** length preference, self-style preference, position sensitivity, and sensitivity to formatting. **This list is not exhaustive and the field is moving; it omits effects specific to particular model families and effects that appear only above certain scales. Safe as a checklist of properties to correlate against when characterising a judge you have; unsafe as a description of any specific judge you have not measured — the day's whole position is that you measure your own.** **The shape of the scale belongs on the list too, and it is the one students never think to correlate against because they chose it.** The account cited at point 1 reports — in hedges that must be kept, *it's been reported* and *seems to* — that judges do better at classification than at numerical scoring, that discrete scoring appears to do better than continuous, and that wider discrete ranges appear to do worse. **No magnitude is asserted for any of that, here or anywhere in this day**, and the operative point is structural rather than numerical: the scale is one of the judge's parameters, not a neutral container for its output, and step 2's off-list property may be it.
5. **Rubrics trade biases,** they do not remove them. A rubric constrains attention, which reduces some effects and introduces rubric-interpretation variance.
6. **Characterising a judge:** score the same set twice reordered, correlate scores against output properties, and compare against a held-back reference.
7. **What to do:** correct, restrict the judge's use, or use a different judge for that subset — each with a cost.
8. **Agreement is not accuracy.** Two judges agreeing is evidence they share a bias as readily as evidence they are right. And **agreement with a human is not published for any evaluator in this stack.** A neighbouring product on the same documentation estate names the statistic it tracks — Cohen's Kappa — and publishes no value for it. Foundry names neither the statistic nor a value. **Every judged score any of you will produce this quarter is therefore uncalibrated, and the sentence reporting one must say so.** This is not a complaint. It is the condition under which the work is done, and stating it is the whole of the remedy available. **Uncalibrated is not unusable, and the two have to be held apart.** The account cited at point 1 declines to make agreement with humans a precondition of use — *even when AI judgments aren't as good as human judgments, they might still be good enough to guide an application's development* — and supplies, in place of a calibration, the guardrails it thinks the method actually needs: see the model and the prompt, fix the judge's sampling temperature so a repeat run is a repeat, never carry a score across judges or across tools that ship the same criterion name, supplement with an exact check or a human, and characterise the evaluation itself as an instrument. **That is a permission to act on a score and not a licence to report it as calibrated**, and the sentence you write still has to say which of the two you are doing.
9. **Defaults are opinions, and one of them decides who the judge is.** Four were chosen for you today. A judge model is a parameter you must supply, and the documentation **ranks candidate models by judge quality** — recommended, acceptable, not recommended — warning that one produces *noticeably less reliable* scores, with **no reliability figure attached to any row and two ranks missing from the published table.** A rubric dimension is scored only when the judge decides it applies, unless someone marked it `always_applicable`, so the judge has a degree of freedom nobody's report mentions. Continuous evaluation **redacts the judge's stated reason by default**, and you get it back only by setting a redaction flag off — so the platform's default posture is that a score travels and its reasoning does not. And a reasoning-model judge requires a flag of its own to be treated as one. **An engineer who cannot state what was chosen for them does not understand their own system.**
10. **What is perishable here and what is not.** Every model name in that ranking, every measured effect, the redaction default and the preview status of half this tooling will move within the year, and the model-judge effects at point 4 are the fastest-moving content in the programme. **The durable claims are that a judge is an instrument, that a systematic component does not average out, that a rubric trades biases rather than removing them, that shift is tolerable and inversion is not, and that a score whose agreement with human judgement is unpublished is uncalibrated whoever publishes it.** If agreement data is published next year, the last claim's instance changes and the claim does not.

**Warrant.** Consolidation indexed to phase 3. Point 4 carries the strong-form scope statement, which is unusually important because the field moves faster than the material. Point 8 is the day's most-needed correction and is now reachable from phase 3's judge-D correlations rather than asserted, per instrumentation rule 7. Points 9 and 10 are the [Copilot Practice Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md)'s required platform content — a default the platform chose, and a perishable point marked as perishable. **The published judge ranking is the day's cleanest documentation silence: a total order over judges with no measurement behind it is a preference presented as a finding, which is the exact object this module exists to detect.**

## Phase 5 · Whole-task learning task (90 min)

**Class 4. The judge and its characterisation are the student's, and this phase produces a build.**

Students use Copilot in agent mode, through the Foundry MCP Server, to **ship a working judge characterisation** for their own S046 criterion. They:

1. **Choose a judge** for it, and state why — naming the model deployment if it is a model, and stating what the documentation's ranking did and did not tell you.
2. **Design the characterisation:** what you will score twice, what properties you will correlate against, what reference you will hold back. **At least one correlated property must not appear on point 4's list and must be drawn from your own outputs.**
3. **Predict the systematic component** and its direction, before running it.
4. **Run it.**
5. **State the systematic component found,** with its direction and rough magnitude.
6. **Decide:** correct, restrict, or replace — and state the cost.
7. **State what your characterisation could not have detected.**
8. **Characterise the agent as a judge of its own work.** Ask Copilot to review the artefact it helped you build, record what it flagged and what it did not, and state the systematic component of *that* judge in the same terms as steps 5 and 7.

**The artefact carries three further elements**, per the [Copilot Practice Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md).

- **Prompt record.** The instructions you gave the agent, in order, including the ones you abandoned.
- **Intervention record.** Every point at which you corrected it, and what the correction was. **An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that the design could have detected an intervention-worthy error — a held-back reference chosen so that a judge agreeing with the student would still be caught is the obvious example and is accepted.
- **Frontier statement.** One thing the agent did faster than you could have, and one thing it did badly that you had to catch. Both specific, both from this build. **Step 8's finding may not be reused as the frontier statement**; they are different measurements and collapsing them loses the one the programme is short of.

**Fading:** step 2's design is worked with the room; step 8's protocol is supplied. **Step 3 is unsupported.**

**Warrant.** Whole-task at class 4, now shipping rather than describing. Step 2's off-list property is [WP-048](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-048.md) §8's objection-1 repair: a student correlating against exactly the four properties on the list has satisfied the step while assuming the list is complete. Step 3's pre-commitment is the module's standing discipline, and step 5's requirement that a systematic component be *found* — not merely looked for — is defended in the whitepaper. **Step 8 is the day's contribution to the programme's central claim about its own tool**, and it is a step rather than a reflection because a self-review characterised in the same vocabulary as any other judge is evidence, and one described in prose is an impression.

## Phase 6 · Error clinic (40 min)

Instructor first: a substrate evaluation where two judges agreed closely and were both wrong in the same direction, and how long it took to discover — with attention to **the fact that the agreement was reported as a reliability figure**.

Then the agent case, and it is the one from phase 1 item 5. A student asked Copilot to review a prompt-based evaluator it had written the day before. The review was substantial and useful: it caught an unhandled empty-context case, tightened two dimension descriptions, and flagged a field-mapping risk. **It did not mention that the evaluator's scoring instruction rewarded a response for restating the question, which the agent had written and which had already inflated the pass rate by a visible margin on the substrate's own data.** Attention goes to why review did not catch it: the human reviewer read a review that had found real problems, concluded the artefact had been reviewed, and stopped — **a review that finds something is more effective at ending scrutiny than one that finds nothing.**

Then students: a time you trusted a judgement because two people made it, or a place today where you accepted agent output you had not checked.

**Warrant.** Error management targeting point 8. High inter-rater agreement reported as evidence of quality is the day's most consequential misreading and appears in published work routinely. The agent case is the [Copilot Practice Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md)'s required addition and is chosen because the review was genuinely good, which is the condition under which self-review is actually dangerous.

## Phase 7 · Peer articulation (30 min)

Pairs. Each student presents **their step 5 systematic component**, and the partner's task is to construct an output set on which that component would produce a *wrong ranking* between two genuinely different-quality outputs.

Then: would the student's step 6 decision handle it?

Then, briefly: **the partner reads the agent's step 8 review against the artefact it reviewed, and adds one item to the missed list.** The student may not remove it.

Per rule 3, the outcome is recorded in the artefact. Per rule 5, this reuses the [S029](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S029.md) dual-defeat structure.

**Warrant.** Interactive-tier articulation with a ranking-inversion demand. A bias that shifts all scores equally is harmless; one that reorders is not, and the distinction is what makes step 6's decision necessary. **The third demand is [WP-048](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-048.md) §8's objection-3 fix**: step 8's missed list was otherwise a self-report about a self-review, made by the person with the most reason to shape it, with an external observer sitting in the next phase — which is instrumentation rule 1 violated in a day about judges.

## When it does not work

The predicted failures and their countermeasures are argued in [WP-048](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-048.md) §5, which the release rule places after the day rather than before it. Restated here as instruction to the person in the room. Nothing below is assessed.

| What you see | What to do | What the design accepts |
|---|---|---|
| A student who has read about model-judge biases predicts length preference at step 3, finds it, and has **designed a characterisation sensitive only to it** | Two things catch this and both are already in the day. Step 2's held-back reference is sensitive to effects nobody predicted, and **step 2's off-list correlated property forbids the checklist being the whole design.** Then phase 7's partner constructs an inversion, which need not use the bias they found | A checklist satisfied completely is one that was assumed complete. The design says so at point 4 and then requires a fifth property |
| A student with no measurement background stalls at step 2's design | It is the demanding step and it is worked with the room. Three named components, a supplied output set to practise on at phase 3, and a criterion three days familiar | Residual risk: their step 3 prediction may be a guess, **and the specification permits that** — the prediction's function is to precede the result, not to be right |
| Judges A and C are characterised quickly once the property correlations appear | Expected. **The difficulty is the rubric-guided pair**, where the traded biases are less obvious and where judge B and judge D are following the same written criterion and disagreeing anyway | If a group characterises all four, ask which judge they would use to compare two *systems* rather than to score outputs. That is a different question, and one where a consistent bias matters less than an inconsistent one |
| The room concludes that evaluation by judgement is unsalvageable and only automatic metrics can be trusted | This is the day's characteristic affective failure and it is **precisely backwards given what [S026](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S026.md) established about metrics.** Three counters: point 7's three responses are constructive and the day requires one to be chosen; the shift-versus-inversion distinction establishes that most bias is tolerable; and then say once, plainly, that **a characterised biased judge is more trustworthy than an uncharacterised metric** | That framing is the day's strongest available and it is stated once. Said twice it becomes reassurance and stops being an argument |
| A student's step 8 record has two identical lists | Ask which of the two happened: either the agent flagged everything it missed, which is checkable against the artefact, or the review was not characterised. **The specification fails the identical case on those terms**, and the partner's phase 7 addition is a checked element that may not be removed | A self-review that finds real problems ends scrutiny more effectively than one that finds nothing. That is the mechanism, not a lapse of attention |

## Assessment

Specification item in ME201 and IS201 jointly. Per rule 1, the checked elements are: a chosen judge with a reason **and its model deployment named where it has one**; a characterisation design with all three components **and one off-list correlated property**; a pre-committed prediction; the characterisation run; a systematic component with direction and magnitude; a correct-restrict-replace decision with its cost; a statement of what could not be detected; **the agent-as-self-reviewer characterisation from step 8**; the phase 7 outcome; **the prompt record**; **the intervention record**; **the frontier statement**. **Nothing else is assessed.**

**A characterisation reporting no systematic component fails the specification** unless the artefact demonstrates that the design could have detected one — the same structure as [S041](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S041.md)'s leak audit, and stated the same way.

**A step 8 record whose two lists are identical fails**, on the same terms: either the agent flagged everything it missed, which is checkable, or the review was not characterised. **The partner's phase 7 addition to the missed list is a checked element and may not be removed.**

**An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that the design could have detected an intervention-worthy error.

## Perishable content in this day

| Durable claim | Platform instance, perishable |
|---|---|
| A judge is an instrument with an error structure | The judge model named in `deployment_name` |
| A judge is a model, a prompt and a sampling configuration, and changing any of the three makes a different judge | `deployment_name`, which the platform makes you name, and the prompt and sampling settings, which it does not |
| A criterion name does not travel between judges | One rubric dimension carried under one name across two tools with two scales |
| A systematic component does not average out | Scaled evaluation adds cases, not judges |
| A rubric trades biases rather than removing them | Judge B and Judge D following one rubric and disagreeing by provenance |
| A ranking with no measurement behind it is a preference | The published recommended / acceptable / not-recommended judge table |
| A score whose agreement with human judgement is unpublished is uncalibrated | No Foundry evaluator publishes it; a neighbouring product names the statistic and not the value |
| A score and its reasoning are different artefacts | The judge's `reason` redacted by default in continuous evaluation |
| Two reviewers are comparable only if severity is normalised before they report | A published severity taxonomy and its risk-scaled depth tiers |

The specific model judges, their measured effects, the published ranking, all magnitudes and every preview marking are **perishable** and are marked at point 10 explicitly. Judge-as-instrument, the characterisation method, rubrics-trade-biases, shift-versus-inversion, agreement-is-not-accuracy and the uncalibrated-score rule are durable and are what the specification assesses. **The two rows added on 2026-07-30 — that a judge is a model, a prompt and a sampling configuration, and that a criterion name does not travel between judges — are durable and are not assessed.** They extend point 1 rather than replacing it, and **the checked elements are unaltered**; step 1's requirement to name the model deployment stands as written, and naming the other two parameters is good practice this day argues for and does not check.

**One human specimen is carried alongside the model judges, and it is the day's cheapest generalisation.** A published review system decomposes reviewing into factual orientation, a fixed set of lenses, a normalised severity taxonomy and a depth tier selected from assessed risk. **Every one of those is a bias-management device for human raters**, and the room recognises them because it has just built the same devices for a model judge. The point is not that the two are the same instrument; it is that **the rubric-trades-biases finding does not become false when the rater is a person**, and that a review process without normalised severity is producing scores whose disagreement cannot be attributed. Perishable in full: the taxonomy's own categories and tier names. See the [Agent Configuration Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/13-Agent-Configuration-Standard.md).

| **A judge is an instrument** | [S026](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S026.md)'s construct-instrument distinction and [S027](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S027.md)'s error taxonomy apply to it unchanged, which is the day's central move |
| **The three parameters** | A judge is **a model, a prompt and a sampling configuration**, and changing any of the three makes a different judge. Two consequences: a characterisation characterises all three together, and the rule for reading somebody else's score is a disclosure rule — do not trust a judge whose model and prompt you cannot see |
| **The systematic component** | The part of a judge's error that **does not average out.** A scaled evaluation supplies more cases, not more judges, so scale increases confidence in a biased estimate |
| **Shift against inversion** | A bias that moves all scores equally is tolerable. One that **reorders two genuinely different-quality outputs** is not, and the distinction is what makes step 6's decision necessary |
| **Agreement is not accuracy** | Two judges agreeing is evidence they share a bias as readily as evidence they are right. **And agreement with a human is published for no evaluator in this stack**, which is why every judged score this quarter is uncalibrated |
| **Uncalibrated, against unusable** | Held apart deliberately. An uncalibrated judgement may still be good enough to guide development; **that is permission to act on a score and not a licence to report it as calibrated**, and the sentence written still has to say which |
| **A rubric trades biases** | It does not remove them. Constraining attention reduces some effects and introduces rubric-interpretation variance, which is why judge B and judge D disagree while following one criterion |
| **The off-list property** | Step 2's requirement that at least one correlated property not appear on point 4's list and be drawn from the student's own outputs. [WP-048](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-048.md) §8's objection-1 repair, because **a student correlating against exactly four listed properties has assumed the list is complete** |
| **The held-back reference** | The third component of a characterisation, and the one sensitive to effects nobody predicted |
| **Instrumentation rule 1** | *Do not use self-report where an external observer is already present.* It is why phase 7's partner adds an item to the agent's missed list, and why the student may not remove it |
| **Instrumentation rule 8** | *An instrument administered after its answer has been taught measures recall.* It is why item 5 is sealed at phase 1 and does not migrate into phase 5 |
| **Prompt record · intervention record · frontier statement** | The three build elements every phase 5 carries. **Step 8's finding may not be reused as the frontier statement.** An empty intervention record fails unless the artefact shows the design could have detected an intervention-worthy error — here, a held-back reference chosen so a judge agreeing with the student would still be caught |
| **The five classes** | Configured control, executable check, observed result, outcome evidence, unsupported inference — [Agent Configuration Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/13-Agent-Configuration-Standard.md). **A rubric is the first**: it establishes that a constraint was stated, and nothing about whether it was applied |

## What this day does not establish

The day demonstrates three things. That one judge scoring one set of twenty outputs twice, reordered, moved scores in a way that correlated with position. That four judges over one shared set disagreed in structured rather than random ways, and that the disagreements between two of them tracked which model produced each output rather than how good it was. And that an agent's review of its own artefact produced a flagged list and a missed list that were not the same list. **All three are observations about one output set, one cohort and one afternoon, and every magnitude in the day is the group's own measurement on its own outputs.** None of them licenses anything about any judge nobody in the room measured, which is the day's whole position stated as a limit.

**The day's newer half is its better-founded half and the split is worth knowing.** The three-parameter account of a judge, the disclosure rule that replaces the calibration nobody publishes, the direction on scoring-system shape and the bound that an uncalibrated score may still be actionable all rest on [research/03-measurement-evaluation/huyen-ai-engineering.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/huyen-ai-engineering.md), **read in full.** Judge bias in human and model raters, the biases a rubric trades rather than removes, and the gap between agreement and accuracy rest on [research/03-measurement-evaluation/zheng-mt-bench-chatbot-arena.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/zheng-mt-bench-chatbot-arena.md), **read from the abstract and structure only.** Direction and mechanism throughout; **no magnitude for any judge effect is asserted here.**

**The hedges in the source are kept in the teaching and must stay kept.** The direction on scoring-system shape — classification against numerical scoring, discrete against continuous, narrower against wider ranges — is reported there as *it's been reported* and *seems to*, and it is repeated in those words rather than firmed up.

The vendor's own list of model-judge failure classes supports **direction and mechanism only, because it says the area has not been systematically studied.** Point 4's list is not exhaustive and its own scope statement says so: it omits effects specific to particular model families and effects that appear only above certain scales.

Three platform silences, none of them a criticism. **The published judge ranking is a total order over judges with no measurement behind it** — no reliability figure on any row, and ranks missing from the table — which is a preference presented as a finding and is the exact object this module exists to detect. **No agreement with human judgement is published for any evaluator here**, and a neighbouring product on the same documentation estate names the statistic it tracks and publishes no value for it. And the judge's stated reason is redacted by default in continuous evaluation, so the platform's default posture is that a score travels and its reasoning does not.

The human review specimen carried alongside the model judges establishes that the rubric-trades-biases finding does not become false when the rater is a person. **It establishes nothing about how well either kind of rater performs**, and its own taxonomy and tier names are perishable in full.

The phase warrants lean on retrieval practice, contrasting cases and the ICAP interactive tier, **none of which has a note under [research/](/research/99-source-register/source-register.md)**. Error management at phase 6 does have one, at [keith-frese](/research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md), for direction and mechanism only.

## What to take from this day

**The claim to hold.** A judge is an instrument with an error structure, and its systematic component does not average out — a scaled evaluation supplies more cases, not more judges. **A score whose agreement with human judgement is unpublished is uncalibrated**, whoever published it.

**What the sources license, and what they do not.** Judge bias in human and model raters, the biases a rubric trades rather than removes, and the gap between agreement and accuracy are verified at [research/03-measurement-evaluation/zheng-mt-bench-chatbot-arena.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/zheng-mt-bench-chatbot-arena.md). **Added 2026-07-30:** the three-parameter account of a judge, the disclosure rule that replaces the calibration nobody publishes, the direction on scoring-system shape and the bound that an uncalibrated score may still be actionable are verified at [research/03-measurement-evaluation/huyen-ai-engineering.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/huyen-ai-engineering.md), read in full on that date. The vendor's own list of model-judge failure classes supports **direction and mechanism only** — it says the area has not been systematically studied. **No magnitude for any judge effect is asserted here**, and every number you meet is one your group measured.

**Guidance for your own work.**

- **Score the same set twice, reordered,** and correlate the scores against properties unrelated to quality.
- **Include one correlated property nobody put on a list.** A checklist you satisfy completely is one you assumed was complete.
- **Read two judges agreeing as evidence of a shared bias** until you have reason to read it otherwise.
- **A rubric is a configured control, not evidence.** It establishes that a constraint was stated, and nothing about whether it was applied — the first row of the [Agent Configuration Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/13-Agent-Configuration-Standard.md)'s ladder.
- **Name the judge's deployment** with every score you report.

**Where this is easy to get wrong.** Asking the agent to review its own work, receiving a genuinely useful review, and stopping. **A review that finds something ends scrutiny more effectively than one that finds nothing.**
