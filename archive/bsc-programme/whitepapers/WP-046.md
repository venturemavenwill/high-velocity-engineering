## In plain terms

**What this paper argues, before the formal version.**

The previous module measured a model. The measurement was against a criterion. The criterion came from somewhere, and nobody asked where.

That question is harder than it sounds because **a criterion presents itself as a fact.** Accuracy looks like a property of outputs. Relevance looks like a property of a match. Quality looks like a property of a thing. Each of those conceals a decision somebody made — this is the property worth valuing, on this population, weighted this way — and the decision served some people at the expense of others. Once it is written down it acquires authority disproportionately fast: it becomes what gets optimised, then what gets reported, and within a year it is the thing nobody questions.

Because there is no correct criterion, the usual check is unavailable. **What is left is traceability.** A criterion should be attributable to people who would recognise it as reflecting something they actually said. That is checkable, it is not a quality judgement, and it catches the failure that matters most — the engineer's own preferences, run through a process until they look like findings.

The hardest party is the one who never asked for the system and was never consulted. They have a real stake, no channel, and often no idea the thing exists. They are also the group whose view is most often dropped and least often noticed as dropped, and giving them a voice by assignment is the most uncomfortable and most useful twenty minutes of the day.

**What this paper concedes, and it is a real concession.** The day's most persuasive quarter-hour is its least warranted. It shows a real evaluation service that cannot represent the unconsulted party, and concludes from that a general claim about instruments — that the evidence a tool can reach determines which people it can represent. **The claim is well founded; the evidence for it here is one preview feature of one product.** A vendor shipping a consultation input next year would change the instance and leave the claim standing, and a student who had learned the lesson as *the tooling excludes affected parties* would then conclude the problem was solved. The day holds no counter-instance — no tool whose inputs *do* reach somebody who never used the system — so the room never watches the claim survive a case where the tooling is better. Assembling one is half a day of staff work, and it has not been done.

## Abstract

This paper defends effecting the programme's second complexity-class transition on a day whose task has no supplied success criterion, and defends failing a criterion that no named party would recognise. The first argument is that class 4 is defined by the student formulating the problem including what would count as an answer, that an evaluation criterion is the purest available instance of such a formulation, and that supplying any standard for judging the construction would collapse the transition. The second argument is that traceability to people is the only check available on a criterion's provenance: correctness is unavailable because criteria are decisions rather than facts, and a criterion nobody would recognise has been constructed by the engineer regardless of how much evidence was gathered. The paper additionally defends including a stakeholder group who did not ask for the system and were not consulted, defends allowing a trade that the disadvantaged party rejects to remain defensible, and defends running the day's four stakeholder positions as four executable criteria on a real evaluation service — where the unconsulted party's position turns out to have no representation in the catalogue and no input channel into the auto-generation path. It concedes that the day's central discipline is unenforceable where the criterion is externally mandated, and that the platform demonstration is a single vendor's tooling from which the day draws a claim about tooling in general.

## 1. The instructional problem this day must solve

M09 measured a model. The measurement was against a criterion, the criterion arrived from somewhere, and at no point did anyone ask where.

That question is harder than it sounds, because a criterion presents as a fact. Accuracy is a property of outputs. Relevance is a property of a match. Quality is a property of a thing. Each of those framings conceals a decision — somebody decided that accuracy was the property to value, on this distribution, weighted this way, and that decision served some parties at the expense of others.

Once written down, a criterion acquires authority disproportionately fast. It becomes the thing optimised, the thing reported, and within a year the thing nobody questions. Phase 6's disclosure is the endpoint: a criterion used for two years whose origin nobody could establish, which had been optimising something the whole time.

The day's second problem is the class transition. **Complexity class 4 is defined by the student formulating the problem including what would count as an answer**, and an evaluation criterion is the cleanest instance available — there is no correct criterion, the constraints are real, and the parties disagree legitimately. Crucially, the design cannot supply a standard for judging the construction without destroying the transition, which is why step 4 is unsupported and why the specification checks provenance rather than quality.

The third problem is what check remains once correctness is unavailable. The answer is **traceability**: a criterion should be attributable to people who would recognise it as reflecting something they said. This is checkable, it is not a quality judgement, and it catches the failure the module most fears — the engineer's own preferences, laundered through a process.

The fourth problem is group D. Every system affects people who did not ask for it and were not consulted, and they are the hardest case: they have a legitimate stake, no channel, and frequently no awareness. Point 4 names them and phase 7 gives them a voice by assignment.

## 3. Theoretical basis for the design

### 3.1 Load profile and element interactivity

**High, and of a kind the module names as dispositional rather than cognitive.** No individual element is difficult. What is demanding is holding four incompatible legitimate positions simultaneously while constructing a single artefact that must serve them unequally and say so.

Management:

- **Four stakeholder records supplied** at phase 3, from staff-conducted sessions, so groups reason from evidence rather than gathering it.
- **Step 3's incompatibility statement worked with the room,** because naming a conflict precisely is harder than noticing one.
- **One live stakeholder window,** bounded at thirty minutes.
- **One line per group** at step 8, which bounds the output sharply.
- **The model, its evaluation and its failure modes are all the student's own** and one day old.

**Step 4 is deliberately unsupported.** This is the class boundary and the position established at [S015](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S015.md) applies: fading is removed from the formulation and retained around it.

### 3.2 Desirable difficulties selected, and those deliberately not

**Selected.**

- *Item 3's dissenting party,* which most students cannot name.
- *Four incompatible legitimate positions,* phase 3.
- *Whose view did your criterion drop,* the phase's closing question.
- *Naming who would and would not recognise it,* step 6 — the day's discriminator.
- *Stating what it fails to capture per group,* step 7.
- *The disadvantaged party's rejection,* phase 7, which may be survivable.

**Deliberately not selected.**

- *A supplied standard for the criterion.* Refused; this is the transition.
- *Multi-criteria decision methods.* Weighting schemes and aggregation rules would supply the judgement the day is asking for.
- *Conducting the stakeholder inquiry.* S037's work; records are supplied and one live window is added.
- *Regulatory and contractual criteria.* Named at point 2's scope statement and excluded.

**Scope, per the [Whitepaper Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md), strong form.** Point 2's four origins omit criteria imposed by regulation or contract, and criteria that exist to make a system look successful. **Safe** for a system whose standard is genuinely open to construction, which is this day's situation. **Unsafe** where the criterion is externally mandated: there the day's method produces an engineer who reconstructs a standard they cannot change, and the correct move is to state what the mandated criterion fails to capture and to whom — a different and simpler act the day does not teach. The second omission is S082's.

**The platform instance, scoped in the strong form.** The day teaches criterion construction using one vendor's evaluation tooling, and the simplification must carry its safe and unsafe conditions because students will otherwise carry it into work this day does not supervise.

*Safe.* Reasoning about who a criterion represents by asking what evidence the tool that produced it could reach; about why a catalogue of general-purpose evaluators cannot cover a party with no channel into the system; about the fact that a scale, a direction and a threshold are three further decisions about the same people; and about the difference between a criterion somebody wrote and a criterion somebody generated. Each of those holds for any evaluation tooling that takes system-authored inputs, and the day's phase 3 makes each observable.

*Unsafe.* Treating the absence of a group-D evaluator as a property of this catalogue rather than of catalogues. **A vendor could ship a stakeholder-consultation input for rubric generation next year, and the day's claim would survive intact while its demonstration collapsed** — the claim is that the reachable evidence bounds the representable parties, not that this catalogue is deficient. Also unsafe as a claim about the built-in evaluators' quality: the day says nothing about whether `customer_satisfaction` measures customer satisfaction well, only about who was and was not consulted in defining it, and a student who leaves believing the day discredited an evaluator has inverted it. The boundary is stated to students at point 9, and step 6's non-recognising-party requirement is where a student applying it outside that range would fail.

**Sampling disclosure, per the [Seminar Day Design Pattern](/archive/bsc-programme/program/04-Seminar-Day-Design-Pattern.md)'s rule 3.** Phase 3's four groups were selected by staff from a larger set. Students are told this and told that others exist. The disclosure is made and **the limitation is not remedied** — students still see four groups chosen by someone else, which is discussed in §8.

### 3.3 Engagement and motivational mechanism

The day runs on **four people who are all right**.

Phase 3's records are real and each position is defensible. The users want outputs that serve them; the downstream handlers want failures that are cheap to correct; the cost owner wants a system that can be afforded; the unconsulted group wants not to be harmed by something they did not choose. None of these is unreasonable and no criterion satisfies all four.

Students expect one of the positions to be revealed as the correct one and none is. The closing question — whose view did your criterion drop — is where the day's content lands, because every group has dropped somebody and most did not notice which.

Phase 7's role assignment is the day's most uncomfortable and most useful twenty minutes. Reading a line you wrote to the party it disadvantages, and hearing them reject it, is a different experience from knowing abstractly that a trade was made.

**The sometimes-yes is the phase's most important feature.** A student whose trade is rejected by the losing party may still have made a defensible trade, and the design says so explicitly. Without that permission the phase teaches accommodation, which is S039's failure mode in a new setting.

The competence risk is that the day has no right answer and students find this destabilising in a way S040 prepared them for.

## 4. Phase-by-phase design and its warrant

**Phase 1 (25 min).** Five items, the fourth a room-spread prediction. Item 3's near-universal failure — most students cannot name a dissenting party — is the day's opening finding and is what makes phase 3's four positions land as a correction rather than as information. **Item 5 is the sealed Copilot prediction**, administered here and not later per instrumentation rule 8.

**Phase 2 (20 min).** The bridge asks which S036 shaping force applies to an inherited metric. The answer is all four with the requester absent, which reframes evaluation as requirements work with no one to negotiate with.

**Phase 3 (45 min).** Four records, four executable criteria, one constructed criterion, then the dropped-view question. Group D is the payload and its result is now produced rather than asserted: there is no built-in evaluator for a party who never used the system, the service will not register a custom one until a judge model and a threshold are supplied, and the documented auto-generation path takes the agent's own instructions, description, reference files and production traces — **four inputs, none of which a group-D member has ever touched.** The rule 3 disclosure is made in the phase rather than in a footnote.

**Phase 4 (50 min).** Nine points. Point 1 makes the S039 correspondence explicit. Point 2 carries the scope statement. Point 3 supplies the only adjudicating discipline the day has — who bears the cost of a wrong output — which is not a decision procedure and is a question that reliably reorders positions. Point 4's mechanism is now reachable from the phase 3 artefact rather than asserted, per instrumentation rule 7. Point 6 states the traceability rule the specification enforces. **Point 8 names five defaults the platform chose** and point 9 marks the perishable layer, which are the [Copilot Practice Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md)'s two required additions.

**Phase 5 (90 min).** Eight steps at class 4 with step 4 unsupported, **shipping a registered custom evaluator rather than describing a criterion.** Step 1's out-of-sample group is this paper's own §8 objection-1 repair, made. Step 5's registration is where the trade stops being expressible in prose: the service requires a scale, a direction and a number at which passing stops, and each is another decision about the same people. Step 6 is the discriminator. Step 8's one-line-per-group form is what makes step 7's failures-to-capture statement concrete rather than a caveat.

**Phase 6 (40 min).** The two-year criterion of unknown origin, then the agent case. The attention to what the criterion had been optimising is the phase's content and connects to S026 point 4's proxy-optimisation mechanism at a twenty-week gap. **The agent case is chosen for a specific property: it contains no wrong line.** Every dimension of the generated rubric is a defensible quality statement, and the defect is that the set is closed under the system's own instructions. A reviewer checking dimensions one at a time cannot find it, which is the review a student would actually perform.

**Phase 7 (30 min).** The disadvantaged party's rejection, with the sometimes-yes stated.

**Phase 8 (20 min).** The room spread, the recognition-claim count, the sealed Copilot prediction opened, and **the live stakeholder's recognition statement taken privately and reported in aggregate** — this paper's §8 objection-2 concession, made, carrying across the expert-briefing countermeasure found at [S038](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S038.md) per rule 5.

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**Students with more relevant prior knowledge.** A student with product or evaluation experience will construct a workable criterion quickly. Predicted failure: it will be the standard one for this system class — a weighted blend of the metrics such systems usually report — and step 6 will name recognising parties who would recognise *any* reasonable criterion rather than this one specifically. Countermeasure: phase 8's live stakeholder is asked, privately, whether they recognise *this* criterion as reflecting what they said, which a generic construction fails.

**Students with less relevant prior knowledge.** Step 4 is unsupported and the day has no right answer. Countermeasures: four evidence records, a worked incompatibility statement, supplied registration mechanics at step 5, and a specification that checks provenance rather than quality. Residual risk: their criterion may be a restatement of one group's position, which step 7's per-group failure statement exposes.

**Phase 3 is too easy.** Constructing *a* criterion is quick. The difficulty is the dropped-view question, which most groups answer incompletely — they name the group they consciously traded and miss the one they never modelled. Fallback if a group identifies all four: ask which of the four would change their position if they knew what the others wanted, which is a genuine question about whether the incompatibility is real.

**Affective failure.** The characteristic risk is **relativism**. A day establishing that criteria are decisions, that four parties are all right, and that no answer is correct can produce the conclusion that evaluation is arbitrary and any criterion is as good as any other. This is the module's version of the fatalism the design has met in every measurement module.

Countermeasures: point 3's who-bears-the-cost question, which is an ordering principle even though it is not a decision procedure; point 6's traceability rule, which makes some criteria worse than others in a checkable way; and step 6, which requires the trade's cost to be stated rather than dissolved. The design's position is that relativism is defeated by the requirement to *name* rather than by any claim to correctness — a criterion whose trade is stated is answerable, and one whose trade is not is not, and that distinction survives the absence of a right answer.

## 7. Assessment and evidence generated

No entrustment judgement — the module's is at S050.

One specification item, jointly in ME201 and PE201. Per rule 1, the checked elements are: identified groups including one unconsulted and one absent from the supplied records; gathered positions with sources; stated incompatibilities; a criterion with its trade named; the criterion registered and run with its scale, threshold and judge model named; named recognising and non-recognising parties; per-group failure statements; one checkable line per group; the phase 7 outcome; and the prompt, intervention and frontier records. **Nothing else is assessed, including whether the criterion is good.**

**A criterion with no named party who would recognise it fails.**

**An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that the design could have detected an intervention-worthy error. The escape clause is the sensitivity discipline [S052](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S052.md) established and [WP-048](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-048.md) §8 corrected, and the accepted instance here is precise: **a criterion containing at least one dimension on which the system could fail for something it was never instructed to do.** That is the exact defect the phase 6 agent case exhibits, and a student who built the detection has done better than one who caught the error by hand.

Programme instrumentation: the item 3 dissenting-party rate is the cohort's contestedness baseline, re-probed at S059; the dropped-view identification pattern at phase 3 shows which group is systematically invisible; **the live stakeholder's phase 8 recognition statement, now taken privately and reported in aggregate, is the day's external check** and is recorded across cohorts; the step 6 no-recognising-party rate is the engineer's-own-criterion measure; **item 5's sealed prediction accuracy is the day's frontier-calibration datum**, read by the following year's cohort through the [Durable and Perishable Register](/archive/bsc-programme/program/09-Durable-and-Perishable-Register.md) per instrumentation rule 9.

The frontier statements are a sample and the day says so, per instrumentation rule 3: one cohort, one substrate, one criterion-construction task chosen by staff to have a known generation failure available. A frontier map assembled from them describes this task and not the agent.

Dispositions: **humility**, at step 7, where stating what your criterion fails to capture about a group you consulted is uncomfortable and easy to soften.

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: the day's stakeholder access is staff-mediated, and its own rule 3 disclosure names this without remedying it.**

At its strongest: phase 3's four groups were chosen by staff, their positions come from staff-conducted sessions, and the one live window is with a stakeholder staff selected. The day discloses this, per the rule the design added after M08 — and disclosure was the rule's whole content. But the substantive problem is untouched: **which four groups a student sees determines which criterion they can construct**, and that selection is the most consequential act in the day's evidence chain. A student is formulating a problem at class 4 from a sample somebody else drew.

The rebuttal is that some mediation is unavoidable. Four stakeholder inquiries per student is not deliverable, the groups must be real, and staff have relationships students do not.
The concession stands and **the fix it named is now in the design**. Rule 3 requires the sampling to be stated; it does not require it to be reduced, and here it could be. Step 1 now requires the student to name at least one group not represented in the supplied records and to state what evidence would be needed to include them, which converts the disclosure into a demand at no cost. What remains unfixed is that the design has no way to check whether the named fifth group is real or invented, since by construction no record of them exists. That is recorded rather than repaired.

**Objection 2: traceability is a weak check that a determined student can satisfy without doing the work.**

At its strongest: the specification fails a criterion with no named recognising party. A student who constructs their own criterion and then finds one sympathetic stakeholder who says "yes, that sounds right" has satisfied it. Recognition is a low bar — people recognise reasonable-sounding statements about their domain routinely.

This objection the design partly concedes and partly disputes. The dispute: step 6 requires **both** recognising and non-recognising parties, and a generic criterion fails the second half — a student who cannot name anybody who would reject their criterion has not constructed one that trades anything, which is the more informative failure and is the one the specification catches.

The concession was about the recognition check's social conditions, and **it too is now fixed**: the check is taken privately and reported in aggregate rather than performed in the room in front of the cohort. That is the expert-briefing countermeasure found at [S038](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S038.md), carried across. **The rule 5 provenance is unchanged and is not tidied away**: this paper recorded the fourth observation of a remedy discovered in one place and not applied where it recurred, and that observation is why the rule's checklist exists. That the remedy has since been applied is the rule working, not evidence that the failure did not happen.

**Objection 3: the day draws a claim about tooling from one vendor's tooling, and the claim is stronger than the demonstration.**

This is the objection that bites on the practical turn and it is the strongest of the three.

At its strongest: phase 3's payload is that the auto-generation path has no input channel for an unconsulted party, from which point 4 concludes that **the evidence a tool can reach determines which people it can represent.** That conclusion is a general claim about instruments and it is being drawn from four documented input types in one preview feature of one product. A different vendor could accept stakeholder interview transcripts as a generation input tomorrow. If that happened, a student who had learned the day's lesson as *the tooling excludes affected parties* would conclude the problem was solved, when the actual position is that the problem moved — the transcripts would still have to be gathered by someone, from someone, chosen by someone, and group D would still be the group nobody thought to interview.

**The objection is conceded.** The general claim is well founded and the day's evidence for it is one instance. The day's most persuasive fifteen minutes are its least warranted, and the persuasion runs in the dangerous direction: a student who has watched a real service fail to represent group D will trust the generalisation further than one instance supports, which is exactly the reasoning failure the measurement strand exists to teach them to detect.

**The cheap fix is one sentence and it is made.** Point 9 now states that a vendor shipping a consultation input next year would change the instance and not the claim, and §3.2's platform scope statement says the same to this paper's reader. What remains unfixed is smaller and is recorded: **the day has no counter-instance.** A second tool whose inputs *do* reach a non-user — a complaints register, an incident log, a regulator's correspondence — would let the room see the claim survive a case where the tooling is better, which is the only construction that distinguishes a general claim from a complaint about a product. Assembling one is a half-day of staff work and is not in the design.

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** Fewer than a third of students will name a substantive dissenting party at item 3, and item 4's predicted room spread will be lower than the actual. *Instrument:* item 3 coded for substantive dissent; item 4 against the observed distinct-position count.

**Prediction 2.** Group D will be the most commonly dropped view and the least commonly identified as dropped. *Instrument:* phase 3 criteria coded for group coverage, against the closing-question answers.

**Prediction 3.** Students with product or evaluation backgrounds will name recognising parties whose recognition is generic rather than specific, at a higher rate. *Instrument:* step 6 claims tested against the phase 8 stakeholder statement, split by the admission background survey.

**Prediction 4.** At S059, students will ask who constructed a benchmark and for what purpose, unprompted, at a higher rate than a comparison cohort. *Instrument:* an S059 protocol coded for unprompted provenance questioning. Untestable until a variant delivery exists.

**Prediction 5.** A large majority of item 5 sealed predictions will state that the agent will get something wrong about the generated criterion, and a small minority will name the provenance closure specifically — that every dimension traces to the system's own instructions. *Instrument:* item 5 responses coded for generic-versus-specific failure identification, against what each student's agent actually produced. **The gap between the two rates is the day's frontier-calibration measure. If the specific rate is high, the cohort already holds the day's central claim and phase 3's group-D result is confirming rather than teaching; if the generic rate is also low, students are not yet predicting agent failure at all and the sealed item is measuring nothing.**

## What a student should take from this paper

**What the design is claiming.** That a criterion presents as a fact and is a decision. Somebody decided which property to value, on which distribution, weighted which way, and that decision served some parties at the expense of others. Once written down it acquires authority quickly and becomes the thing optimised. Because there is no correct criterion, the only check left is traceability — a criterion should be attributable to people who would recognise it as reflecting something they said.

**What the evidence licenses.** Criterion construction, construct validity and stakeholder-derived standards are **class 1**, grounded in [research/03-measurement-evaluation/shankar-evalgen.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/shankar-evalgen.md) and [research/03-measurement-evaluation/husain-your-ai-product-needs-evals.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/husain-your-ai-product-needs-evals.md), with the inquiry apparatus in [research/05-fde-craft/beyer-holtzblatt-contextual-design.md](/research/05-fde-craft/beyer-holtzblatt-contextual-design.md). The tooling is **class 4**, read on 26 July 2026 and marked preview: auto-generation takes the agent, its own instructions, its reference files and its production traces, with **no rationale published for the weight heuristic and no agreement with human judgement published for any evaluator**. **The class-4 construction the day asks of you is class 3.**

**What to do with it.**

- Ask who bears the cost of a wrong output. It is not a decision procedure and it reliably reorders the positions.
- Name a party who would reject your criterion as well as one who would recognise it. If you cannot name a rejecter, your criterion trades nothing.
- Sort your claims by the five classes of conclusion on the [Agent Configuration Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/13-Agent-Configuration-Standard.md). A registered evaluator is a configured control and its run is an executable check; that the criterion represents anyone is an unsupported inference until a person says so.
- Ask what evidence the tool producing a criterion could reach. The reachable evidence bounds the representable parties.

**The concession worth remembering.** The paper concedes that its most persuasive fifteen minutes are its least warranted — a general claim about instruments drawn from four documented input types in one preview feature of one product. The day holds no counter-instance, no tool whose inputs do reach somebody who never used the system, so you never watch the claim survive a case where the tooling is better. Assembling one is half a day of staff work and has not been done.
