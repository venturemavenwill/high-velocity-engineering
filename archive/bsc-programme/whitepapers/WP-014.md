## In plain terms

**What this paper argues, before the formal version.**

Graphs are normally taught back to front. You are shown a representation, then depth-first search, then breadth-first search, then shortest paths, and you finish able to implement all of them and unable to notice a graph when one turns up in a sentence somebody actually said. **The implementation is not where the difficulty is.** Once you have correctly worked out that a question is about *reachability* — can this thing get to that thing — the algorithm is close to inevitable, it is standard, and you will produce it. The whole cost is upstream, in noticing that "can a change to this service affect that one" and "can this account see that account's data" are the same question.

You cannot teach that by explaining it. Explaining a recognition skill produces students who can recite the categories and still not see them. **You have to arrange encounters where two problems look nothing alike and are the same, and where two problems look nearly identical and are not** — and then make people commit to an answer before anyone adjudicates. That is what the day is built out of, and the instruction afterwards mostly names what the encounters already did.

The day's other half is about who the answer is for. Earlier days ended by asking you to name a caller. This one goes further: a real user's real question, in their words, not translated into engineering language first — because translating it is the step being taught. And the answer has to go back in their words too, with a limitation stated. **How good your model is shows up in the follow-up question, never in the first answer.**

**What this paper concedes, and it is a fair hit.** The day's platform argument is that a vector index is a graph and its settings are graph settings. The two cases that would actually show you that — change the settings, watch the answers move — are done by a member of staff while you watch, because a first-year cohort in week fourteen is not given write access to a live index. **What the room itself operates amounts to opening a configuration file and finding a word in it.** The paper says so at §8, offers a cheap repair that makes you commit a prediction before the file is opened, and admits that what you end up with is still a prediction against somebody else's keystrokes.

## Abstract

This paper defends inverting the conventional order of graph instruction — recognition of problem shape first, traversal algorithms last — and defends closing the day by requiring students to answer a real user's question in the user's own words. The first argument is that traversal algorithms are nearly implied by a correctly identified question type, so the scarce competence is recognition rather than implementation, and recognition is trainable only by contrasting problems whose deep structure is shared and whose surface features are not. The second argument is that a model's adequacy is revealed by the follow-up question, not by the first answer, and that requiring a non-technical answer plus a stated limitation is the cheapest available rehearsal of the communication competence assessed at S083 and S089. The paper additionally defends reading a live vector index as the day's platform case, on the ground that it is the strongest available instance of the day's own discrimination: the caller asks a distance question and the system answers it by traversing a graph, approximately, so that a wrong answer is a **reachability** failure wearing a distance failure's clothes. It also defends treating this day as the point where S008's inductive correspondence becomes the working method. It concedes that the day's transfer probe may reward pattern-matching on a construction the design itself supplied, and that half its platform cases are demonstrated rather than operated.

## 1. The instructional problem this day must solve

Graph instruction conventionally proceeds from representation to algorithm: here is an adjacency list, here is depth-first search, here is breadth-first search, here is a shortest-path algorithm. Students finish able to implement all of them and unable to recognise, in a problem stated in ordinary language, that a graph is what they are looking at.

The mismatch is severe because the recognition step is where nearly all the difficulty lives. Once a problem is correctly identified as a reachability question over a stated entity-relationship structure, the algorithm is close to determined — there is essentially one answer, it is standard, and a competent student will produce it. The hard part is entirely upstream: noticing that a question about whether a change to one service can affect another service is the same question as whether one account can see another account's data, and that both are reachability.

Recognition is trainable, but not by exposition. It requires contrasts in which the deep structure is shared and the surface features are not, which is why phase 1 item 3 pairs two problems with no vocabulary in common, and why phase 3's sixth question is a near-miss — almost identical phrasing, different deep structure. Those two constructions do the day's work; the instruction merely names what the constructions produced.

The day's second problem is one the module has been building toward. S011, S012 and S013 all closed by asking students to name a caller. S014 goes further: the caller is a real substrate user, their question is supplied verbatim rather than translated into engineering language, and the answer must go back in their words. That is a materially harder demand and it is the module's terminal one, placed here rather than at S015 because S015's difficulty is the choice itself.

The third problem is the retention of S008. Four weeks after recursion was taught as an inductive argument, the day requires the induction hypothesis to be written before the traversal procedure, without re-teaching the correspondence and without framing it as an exercise in the technique. If S008 took, this is invisible scaffolding; if it did not, phase 2's final item and step 3 both fail visibly.

## 3. Theoretical basis for the design

### 3.1 Load profile and element interactivity

**Moderate, and deliberately lower than S013's.** The day sits after the module's high point and its individual elements are largely separable: identifying entities, identifying the question type, writing the hypothesis and costing the traversal can each be done without holding the others.

The interactivity that exists is in the recognition step, where the entity choice and the question type constrain each other — a problem can be modelled with different entities, and the choice determines which question type applies. That is genuine and it is where phase 3's near-miss operates.

Management:

- **One dataset across phase 3,** with five questions over it, so all variation is in the question.
- **Steps 1 and 2 worked with the room** on a sixth question before the independent work.
- **The hypothesis skeleton supplied at step 3,** which is the same support S008 gave and is retained because the day does not want to re-test S008's fading.
- **Traversal implementation is expected to be quick.** The day's time budget assumes step 4 takes fifteen minutes, which is realistic once step 3 is done and is the design's practical demonstration of point 1.
- **No comparison across graph representations.** Adjacency structure is named at point 5 and not explored.

### 3.2 Desirable difficulties selected, and those deliberately not

**Selected.**

- *Item 3's surface-dissimilar pairing,* which is a transfer probe by construction.
- *The near-miss sixth question* in phase 3, and the demand to say what in the phrasing distinguishes it.
- *Writing the induction hypothesis before the procedure,* step 3, now the expected order.
- *Answering in the user's words with a stated limitation,* step 6.
- *The partner's follow-up question,* phase 7, which is where the model's adequacy is actually tested.

**Deliberately not selected.**

- *Implementing shortest-path or connectivity algorithms from scratch.* Standard library or supplied. The day's argument is that these follow; implementing them would contradict it.
- *Withholding the five question types.* Named at point 2. They are vocabulary and the recognition skill needs them to be nameable.
- *Graph representation trade-offs.* Named at point 5, deferred.
- *Weighted, directed and multi-relational variants* beyond what phase 3's dataset requires.
- *Building or tuning a vector index.* Refused, and the refusal is the reason two of the four platform cases are demonstrated rather than operated. Index construction is IS203's and Q6's.
- *Assessment stakes on the same day.*

**The scope statement on the platform instance, in strong form.**

The day teaches that a vector index is a proximity graph and that its parameters are graph parameters. The simplification has a stated safe range and a stated unsafe one.

It is **safe** for reasoning about what a nearest-neighbour search is doing and about how its failures present: that the search is a traversal, that the traversal is bounded by a candidate-list size, that the answer is approximate, and therefore that a miss is a reachability failure rather than an arithmetic one. That reading is durable across implementations because it is a property of graph-based approximate search rather than of a product.

It is **unsafe** in three conditions, all stated at point 10. It is unsafe **at any version other than the one in front of you**: the parameter names, the defaults of 4, 400 and 500, the permitted ranges and the cosine default are this vendor's and this API version's, and the documentation itself carries a migration note showing that the containing object has already been renamed once. It is unsafe as a claim about **other vector stores**, which use different graph constructions and different parameter vocabularies and sometimes no graph at all. And it is unsafe as a claim about **retrieval quality**, because the day observes which neighbours move under `k` and never establishes which neighbours were correct — that is [S057](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S057.md)'s and [S058](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S058.md)'s work, and the day names the deferral rather than implying an answer.

The artefact element enforcing the boundary is step 6's stated limitation. A student whose user-facing answer claims completeness for a result obtained from an approximate index fails the specification, because the limitation is the element and an approximate answer presented as exhaustive is the failure the element exists to catch.

### 3.3 Engagement and motivational mechanism

The day runs on **two problems that turn out to be the same problem**, and it is the module's cleanest intellectual pleasure.

Phase 1 item 3 asks which two of five problems are identical. The two share no vocabulary, no domain and no obvious structure. Students who find it report a distinct satisfaction — the recognition is sudden and complete — and students who do not find it are shown the answer at phase 8, at which point the pairing is obvious and its obviousness is the lesson.

The stronger motivational element is step 6 and phase 7. This is the first day in the programme where a student answers a real person's real question, in that person's language, and then defends the answer to someone playing them. Students consistently treat work with a named recipient more seriously than work with an assessor, and the design uses this deliberately at the module's close.

The competence risk is at phase 7 rather than in the content: a student whose model cannot answer the follow-up has produced work that fails in front of a peer playing a user. Countermeasure: the follow-up is *designed* to be answerable by roughly half of models, and the instructor states in advance that a model which cannot answer the follow-up is a normal outcome and is the phase's information rather than its failure.

## 4. Phase-by-phase design and its warrant

**Phase 1 (25 min).** Five ordinary-language problems, real, none mentioning graphs. Item 2's one-word classification forces commitment to a type before any analysis. Item 3 is the transfer probe and the day's most interesting instrument. Item 4 makes finding or not finding it a calibration event.

**Phase 2 (20 min).** The final item requires *writing* an induction hypothesis rather than recalling one, which is retrieval into use. This is the design's preferred final-item form once a technique is meant to be operational, and it appears here for the first time.

**Phase 3 (45 min).** Five domain-language questions over one real dataset, then a near-miss sixth, then four cases on a live vector index. The near-miss is the language half's payload and it works because groups have just built confidence on five successes — the sixth arrives after a pattern has formed and violates it minimally. The closing task asks what in the *phrasing* distinguishes them, which is the recognition skill made explicit. Case A is the systems half's payload and does the same work in a different medium: the room predicts what kind of structure sits behind a similarity search, is wrong, and reads the correction out of a configuration rather than receiving it from the front. Cases C and D are **demonstrated rather than operated** because varying `efSearch` or `m` writes to an index definition, which this quarter does not do; the day says so and the specification does not assess them.

**Phase 4 (50 min).** Ten points in inverted order: shape, types, then algorithms at point 3. Point 2 now states what the five-type list omits and why, which is the repair §8 objection 1 identified. Point 3 frames depth-first and breadth-first as two well-founded orderings rather than two algorithms, which is S008 doing the work and is the day's cleanest instance of a prior day becoming infrastructure. Point 6's wrong-shape material is unusual in graph instruction and is included because it is where real cost accrues. Points 8 and 9 are the platform's contribution and point 9 is the sentence the day exists to hand forward — an approximate answer fails as a traversal, not as an arithmetic — which is available here and nowhere else in the programme before Q6.

**Phase 5 (90 min).** Six steps on a verbatim user question, ending in a shipped answer. The verbatim requirement is load-bearing: a translated question has already had the recognition step performed on it, which is the step being taught. Step 4 supplies the step 3 induction hypothesis to the agent as part of the instruction, which is the day's cheapest available demonstration that a specification and a prompt are the same object stated in two registers. Step 6's answer-in-the-user's-words is the module's terminal demand.

**Phase 6 (40 min).** Two disclosures of the same failure class. A non-binary relationship forced into a binary model, chosen because shape errors are expensive, are rarely disclosed, and are invisible to anyone reviewing the code — the model is internally consistent and wrong about the domain. Then the agent case, in which a correct breadth-first search answers a reachability question that was a shortest-path question, and every downstream artefact is consistent with the wrong question. The pairing is the argument: **a shape error survives review whether a person or an agent commits it, and for the same reason** — review checks internal consistency, and a shape error is internally consistent.

**Phase 7 (30 min).** The partner plays the user and asks the follow-up. This is the first role-play in the programme and the first time a student defends work to a non-technical reader. It is deliberately unassessed.

**Phase 8 (20 min).** The item 3 pairing rate, and — more informative — whether students who found it can say what made it findable. A student who found it and cannot say why has pattern-matched rather than recognised, which is the §8 objection stated as a measurement.

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**Students with more relevant prior knowledge.** A student with a strong algorithms background will classify phase 1 quickly and may find phase 3's first five trivial. Predicted failure: they implement fluently at step 4 and treat steps 1, 2 and 6 as preamble, which inverts the day's priorities exactly. Countermeasure: the specification fails an answer written in graph vocabulary, which their fluency actively works against — the more comfortable a student is with the technical framing, the harder step 6 is for them. This is the rare case where prior knowledge makes the assessed element harder rather than easier, and it is deliberate.

**Students with less relevant prior knowledge.** A student with little graph exposure faces recognition, modelling, recursion and costing in one day. Countermeasures: the question types are named, the hypothesis skeleton is supplied, traversals may be taken from a library, and steps 1 and 2 are worked. Residual risk: step 5's three-term cost on a real graph may be beyond them, and the fallback is that they state the operation count term and mark the other two as unmodelled — which is an honest artefact and passes.

**Phase 3 is too easy.** The first five questions are expected to be answered correctly by most groups; that is the design, since the near-miss requires a formed pattern to violate. Fallback if a group also resolves the sixth immediately: ask them to write a seventh question that is a near-miss for a *different* pair of types, which is a construction task and is genuinely hard.

**Affective failure.** The characteristic risk is at phase 7 and is **defensiveness toward the user**. A student whose model cannot answer the follow-up may respond by explaining why the follow-up is unreasonable, or why the user should have asked differently. This is the precise attitude the professional-formation strand exists to prevent, and it appears here in its earliest and most correctable form. Countermeasure: the partner is instructed to stay in role and not to accept engineering-side explanations, and the instructor names the pattern to the room before the phase begins, framed as normal and worth noticing rather than as a fault.

## 7. Assessment and evidence generated

No entrustment evidence.

One specification item in CF102 requiring the full chain from entities to a user-facing answer, plus the three artefact elements.

The distinctive clause: **an answer written in graph vocabulary fails.** The user asked in their own terms and is not owed a translation of the engineer's model. This is the programme's first assessed communication requirement and it is binary rather than graded, which is defensible only because the standard is checkable — either the answer uses the user's terms or it does not.

The second distinctive clause: **an intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the build compares its output against a hand-computed answer on a graph small enough to check. The exemption is a property of the artefact rather than a claim about detectability, which is what makes it markable.

Programme instrumentation: phase 1 item 3's pairing rate, and more importantly the articulation rate among those who found it, is the recognition-versus-pattern-match discriminator; the phase 7 follow-up answerability rate is the model-adequacy baseline, repeated at S038; step 6 specification failures are the cohort's first communication measure, carried to S083; the phase 3 case A written predictions are the structure-expectation baseline carried to S058; and the phase 1 Copilot prediction, scored at phase 8, extends the frontier map. Rule 4 branch for the frontier item: a cohort that expects the agent to state a shape before writing code, and is wrong, receives a shape-first clause in the CF102 instructions file exercise at [S015](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S015.md), whose conformance is checked there.

Dispositions: **humility**, observable at phase 7 in whether the student engages the follow-up or explains it away.

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: the five question types are a convenience with no principled basis, and teaching them as a taxonomy will constrain rather than enable.**

At its strongest: reachability, ordering, distance, connectivity and cycle detection are not an exhaustive or a natural partition of graph problems. Flow, matching, colouring, clustering, centrality and spanning structure are all absent, and several of the five overlap — connectivity is a reachability property, and cycle detection is often a byproduct of ordering. A student given five boxes will put problems in boxes, and will misclassify anything that does not fit rather than concluding the taxonomy is incomplete. The day therefore trains a recognition skill bounded by an arbitrary list, which is worse than training no taxonomy at all, because the list will feel authoritative.

The rebuttal is that a recognition skill requires nameable categories, and that the alternative — recognition without vocabulary — is not a coherent position. The five are chosen because they cover the questions that arise in the problem class the programme cares about, they are distinguishable by the phrasing of the question rather than by the structure of the solution, and each maps to a traversal students can implement in fifteen minutes. Flow and matching are genuinely important and genuinely require machinery the day cannot afford.

**The concession is discharged.** This paper previously conceded that the day did not tell students the list is incomplete, and identified it as the second instance in one module of presenting a tractable enumeration as complete, after [WP-012](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-012.md)'s three-term decomposition. **Point 2 now names five omitted types, states that two of the five taught overlap, and gives the safe and unsafe conditions**; WP-012 point 2 makes the equivalent repair. Both are annotated at the point of change. **The pattern finding stands and is not retracted by the repairs.** Two instances repaired in the same module is evidence that the habit was real, not evidence that it is gone, and the design records it as a standing tendency: this design reaches for the enumeration it can teach and states it as the enumeration there is. The third occurrence, when it comes, should be treated as confirmation rather than as news.

**Objection 2: item 3's pairing may reward pattern-matching on a construction the design supplied, not transfer.**

At its strongest: the design chose two problems with shared deep structure and disjoint surface features, and then asks students to find the pair. Students know a pair exists because the item says so. The task is therefore a search over ten candidate pairs with a guaranteed answer, and a student can find it by elimination, by noticing which two problems the instructor spent most care phrasing, or by any number of test-taking heuristics that have nothing to do with recognising structural similarity. Worse, the design intends to read the result as a transfer measure — and a transfer measure with a signalled answer is not measuring transfer.

This objection has force and the design's response is a partial one. Phase 8 asks students who found the pairing to say **what made it findable**, and the design's operative measure is that articulation rate rather than the raw pairing rate. A student who found the pair by elimination cannot describe the shared structure; a student who recognised it can. This is a real discriminator and it is the reason §7 names the articulation rate rather than the pairing rate as the instrument.

What is conceded is that the item still signals that a pair exists, which inflates the pairing rate and makes the raw figure uninterpretable — the same asymmetry WP-006 identified about its own transfer probe, appearing again. The fix is available and cheap: ask whether *any* two of the five are the same problem, without asserting that a pair exists, and include a cohort where none is. That change is not made, and the reason is that a null-answer variant would need to run on a different cohort to be informative, which the programme's single-cohort structure does not permit. The honest position is that item 3 is, like S006's item 3, an investigation trigger rather than a measurement.

**Objection 3: half the platform cases are demonstrated rather than operated, and the two that matter most are the demonstrated ones.**

At its strongest, and this is the objection that bites on the practical turn: the day's whole platform argument is that a vector index is a graph whose parameters are graph parameters. The cases that would prove it are C and D — vary `efSearch`, vary `m`, watch the answers change — because a parameter you cannot move is indistinguishable from a constant. What the room actually operates is case A, which is *reading a configuration file*, and case B, which varies `k` at query time and therefore demonstrates nothing about the graph at all, since changing how many results you ask for is not changing how the search walks. So the operated portion of the day's platform layer establishes that a JSON document contains the string `hnsw`, and the portion that would establish the day's claim is performed by a member of staff while the students watch. The Standard permits this and requires it to be declared, which the day does; declaring it does not make it good.

**This objection is conceded, and the cheap fix is named.** The design does not argue that case A carries the weight of cases C and D. It argues that the alternative — giving a first-year cohort write access to an index in week fourteen, in a quarter whose platform anchor is explicitly the *read-only* server — trades a genuine and rare thing, a live production configuration, for a sandbox whose parameters mean nothing because nothing depends on them. **The cheap fix, which is in the design, is to make case A a prediction event rather than a reading exercise:** before the configuration is opened, each group commits in writing to what kind of structure they expect and what they expect its parameters to control, and after cases C and D are demonstrated each group states which of *their own* predictions the demonstration falsified. This costs two minutes, it converts a passive demonstration into an adjudicated one, and it means the demonstrated cases produce a student artefact even though they produce no student action. What remains unfixed is that the artefact is still a prediction against somebody else's keystrokes, and the day says so rather than implying otherwise.

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** The phase 1 item 3 pairing rate will substantially exceed the rate at which those students can articulate the shared structure at phase 8. *Instrument:* pairing rate against articulation rate. **The gap is the pattern-match estimate from §8 objection 2; a small gap would indicate the item is measuring what the design hoped.**

**Prediction 2.** Groups will misclassify the phase 3 near-miss sixth question at a high rate, and the misclassification will be toward the type of the question it most resembles in phrasing rather than randomly distributed. *Instrument:* sixth-question classifications coded by error direction.

**Prediction 3.** Roughly half of models will fail to answer the phase 7 follow-up, and failure will correlate with whether the student's step 1 discard statement was substantive. *Instrument:* follow-up answerability against blind-rated step 1 discard statements.

**Prediction 4.** At S038, students will identify entities and state discards without prompting at a higher rate than they did at S014 step 1. *Instrument:* an S038 protocol coded for unprompted discard statements, compared against S014 artefacts.

**Prediction 5.** Before case A is opened, fewer than one in five groups will name a graph as the structure behind a similarity search, and fewer than one in twenty will name an out-degree parameter. *Instrument:* the written case A predictions, coded for structure named and parameter class named. **A high rate would mean the platform case is redundant for this cohort and should be replaced by the S058 version.**

Untestable until a variant delivery exists; recorded because point 9 is the day's principal forward contribution and would otherwise travel unchecked.

## What a student should take from this paper

**What the design is claiming.** That once a problem is correctly identified as a reachability, ordering, distance, connectivity or cycle question, the algorithm is nearly determined — so the scarce competence is recognition, and recognition is trainable only by contrasts whose deep structure is shared and whose surfaces are not. A model's adequacy shows up in the follow-up question, never in the first answer.

**What the evidence licenses.** The graph-based approximate-search material behind points 8 and 9 is **class 1**, and the paper asserts mechanism and direction from those notes and **no experimental figure from either**. Analogical transfer, near-miss discrimination and worked-example fading are **class 2**, direction and mechanism only. The index parameters and their defaults are **class 4**, read at a stated date from a preview reference whose containing array has already been renamed once — and **no recall figure is published at any parameter setting**, so none is quoted. The five-question-type taxonomy is **class 3**.

**What to do with it.**

- **Classify the question before choosing an algorithm.** The traversal follows; the classification does not.
- **Answer in the user's words.** An answer written in graph vocabulary fails, and an approximate result presented as exhaustive fails.
- **Commit your prediction before the configuration is opened,** then say which of your own predictions the demonstration falsified. Watching is not evidence unless you bet first.
- **A shape error survives review because review checks internal consistency.** A correct traversal answering the wrong question is a configured control's output and not outcome evidence — which is why the five classes on the [Agent Configuration Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/13-Agent-Configuration-Standard.md) separate what ran from what it establishes.

**The concession worth remembering.** The paper concedes that the two cases which would actually prove its central claim — moving the graph's own parameters and watching the answers change — are demonstrated by staff rather than operated by you, and that what the room operates establishes little more than that a configuration document contains a string. **The resulting artefact is a prediction against somebody else's keystrokes,** and declaring that does not make it good.

**Grounded in vendor documentation, with its version and its silence recorded.**

*What the documentation says.* Microsoft Learn documents Azure AI Search's vector search algorithm configuration with `kind` set to `hnsw` for "the Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW) algorithm", and documents four parameters: **`m`**, "the number of bi-directional links created for every new element during construction", default **4**, permitted range 4 to 10, with the stated trade that "larger values lead to denser graphs, improving query performance, but require more memory and computation"; **`efConstruction`**, "the size of the dynamic list for the nearest neighbors used during indexing", default **400**, range 100 to 1,000; **`efSearch`**, the equivalent list "used during search time", default **500**, range 100 to 1,000, with the stated trade that increasing it "may improve search results, but it slows down query performance"; and **`metric`**, default **`cosine`**, with `euclidean` and `dotProduct` permitted ([Create or Update Index, preview REST API](https://learn.microsoft.com/rest/api/searchservice/preview-api/create-or-update-index#definitions)). The Azure MCP Server documents a **Get search index** tool that "retrieves detailed information about Azure AI Search indexes, including index schema, fields, analyzers, scoring profiles, and other index properties", annotated **Read Only** and **Idempotent**; of the service's read tools, only **Retrieve from knowledge base** carries the **Open World** annotation ([Azure MCP Server tools for Azure AI Search](https://learn.microsoft.com/azure/developer/azure-mcp-server/tools/azure-ai-search)).

*At what version and date.* All pages retrieved 2026-07-26. The HNSW parameter definitions are cited from the preview REST API reference; Microsoft's own migration guidance records that the containing array was renamed from `algorithmConfigurations` to `algorithms` at the 2023-11-01 API version and that field-level configuration moved to profiles, which is direct evidence that this surface has already changed once and is why point 10's unsafe condition is stated as *any version other than the one in front of you*. The Azure MCP Server pages carry no version stamp in their own text.

*Flagged 2026-07-30, not yet re-verified:* the parameter names, defaults and permitted ranges above are quoted from the **preview** REST reference, and this paper never states which API version the live index read in phase 3 case A is served at — so it is not established that the quoted defaults are the ones in front of the room; the index's own API version should be recorded at the next re-verification.

*What the documentation does not report that a reader of this day would need.* Three silences, all shown to students. **It states no recall figure at any parameter setting.** The trades are given as directions — denser graphs improve query performance, a larger `efSearch` may improve results — with no magnitude, no benchmark and no interval anywhere, so a reader cannot compute what a change buys and the day asserts nothing about it. **It does not state what "approximate" costs**, which is the quantity point 9 is about; the day therefore teaches the *failure mode* and defers the *rate* to S057 and S058, where it is measured rather than quoted. **And it does not say what happens to existing vectors when a parameter changes**, which is why cases C and D are demonstrated with an explicit statement that `m` requires a rebuild rather than being presented as a live control.
