## In plain terms

**What this paper argues, before the formal version.**

A proof is not a form you fill in. It is an argument aimed at a particular reader who is entitled not to believe you and who will not be moved by the shape of the page. Students routinely hand in documents with the right shape and the crucial step missing — and when you ask them for the missing step, most of them can give it. **So the problem is not that they cannot reason; it is that nobody ever gave them a standard against which the step was missing.** A template supplies the structure that the thinking was supposed to supply, and once the template is satisfied the thinking has nowhere to go.

Remove the template and the question comes back: what will *this* reader not grant me? That question is the actual work of writing a proof, and it is the same work as defending a capacity estimate to a sceptical engineer, telling a manager what a measurement does not license, or arguing a safety case to somebody whose job is to press on it. Every one of those arrives later in the degree and every one is this day in different clothes.

The day's mechanism is blunt on purpose. **You are told to reject your partner's proof — you are not allowed to say it is fine.** In week four that is the only reliable way to get real criticism into a room, because a rejection you were assigned is not an opinion you volunteered, and nobody has to take it personally. The reason it must not be your only reviewer is that a tool will object on demand, at no social cost, at any hour, and the whole point of the exercise is that it costs something.

**What this paper concedes, in the same plain terms.** Telling students that how much detail is enough depends on who is reading risks teaching them that rigour is negotiable — that a thin argument is fine for a busy manager who will accept it. The design's entire protection against that is the order of one pretest item and one sentence in the consolidation. **That is an intention, not a safeguard**, and the task that would actually test it is not in the design. The paper concedes separately that it makes rejection compulsory and never says when it stops being so.

## Abstract

This paper defends teaching mathematical proof as reader-directed argument rather than as formal derivation, and defends the mandatory-rejection protocol that structures the day's main task. The argument has two parts. First, the formalist framing produces a well-documented pattern in which students satisfy proof templates without constructing arguments, because the template supplies the structure that the reasoning was supposed to supply; reader-direction removes the template by making sufficiency contingent, which forces the student to model what actually needs establishing. Second, the professional competences this programme certifies — defending a capacity argument, a release decision, a safety case — are the same competence under different subject matter, and are the competences that conventional mathematics instruction most reliably fails to produce. The mandatory-rejection protocol is defended as the only reliable way to make critique a role rather than an interpersonal judgement in week four of a programme. **The paper additionally defends putting an agent in the reviewer's chair at step 5, on the ground that the protocol was designed to solve a problem in students — that critique defaults to accommodation unless a form requires otherwise — and that the same problem, in a more extreme form and with no social cause, appears in a tool.** The paper concedes that the day risks teaching students that rigour is negotiable, and that its defence against this is a design intention rather than a demonstrated safeguard.

## 1. The instructional problem this day must solve

Proof instruction has a characteristic failure that is visible to anyone who has marked it. Students produce documents with the correct shape — a statement, a supposition, a chain, a conclusion — in which the load-bearing inference is absent, hand-waved, or assumed. Asked to justify the missing step they frequently can, which establishes that the deficit is not reasoning capacity. The deficit is that they were never modelling a reader, so they had no standard against which the missing step was missing. They were satisfying a form, and the form was satisfied.

The reader-directed framing removes the form. If sufficiency depends on who you are addressing, no template can discharge the obligation, and the student must ask what this particular reader will not grant. That question is the actual content of proof construction.

The second problem is the one the programme exists for. At S020 a student must argue that a capacity allocation is adequate before observing it. At S050 they must state what a measurement does not license, to someone who wants it to license more. At S065 they must defend a cost position to a person paying it. At S087 they must construct a safety case, and at S090 defend it under what-if perturbation from assessors whose job is to press. Every one of these is the same act: transferring warranted belief to a reader who is entitled to disbelieve you and who will not be moved by form.

Conventional mathematics instruction does not build this, and frequently damages it, by training students that arguments are addressed to a marker who already knows the answer and is checking your compliance. That is the opposite of the professional situation, in which the reader does not know the answer, has other things to do, and will act on what you tell them.

The third problem is that the programme's assessment architecture depends on students being able to *receive* rigorous challenge without threat. The entrustment-based discussion at year three subjects students to what-if perturbation of their own decisions by multiple assessors. A student meeting that protocol for the first time in year three will experience it as an interrogation. Phase 7 introduces it in week four, stakes-free, adversarial, and reciprocal.

## 2. Entry state and prior knowledge assumptions

- **Highly variable proof exposure.** Some students have had a rigorous discrete-mathematics course; many have had none and associate proof with school geometry. The day's design is largely insensitive to this because its content is a stance rather than a technique.
- **A shared object to prove things about.** S002's machine is deliberately reused in phase 5, so that no student is disadvantaged by unfamiliarity with the subject matter of the claim.
- **Three days of established norms**, including two error clinics with successful disclosure and one experience, at S003, of being confidently wrong in public.
- **The S003 habit,** which phase 5 step 5 extends: naming what a choice costs.
- **No prior experience of mandated critique.** Students have received critique; almost none have been required to deliver it, and the discomfort of the delivering role is a design factor.

## 3. Theoretical basis for the design

### 3.1 Load profile and element interactivity

**Low intrinsic interactivity, deliberately.** The claims proved in phase 5 are small properties of a machine the cohort has traced by hand, chosen so that the mathematical content is nearly trivial. This is a considered sacrifice: the day is about the argumentative act, and any interesting mathematical content would consume the capacity that the reader-modelling requires.

The design's principal load risk is not the mathematics but the **social load of phase 5's rejection cycles**. Delivering a rejection, receiving one, and repairing under it are three demands stacked in ninety minutes, and the interpersonal component is genuinely costly for many students in week four. The countermeasures are structural: rejection is mandatory and therefore not a signal about the recipient's work; the rejecting student is playing a *named role* supplied by the exercise rather than speaking as themselves; and the rejection has a required form — state specifically what you were not given — which removes the need to compose a critique under time pressure.

Extraneous load is reduced by keeping all three rounds on one claim, one page, one partner pair, with no context switching.

### 3.2 Desirable difficulties selected, and those deliberately not

**Selected.**

- *Mandatory rejection.* The day's defining difficulty. "It's fine" is unavailable, which forces the rejecting student into genuine evaluation rather than social accommodation.
- *A second rejection by a different reader with a different standard,* which prevents the student from treating round 2 as the answer and forces the recognition that adequacy is plural.
- *Constructing a fourth reader* in phase 3, which is harder than characterising the three given ones and which most groups do incompletely.
- *Interleaved retrieval in phase 2* with a discrimination item spanning two days.
- *Adversarial articulation in phase 7,* pressing a claim until it is qualified or withdrawn.

**Deliberately not selected.**

- *Difficult mathematics.* Explicitly refused, for the load reason above.
- *Withholding the failure classes.* Phase 4 point 5 names the four failure classes directly. Discovering these by generation would take a term and they are diagnostic vocabulary, not insight.
- *Formal notation.* The day uses precise natural language. Notation is introduced in MS101 on its own schedule; requiring it here would make the notation the difficulty instead of the argument.
- *Public performance.* No student presents a proof to the room. The rejection cycle is pairwise, because a first experience of mandated critique in front of thirty people would recruit threat response and teach avoidance.
- *Replacing either human rejection with the agent.* Refused, and the refusal is the day's most consequential platform decision. The rejections at steps 2 and 4 do affective work that a tool cannot do: they require a student to *deliver* discomfort and to *receive* it, and a reviewer who objects on demand at no social cost removes exactly the difficulty the protocol exists to create. **The agent is a third reviewer whose review is adjudicated, and it is placed after both human rejections so that the student has a standard against which its thinness is legible.**

**The scope statement for the platform instance, in strong form.** The day teaches a simplification: **that a review standard stated in a specification produces a different review from one left unstated.** Step 5's two agent reviews — one unguided, one under a stated rejection form — are the demonstration.

That simplification is **safe** for deciding what a review brief must contain, for diagnosing a review that produced nothing useful, and for recognising that an unstated standard is not an absent one. It is **unsafe** in three conditions.

- **As a claim that the stated form produces good reviews.** It produces reviews of the stated shape. Shape and substance are different, and the day's own adjudication categories exist because a review can satisfy a form and be manufactured. **A student who concludes that a better rejection form yields better reviews has drawn the conclusion the day is arguing against**, one level up.
- **As a claim about human reviewers.** The mechanism in a tool is that an unspecified disposition is filled by a default. The mechanism in a person is social cost, and the two are not the same mechanism even where the observable behaviour matches. The design's own collusive-softening countermeasure is aimed at the human version and would do nothing to the machine version.
- **On any version other than the one in front of the student.** Which disposition a given release supplies when unasked is not documented anywhere and is not stable. The durable half is that an unspecified standard is not an absent standard; the release-specific half is which standard arrives.

The artefact element that makes the boundary checkable is specification item 3: **every concern raised by either review receives a verdict with a reason, and at least one *manufactured* verdict must be reasoned out for an empty intervention record to pass.** A student who accepts every concern the agent raised has not adjudicated, and fails.

### 3.3 Engagement and motivational mechanism

The day's motivational structure is unusual: it runs on **relatedness through structured conflict**.

Phase 1 opens with the room disagreeing about argument B. That disagreement is real, visible, and about something students expected to be objective. It establishes immediately that the day has a genuine question in it, and it does so without anyone being wrong — the disagreement is the phenomenon.

Phase 5's rejection protocol is the engagement engine and it works because the roles are assigned. A student told to reject is not being unkind; they are doing the exercise. This converts what would otherwise be an interpersonally expensive act into a low-cost one, and students consistently find it enjoyable in a way that voluntary peer critique is not. The requirement that a vague rejection fails the *rejecting* student's specification is what keeps it substantive.

Competence is supported by the small mathematical content: nearly every student can produce a valid round-1 proof, so the rejection is about sufficiency rather than correctness, and being rejected does not imply being wrong. That distinction is stated explicitly before round 1 begins.

Autonomy enters at phase 8, where the student names a claim of their own that they cannot yet defend, and chooses whether to defend or withdraw it by S008.

## 4. Phase-by-phase design and its warrant

**Phase 1 (25 min).** Three arguments, all reaching a true conclusion, differing in validity and completeness. Argument C — a genuine gap with the right answer — is the item that does the work, because it separates *correct conclusion* from *warranted conclusion*, which is the distinction the entire measurement strand later depends on. Item 3's room-agreement prediction is the calibration instrument and is resolved same-day.

**Phase 2 (20 min).** Free recall across three days, with a final item requiring comparison between two days' error taxonomies. Comparison items are the cheapest available interleaving and are used in every phase 2 from here on.

**Phase 3 (45 min).** One theorem, three proofs, three readers. Surface variation with invariant deep structure. The fourth-reader construction is the generation demand; the design expects groups to produce a reader needing *more* detail and to miss the reader needing *different* detail, and phase 4 point 2 resolves that specific gap.

**The four executions hold the theorem constant and vary only the named reader**, which is the same matched-cases construction the read half of the phase uses, applied to a producer instead of to three artefacts. The payload is that the four outputs differ in register and agree in content, and the design's confidence in it comes from the same place as its expectation about groups: **producing a document for a reader who wants different detail requires modelling what that reader will not grant, and neither a novice nor a tool reliably does that from a role label alone.** The finding is available only because the room committed its own account of reader-difference first; without that, four differently-worded documents would look like success. The second finding — that a reader specified by what they will act on produces a different document from one specified as needing more detail — is [S003](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S003.md) point 6 in a register with no code in it, and it is the more useful of the two for groups whose fourth reader was underspecified.

**Phase 4 (50 min).** Eight points, with the professional transposition last. The techniques at point 3 are introduced by the situation that calls for them rather than as a list, which is the difference between a repertoire and a menu. Point 4 gives the counterexample first-class status, because disproof is the move students will use most and value least. **Points 6 and 7 are placed between the failure classes and the transposition** because both are claims about what a reviewer supplies when unasked, and both need point 5's vocabulary. Point 6's distinction — the grounding tool closes the citation gap and not the inference gap — is the day's answer to the student who believes that a cited claim is a warranted one, and it is placed on this day rather than at S001 because the failure classes are what make the inference gap nameable.

**Phase 5 (90 min).** The three-round rejection cycle. The two-reader conflict in round 3 and the cost statement in step 5 are the phase's payload; rounds 1 and 2 exist to make round 3 possible. Reusing the S002 machine as subject matter is a deliberate load decision and a deliberate retrieval one. **The agent review is appended to step 5 rather than given its own step** because the phase has no spare minutes and because its value depends entirely on the two human rejections having already happened — a student who has not yet been told specifically what they failed to give has no standard against which an agreeable review reads as thin. The two-review construction, one unguided and one under a stated form, is the day's build, and the thing built is a review standard whose effect on a reviewer is demonstrable.

**Phase 6 (40 min).** The instructor discloses an argument that was *accepted and wrong*, ideally from this programme's own materials. Choosing an accepted error rather than a caught one is the phase's design decision: it demonstrates that peer acceptance is not validation, which is the fact the whole assessment architecture's rater discipline rests on. **The agent case examines the student's use of the review rather than the review**, which is the only form in which this failure is correctable, and it is the day's sharpest instance of confident wrongness surviving review — sharper than S002's, because the student had a substantive human rejection in hand and preferred the agreeing reviewer anyway.

**Phase 7 (30 min).** Adversarial pairs, pressing a personal claim until qualification or withdrawal. Nearly all claims qualify, and the phase's reported experience is relief rather than defeat. This is the entrustment-discussion rehearsal.

**Phase 8 (20 min).** Item 3 distribution shown. Personal failure-class identification, which is diagnostic. The undefended-claim commitment carries to S008, which is the module's first cross-day personal thread.

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**Students with more relevant prior knowledge.** A student with a rigorous proof background may find the mathematics insultingly easy and read the day as a soft-skills exercise dressed up. Predicted failure: they write formally impeccable round-1 proofs, receive rejections they consider unreasonable, and dismiss the exercise. Countermeasure: their assigned reader is the maintainer, whose standard the formalist proof reliably fails — not because it is wrong but because it does not say what may be relied upon. The rejection lands because it is correct. This is the design's best case for the day's thesis and it is aimed at exactly this student.

**Students with less relevant prior knowledge.** A student with no proof background may be unable to produce a valid round 1 at all, at which point the rejection cycle has nothing to operate on. Countermeasures: the claims are small enough to be provable in two or three sentences; a worked example is completed with the room before round 1; and the exercise supplies a partial proof for students who request one, so that the rejection cycle can still run. The design accepts that these students practise repair rather than construction on this day and construct at S005.

**Phase 3 is too easy.** A group may characterise the three readers quickly and accurately. This is common and inexpensive, because the fourth-reader task remains hard regardless. Fallback: ask the group to write the *proof* for their fourth reader, which is substantially harder than describing the reader and typically exposes that their reader was underspecified.

**Affective failure.** Two, and they are opposite. The first is that rejection is delivered as personal criticism, or received as such, and the pair's working relationship is damaged for the quarter. Countermeasures: role assignment, required rejection form, and an explicit pre-brief distinguishing insufficiency from error. The second and subtler failure is **collusive softening** — pairs agreeing tacitly to deliver token rejections so that neither is uncomfortable. This is the more likely failure and it is invisible in the artefact unless specifically checked. Countermeasure: the specification failure attaches to the rejecting student, which makes softening costly to the softener rather than to their partner. Whether that is sufficient is unknown.

## 6. Retention architecture

Retrieved by S006 (invariants, where an unstated assumption becomes an unwritten invariant), S009 (testing as hypothesis, where the counterexample becomes the test case), S023 (reduction as an argumentative move), S029 (designing an instrument, where the reader becomes the audience for a measurement), S050 (saying what the measurement does not license) and S083 (communicating risk to a decision-maker).

The S050 and S083 retrievals, at gaps of roughly four and seven quarters, are the day's principal long-range targets and are the professional transposition made real. The claim being tested is that a student asked to defend an evaluation to a hostile reader recognises the situation, rather than treating it as a new genre.

Phase 4 point 5's four failure classes are the day's retained taxonomy — deliberately four items, because a taxonomy the retrieval schedule can maintain over three years must be short.

The day's own schedule: next morning, day 3, week 2, week 6.

## 7. Assessment and evidence generated

No entrustment evidence.

One specification item in MS101, in six binary elements. Item 1 — the three-round artefact with both human rejections recorded verbatim, both repairs, and the round-3 cost statement — is the original specification, unchanged. Items 2 to 6 are new: the rejection-form instructions file and the two agent reviews it governed, the adjudication, the prompt record, the intervention record and the frontier statement.

The rejection-quality requirement attaches to the rejecting student, which is unusual and is stated in advance so that it is not experienced as a trap. **It applies only to the two human rejections.** The agent is not a student, its reviews are adjudicated rather than graded, and the specification does not judge whether the student's adjudication agrees with the assessor's — only that every concern received a verdict with a reason. The design is deliberate: an adjudication assessed for agreement would be a rater-reliability exercise in week four, and rater reliability in this class of judgement is exactly what the [Assessment Architecture](/archive/bsc-programme/program/03-Assessment-Architecture.md)'s published reliability position says is poor.

Programme instrumentation: the phase 1 argument-B distribution is the cohort's baseline disagreement rate on sufficiency; the rate of vague rejections in phase 5 is the collusive-softening indicator described in §5 and is tracked across the quarter; **and the phase 1 item 4 responses — how many rejections students predicted from the agent, and whether they expected it to find argument C's gap — are the fourth point on the frontier map.** Instrumentation rule 9: the map's reader is next year's cohort.

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: reader-relative sufficiency teaches that rigour is negotiable.**

At its strongest: a proof is valid or it is not, and this is not a matter of audience. A day that tells students the required level of detail depends on who is reading risks producing engineers who calibrate rigour to what they can get away with — who supply a thin argument to a busy manager because the manager will accept it. Given that the programme's stated purpose is to certify judgement in exactly the situations where a thin argument is convenient, this is not a small risk.

The rebuttal distinguishes two things the objection conflates. **Validity** is not reader-relative and the day never says it is: an invalid argument fails for every reader, and argument C in phase 1 is included precisely to make that point before anything else is said. What is reader-relative is **what may be left implicit** — which steps need writing down. A proof for a mathematician may assume a standard result; the same proof for a maintainer must say which assumptions the code now depends on. Neither is less rigorous; they discharge different parts of the same obligation.

The objection nonetheless identifies a genuine slippage risk, and the design's protection is thin: it consists of phase 1's ordering and an explicit statement in phase 4 point 2. That is an intention, not a safeguard, and the day would be improved by a task in which a reader-appropriate but *invalid* argument is rejected on validity grounds by a reader who would have accepted a thinner valid one. That task is not currently in the design.

**Objection 2: the mandatory-rejection protocol trains a behaviour that is professionally counterproductive.**

At its strongest: real engineering teams do not reject every artefact they review, and a norm of compulsory objection produces reviewers who manufacture concerns to satisfy a form. This is a recognised pathology in code review. Teaching it in week four, before students have any calibration about when critique is warranted, may install the pathology as a habit.

This objection the design partly concedes. The protocol is a **scaffold**, justified by the week-four context — students will not otherwise deliver substantive critique, and no amount of encouragement changes this reliably — and it is intended to be faded. But the fading is not currently specified anywhere. The day says rejection is mandatory; no later day says it stops being mandatory, and no day teaches the judgement of when a rejection is warranted. That is a genuine gap in the module's design, not merely in this day's, and the correct fix is a phase at S007 or S009 in which the reviewing student must decide whether to reject and defend the decision either way. It is recorded here as an open item rather than resolved.

**The step 5 adjudication is a partial and unplanned discharge of this gap and should be counted as one.** A student asked to classify every concern as genuine-and-addressed, genuine-and-declined, or manufactured is exercising precisely the judgement the objection says the day never teaches — *is this rejection warranted* — with the risk removed, because the reviewer being adjudicated is not a person whose feelings are at stake. That is a cheaper route to the judgement than the S007 phase and it arrives three days earlier. **It does not close the gap**, because the student is judging a review they received rather than deciding whether to issue one, and issuing is the harder half. The S007 commitment stands.

**Objection 3: putting an agent in the reviewer's chair on the day critique is introduced teaches students that review is a thing you obtain rather than a thing you do.**

At its strongest: the day's affective achievement is that a room of week-four students delivers and receives substantive critique. Adding a reviewer that will critique on demand, at no social cost, at any hour, undercuts that achievement in the most direct way possible — by making the expensive thing optional. A student who learns on this day that a review can be requested has learned something that will compete with the thing the day was built to teach for the next three years, and the competition is not close, because one of the two options is free.

The rebuttal is that the competition exists whether or not the day acknowledges it, and that a day which pretended otherwise would be teaching a norm the students would abandon privately within a week — which is [WP-001](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-001.md) §1's argument about study strategies, transposed. The design's response is ordering: **the free reviewer is met only after two costly ones, and it is met as an object of adjudication rather than as a source of judgement.** A student's first experience of an agent review on this day is being required to find something wrong with it.

The objection's residual force is real and is conceded. The design has no evidence that ordering is sufficient, and the honest expectation is that some students will leave the day having learned that reviews are obtainable and having filed the rest as coursework. **The cheap fix available is the one the design has already used for the collusive-softening problem: attach the cost to the party who would benefit from cutting the corner.** Item 3's requirement that a *manufactured* verdict be reasoned out for an empty intervention record to pass does exactly that, and it is why the requirement is worded as a condition on the intervention record rather than as an exhortation.

**Objection 4: the day now teaches against one vendor's default reviewing disposition, and that disposition is undocumented, unstable and possibly not real.**

At its strongest: phase 4 point 7 asserts that where a review standard is unstated the tool supplies an agreeable one. **No vendor documentation states this.** It is an observation from this design's own runs, on one model, at one date, and the day teaches from it. That is precisely the practice the programme forbids everywhere else — the [Microsoft AI Platform Map](/archive/bsc-programme/program/11-Microsoft-AI-Platform-Map.md) prohibits asserting any evaluator's agreement with human judgement on the ground that none is published, and this is the same shape of claim with the same absence behind it.

The objection is correct on the facts and the design's response is to narrow the claim rather than to defend it. **What phase 4 point 7 is entitled to assert is that an unspecified standard is not an absent one, and that the room observed a specific disposition today.** The first half is durable and is what is assessed. The second half is a phase 3 and phase 5 observation, is marked perishable, is stated as an observation of this room on this date, and grounds nothing. The day's material is worded accordingly and the whitepaper records the narrowing here so that a future reader can check whether the wording held.

**The cheap fix is the one this paper is applying**, and it costs a sentence in the material: never state what the tool's disposition *is*, state what the room *observed*, and let the observation do its work as one data point rather than as a property.

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** The room will divide on phase 1 argument B, with neither response commanding a large majority, while agreeing substantially about arguments A and C. *Instrument:* phase 1 response distribution per argument. If the room agrees about B, the arguments were mis-constructed and the day's opening premise is not established for that cohort.

**Prediction 2.** Students with formal proof backgrounds will receive *more* substantive rejections in phase 5 round 1 than students without, when their assigned reader is the maintainer. *Instrument:* rejection counts and rejection specificity, split by the admission background survey.

**Prediction 3.** Vague-rejection rate will decline across S004, S007 and S009 without further intervention, as role-based critique normalises. *Instrument:* rejection specificity coded against the stated form, tracked across the three days. **If it does not decline, the collusive-softening failure mode is active and the specification-failure countermeasure is insufficient.**

**Prediction 4.** At S050, students will spontaneously identify the reader of an evaluation report before writing it, at a higher rate than a comparison cohort. *Instrument:* an S050 protocol coded for unprompted audience identification. Untestable until a variant delivery exists.

**Prediction 5.** The unguided agent review at step 5 will raise fewer substantive concerns than either human rejection, and the review issued under the stated rejection form will raise more concerns of which a higher proportion are adjudicated *manufactured*. *Instrument:* concern counts and adjudication verdicts, per student, across the three reviewers. **If the form-guided review raises more concerns without raising the manufactured proportion, the day's claim that a form produces shape rather than substance is wrong for this cohort**, and §3.2's second unsafe condition must be withdrawn.

**Prediction 6.** Students will predict at phase 1 item 4 that the agent will identify argument C's gap, and it will not. *Instrument:* item 4 responses against the phase 8 run. This is the module's third consecutive item in which the cohort expects a task that looks mechanical to be performed mechanically, after [WP-002](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-002.md) §9 prediction 6 and [WP-003](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-003.md) §9 prediction 6. **If it holds a third time, the finding is no longer a per-day result and is reported to the cohort at S010 as a named cohort-level miscalibration**, which is the branch instrumentation rule 4 requires this instrument to name.

## What a student should take from this paper

**What the design is claiming.** That a proof is an argument addressed to a reader entitled to disbelieve you; that validity is not reader-relative while what may be left implicit is; and that in week four critique becomes possible only when rejection is a role somebody was assigned rather than an opinion somebody volunteered.

**What the evidence licenses.** The entrustment-discussion protocol phase 7 rehearses, and the psychological-safety conditions phases 5 and 6 depend on, are **class 1** — verified here, with the notes cited. Contrasting cases, the generation effect and the documented pattern of template-satisfying proof production are **class 2**, direction and mechanism only. The three-round protocol and the four failure classes are **class 3**: this design's own reasoning, untested. And phase 4 point 7's claim about a reviewer's default disposition rests on nothing published at all — the paper narrows it to what one room observed on one date, and you should quote it that way or not at all.

**What to do with it.**

- **Separate validity from sufficiency.** An invalid argument fails for every reader; argument C exists to establish that before anything else is said.
- **Reject by naming what you were not given.** A vague rejection fails the person delivering it, which is where the cost belongs.
- **Adjudicate a review; do not accept one.** Every concern gets a verdict with a reason, and at least one *manufactured* verdict must be reasoned out before an empty intervention record can pass.
- **A control is not evidence.** A stated rejection form is a configured control, and the review it produced is not evidence that your proof is sufficient. See the five classes on the [Agent Configuration Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/13-Agent-Configuration-Standard.md).

**The concession worth remembering.** The paper concedes that its only protection against reader-relative sufficiency sliding into *rigour is negotiable* is one item's ordering and one sentence in phase 4 — **an intention, not a safeguard** — and that the task which would test it is not in the design. It concedes separately that the mandatory-rejection scaffold has no specified fading anywhere in the module: the day says rejection is compulsory, and no later day says it stops being so.
