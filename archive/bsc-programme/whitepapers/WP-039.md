## In plain terms

**What this paper argues, before the formal version.**

A requirement arrives with conditions attached. Always available. Ten thousand of them. Do not change the existing interface. You treat those as facts about the world and build to them.

They are not facts. Each one was decided by somebody, for a reason, under circumstances that may have stopped applying, and each is revisable in principle. An engineer who does not know that produces one of two failures, and the common one is invisible. **You meet a condition nobody would have missed, at a cost that falls on the work you did not do instead.** Nobody complains, because the system does more than was asked. The rarer failure is worse: you accept something that cannot be done, work proceeds, and the impossibility arrives at the deadline.

What prevents both is a single change of order. **Ask what the constraint is for, before asking whether it can move.** That turns the conversation from a contest into a piece of research, and the engineer in the day's best transcript wins by neither arguing nor refusing but by finding out what the condition was protecting and showing it protected another way.

Then there is what happens when you cannot find out. Every constraint on your list carries either a purpose or an explicit *I do not know*, and a list with no such marks fails the specification. That looks perverse and it is the most useful thing the day asks for: **a student who claims to know the purpose of every constraint has stopped asking**, and the marks are a map of exactly where negotiation is currently impossible and who could make it possible.

The last idea is the ethical one. If you offer somebody a trade they cannot assess, and they agree, you have obtained agreement and nothing else. They have consented to something they do not understand, and the cost arrives later, attached to your name. So evaluability is part of the offer, not a courtesy.

**What this paper concedes, and it is a real concession.** The whole method assumes a requester who can and will tell you what a condition is for. The constraints that actually stop work in organisations are protecting something nobody will say out loud — a budget line, a commitment, a boundary somebody is defending. The paper tells you the method is unsafe there. **It does not teach you to tell *cannot* from *will not***, which are different situations needing different responses, and it names the boundary without giving you any way of noticing that you have crossed it.

## Abstract

This paper defends treating a requirement as a revisable agreement rather than a given, and defends requiring students to mark the constraints whose purpose they cannot state. The first argument is that constraint mobility is not a property of the constraint but of whether anyone can say what it is for, that most stated constraints are movable once their purpose is identified, and that the two failures students otherwise produce — silent over-delivery and silent acceptance of the impossible — are both consequences of never asking. The second argument is that a do-not-know mark is the day's most valuable output because it identifies the constraints where negotiation is currently impossible and locates the person who could make it possible, which is a finding rather than a gap. The paper additionally defends the requirement that a trade be evaluable by the party accepting it, and defends the day's practical turn — that students assemble the utilisation evidence themselves rather than receive it — on the ground that the constraint they are negotiating has an owner who has published what will persuade them, which converts the day's method from a rhetorical claim into a research task with a stated target. It concedes that the day teaches negotiation as an evidence problem while conceding in its own scope statement that the hardest cases are not, that its live negotiation is with a party whose incentives are not a real requester's, and that the evidence pack is the most likely place in the module for a build to displace the thinking it was meant to support.

## 1. The instructional problem this day must solve

A requirement arrives with constraints attached: this must be available at all times, this must handle ten thousand of them, this must not change the existing interface. Students treat these as facts about the world and build to them.

They are not facts. Each was decided by somebody, for a reason, under conditions that may no longer hold, and each is revisable in principle. The engineer who does not know this produces one of two failures.

**Silent over-delivery** is the common one and is invisible. A constraint that was never load-bearing — an availability target nobody would have missed, a scale requirement based on a projection that did not happen — is met at a cost that falls on work not done. Nobody complains, because the system does more than was asked. Point 6 names this and the reason it goes unexamined.

**Silent acceptance of the impossible** is the rarer and more damaging one: a constraint that cannot be met is accepted, work proceeds, and the impossibility surfaces at the deadline. S024's duty applies but requires the engineer to have noticed, which requires having examined the constraint.

The move that prevents both is at point 2: **ask what the constraint is for before asking whether it can move.** Transcript D's engineer in phase 3 succeeds not by arguing or refusing but by discovering the constraint's purpose and showing it served another way — which is a research act rather than a negotiating one.

The day's second problem is what to do when the purpose cannot be found. Step 3 requires each constraint to carry a purpose statement or an explicit do-not-know, and the specification **fails a list with no do-not-know marks**. The clause looks perverse and is the day's most useful requirement: a student who claims to know the purpose of every constraint has stopped asking, and the marks identify precisely where negotiation is blocked and who could unblock it.

The third problem is transcript C, which is the subtlest case in the phase. An engineer who offers a trade the requester cannot evaluate obtains agreement, and the agreement is worthless — the requester has consented to something they do not understand, and the cost surfaces later as a complaint about the engineer. Point 5 makes evaluability a requirement of the trade, which is the day's ethical core and connects to the concealment question S013 phase 7 left open eighteen weeks earlier.

**The fourth problem is one the platform solves rather than creates, and it is why this day was the easiest in the module to re-anchor.** Point 2's method — find the purpose before testing the mobility — is asserted, and a student can complete a paper exercise without ever meeting a constraint whose purpose is discoverable. The substrate's own capacity constraint is not like that. Model-deployment quota is a policy limit that is explicitly not a capacity guarantee, it is increased by asking, and the party who decides has published the criterion: **priority goes to those actively using their existing allocation, and requests that do not meet that condition may be denied.** That is a constraint's purpose, stated by its owner, with the persuading evidence named. The day's method stops being a recommendation and becomes a specification for an artefact — which is what step 5 builds.

## 3. Theoretical basis for the design

### 3.1 Load profile and element interactivity

**Moderate cognitively, high socially.** The classification at step 2 and the purpose identification at step 3 are individually simple; the interactivity is at step 6, where cost evidence, purpose evidence and an alternative must be assembled into a single trade that the other party can assess.

The genuine demand is step 7: a live negotiation with real people, six minutes, in front of the room, on a constraint the student has decided to challenge.

**Step 5 is where the day's load could get away from it**, and the containment is that the query is small and the discipline is large. The student is not building a monitoring system; they are running one query, splitting it one way, and stating one floor. The design's position is that a student who cannot do that in twenty minutes with an agent has a problem the day should surface rather than route around.

Other management:

- **Step 3 is worked with the room,** because asking what a constraint is for is an unfamiliar move and students otherwise produce restatements.
- **The trade form is supplied** at step 6.
- **Four transcripts supplied at phase 3** with their costs and their evidence packs, so groups judge and rebuild rather than construct from nothing.
- **Six minutes per student,** bounded by the requester's availability.
- **The requirement, the vocabulary, the observation record and the telemetry are all the student's own** and are one to four days old.

### 3.2 Desirable difficulties selected, and those deliberately not

**Selected.**

- *Predicting constraint mobility,* item 4, against the substrate's real history.
- *Transcript C,* where agreement was obtained and was worthless.
- *Rebuilding four engineers' evidence packs,* phase 3, and discovering that three of them had the same data available and did not use it.
- *Marking what you do not know,* step 3 — the day's discriminator.
- *Assembling an evaluable trade,* step 6.
- *Stating your own evidence's floor,* step 5.
- *Negotiating live,* step 7.
- *The absent-party test,* phase 7, with a one-question limit.

**Deliberately not selected.**

- *Negotiation technique.* No frameworks, no tactics, no scripts. The day's position is that the preparation is the negotiation and that technique taught without preparation produces transcript C.
- *Requirements notation.* None.
- *Capacity planning.* The evidence pack describes consumption; it does not forecast it. Forecasting is IS301's material and would convert the day into a capacity day.
- *Multi-party negotiation.* Named at point 3's scope statement and deferred to S082 and S085.
- *Constraints protecting unstated interests.* Named and deferred; the day's method does not address them and says so.

**Scope on the enumeration, per the [Whitepaper Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md), strong form.** Point 3's four constraint kinds omit the constraint that exists to protect an interest nobody will state — a budget line, a reporting relationship, a prior commitment. **Safe** when the requester can and will tell you what a constraint is for, which is this day's situation. **Unsafe** when they cannot or will not: treating such a constraint as an evidence problem will fail, and pressing for a purpose that is being withheld will damage the relationship the negotiation depends on. That case is S082's and it is the more common one in large organisations, which the day states.

**Scope on the platform instance, strong form.** The day teaches purpose-before-mobility using a quota with a published criterion, and the correspondence must carry its boundary because the published criterion is the least representative feature of the instance.

*Safe.* Assembling evidence against a criterion the other party has stated; distinguishing a policy limit from a physical one; checking what a figure excludes before using it to overrule somebody; and recognising that the quantity you were asked for may not be the quantity that is purchasable. All four transfer.

*Unsafe* in two directions, and the day states both. **It is unsafe as a claim that constraints come with published criteria.** Almost none do. This one does because a commercial party has an interest in rationing transparently, and a student who goes looking for the published criterion, finds none, and concludes there is no criterion has learned to stop at the point where the real work starts. **And it is unsafe as a general model of the counterparty.** The party enforcing this quota is a vendor with a documented policy and no relationship with the student; the party enforcing most constraints is a colleague with an unstated reason and a long memory. **The published criterion makes the method legible and makes the situation atypical**, and the day pays for the first with the second. Point 11 states the boundary to the room, and step 3's do-not-know marks are where a student who expects every constraint to be documented finds out otherwise.

### 3.3 Engagement and motivational mechanism

The day runs on **a prediction the substrate's history refutes**.

Item 4 asks how many of five stated constraints will turn out to be movable. Students predict one or two. The substrate's actual history for that requirement is checkable and the answer is usually three or four, and the checkability is what makes the finding land — this is not a claim about requirements in general but a fact about a requirement in this system.

Phase 3's rebuild supplies the day's second and more useful finding. Groups reconstruct four engineers' evidence packs and discover that the difference between the one that worked and the three that did not is not skill, nerve or seniority: **three of them had the same telemetry available and one of them split it by outcome.** That is a fact about an artefact rather than about a person, and it is the form in which the day's method is most easily believed.

Step 7 supplies the day's most engaging and most exposed twenty minutes. Real people, six minutes, a constraint the student has chosen to challenge, in front of the room. Students prepare for this more thoroughly than for anything else in the module.

Phase 8's closing — the requester and the capacity owner say which negotiation they found most useful and why — is the third external judgement in three days, after S038's expert preference and S037's user session. The design has concentrated external judgement in the module's back half deliberately: the module's whole claim is that the answers are outside the room.

The competence risk is at step 7 and is real: a student whose negotiation fails in front of the room has failed publicly. Countermeasures: the specification assesses the preparation and the recording, not the outcome; a recorded refusal with its reason passes; and the requester is briefed to refuse clearly and give a reason rather than to soften.

## 4. Phase-by-phase design and its warrant

**Phase 1 (25 min).** Five stated constraints. Item 2's exchange question is asked before students know that trades require purpose evidence, and their answers — usually offers of scope or time — are compared at phase 4 against what transcript D offered instead. **Item 5 is the sealed Copilot prediction**, administered here per instrumentation rule 8, and it asks about the student's own consumption rather than about the agent's competence, which is the only form in which the prediction is checkable within the day.

**Phase 2 (20 min).** The bridge asks which S029 step corresponds to what will change depending on the answer, and what its requirements equivalent is. The answer is that a requirement is justified by the decision it enables, and a requirement whose decision nobody can name is a candidate for removal — which is the day's frame delivered as retrieval.

**Phase 3 (45 min).** Four transcripts with real cost outcomes, read; **four evidence packs, rebuilt**. Transcript C is the phase's payload and is the one groups rate most highly before the outcomes are supplied. The closing task — what did D's engineer know — has a one-word answer that students reliably fail to produce and immediately accept. **The rebuild is what converts the phase from a judgement about four people into a checkable property of four artefacts**, and its finding is the one the day most needs: the difference was a dimension, and it was available to all four. The quota-increase submission itself is demonstrated rather than operated and is not assessed, because the programme does not submit speculative capacity requests on a live subscription and could not control the outcome if it did.

**Phase 4 (50 min).** Fourteen points. Point 2 is the day's method. Point 3 carries the enumeration scope statement. Point 5 is the ethical core. Point 8 exists to prevent the day producing engineers who negotiate everything, which is the module's characteristic overreach in its final form. **Point 9 supplies the platform's version of point 2** — a constraint whose owner publishes its purpose and the evidence that moves it — and **point 10 names a default the platform will not let you choose**, which is the [Copilot Practice Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md) requirement and is chosen because a student who does not know that requests per minute are not independently purchasable will agree in phase 5 to something that cannot be bought. Point 11 carries the platform scope statement in its strong form.

**Points 12 and 13 were added 2026-07-30** on a reading of the engineering playbook in full, which recovered two artefact specifications the site does not index. *This paragraph previously read "Eleven points", and the preceding paragraph previously read "Thirteen points"; both are superseded and are quoted rather than deleted, because the sequence of readings is what the count records.* **Point 12 is a fact rather than an argument and is taught as one**: the playbook's story-scope and milestone-scope design reviews differ by exactly one heading, `Operationalization`, and by nothing else — which the design takes as the most precise available statement of what design scope is, namely the point at which a design acquires a runtime owner. A student can verify it in a minute against the source, which is why it is expected to survive where a paragraph about proportionate design writing would not. **Point 13 restricts rather than adds.** The playbook's non-functional-requirement form has eight fields; this design marks two, on the ground that a requirement which cannot name its verification method has not been written and the remaining six are useful without being evidence. The same page's threshold catalogue is handed to students **labelled sourceless**, which is the day's second unwarranted-magnitude specimen and the subtler of the two — the catalogue makes no claim at all, and will be read as a benchmark by anyone who does not check.

**Point 14 was added on the same date and closes a gap points 12 and 13 opened.** Those two specify what a design document contains; neither says how it gets agreed, and a specification of contents that is silent about the agreement has described a form rather than a practice. The source supplies the workflow — design documents in a repository directory excluded from continuous-integration triggers, reviewed by pull request with named reviewers — and one rule inside it: **after two round trips of question and response, the thread moves to a synchronous conversation.** **The design teaches the rule's construction rather than the rule**, because the construction is the transferable part: the bound is a count of exchanges and not a duration, so crossing it is established by reading the thread rather than by adjudicating whether a delay was reasonable, and escalation becomes the process instead of an admission that the writing failed. That is point 5's evaluability requirement applied to a process rather than to a trade, which is why the point is placed after point 13 and not before point 12. **The point also carries an observation about the source rather than from it**: the playbook states exactly one service level anywhere on the site, and it is a review window for pull requests that are not urgent. A standard that prescribes artefacts, prohibitions and named roles throughout, and commits once to how long anybody will wait for anything, is telling a reader which of its instructions somebody expects to be held to — and that inference is this design's, not the source's, and is recorded as such in the evidence status.

**Phase 5 (90 min).** Eight steps at class 3 with a live requester and a live capacity owner. Step 3's do-not-know marks are the discriminator. **Step 5 is the practical turn and its placement before step 6 is deliberate**: a trade assembled from evidence you gathered is a different object from one assembled from evidence you were given, and the difference shows in what the student is willing to concede. Step 8's three-part recording reuses S034 without re-teaching, which is the design's standing method for making a prior day's apparatus free. **Steps 1 and 6 were extended 2026-07-30**: a non-functional requirement is stated in the eight-field form, and the trade is written at a named scope in the template that matches it. **Neither extension adds work to the phase and both add a judgement to it** — the form is a container for something the student was already producing, and choosing the scope takes a minute and is the part that can be got wrong.

**Phase 6 (40 min).** An inherited constraint with no purpose, discovered two years late, then the agent case. The attention to who could have said so and why nobody asked is the first disclosure's content and is the argument for step 3's marks. The agent case is the module's third and last encounter with the same failure — a correct query against a metric whose definition excludes what was being asked about — and it is placed here because **this is the first time the room sees it used against a person.** A measurement error becomes an ethical failure at the moment it overrules somebody, which is point 5's evaluable-trade requirement seen from the other end.

**Phase 7 (25 min).** The absent-party test with one question. The one-question limit is the phase's design: an agreement that requires extended explanation to survive contact with someone who was not present is not recorded adequately, and the limit makes that checkable.

**Phase 8 (20 min).** Predictions against the substrate's history, the do-not-know distribution, **item 5 opened**, the requester's and capacity owner's own preferences, and **the statement of what was simulated and what was not** — which is discussed in §8.

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**Students with more relevant prior knowledge.** A student with workplace negotiating experience will handle step 7 fluently. Predicted failure: they negotiate on scope and time — the currencies they know — rather than on purpose, and will obtain a concession without discovering what the constraint was for. That is transcript A or C depending on how it lands. Countermeasure: step 6 requires all three components including purpose evidence, and a trade submitted without it fails the specification regardless of whether the negotiation succeeded.

**Students with less relevant prior knowledge.** Step 7 is intimidating and six minutes is short. Countermeasures: the preparation is the assessed content; the requester is briefed to be direct; and a recorded refusal passes. Residual risk: a student may prepare well and freeze, and the design's only remedy is that the prepared trade is submitted in writing regardless of what happened in the room.

**Students whose evidence pack becomes the day.** New, and the practical turn's characteristic failure. A student who finds the query more tractable than the conversation will produce an elaborate pack and a thin purpose analysis, and will arrive at step 7 with numbers and no argument. Countermeasures: step 5 is bounded to one query, one split and one stated floor; the specification does not assess the pack's sophistication; and step 6 requires purpose evidence that the pack cannot supply, which means an elaborate pack cannot substitute for the missing component. Residual risk: the student presents the pack anyway and the requester, who cannot evaluate it, agrees — which is transcript C, produced by the day's own build, and the design finds that uncomfortable enough to have written §8's third objection about it.

**Phase 3 is too easy.** Transcripts A and B are judged correctly by nearly all groups. Transcript C is rated highly before the outcome is revealed, which is the phase's finding. Fallback if a group identifies C's problem: ask what the engineer should have done given that the requester genuinely could not evaluate the trade, which has no clean answer and previews S083.

**Affective failure.** Two.

The first is **negotiation experienced as adversarial**. A student who frames step 7 as a contest will press, and a requester who feels pressed will refuse on principle. Countermeasure: point 2's framing makes the move investigative rather than positional, and phase 3's transcript D models it. **The expert-politeness countermeasure imported from [S038](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S038.md) applies here in its inverse form** — the requester is briefed to refuse clearly rather than to soften, for the same reason the domain expert is briefed to stumble rather than to accommodate, and per instrumentation rule 5 this is a repertoire remedy applied rather than rediscovered.

The second is **the do-not-know marks read as failure**. A student who marks four of five constraints unknown may feel they have prepared badly, when they have produced the day's most useful artefact. Countermeasure: the specification fails a list *without* marks, which is stated before step 3 begins, and phase 8's distribution shows the cohort's counts.

No entrustment judgement — the quarter's is at S040.

One specification item in PE201. Per the [Assessment Architecture](/archive/bsc-programme/program/03-Assessment-Architecture.md)'s rule 1, the checked elements are: the requirement stated in the S038 vocabulary **and, where it is non-functional, in the playbook's eight-field form**; constraints classified by kind; a purpose statement or explicit do-not-know for each; an identified highest-leverage constraint; **an evidence pack with its outcome split and its stated sampling floor**; a trade containing all three components **and written at a named scope in the matching design-review template**; a recorded negotiation outcome; an agreement or refusal in three-part form; **the phase 7 absent-party outcome including the question asked** — which is required by rule 3, since phase 7 performs a discrimination the specification would otherwise discard; **and the prompt record, intervention record and frontier statement required by the [Copilot Practice Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md)**. **Nothing else is assessed**, including whether the negotiation succeeded and including the pack's sophistication.

**The two elements added 2026-07-30 are narrower than they look and the narrowing is the point.** Of the eight fields, **only the measurement or metric and the verification method are marked**; the other six are required and unassessed, because a form marked field by field is a form filled field by field, which is the failure the playbook's own worked trade study exhibits against its own template. And the scope element marks the **choice** rather than the completion — a fully populated template at the wrong scope fails, and a thin one at the right scope with a stated reason does not.

**A constraint list with no do-not-know marks fails.**

**A non-functional requirement with no verification method fails**, and **a threshold justified by the playbook's example catalogue fails**. The catalogue is supplied labelled sourceless; borrowing from it is a citation of an illustration.

**An evidence pack that reports an availability or success figure without stating which response codes it excludes fails.** This is the day's newest fail condition and it is the phase 6 case converted into a rule: the failure it names is one the room will have watched happen to a person an hour earlier.

**An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that the pack's own design would have caught an intervention-worthy error. A stated sampling floor, or an assertion that the status-code split accounts for the whole request count, both satisfy it.

Programme instrumentation: the item 4 mobility prediction against the substrate's history is the cohort's constraint-mobility baseline, re-probed at S064; the do-not-know distribution is the purpose-inquiry measure; the phase 7 absent-party acceptance rate is the agreement-recording measure carried to S085; **the requester's and the capacity owner's phase 8 preferences are recorded verbatim across cohorts**; **item 5's accuracy is the day's frontier-calibration datum**, read by the following year's cohort through the [Durable and Perishable Register](/archive/bsc-programme/program/09-Durable-and-Perishable-Register.md) per instrumentation rule 9.

Dispositions: **integrity**, at step 8, where recording a refusal accurately is less attractive than recording a partial agreement.

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: the day teaches negotiation as an evidence problem and concedes in its own scope statement that the hardest cases are not.**

At its strongest: point 3's scope statement admits that constraints protecting unstated interests are common, are not addressed by evidence, and belong to S082. But those are the constraints that actually block work in organisations — a budget line somebody owns, a commitment somebody made, a boundary somebody is defending. The day's entire method assumes a requester who can and will explain their reasoning, which describes a cooperative individual and not an organisational setting. A student who leaves believing that purpose-inquiry moves constraints will apply it where it does not work, will press a requester who cannot answer, and will damage a relationship the module spent four days establishing.

**The re-anchoring makes this objection stronger rather than weaker.** The day now teaches against a constraint whose owner has published its purpose *and* the evidence that moves it, which is the most cooperative counterparty imaginable and is not a counterparty at all in the ordinary sense — it is a policy. A student who succeeds against a published criterion has practised the easiest instance of the method on the easiest available object.

The rebuttal is that the sequencing is defensible. Purpose-inquiry is the correct first move even where it will fail, because its failure is informative: a requester who cannot state a constraint's purpose has told you something, and the do-not-know mark at step 3 is precisely how that is recorded. The method does not claim to move every constraint; it claims to identify which ones it cannot. And the published criterion is used to make the *method* legible, not to make the *situation* representative — which point 11's scope statement now says to the room in exactly those terms.

The concession is unchanged and is not repaired by the re-anchoring: the day does not teach the *failure mode's signature*. A student is told the method is unsafe when the requester cannot or will not explain, and is not taught to distinguish cannot from will not — which are different situations with different responses, and which S082 addresses at a level of organisational analysis this day cannot reach. **The strong-form scope statement names the boundary and supplies no way to detect that you have crossed it**, which is the same weakness [WP-031](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-031.md) §8 identified in a different register and which the standard's strong form was introduced to fix. That it recurs here suggests the strong form is necessary and not sufficient.

**Objection 2: the requester in step 7 has none of a real requester's incentives.**

At its strongest: the negotiation is with a substrate stakeholder who has agreed to spend thirty minutes with a class. They are not defending a budget, not accountable to anyone for the outcome, not going to live with the consequence, and are aware that a teaching exercise is occurring. Every pressure that makes a real negotiation hard is absent, and a student who succeeds here has learned that constraints move when you ask nicely with evidence — which will not survive contact with a requester who has something at stake.

This objection the design does not fully answer. The requester is real and their requirement is real, which is more than a role-play supplies, and the constraints are ones they actually stated. **The capacity owner, added at the re-anchoring, is a genuine improvement and a partial one**: they do have something at stake, since the substrate's ceiling is theirs to defend, and they are the only party in the room who can say no and mean it. But the incentive structure is still not reproducible: nobody in this arrangement loses their own budget.

Two things follow. The first is that step 7's *outcome* is correctly excluded from the specification — the design already assesses the preparation and the recording rather than the result, which is the right response to an unrepresentative negotiation and was arrived at for a different reason.

The second is that the day should say this to students. **The repair this paper named is now made**: phase 8 states plainly that the easiest part of a real negotiation has been simulated and the hardest part has not, and names S085 as where the rest arrives. That closes what this paper recorded as the second instance in this module of a known limitation documented in a whitepaper and withheld from the students it concerns, after [WP-036](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-036.md) §8's undisclosed curation — **and both instances are now disclosed, which does not retire the finding, because in both cases the disclosure cost one sentence and was omitted until a whitepaper was written about it.**

**Objection 3: the evidence pack is the module's most likely place for a build to displace the thinking it was meant to support.**

This is the objection that bites on the practical turn, and on this day it is sharper than on any other in the module, because the day already contains the failure it predicts.

At its strongest: the day's method is purpose-before-mobility, and the pack contains no purpose evidence — it cannot, because consumption data says what happened and never says what a constraint was for. So the practical turn adds an artefact that addresses one of the trade's three components and is by far the most visible of the three. A student under time pressure will finish the pack, because it is finishable, and will take an unfinished purpose analysis into the room. **They will then present numbers to a requester who cannot evaluate them, and obtain agreement — which is transcript C, reproduced by a student who spent the morning learning why transcript C fails, using an artefact the day required them to build.** The design has installed its own worst case as a default path.

The rebuttal has two parts and the first is genuine. **Step 6 fails a trade missing any of its three components**, so a pack without purpose evidence does not satisfy the specification however good it is — the day cannot prevent the student from presenting it, and it does not credit them for it. And point 5's evaluability requirement bites on the pack directly: a figure the requester cannot assess is not evidence in the day's sense, it is decoration, and phase 6's case shows what decoration costs when somebody acts on it.

The second part is weaker and the design states it as weak. The pack is bounded — one query, one split, one floor — precisely so that it cannot expand to fill the phase. That is a design intention rather than a mechanism, and nothing enforces it beyond the specification's silence about sophistication.

**The objection is conceded and the fix is named and made.** The fix is the ordering and the fail condition together: step 5 comes after step 3's purpose work rather than before it, so the pack is built to support an argument the student has already had to make; and the specification's new clause fails any pack that reports a figure without stating what it excludes, which is the one property that turns a number into evidence. **The fix that is named and not made** is the stronger one: requiring the requester to state, at step 7, whether they could evaluate what they were shown, and recording that in the artefact. It would put point 5's ethical core inside the assessed record and it costs one question. It is not made because the requester is already carrying a preference statement at phase 8 and the design is not confident it can ask them for a second judgement about each student — which is a resource argument of exactly the kind [WP-038](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-038.md) §8 called the weakest in that paper, arriving here one day later and no better.

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** Item 4 predictions will be lower than the substrate's actual mobility count for a large majority. *Instrument:* predictions against the recorded history of that requirement.

**Prediction 2.** Transcript C will be rated in the top two by a majority of groups before outcomes are supplied. *Instrument:* phase 3 committed rankings.

**Prediction 3.** Do-not-know counts will be lower on first attempt than after the phase 6 clinic, for students permitted to revise. *Instrument:* step 3 marks before and after, where revision occurs.

**Prediction 4.** Students with workplace negotiating experience will produce trades missing the purpose-evidence component at a higher rate than students without. *Instrument:* trade component completeness split by the admission background survey. **This is the wrong-currency failure stated as a prediction.**

**Prediction 5.** In phase 3's rebuild, a majority of groups will reproduce pack A, B or C without noticing that the status-code dimension was available to those engineers. *Instrument:* group rebuild artefacts coded for whether the split was applied unprompted. **A high unprompted rate would indicate S037's reconciliation transferred one day later, which would be the strongest transfer evidence in the module and is not what the design expects.**

**Prediction 6.** Evidence packs will be rated substantially higher in completeness than purpose analyses by the same students, and the gap will be larger for students who completed the pack first where ordering was not enforced. *Instrument:* blind-rated completeness on both, paired within student. **This is objection 3's failure mode stated as a prediction, and the second half is the test of whether the ordering fix does any work.**

**Prediction 7.** Where a student's decision genuinely sits at the milestone scope, a majority will nonetheless select the story-scope template, and the omitted section will be `Operationalization` rather than any other. *Instrument:* step 6 scope selections adjudicated against the requester's own statement of who will operate the result. **The prediction is about the specific omission, not about the error rate: a student who under-scopes by leaving out risks or dependencies has made an ordinary mistake, and a student who under-scopes by leaving out operation has made the one this day exists to name.**

**Prediction 8.** Among students who state a numeric threshold in the eight-field form, those given the playbook's catalogue will justify their number by reference to it at a higher rate than they justify it from their own evidence, on first attempt and despite the sourceless label. *Instrument:* step 1 thresholds coded for justification source. **A high rate would show the label doing no work, which is the result the design expects and the reason the catalogue is handed over at all rather than withheld.**
**Prediction 9 — point 14's own, added 2026-07-30.** Asked at the next-morning retrieval to restate the escalation rule, **a majority of students who reproduce it correctly in substance will state it as a duration rather than as a count** — *after a day or two*, *if it drags on* — and the reversion rate will be higher among students who have worked in an organisation with a stated response-time norm. *Instrument:* the next-morning between-day responses coded into three classes — count-stated, duration-stated, and neither — split by the admission background survey. **The prediction is that the rule's content survives and its form does not**, which matters because the form is the whole of what makes it checkable; if the count is retained at a high rate, point 14's second half is doing no work and the point can be compressed to the workflow alone.

## What a student should take from this paper

**What the design is claiming.** That a constraint is not a fact but a decision somebody made, under conditions that may no longer hold — so the question is what it is for, asked before whether it can move. It also claims a trade the other party cannot evaluate is worthless even when they agree to it, and that marking a constraint whose purpose you cannot find is the day's most useful output.

**What the evidence licenses.** Requirements as negotiated agreements, purpose inquiry and over-delivery as an invisible cost are **class 1**, grounded in [research/05-fde-craft/fisher-ury-getting-to-yes.md](/research/05-fde-craft/fisher-ury-getting-to-yes.md) and [research/05-fde-craft/beyer-holtzblatt-contextual-design.md](/research/05-fde-craft/beyer-holtzblatt-contextual-design.md). The quota is **class 4**, read on 26 July 2026: increase requests are documented as prioritising customers who actively use their existing allocation, **with no turnaround, no approval rate and no definition of what active use means**. **That purpose-before-mobility is teachable in a day is class 3.** You may assemble evidence against the published criterion; you may not predict the outcome.

**What to do with it.**

- Mark the constraints whose purpose you cannot state. A list with no marks means you stopped asking.
- Before offering a trade, ask whether the other party can assess what you are offering. If they cannot, their agreement is worthless and the cost comes back to you.
- **Human approval is a stop condition and not a field an agent may fill**, per the [Agent Configuration Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/13-Agent-Configuration-Standard.md). No evidence pack decides a quota increase; a person does, and your job is to give them what they said would persuade them.
- Never report an availability or success figure without saying which response codes it excludes.

**The concession worth remembering.** The paper concedes that its method assumes a requester who can and will explain their reasoning, while the constraints that actually block work protect interests nobody will state. It tells you the method is unsafe when a requester cannot or will not answer, and it does not teach you to tell *cannot* from *will not*, which are different situations with different responses. The boundary is named and no way of detecting that you have crossed it is supplied.
