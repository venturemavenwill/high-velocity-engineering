## In plain terms

**What this paper argues, before the formal version.**

Ask a room what could go wrong with a component and you will get a long list. The list is not wrong and you cannot do anything with it, because its entries are hazards rather than threats. *The model could be biased. Someone could feed it bad data.* Nobody is named in either sentence, so neither has a cost, a difficulty or a likelihood anyone can argue about.

**A threat has an actor, with access, and a goal.** Those three are what turn a worry into something you can prioritise, and the day makes students produce the worry list first and then dismantle it themselves, because watching your own comprehensive-feeling list collapse under a question you asked is worth more than being given the definition.

The second argument is about the list you are *not* going to handle. Students who take security seriously produce threat models in which everything is covered, and that is not diligence — it is either an unfinished enumeration or a claim no real system supports. **Naming what you are not handling, with its consequence and the condition that would change your mind, is the output rather than the confession**, and a model with an empty accept list fails.

The third is the uncomfortable one. Harm is intuitively indexed to intent, so the actor with no goal at all — the one who is simply careless — gets ranked last or left off entirely. Multiply likelihood by damage and they frequently come top. That reframes the whole subject from defeating an adversary to managing a consequence, which is a better thing to have learned in an afternoon.

**What this paper concedes, and it is a real concession.** The day ends with four published checks run by hand against the student's own deployment template. They are short, they are answerable, they take minutes, and they are the only part of the day that finishes. **Students weight what closes.** So a cohort whose first security session ends with four boxes will carry four boxes, and everything that actually transfers will be remembered as context around a checklist. The design's position is that a strand arriving this late is better served by a floor a student can stand on than by a syllabus the programme has no room for — which defends the decision and does not answer the objection. Worse, the checklist being genuinely good is what makes this dangerous: **a bad checklist would be safer.** Nothing in the programme yet re-runs those checks against anything larger, so the floor is installed and nobody is shown building on it.

## Abstract

This paper defends requiring every threat to name an actor with access and a goal, and defends failing a threat model whose accept list is empty. The first argument is that students asked what could go wrong produce hazards rather than threats, that a hazard has no feasibility and no cost and therefore cannot be prioritised, and that the actor requirement converts an unbounded worry list into a decidable set. The second argument is that an empty accept list is not a strong threat model but an unfinished one. The paper additionally defends including a careless actor with no goal, and defends one change that alters the day's character: **the four published infrastructure-as-code checks are run by hand against the deployment template that will deploy the student's own model two days later.** They are run because they can be — four yes-or-no questions about a file a student can read — while the product that automates them requires a paid plan and tenant rights the programme does not hold and is therefore demonstrated rather than operated. The room's first finding is that the template fails, not through carelessness but because the fastest path to a working deployment has no network isolation and the isolation cannot be added later without redeploying. It concedes that this is the security strand's first day and carries a taxonomy whose most consequential omission is where real incidents come from, that its central estimation step asked for a number it had not equipped students to reason about, and that four checks is a floor so low that teaching it may license the belief that security is a checklist.

## 1. The instructional problem this day must solve

Asked what could go wrong with a component, students produce a list. The list is long, it is not wrong, and it cannot be acted on — because its entries are hazards rather than threats. "The model could be biased." "Someone could feed it bad data." "It might be wrong about an important case." None of these has an actor, so none has a feasibility, a cost to the attacker, or a probability that can be estimated.

The actor requirement is the day's central move and it is deliberately produced rather than stated. Item 1 asks for the list, item 2 asks who would have to do what for each entry, and item 3 asks which entries have no actor. Typically more than half the list falls away, in the student's own handwriting, which is a stronger foundation than a definition.

The day's second problem is what makes a learned component different. Its behaviour depends on data somebody supplied, which means an upstream contributor is inside the trust boundary. It can be probed, which means query access is a capability rather than an interaction. Its outputs may reveal properties of its training data. And its failures can be induced by inputs that look ordinary, which means input validation does not help. None of these is a code defect and none appears in a review of the implementation.

The third problem is the accept list. Students who take security seriously produce threat models in which everything is handled, and this is not diligence — it is either an unfinished enumeration or a claim no real system supports. Point 6 imports S033's discipline directly: name what you are not handling, with probability and consequence. **The specification fails an empty accept list**, which is the day's sharpest clause and its most likely to be misread.

The fourth problem is the strand's position. This is the security strand's first seminar day, in week forty-four, and the [Outcomes and Accreditation Map](/archive/bsc-programme/program/01-Outcomes-and-Accreditation-Map.md) already records that as a concession rather than a design. The day cannot repair the lateness and must not pretend to cover the strand.

## 3. Theoretical basis for the design

### 3.1 Load profile and element interactivity

**Moderate.** The individual concepts are simple and the interactivity is at step 4, where probability and consequence must be estimated for actors whose behaviour is chosen rather than distributed — which is genuinely harder than S033's failure probabilities and is discussed in §8.

Management:

- **Actors supplied at phase 3** with the access model revealed afterwards, so groups reason rather than enumerate.
- **Step 2 worked with the room,** since enumerating actors is unfamiliar and students otherwise produce two.
- **The estimation form supplied** at step 4, carrying the confidence field that the [Assessment Architecture](/archive/bsc-programme/program/03-Assessment-Architecture.md)'s rule 2 now requires.
- **The student's own model,** trained and evaluated, so no new context.
- **One detection signal** at step 6, not a monitoring design.

### 3.2 Desirable difficulties selected, and those deliberately not

**Selected.**

- *The list-then-filter construction,* items 1 to 3.
- *Actor D,* the careless insider with no goal.
- *The access-model reveal,* where two actors have more access than assumed.
- *Requiring an accept list,* step 5 — the day's discriminator.
- *The timing demand,* phase 7: does the signal fire in time.
- *Estimating adversarial probability with stated confidence,* step 4.

**Deliberately not selected.**

- *Attack implementation.* No attacks are executed. S076 and S077 do that, against generative components, with the substrate protections the [Substrate and Infrastructure](/archive/bsc-programme/program/10-Substrate-and-Infrastructure.md) page specifies.
- *Infrastructure security.* SG201's, and named as the scope statement's principal omission.
- *Formal threat-modelling methodologies.* A structure is supplied; the methodology apparatus would substitute for the reasoning.
- *Generative-model attacks.* Deferred to S076 with a named day, per the standard's deferral register.

**Scope, per the [Whitepaper Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md), strong form.** Point 3's four attack families cover attacks on the model. They omit attacks on the surrounding infrastructure — credentials, transport, storage, dependencies — **which are where most real incidents involving machine-learning systems originate**, and they omit attacks specific to generative components. **Safe** as a starting taxonomy for a supervised component the student owns and is about to deploy. **Unsafe** as a security review of a deployed system, which must cover the infrastructure this list excludes; a student who applies this taxonomy as a review will produce a document that is complete on its own terms and silent about the likely cause.

That omission is assigned to SG201 and, per the standard's cross-course clause, **the artefact now carries an element requiring the student to state which SG201 material they applied** — the repair to the rule violation §8 recorded, made at step 1 and annotated at the point of change on the seminar page.

**The platform instance, scoped in the strong form.** The day runs four published checks against a real deployment template and shows a product it does not run.

*Safe.* Reasoning about whether a specific template is reachable from the public internet, authenticates with a copyable secret, or runs as an identity rather than a key; about why the default posture is the exposed one; and about why a configuration decision taken at creation time can stop being a decision. Each of the four checks is a yes-or-no question about a file in front of the student, which is why they are assessed.

*Unsafe* in three directions, and the day names all three. **First, as a security review.** Four checks is a floor. A template passing all four can still be insecure in every way the four do not address, and §8's objection 3 concedes that the day's most operable content is its least representative. **Second, as a vendor-independent list.** These are one cloud provider's current checks for one family of services; the durable content is the two ideas underneath them — stop being reachable from everywhere, stop authenticating with a secret that can be copied — and the day teaches those as the thing that transfers. **Third, as a claim about what Defender for Cloud does.** The AI bill of materials, the continuous multicloud discovery and the attack path analysis are **demonstrated rather than operated**, require the Defender CSPM plan and a repository connector, and **are not assessed**; nothing in the artefact depends on them.

A fourth condition is temporal rather than logical and the day states it with a date. **From 1 July 2026, AI agent discovery and security posture for Foundry agents require a Microsoft Agent 365 licence**, with Defender CSPM continuing to discover accounts and projects. The durable claim is that governance tooling has a licensing boundary and that the boundary moves; the date is the instance, and it is shown to students as a worked example of perishability rather than removed as an inconvenience.

The boundary is delivered at points 8, 9 and 10 rather than left to this paper, per the widening recorded on the [Whitepaper Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md). **The artefact element under which a student applying it outside its safe range would fail is step 8**, which requires each check to be recorded as passed, remediated or accepted with the actor the acceptance exposes them to — a formulation under which "all four passed" cannot be submitted as a security conclusion.

### 3.3 Engagement and motivational mechanism

The day runs on **a list that halves**.

Item 1 produces a long list quickly; students are good at imagining what could go wrong. Item 3 removes the entries with no actor and the removal is substantial and self-administered. Watching one's own comprehensive-feeling list collapse is a more effective introduction to the actor requirement than any definition, and item 4's prediction makes the collapse a calibration event.

Actor D supplies the day's most useful surprise. Groups rank the four actors by expected harm and reliably rank the careless insider last or omit them, because harm is intuitively indexed to intent. Point 5's arithmetic — probability times damage — puts the actor with no goal at the top, which is both correct and uncomfortable, and it reframes security from adversary-defeat to consequence-management.

The access-model reveal is the phase's second mechanism and it works because it is real: the substrate's actual access model is wider than students assume, and the assumption they made is the assumption a working engineer makes.

The competence risk is at step 5. A student who lists many accepted threats may feel they have produced a weak model, when the specification's clause says the opposite. The clause is stated before step 5 begins.

## 4. Phase-by-phase design and its warrant

**Phase 1 (25 min).** List, attribute, filter, predict. The design's decision to have students produce and then dismantle their own list is the day's foundational move and is preferred to any presentation of the actor requirement. **Item 5 is the sealed Copilot prediction**, administered here per instrumentation rule 8, and its prompt — *write me the deployment template for this model* — says nothing about security, which is how such prompts are written and is why the sealed answer is worth having.

**Phase 2 (20 min).** The bridge modifies blast radius for an adversary. Stating that an attacker selects the worst case rather than sampling from it is the single most important adjustment to the students' existing risk apparatus and is delivered as retrieval.

**Phase 3 (45 min).** Four actors, one component, **and an adjudicator the groups run themselves**. The four published infrastructure-as-code checks are applied by hand to the actual template that will deploy the student's model at S045, one at a time, and each returns an answer in minutes. Actor D remains the payload for expected harm. **The template's failure is the phase's second and newer payload**, and it defeats expectation in the direction that matters: students expect a security exercise to find carelessness, and it finds a default. Nobody chose an exposed posture; somebody chose a working deployment and the exposure came attached, because the basic setup does not support private network isolation at all. The phase then closes on the fact that makes it a design lesson rather than a configuration lesson — **outbound networking cannot be changed on an existing resource; acquiring it means redeploying** — so the failed check is not a finding for next sprint but a decision already made by whoever typed the first prompt.

**Phase 4 (50 min).** Eleven points — ten when this paper was written, plus point 11 added 2026-07-30. Point 1 is the retained sentence and the specification's basis. Point 3 carries the scope statement. Point 5 does the actor D arithmetic explicitly. **Point 6 imports S033 and now carries the correction §8 identified**: adversarial probability is a statement about the target's value and the attack's cost, not about frequency. Point 7's framing — a decision record, not a document — is what prevents the artefact becoming a compliance exercise. **Point 8 states what four checks are and are not**, reducing them to the two transferable ideas and naming where those recur. **Point 9 names four defaults**, all of them security decisions, of which the sharpest is that the recommended authentication option and the default authentication option are not the same option. **Point 10 carries the day's perishability with a date on it.**

**Point 7 was extended 2026-07-30**, on a reading of the engineering playbook in full, with the observation that the playbook's own worked threat model marks **every threat in it as not mitigated** — a real embedded system, fully enumerated, with its assumptions stated separately — and that this is what the genre produces at first writing rather than a defect in the example. **The design takes it as evidence for a claim it was already making and could not previously ground**: the accept list is the output. The extension is placed at point 7 rather than at phase 3 because it is a fact about the artefact's genre and not about the students' template, and delivering it earlier would supply the answer to the phase 5 judgement it exists to protect — which is that a full mitigation column on a first pass is a signal about the author rather than about the system.

**Phase 5 (90 min).** Eight steps on the student's own model. Step 1 carries the infrastructure clause and the named SG201 material, which is the cross-course repair. Step 4 carries the confidence-in-the-same-field requirement, which is the first day designed after rule 2 and applies it, and now permits the value-and-cost substitute where a frequency is not estimable. Step 5 is the discriminator. Step 7 is the expiry discipline. **Step 8 is the day's build**: run the four checks against your own template and record each as passed, remediated with the change shown, or accepted with a reason and the actor it exposes you to. It is deliberately small and produces two things — a template a student can deploy on Friday, and a four-line record of what they decided about their own exposure. **An accepted failure is a pass; an unexamined one is not**, which is S041's leak-audit discipline applied to a template.

**Phase 6 (40 min).** A non-attack incident, then an agent failure. The instructor disclosure is deliberately not an attack, because a day built on adversaries risks producing students who model only deliberate harm and the largest category is unowned change. **The agent case is a correct, careful security improvement whose summary was entirely true and which left the resource publicly reachable** — managed identity added, key removed, least privilege commented, network untouched because nobody asked. It is chosen because review passed it on the strength of a good diff, and because it makes the day's structural point about scope: a review bounded by the change under review cannot find what the change did not touch, which is why the four checks run against the template and not against the diff.

**Phase 7 (30 min).** Role-play plus timing. The timing half is the new demand: a signal that fires after the damage is not a detection, which sharpens S018's material against an adversary who can control when they act.

**Phase 8 (20 min).** The list-collapse fraction, the actor D ranking before and after the checks, **the cohort's four-check results as four counts**, **item 5's sealed prediction opened and compared against the room's phase 3 finding on the supplied template**, and the S043 contingency revisit if triggered.

**The contingency itself** remains the day's most unusual feature: if S043's setting D identification rate was low, phase 2 becomes a direct re-probe and phase 5's threat model is scoped to include the evaluation pipeline as an attack surface. This is legitimate content — an evaluation pipeline is an attack surface, and a tuned-on-test model is a self-inflicted poisoning — and it is the branch [WP-043](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-043.md) §8 said was missing, specified here.

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**Students with more relevant prior knowledge.** A student with security exposure will produce a structured model quickly. Predicted failure: their accept list will be short and their probability estimates confident, because security training rewards comprehensive coverage and rarely rewards stating what you are not doing. Countermeasure: the empty-accept-list clause, and phase 7's partner who takes an accepted threat's role — a short accept list gives the partner little to work with and the pair notices.

**Students with less relevant prior knowledge.** Adversarial reasoning is entirely new and step 4's estimation is the hardest demand. Countermeasures: actors supplied at phase 3, step 2 worked, the estimation form supplied, and stated low confidence acceptable. Residual risk: their probabilities may be arbitrary, and the confidence field is the only guard.

**Phase 3 is too easy.** Actors A, B and C are analysed adequately by most groups. Actor D's ranking is the difficulty. Fallback if a group ranks D first: ask what access actor A would need to become as harmful as D, which forces reasoning about access rather than intent and previews S078.

**Affective failure.** The characteristic risk is **paranoia without proportion**. A day introducing adversaries to students who have never modelled one can produce security theatre — long threat lists, everything handled, nothing shipped. This is the security-strand equivalent of the refusal reflex M05 produced and it is countered the same way: point 5's expected-harm arithmetic, which deprioritises dramatic low-probability threats; point 7's decision-record framing; and the accept list, which makes not-handling a required output rather than a confession.

## 7. Assessment and evidence generated

No entrustment judgement — the module's is at S045.

One specification item, jointly in SG201 and IS201. Per rule 1, the checked elements are: deployment context with access paths and the named SG201 material, actors with access and goal, an attack and cost per actor, probability and consequence with confidence in the same field or the value-and-cost substitute, a handle list, an accept list, one detection signal, the change-of-decision condition, **the four-check result on the student's own deployment template**, the phase 7 outcome, **and the prompt record, the intervention record and the frontier statement**. **Nothing else is assessed.**

**A threat with no actor fails. An empty accept list fails.**

**A model in which nothing is marked mitigated is not penalised for that**, added 2026-07-30 and warranted by the playbook's own worked example, in which every threat is marked not mitigated. **This adds and removes no checked element**; it constrains how an existing one is read, which is why the specification's closing sentence is untouched. The design's reason for stating it at all is that markers reward completeness by reflex, and a student who fabricates a mitigation to fill a column has produced the one artefact this day is built to prevent — a threat model that reads as finished and records no decision.

**A check recorded as accepted — with a reason and the actor the acceptance exposes you to — passes. A check not run fails.** The specification requires the looking, not the passing, which is S041's leak-audit discipline applied to a template and is deliberate: several students will find that check 1 is not available to them at all on the setup they hold, and that is the correct finding rather than a failure.

**Defender for Cloud's own reporting is demonstrated rather than operated and is not assessed**, per the [Copilot Practice Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md), because it requires the Defender CSPM plan and a repository connector with tenant-administration rights the programme does not hold.

Programme instrumentation: the item 1 to item 3 list-collapse fraction is the cohort's hazard-versus-threat baseline; the actor D ranking rate is the intent-indexing measure, re-probed at S076; **the phase 7 timing outcome is the first detection-timeliness measure in the programme** and is carried to S068 and S079; **the four-check result distribution is new and is the security strand's first quantitative record of the cohort's actual posture**, carried to [S060](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S060.md) per §8's objection 3; **item 5's sealed prediction accuracy** feeds the running frontier map per instrumentation rule 9, and is unusual in this module because the same prediction can be scored twice — once against the supplied template at phase 3 and once against the student's own at phase 5.

Dispositions: **integrity**, at step 5, where a short accept list is more impressive and less honest; and at step 8, where recording an accepted check is an admission in writing that a named actor can reach you.

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: the day violates a rule this design added three days ago.**

At its strongest: the [Whitepaper Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md)'s cross-course deferral clause, added after [WP-041](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-041.md) §8, requires a day deferring material to a concurrent course to carry an artefact element naming which course material the student applied. This day defers infrastructure security to SG201 — its single largest omission, covering where most real incidents originate — and the artefact carries no such element. The rule was written for exactly this situation, three days earlier, by this design, and this day does not comply.

There is no rebuttal on the merits. The omission is a straightforward failure to apply a rule the design holds.

What can be said is diagnostic rather than exculpatory. The rule was added in response to a leak class; this day's deferral is to a whole domain, which is larger and therefore harder to compress into a single artefact field — and the design appears to have registered the rule as being about *classes within a taxonomy* rather than about deferral as such. That is a real pattern in how rules propagate through this design: they are applied where they were discovered and not where they generalise. **This is the second observation of that pattern**, after [WP-042](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-042.md) §8 found a rule 3 failure in the first module written after rule 3.

The repair is one artefact element: step 1's deployment context should require the student to state which infrastructure access paths they identified and which SG201 material they used to identify them. **It is now made**, annotated at the point of change on the seminar page, and the compliance register records the finding as fixed.

**Objection 2: step 4 asks for probabilities against an adversary, and an adversary is not a distribution.**

At its strongest: point 6 imports S033's accept discipline, which requires probability and consequence for each unhandled failure. That discipline was built for component failures, which have base rates. An adversary has no base rate: they choose whether to act, and their choice depends on the defence, which means the probability the student estimates is conditional on their own decisions in a way the form does not represent. Worse, an adversary's probability is not stable — a threat with negligible probability today becomes certain the moment the target becomes valuable — so a number recorded now is not a number that will remain true.

This objection the design does not fully answer. The estimation is genuinely different in kind from S033's and the day treats it as the same discipline in a new setting, which is not accurate.

Two things partially address it. Step 7 requires the condition that would change the acceptance decision, which is where the instability belongs — an accept decision with a stated trigger is a decision with a shelf life rather than a permanent judgement. And the confidence field required by rule 2 lets a student record that the estimate is weak, which for most adversarial probabilities it is.

Neither closes the conceptual gap, and what the day should say is that **adversarial probability is a statement about attractiveness and cost, not about frequency** — an attacker acts when the value exceeds their cost, so the estimable quantities are the value of the target and the cost of the attack, and probability is downstream of both. That reframing is available and is standard. **It is now made**, at point 6 and at step 4, where a student who cannot estimate a frequency estimates those two instead and says so; the seminar page annotates the change at the point of change. What remains conceded is that the reframing arrives in the strand's first session, from an instructor, rather than being reached by the students — the day has no phase 3 case that would produce it, and constructing one would require an adversary whose behaviour changes visibly with the target's value, which the design has not built.

**Objection 3: four checks is a floor so low that teaching it may license the belief that security is a checklist.**

This is the objection that bites on the practical turn and it is the sharpest of the three, because it attacks the day's best feature.

At its strongest: the day's most operable content is its least representative. Everything else here is judgement — who would act, at what cost, what you will accept, when your signal fires — and all of it is hard, uncertain and unscored. The four checks are none of those things. They are four yes-or-no questions, they take ten minutes, they produce a result the student can point at, and they are the only part of the day that feels like it was completed. **Students weight what closes.** A cohort whose first security session ends with four boxes will carry four boxes, and the transferable content — that a threat is an actor with access and a goal, that the accept list is the output, that a signal firing late is not a detection — will be remembered as context around a checklist. The risk is worse because the checklist is genuinely good: it is short, it is published, it is applicable, and three of the four address real and common exposures. **A bad checklist would be safer.**

There is a second form. These are one vendor's current checks for one family of services. A student who internalises *the four checks* has learned a recommendation list with a revision date, and this paper's own §3.2 already records that the surrounding product moves — a licensing boundary shifts on a published date, and the checks themselves are described as *current*.

The objection is conceded, and the design's position is that running them anyway is still right: **a strand arriving in week forty-four is better served by a floor a student can stand on than by a syllabus the programme does not have room for.** That is a defence of the decision and not a rebuttal of the objection.

**Two cheap fixes are made.** Point 8 states explicitly that four checks is a floor and not a review, and reduces them to the two ideas that actually transfer — stop being reachable from everywhere, stop authenticating with a secret that can be copied — naming where each recurs at SY202 and SG301. And step 8's recording form makes "all four passed" unsubmittable as a conclusion: each check is recorded as passed, remediated, or accepted **with the actor the acceptance exposes you to**, which forces every line back through the actor requirement the day is actually about.

**What remains unfixed is that nothing in the programme ever re-runs the checks against a larger system**, so the day installs a floor and never tests whether the student raised it. Under instrumentation rule 6, a deferred response must name its occasion and that occasion must carry an artefact element consuming the result. **The occasion is [S060](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S060.md), where retrieval is shipped into production**, and the element it must carry is a re-run of the same four checks against a system that is materially larger than a single model deployment, with the student required to name at least one exposure the four do not cover. That commitment is made here on S060's behalf and is recorded so that its absence is checkable rather than forgettable. Until it is discharged, the honest statement is that this day gives the security strand a floor and the programme does not yet demonstrate that anyone builds on it.

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** More than half of each student's item 1 list will fail the item 3 actor filter, and item 4 predictions will underestimate the collapse. *Instrument:* paired list lengths against item 4 predictions.

**Prediction 2.** Fewer than a fifth of groups will rank actor D first before the point 5 arithmetic. *Instrument:* phase 3 committed rankings.

**Prediction 3.** Students with security backgrounds will produce shorter accept lists than students without, controlling for total threat count. *Instrument:* accept-list length as a proportion of enumerated threats, split by the admission background survey.

**Prediction 4.** In phase 7, the detection signal will fail the timing test for a majority of accepted threats. *Instrument:* phase 7 outcomes coded for fires-and-in-time, fires-too-late, and does-not-fire.

**Prediction 5.** A large majority of student deployment templates will fail at least two of the four checks on first run, and the most common failure will be check 1 rather than check 3 or 4. *Instrument:* step 8 results by check. **The prediction is about defaults rather than about students: if templates pass on first run, either the cohort has been more deliberate than the design assumes or the platform's defaults have changed, and phase 3's payload has to be rebuilt either way.**

**Prediction 6.** A large majority of item 5 sealed predictions will say the agent will produce a working template, and a small minority will name the specific consequence — publicly reachable, key-authenticated. *Instrument:* item 5 responses coded for generic-versus-specific failure identification, scored twice: against the supplied template at phase 3 and against the student's own at phase 5. **A cohort that names it after phase 3 and not before has learned it on the day rather than predicted it, which is the distinction the sealed item exists to preserve.**

**Prediction 7 — point 11's own, added 2026-07-30.** Among step 4 entries whose probability or consequence rests on a value the student **inferred** rather than observed — an assumed transport, an assumed authentication mode, an assumed classification — a majority will record a provenance naming the reasoning (*best practice*, *standard for this transport*, *the template does this*) **without recording that the value itself was unavailable.** *Instrument:* step 4 provenance fields re-coded into three classes — observed value with source, inferred value marked as inferred, inferred value presented as observed — against the student's own step 1 deployment context; no new artefact element is required, since provenance in the same field is already a checked element. **The third class is point 11's failure occurring in the student's own artefact rather than in somebody else's pipeline**, and it is the only place this day can observe it. If the third class is rare, the inference-laundering trace is being learned as a story about a system rather than as a property of threat models, and it belongs at [S068](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S068.md) rather than here.

## What a student should take from this paper

**What the design is claiming.** That asking what could go wrong produces hazards rather than threats. A threat has an actor with access and a goal, which is what gives it a feasibility, a cost and a probability anyone can argue about; a hazard has none of the three and cannot be prioritised. It also claims a threat model in which everything is handled is unfinished rather than strong, and that the actor with no goal at all often carries the highest expected harm.

**What the evidence licenses.** The actor-access-goal frame and the attack families are cited only at folder level in [research/02-technical-foundations/](/research/02-technical-foundations/collected-materials.md) and [research/05-fde-craft/](/research/05-fde-craft/collected-materials.md): the paper records that no note carries these claims. The checks are **class 4**, read on 26 July 2026 and quoted verbatim, alongside the fact that outbound networking cannot be changed on an existing resource. **No detection rate and no false-positive rate is published for any of it, and none is asserted.** The day's own construction is **class 3**.

**What to do with it.**

- Attribute every entry on your list. The ones with no actor are not threats and should leave the list in your own handwriting.
- Write the accept list. Naming what you are not handling, with its consequence and the condition that would change your mind, is the output rather than the confession.
- **A control is not evidence**, per the [Agent Configuration Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/13-Agent-Configuration-Standard.md). Four checks passing is four controls that ran, not a finding that your deployment is safe — which is why each result must name the actor an acceptance exposes you to.
- Ask when your detection signal fires. One that fires after the damage is not a detection.

**The concession worth remembering.** The paper concedes that four checks is a floor low enough that teaching it may license the belief that security is a checklist — and that the checklist being genuinely good makes this worse, because a bad one would be safer. Students weight what closes, and this is the only part of the day that closes. Nothing in the programme yet re-runs these checks against anything larger, so the design installs a floor and does not demonstrate that anyone builds on it.
