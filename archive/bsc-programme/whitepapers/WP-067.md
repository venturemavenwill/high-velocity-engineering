## In plain terms

**What this paper argues, before the formal version.**

Two numbers disagree. The offline evaluation said one thing and the live system says another, and every instinct a competent engineer has says *work out which one to trust*. That instinct is right almost everywhere and produces nothing here, **because the two numbers are not measurements of the same quantity.**

The offline set was *drawn* — by somebody, at some point, under a sampling frame, scored against a criterion a person chose. The online number belongs to a population that *arrived*, with a composition nobody selected, over some period, through one collection path, under one deployment, minus everybody who gave up and left. They are related the way a photograph is related to a room. So the useful question is never which is right; it is **what differs between them**, and there are only so many things that can.

The pull toward treating the live number as the truth is enormous, and it is half correct: the live number is closer to what anyone cares about. It is still a sample — of a window, of a version, of a pipeline, of the people who stayed — and a student who leaves believing it is truth has swapped one uncritical reference standard for another and will be more confident about the second.

The hardest half hour is about who breaks. The room already knows that an average conceals a group. What they do not know is that **online, the group is usually defined by a person's circumstance rather than by a property of the input** — how they reached you, what device they used, what assistive technology they run, what time zone they live in — and circumstances are not dimensions in anybody's evaluation taxonomy. So the room is given a case, every recorded dimension, the whole record, and every chance to succeed with the method they already have. They cannot find the group, because it is not a field.

Then they try the platform's own automated clustering on it, and it does not find the group either. **That is not a fact about the tool.** It clusters what it is given; if the circumstance leaves no trace in the text, no clustering over the text will recover it. A student who leaves thinking the tool was wrong has taken the opposite of the lesson, which is why the day now makes them name the attribute that would have had to exist — turning a negative result into tomorrow's instrumentation requirement.

**What this paper concedes, and it is the one to remember.** The day's most important sentence is that an online measurement is itself a sample with its own error structure — and it is delivered in a single point at the end of forty-five minutes in which all four contrasting cases used the online result as the adjudicator. **The room is taught by construction that online is where the answer is, and then told once that it is not.** The fix is one extra row in a table that already exists: a case where the online measurement was the broken one. It is named, it is nearly free, and it is not built.

## Abstract

This paper defends replacing the question *which number is right* with the question *what differs between them*, and defends supplying five named differences as the frame a student attributes a gap with. It defends a phase 3 in which the sign and size of the gap are held fixed across four cases so that no group can succeed by reading the magnitude, and defends a payload whose second surprise is that the affected class is not recoverable by slicing on any recorded dimension. **It defends the day's practical turn twice over: the four cases are now executed, and the room applies the platform's own automated class-discovery feature to a real result set and finds that it returns classes of response where the class that broke is a class of person.** It builds the residual class-construction technique [WP-053](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-053.md) §8 named and declined, and discharges the forward links [S049](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S049.md), [S053](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S053.md) and [S054](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S054.md) placed on this day. It then concedes that the day's most important point — that an online measurement is also a sample with its own error structure — is asserted at phase 4 while all four phase 3 cases use online as the adjudicator, **the third instance of a standing observation, which is therefore elevated to a rule.** It concedes separately that thirty concurrent student releases on one substrate is the multi-change case the day's own scope statement declares unsafe, **that the day's cluster-analysis finding is contingent on what the telemetry carries and was first written as though it were structural, and that the design draws the sampling rule for the students' own online measurement and then assesses them on stating sampling rules.**

## 1. The instructional problem this day must solve

The reflex this day exists to break is a good one everywhere else. When two measurements of the same quantity disagree, a competent engineer asks which is more trustworthy and reasons about the error in each. That is correct procedure and it produces nothing here, because the two measurements are not of the same quantity.

An offline evaluation measures the performance of a system on a set that was **drawn** — by someone, at a time, under a sampling frame, scored against a criterion somebody chose. An online measurement measures the outcome for a population that **arrived** — with a composition nobody selected, over a period, through one collection path, under one deployment, minus everyone who left. The two are related in the way a photograph is related to a room.

Students will not believe this from being told, because the offline number is the one they built and the online number is the one that has users in it, and the pull toward treating the second as truth is enormous. The pull is also half right: the online number is closer to what anyone cares about. It is not therefore a reference standard, and phase 4 point 8 says so at the end of a day that has spent forty-five minutes using it as one. §8 objection 1 treats that as a defect rather than as a subtlety.

Three specific difficulties sit underneath.

**The class problem.** [S053](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S053.md) taught that aggregates hide class-specific catastrophe and taught the remedy of per-class reporting, and [WP-053](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-053.md) §8 conceded that the remedy shares a defect with the failure it corrects: both find only what was already thought of. Offline that concession is uncomfortable. Online it is disqualifying, **because the class is a person's circumstance rather than an input's property** — an access path, a device, a locale, an assistive technology, a time of day — and circumstances are not dimensions in anybody's evaluation taxonomy. [WP-053](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-053.md) §6 recorded that this day's retrieval was the one most likely to fail for exactly that reason. The response is to name the case, and to build the technique WP-053 declined.

**The unit problem.** [S049](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S049.md) established that an interval is a property of a procedure applied to a dependency structure. Offline the unit is an item. Online the unit is a user, and users cluster by everything. An interval computed correctly over items and an outcome computed correctly over users are not comparable, and the students will compare them, because both are numbers with the same name.

**The turn.** [S054](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S054.md) taught that optimising against a proxy improves the true objective, then stops, then harms it, and that the proxy reports improvement throughout. That day could only demonstrate the turn retrospectively on supplied models, and [WP-054](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-054.md) §6 named this day as its primary target because the turn is a live-system phenomenon. Here it is watchable, and it is watchable for exactly one reason: the online outcome is a measure the offline criterion does not control, which is the structural requirement [S054](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S054.md) step 7 made students argue for and had no way to demonstrate.

## 3. Theoretical basis for the design

### 3.1 Load profile and element interactivity

**High, and the module's peak.** Phase 5 requires a student to hold an offline result with its structure, a prediction, a pre-committed attribution, a live release, a residual analysis and a gate decision, and the residual analysis is a genuinely new technique arriving at the point of highest activity.

The interactivity runs steps 1 → 3 → 6: the dependency structure determines which differences are even candidates, the pre-commitment at step 3 constrains what step 6 may claim without embarrassment, and step 6's distinguishing evidence must come from measurements taken at step 4, which happened before the attribution was formed. That last dependency is the day's hardest structural feature and it is deliberate — an attribution that requires evidence you did not collect is an attribution you cannot make, which is the lesson.

Management:

- **All four phase 3 cases are supplied with complete records**, offline and online, already assembled.
- **The residual-grouping harness is supplied** and step 5 is worked with the room, which is the heaviest scaffolding in the module.
- **The release tooling, traffic split and disclosure conditions are the substrate's** and require no student work.
- **The component and change were chosen yesterday** and are not revisited.
- **The candidate-factor checklist is inherited** rather than constructed; the student adds one online-only factor, not a set.
- **The measurement path's tooling and credentials are supplied**, so the construction at step 4 is confined to the three decisions rather than to the plumbing.

**Step 3 is unsupported.** Expertise reversal applies for a student who has run A/B tests professionally: for them points 1, 3 and 4 are review, and the day's content is points 6, 7 and 8, of which point 7 is the technique and point 8 is the one experienced practitioners most often lack. **Point 9 is content for everybody**, because the sampling defaults are new and are the kind of fact that experience does not supply.

### 3.2 Desirable difficulties selected, and those deliberately not

**Selected.**

- *Predicting the online number before releasing,* item 1 and step 2.
- *A historical pair whose gap ran the other way,* item 3, which defeats any single-mechanism answer to item 2.
- *Four cases with the same gap and four different causes,* phase 3.
- *Committing the dominant difference before measuring,* step 3.
- *Constructing a class from residuals rather than selecting one,* step 5.
- *Requiring evidence that distinguishes one attribution from four rivals,* step 6.
- *A partner who must produce a rival attribution and the measurement that separates it,* phase 7.

**Deliberately not selected.**

- *Experiment design and assignment mechanics.* Randomisation units, sequential testing and stopping rules are ME301's; the day treats the split as given and says so.
- *Interference between concurrent changes.* Named in the scope statement and given to [S069](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S069.md), where staggering is a governance decision. §8 objection 2 argues the day cannot escape this as cleanly as the scope statement implies.
- *Instrumentation-induced difference.* Named and given to [S068](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S068.md).
- *Long-horizon drift.* [S079](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S079.md)'s. The day measures a gap at release, not a trajectory.

**Scope, per the [Whitepaper Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md), strong form.** Point 2's five differences are **safe as a diagnostic frame for attributing a gap on a single change you released, on a system you can inspect, over a period in which nothing else changed.** They are **unsafe in a system releasing several changes at once**, which is the ordinary condition of every organisation these students will join: with concurrent changes the five differences cannot be separated by any evidence available after the fact, and the correct move is not a better attribution but a different release schedule. The day states this, and the student's artefact must record how many other changes were live in their measurement window — which is the artefact element that makes the boundary checkable rather than merely stated.

**The platform instance, scoped in the strong form.** The day uses trace evaluation, pre-existing response scoring and cluster analysis as the concrete forms of an online measurement, and the simplification carries its conditions to students at point 11.

*Safe.* Reasoning about any production measurement the student is handed: what was sampled, by what rule, over what window, through what path, and what fraction of what was requested actually arrived. Every one of those has a documented answer here and is the right question everywhere.

*Unsafe.* Two conditions. **Unsafe as a description of what these features will do next term** — the sampling defaults, the seven-day ceiling, the strategy names and the preview labels are product decisions. And **unsafe as a general claim that automated class discovery cannot find a circumstance-defined group.** The phase 3 finding is that a clustering over model outputs and feedback signals returns classes of output; **it is contingent on what the clustered data contains**, and a group defined by a circumstance that *is* present in the telemetry could be recovered by exactly that method. Presenting the finding as structural would teach a student to stop looking, which is the opposite of the intended lesson. **Step 5 is where a student applying it outside its range would fail**: where cluster analysis does not recover the constructed class, the artefact must name the attribute that would have been needed, which converts *the tool cannot do this* into *this is what I would have to instrument*, and hands the result to [S068](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S068.md).

### 3.3 Engagement and motivational mechanism

The day runs on **O3's second surprise**.

A room that has done [S053](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S053.md) expects the aggregate to hide a class, and O3's aggregate agreement is therefore not a surprise; it is a confirmation and the groups enjoy it. They then try to find the class, with the whole record in front of them and every recorded dimension available, and they cannot, **because the group is defined by something the substrate does not store as a field.** That is the moment the day exists for. It converts per-class reporting from a discipline the cohort already holds into a method that is insufficient, and it arrives after they have been given every opportunity to succeed with the method they have.

The second mechanism is item 3. A student answers item 2 with one reason, and is immediately shown the same change on the same component eleven months earlier with the gap running the other way. There is nothing to defend. One mechanism cannot produce a gap in both directions, so the student learns before phase 4 that they need a frame rather than an explanation — and phase 4 point 2 arrives as the thing they have just discovered they lack.

The third is phase 6's ending. Eleven weeks, four locales, every dashboard correct, and the failure ends when a user writes in. That detail is placed deliberately and is picked up the next day: the only detector that worked was a person, which is the observation [S068](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S068.md) opens on and the reason its scope statement carries a user-visible-only failure class.

Autonomy: the student releases their own change to real users and decides afterwards whether their own gate should change. Competence: the residual technique is simple and produces a result within the phase. Relatedness: the users in phase 6 are the substrate's, and the person who wrote in is real.

The competence risk is at step 5, where a student may find no class — either because their change genuinely affects everyone alike or because their slice is too small. **The instructor states before phase 5 that "the residuals do not group, and here is the analysis that shows they do not" is a complete answer**, and that a manufactured class is worse than none. §8 objection 2 argues the second cause will be more common than the design admits.

## 4. Phase-by-phase design and its warrant

**Phase 1 (25 min).** Five sealed items on a change that has not yet been released. Item 3 is the day's cheapest and most effective construction: it defeats every single-mechanism answer to item 2 without any instruction. **Item 5 is the sealed Copilot prediction**, administered here per instrumentation rule 8.

**Phase 2 (20 min).** Four retrievals and a bridge that converts the gate's threshold into an object with a provenance. The room answers it from S041 and S049.

**Phase 3 (45 min).** Four cases with the gap's sign and size held fixed, **executed rather than read**. O3 carries two surprises and the second is the phase's content. **The cluster-analysis run is the executed form of that second surprise** and its finding is scoped at §3.2 rather than stated as structural.

**Phase 4 (50 min).** Eleven points. Point 2 carries the strong-form scope statement for the five differences. Points 6 and 7 discharge the S053 transfer. Point 8 is the most important and the least supported, per §8. **Points 9 and 10 are the platform points the [Copilot Practice Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md) requires** — the sampling defaults, and the collection path as an error source — and point 11 delivers the perishability boundary.

**Phase 5 (90 min).** Eight steps with a real release at bounded exposure, **now building the online measurement path as well as consuming it.** Step 3 before step 4 makes the attribution a pre-commitment. **Step 5 introduces the residual class construction as a proposed remedy under the amended rule 5**, in its first use, and now additionally requires the missing-attribute statement that hands the result to S068. **Steps 1 and 8 carry the [S059](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S059.md) ceiling forward and set it against survival**, which is the rule-6 element that day named. **Step 7 is the day's own instrument check** and is the cheapest available staging of point 8. The artefact carries the prompt record, the intervention record and the frontier statement.

**Phase 6 (40 min).** Eleven weeks, four locales, correct dashboards, and a user who wrote in. **Then the agent case: a correct online measurement reported without its denominator.**

**Phase 7 (30 min).** Rival attribution and a distinguishing measurement, with the predicted gap withheld. **The S049 found-factor rate is retaken here**, which is the instrument S049 carried to this day.

**Phase 8 (20 min).** Gaps split by sign; item 3's bidirectional frames; item 4's named groups against step 5's constructed classes and against what clustering recovered; the step 3-against-step 6 matrix; **item 5's frontier hit rate; the pooled step 7 reconciliations**; the two found-factor rates side by side and the checklist extended.

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**Students with more relevant prior knowledge.** A student who has run online experiments will diagnose O1, O2 and O4 immediately and will predict their own gap well. Predicted failure: they attribute confidently at step 6 without the distinguishing evidence, because in their experience the attribution has usually been obvious. Countermeasure: step 6's second clause is a fail condition, and phase 7's partner is required to produce a rival that fits the same gap — which, for a confident attribution made without discriminating evidence, is usually easy.

**Students with less relevant prior knowledge.** The risk is step 5. Countermeasures: the harness; the procedure worked with the room; the instructor's pre-statement that an honest negative result passes; and phase 3's O3, which has already shown the room what the output looks like when it works.

**Phase 3 is too easy.** O1 and O4 are separable by inspection for a strong group, and a group that has read about composition effects will name O4 quickly. The difficulty is O2, where the criterion difference and the population difference produce identical aggregate signatures, and O3, where the class is unrecoverable. Fallback if a group handles all four: give them the four cases with the online results removed and ask which of the five differences they would be unable to rule out, which is a question about what evidence a release should have collected and has no supplied answer.

**Affective failure.** The characteristic risk is **fatalism about offline evaluation** — if the offline number measures a different thing, the interval assumed the wrong structure, the criterion was a proxy that has turned, and the class that breaks is not in the taxonomy, a student may conclude that offline evaluation is a formality performed before the real measurement. That conclusion would gut [S066](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S066.md)'s gate, would produce engineers who ship to find out, and is the most dangerous disposition this module could install.

Countermeasures: O1's gap is closed by a better offline draw and this is shown, so the day does not teach that offline evaluation cannot be improved; step 8 requires a constructive decision about the gate rather than a critique of it; point 8 puts the online measurement under the same discipline, so the argument is symmetric rather than a demotion of one side; and the instructor states once that shipping to find out is available to everybody and that what distinguishes an engineer is knowing what they will find out and at whose expense.

**A second affective risk arrives with the platform layer: tool nihilism.** A room that has watched an automated class-discovery feature return the wrong kind of class, and then been shown that the sampling rule, the window and the row count were all somebody else's defaults, may conclude that production measurement is theatre. That is the same fatalism one level down and it is more attractive, because it comes with a villain. Countermeasures: **point 9 is delivered as a list of things you can now change**, not as a list of things done to you; the cluster-analysis finding is scoped at §3.2 and the scoping is delivered to students, so the feature is shown answering its own question correctly rather than failing; **step 5's missing-attribute clause converts the negative result into tomorrow's design work**; and the instructor states once that a documented default is a gift and that the platforms to be frightened of are the ones with the same defaults and no documentation.

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: the day's most important point is the one it does not demonstrate, and this is the third time.**

At its strongest: point 8 states that an online measurement is also a sample — of a period, a deployment, a collection path, and the users who arrived and stayed — and is not the truth against which the offline number is scored. That is the day's most consequential claim, because a student who leaves believing online is truth has replaced one uncritical reference standard with another, and will do it for the rest of their career with more confidence than before.

**All four phase 3 cases use the online result as the adjudicator.** In O1, O2, O3 and O4 the online record is what reveals the offline evaluation's defect. Forty-five minutes of contrasting cases teach, by construction, that online is where the answer is; phase 4 then asserts the opposite in one point and moves on. A student who leaves holding point 8 holds it because they were told, which §1 of this paper says is the condition under which a belief does not move.

The fix is nearly free and the design should have taken it. **A fifth case — the same change, an online measurement that is itself wrong, and an offline result that was right — costs one row in a table that already exists.** The natural construction is available on the substrate's record: a collection path that dropped events from one client version, producing an online decline that was an instrumentation artefact. That case would put point 8 on the same evidential footing as points 3 to 7, and it would additionally seed [S068](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S068.md)'s pipeline-independence material one day early.

**This is the third instance of the pattern [WP-049](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-049.md) §8 opened and [WP-053](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-053.md) §8 recorded at two: the design stages what it expects students to resist and asserts what it finds obvious once stated.** The mechanism is now clear enough to state. A designer builds contrasting cases for the claims they expect an argument about, and writes the remaining claims into phase 4 because they are true, are short, and feel settled. The claims that feel settled to the designer are frequently the ones the folk model has no purchase on and therefore survives intact — WP-053's point 4, WP-049's expression restriction, and this day's point 8 are all of that shape.

**Per the programme's convention, three instances elevate an observation to a rule.** The rule this paper argues for is a seventh instrumentation rule at the [Seminar Day Design Pattern](/archive/bsc-programme/program/04-Seminar-Day-Design-Pattern.md), in the same form as the six that precede it, and it is stated in §9's terms rather than as an exhortation: **every phase 4 point that breaks a belief the day's own phase 1 elicits must be demonstrated by that day's phase 3, or the whitepaper must name it as asserted and say why the demonstration was not available.** The second branch is what makes the rule cheap: it does not require every claim to be staged, only that the asserted ones are declared, which is exactly the discipline the scope rule applies to enumerations. This paper cannot amend the pattern page; the amendment is argued here and must be made there.

**Objection 2: thirty concurrent releases on one substrate is the case the day's own scope statement calls unsafe.**

At its strongest: §3.2 states that the five differences cannot be separated in a system releasing several changes at once, and that the correct response is a different release schedule. Phase 5 releases a change per student, inside the same ninety minutes, on the same substrate, to the same users. **The day's method is applied under the exact condition the day declares it invalid in.**

The design's answer is that the substrate's tooling assigns each student a disjoint traffic slice by hash, so the changes do not overlap on any user. That answer solves the interference problem and creates a worse one. **Each student's online population is now a fraction of the arriving population, and step 5's residual class construction is a search for a small group defined by an unrecorded circumstance.** [S049](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S049.md) point 6 is the relevant apparatus and it applies to the design rather than to the student: a design that cannot detect the effect it is looking for has not produced a negative result. A rare circumstance-defined group may not appear in a thirtieth of traffic at all, and the student will correctly report that the residuals do not group — which the instructor has pre-authorised as a passing answer, and which will therefore be indistinguishable from an honest negative.

**This objection is not fully answered.** The available fix is to sequence releases across the following week, with phase 5 producing the offline statement, the prediction, the pre-committed attribution and the detection plan, and the measurement arriving in the between-day protocol. That restores power and it costs the day its whole-task integrity: the pre-commitment and the result are separated by days, the attribution is made outside the room, and phase 7's rival-attribution work has nothing to operate on. It also breaks the module's sequence, since [S068](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S068.md) the following day consumes this day's results.

The design keeps the same-day release and does not take the fix. **The reason is that the module's sequence is convenient, which is not a good reason**, and the honest position is that the design has not chosen between the two goods and has defaulted to the one that preserves the timetable. What it does instead is cheap and partial: step 5 requires the student to state the size of their slice and the smallest group their residual analysis could have detected, per [S049](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S049.md) point 8's fifth item, so that a null result is reported with its sensitivity rather than as an absence. That converts an undetectable failure into a stated limitation and does not restore the power.

**Objection 3: the day's cluster-analysis finding was written as structural and is contingent.**

This is the objection that bites on the practical turn and it is conceded.

At its strongest: phase 3 has the room apply a real automated class-discovery feature to a real result set and discover that it does not recover O3's group. The finding is delivered as *a clustering over responses returns classes of response, and the class that broke is a class of person* — which reads as a statement about what such methods can do. **It is not.** The feature clusters evaluation samples using embedding-based similarity over model outputs and feedback signals, and whether a circumstance-defined group is recoverable that way depends entirely on whether that circumstance leaves a trace in what is being clustered. A locale that shows up in the language of a response is recoverable. An assistive technology that changes nothing about the text is not. **The day as first written taught a contingent result as a property of the method**, and a student who took that lesson would stop reaching for the tool in exactly the cases where it works.

**The fix is one clause and it is made.** Step 5 now requires, where clustering did not recover the constructed class, that the student **name the attribute that would have had to be present for it to.** That converts a negative result into a design requirement, it is checkable, it costs a sentence, and it hands its output directly to [S068](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S068.md), where an attribute you wish you had is a detection requirement. The scope statement at §3.2 carries the same boundary to the whitepaper.

**What remains conceded is that the day's payload now has a rhetorical advantage its evidence does not.** The room watches a confident, well-labelled, automatically-described set of clusters fail to contain the group, and that is a vivid moment; it is vivid because the tool is good and the question was different, and a student who leaves with *the tool was wrong* has taken away the opposite of the point. The countermeasure is that the feature is shown answering its own question correctly before it is shown answering the wrong one, and the design does not know whether that is sufficient.

**Objection 4: the design draws the sampling rule for the students' own measurement and then assesses them on stating sampling rules.**

At its strongest: point 9's whole content is that an online number is a sample drawn by a rule you probably did not write, and step 4 requires three sampling decisions to be made rather than accepted. **Then the students measure through a traffic split the substrate imposes, over a window the timetable fixes, with tooling the design supplies, inside a ninety-minute phase.** Their slice size is not theirs. Their window is not theirs. The disjointness that objection 2 relies on is a sampling rule the design chose, and it is the single largest determinant of what any of them can detect. So the day assesses a discipline it exempts itself from, in the same phase, on the same measurement.

This is the same structure [WP-066](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-066.md) §8 objection 2 found — the programme teaches a check it does not apply to itself — and the rebuttal is the same shape and no stronger. **The design does disclose the constraint**: step 7 requires the slice size and the smallest detectable group, and phase 8 reports the reconciliations, so the imposed rule is visible in every artefact rather than hidden. Disclosure is not exemption from the rule; it is the rule, applied by the design to itself, which is more than objection 2 of WP-066 could say.

**What is conceded is that disclosure was reached by a different route than the discipline it teaches.** The students are told the slice size; they are not asked to decide whether it is adequate before measuring, which is what step 4 asks of them. **The cheap fix is available and is declined: step 3 could require each student to state, before releasing, the smallest effect their slice could detect and whether that is smaller than the effect they predicted at step 2** — a pre-commitment rather than a post-hoc disclosure, costing two minutes, and producing a cohort-level result about whether students release into measurements that cannot answer their own question. It is declined because step 3 already carries the day's other pre-commitment and the design judges that two pre-commitments in one step will collapse into one. **That judgement is untested and the fix is two minutes**, and this objection is recorded as not fully answered.

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** At item 2, more than three-quarters of students will name population as the single most likely reason, and item 3's opposite-direction case will be explained by a majority with a second mechanism rather than by revising the first. *Instrument:* item 2 responses coded against the five differences; item 3 responses coded for whether the student revised or added. **The add-versus-revise split is the claim**: adding indicates a frame forming, revising indicates a single-cause model defending itself.

**Prediction 2.** At step 5, more than half of students will construct a class that is not in the substrate's taxonomy, and the classes will cluster on access path and device rather than on any content property. *Instrument:* constructed classes coded for taxonomy membership and for defining dimension. **If most constructed classes turn out to be recoverable from existing dimensions, O3's second surprise did not transfer** and step 5 is being performed as a slicing exercise.

**Prediction 3.** Step 3's pre-committed dominant difference will match step 6's attribution for fewer than half of students, and the largest off-diagonal cell will be predicted-population against attributed-criterion. *Instrument:* the step 3 by step 6 matrix.

**Prediction 4.** The [S049](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S049.md) found-factor rate will be higher online than it was offline — partners will find more omitted clustering factors when the unit is a user. *Instrument:* the two found-factor rates, paired by student. **If the online rate is not higher, either the checklist transferred completely or the online factors are invisible to partners as well**, and the two are distinguished by whether the online-only extensions at step 1 were populated.

**Prediction 5.** At step 7, more than half of students will find that the number of rows scored was smaller than the number requested, and fewer than a third will have noticed before the step required it. *Instrument:* step 7 reconciliations, with the artefact's revision history used to establish whether the discrepancy was recorded before or after the step was read. **If the discrepancy is rare, the day's point 10 is a documentation curiosity rather than an operational fact** and phase 6's agent case should be replaced.

**Prediction 6.** At step 5, more than half of students whose constructed class is absent from the taxonomy will report that cluster analysis did not recover it, and the modal missing attribute they name will be an access-path or device property rather than a content property. *Instrument:* step 5 clustering results and missing-attribute statements, coded by attribute type, cross-tabulated against the constructed class's defining dimension. **The modal attribute is the claim**: if students name content properties, they have not understood that the class is a class of person, and §8 objection 3's fix has not landed.

## What a student should take from this paper

**What the design is claiming.** That an offline number and an online number are not two measurements of one thing, and that the useful question is never *which is right* but *what differs between them*. The offline set was drawn; the online population arrived, minus everyone who left.

**What the evidence licenses.** Distribution shift, selection effects that remove their own diagnostic evidence, the unit-of-analysis argument and judge error structure are **class 1**, verified here. The production measurement path is **class 4**: sampling strategies, a lookback default that is also the maximum, and a row cap documented as a ceiling rather than a guarantee — with **no accuracy published for cluster analysis and no evaluator agreement published**. The five differences, the residual class construction and the fail conditions are **class 3** — this design's own reasoning, untested.

**What to do with it.**

- **Report an online number with its denominator**, and reconcile rows requested against rows scored before you believe either.
- **Look for the class that is a class of person**, not of input. Access path, device and assistive technology are not in anybody's taxonomy.
- **Treat an online result as an observed result and nothing more** — see the [Agent Configuration Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/13-Agent-Configuration-Standard.md). It licenses what it reported, at that revision, on that path, for the people who arrived.
- **Record how many other changes were live in your window.** With several, the five differences cannot be separated afterwards by any evidence.

**The concession worth remembering.** The paper concedes that its most important point — that an online measurement is itself a sample with its own error structure — is asserted in one sentence while all four contrasting cases spend forty-five minutes using online as the adjudicator. **Hold the design to that.** A student who leaves believing online is truth has swapped one uncritical reference standard for another, with more confidence than before, and the one-row fix that would have prevented it is named and not built.
