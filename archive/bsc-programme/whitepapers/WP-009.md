## In plain terms

**What this paper argues, before the formal version.**

You write the code, then you write tests to show it works, and they pass, and you conclude it is right. Every step of that is reasonable and the whole thing is close to worthless — for a structural reason rather than a careless one. **The tests were chosen by the person who already believed the code was correct, using the same picture of the program that produced it.** Defects live exactly where that picture is wrong, which is exactly where you will not think to look.

Worse, you cannot tell from the inside. Forty passing tests written to confirm and eight written to break are both green. There is no metric and no review that separates them, which is why the fix has to happen at the moment of writing: state the claim first, then choose the input most likely to *break* it — not one merely capable of failing.

Coverage does real damage here because it is a number, and numbers get improved. It measures which lines ran, not which claims were checked, and a suite that establishes nothing can max it out.

The asymmetry underneath is simple and it runs for the rest of the degree. **Passing is weak evidence. Failing is strong.** A green suite that survived genuine attacks is worth a great deal; a green suite that was never attacked is worth almost nothing, and they look identical. That is why a submission in which nothing failed passes only if you can say why the attacks were real.

The same logic then goes somewhere it has not gone before: an instructions file that has never produced non-conforming work has never been tested. And it has a failure with no analogue in ordinary testing — the file can simply not be in force, in which case nothing in the output will ever tell you, because the output is just what would have happened anyway.

**What this paper concedes, in the same plain terms.** It asks you to hunt for evidence that your own work is wrong, and then hand that work in for assessment two days later. Every incentive you have ever had — here and everywhere else — rewards work that looks sound. The programme's answers are real but partial, and **the design cannot claim its assessors are immune to a bias it has documented elsewhere in this wiki.** The tension is structural and unresolved. It concedes separately that testing a specification is expensive in a way testing a program is not, that it has fixed only the repeatability half, and that its own forecast about whether the practice survives outside the programme is a forecast of failure.

## Abstract

This paper defends teaching test design as falsification of stated claims rather than as demonstration of working behaviour, and defends siting the programme's first encounter with evidential asymmetry in a programming course seventeen days before any statistics instruction. The first argument is that a confirmatory test suite is indistinguishable, from the inside, from an informative one — both consist of passing tests — so the deficiency cannot be detected by inspection and must be prevented by construction. The second argument is one of encoding context: falsification learned as a statistics topic is retrieved in statistics contexts, and the programme requires it at every release decision, every evaluation and every safety case. The paper also defends using the student's own prior artefact as the object of attack, the specification clause that fails a suite in which nothing failed and nothing is explained, and the extension of both to the module's second register: **an instructions file that has never produced non-conforming work has never been tested**, and a defect in its scope is invisible in its output. It concedes that the day may substitute a new ritual for the old one, that its central instruction asks students to act against how they are assessed everywhere else, and — the objection that bites on the practical turn — that testing a specification costs orders of magnitude more per test than testing a program and produces a judgement rather than a result.

## 1. The instructional problem this day must solve

Students write tests after writing code, to show that the code works. The suite grows, all tests pass, and the student concludes the program is correct. Every step of this is locally reasonable and the whole is close to worthless as evidence, for a reason that is structural rather than careless: **the tests were selected by the person who believed the code was right, using the model of the program that produced the code.** Defects arise precisely where that model is wrong, which is precisely where the author will not think to look.

The failure is undetectable from inside. A suite of forty passing tests written confirmatorily and a suite of eight written to falsify are both green. There is no inspection, no metric and no review practice that reliably separates them, which is why the problem must be addressed at the point of construction — by requiring the claim first.

This is also where coverage does damage. Coverage is measurable, improvable and institutionally endorsed, and it measures the wrong thing: which lines executed, not which claims were checked. It can be maximised by a suite that establishes nothing, and because it is a number, it will be. Displacing it requires more than an argument, which is why phase 6's disclosure is a high-coverage failure.

The day's second problem is the programme's. Evidential asymmetry — that passing is weak and failing is strong — is the foundation of ME101, of the evaluation design at S029, of the release gate at S066 and of the safety case at S087. Taught first as a statistics topic, it would be encoded as one. Taught here, in code, on the student's own work, it is encoded as a fact about engineering claims, which is where it must fire.

The third problem is the module's closure. S006 supplied invariants, S007 supplied bets, S008 supplied inductive arguments. All three are claim sources, and none of them was ever attacked. S009 attacks all of them, which is what makes the module a module rather than four days.

The fourth problem is the one the module's second register creates and cannot close by itself. S006 established that an instructions file is a specification and required a single attack on it. S007 decomposed the surface. Neither day supplies the discipline that makes those artefacts worth anything, which is that **an untested specification is an unvalidated instrument**, and a repository full of instructions files nobody has attacked is a green suite written by the author of the code. Worse, the register has a defect class that has no analogue in ordinary testing: a file can fail to be in force at all, and no amount of examining the output will reveal it, because the output is merely what the model would have produced anyway. That is the day's sharpest available demonstration of its own asymmetry — an occasion where nothing failed, and nothing failing was the finding.

## 3. Theoretical basis for the design

### 3.1 Load profile and element interactivity

**Moderate, with the interactivity concentrated in a single relation** — between a claim and an input chosen to violate it. That relation is the day's content and everything else is arranged to leave capacity for it.

- **Phase 3 requires no construction.** Four suites supplied, two defects introduced. Groups predict and analyse.
- **Phase 5 uses the student's own S007 component,** so comprehension load is zero and the artefact carries continuity for free. This is the module's cheapest available load reduction and it is used deliberately at its highest-interactivity point.
- **Three claims, not a suite.** The task is bounded to three, because the objective is the quality of the attack, and a size target would reintroduce the volume-orientation the day is displacing.
- **Fading across the three claims:** worked, strategy-supplied, independent.
- **Framework specifics are marked perishable** and excluded from retention, which removes a substantial and genuinely irrelevant load.

The unmanaged load is step 6 — deciding whether the claim or the code is wrong — which is genuinely high-interactivity and appears without scaffolding. The design accepts this because the step's difficulty is its content, and because it is the last step of the last full task in the module.

### 3.2 Desirable difficulties selected, and those deliberately not

**Selected.**

- *Claim before test,* phase 5 step 1. The module's third instance of commit-before-result and its most consequential.
- *Most likely to falsify, not merely capable of failing,* step 2. A materially harder demand and the one that separates the day from a boundary-testing exercise.
- *Predicting which will fail,* step 3, adjudicated within the phase.
- *Step 6's claim-or-code decision,* which admits two legitimate answers.
- *Independent construction then comparison,* phase 7, where divergence is the product.
- *Item 3 in the pretest,* which most students fail because they look for a test to add rather than a claim to attack.

**Deliberately not selected.**

- *Withholding the asymmetry.* Phase 4 point 2 states it directly. It is a logical fact and generating it is not a realistic task.
- *A test-count or coverage target.* Explicitly refused, since the day's argument is that both are the wrong orientation.
- *Property-based or generative testing tools.* Genuinely powerful and genuinely a different topic; introducing a tool here would let the tool do the claim-attacking.
- *Unfamiliar code.* The student's own S007 work is used precisely to avoid it.
- *A model comparison, or a repeated-run protocol on the prompt tests.* Both refused here and both real. Repetition is required at [S006](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S006.md) step 7, where the object is one file and the cost is three prompts; requiring it for three claims on this day would consume the phase. The consequence is stated at §8 objection 3 and is not disguised.
- *Assessment stakes on the same day,* though S010 is two days later and the module artefact is due.

**The strong-form scope statement, and where it binds.** *Passing is weak evidence, failing is strong* is safe wherever the claim is one you stated and the outcome is one you can observe independently of the system under test — a program's behaviour, an instructions file's output read by you, a retrieval's contents. **It is unsafe in one condition that arrives immediately and is attractive: where the observation is produced by the system under test.** An agent asked whether its own output conformed supplies both the claim and the evidence, and the asymmetry collapses, because a failure it does not report is indistinguishable from a failure that did not occur. That is [S048](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S048.md)'s subject and is not resolved here. The boundary is bound to the artefact: **an attack that consists of asking the agent whether its own output conformed fails the specification**, whatever it returned. This is the widened form of the scope rule in the [Whitepaper Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md), and it is applied here because the unsafe move is one keystroke away from the safe one and looks identical in an artefact.

### 3.3 Engagement and motivational mechanism

The day runs on **a suite that looks good and is not**, and its opening is the strongest in the module.

Phase 1 gives students twelve passing tests and asks what they establish. The answers are confident and overstated. Then item 3 asks for a failing test, which exists, and most students cannot find it — not because it is well hidden, but because they are searching in the wrong mode. Twenty-five minutes in, the room has produced its own evidence that a green suite and a confident reading of it can coexist with a defect nobody located.

Phase 3's two-defect structure extends this. One defect would let a group conclude that suite B is simply best and the lesson is "write suite B." Two defects, caught by different subsets, force the conclusion the day wants: each suite is a hypothesis about *where* defects live, and no suite is a hypothesis about everywhere.

The competence risk is real and is specific to this day: students who have taken pride in their testing practice are being told it establishes little. Countermeasures are framing and evidence rather than reassurance. The framing is that confirmatory testing is the natural consequence of writing tests after code, not a personal failing; the evidence is phase 6's high-coverage production failure, which locates the problem in an industry-standard practice rather than in the student.

Autonomy is at phase 8, where the student names an untested claim in their own work.

## 4. Phase-by-phase design and its warrant

**Phase 1 (25 min).** Five items on a green suite with a real defect. Item 1's overstatement is the day's subject in the students' own words and is read back verbatim at phase 8. Item 3's failure mode — searching for a test rather than a claim — is diagnostic and is named in phase 4 point 1.

**Phase 2 (20 min).** The second item is a direct hand-off from S008 phase 6 and delivers the day's premise through retrieval rather than exposition, which is the design's preferred way to open when a prior day supplies the opening.

**Phase 3 (45 min).** Four suites, two sequential defects, prediction before adjudication; then four prompt sets against one instructions file, two further defects, and the same prediction discipline. The mechanism is deliberately identical to S007's, so that the structure costs nothing and all attention goes to the content. The closing task — what is each suite a hypothesis about — is the phase's abstraction and is generated by groups after both adjudications. **The executed half's second defect is the phase's payload and is the only defect in the module that no test of output can catch**: the glob is narrowed by one character, output degrades, nothing fails, and the evidence is a file name absent from a list. Running the preview customization analyzer afterwards is the phase's closing move and it is chosen to fail: the analyzer reports on wording and the defect is not in the wording.

**Phase 4 (50 min).** Eleven points. Point 3 makes the module cumulative by naming all three prior days as claim sources. Point 4 attacks coverage and is placed after phase 3's suite C has already demonstrated the problem, because an argument against a measured metric needs a demonstration in front of it. Point 6 is the single forward link to S050. Points 8 and 9 consolidate the executed half; point 9 exists because the analyzer is the precise object students will mistake for a test, and it is named rather than left implicit. Point 10 is the day's perishable content and its recorded silence. Point 11 is the strong-form scope statement.

**Phase 5 (90 min).** Six steps on the student's own artefact. Steps 1 to 3 are the commitment, step 4 the evidence, steps 5 and 6 the reconciliation. Step 5 — what you are still not entitled to conclude — is the sentence the entire measurement strand later depends on, appearing here in its cheapest possible form. **Step 6 acquires a third answer on this day** — the claim was wrong, the code was wrong, or the file was not in force — and the third is the one students will not reach for, which is why the References list is required alongside every prompt result at step 4.

**Phase 6 (40 min).** The high-coverage production failure. Chosen because argument alone will not displace an institutionally endorsed metric, and a concrete case in the system the students can see will do more than phase 4 point 4 can.

**Phase 7 (30 min).** Independent attack construction then comparison. Divergence between two competent attacks is the phase's designed output and establishes that test design is design — a claim phase 4 asserts and phase 7 demonstrates.

**Phase 8 (20 min).** Phase 1 item 1 responses read back unattributed against what the suite established. Step 3 prediction accuracy distributed. The commitment carries into S010.

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**Students with more relevant prior knowledge.** A student with professional testing experience may already write boundary tests and may read the day as basic. The predicted failure is that they perform steps 1 to 4 competently and skip step 5, because their existing practice has no equivalent of *what you are still not entitled to conclude* — professional testing culture does not usually require it. Countermeasure: step 5 is the most heavily weighted specification element and is checked first. Their phase 7 partner is also briefed to press on claim strength rather than on test technique.

**Students with less relevant prior knowledge.** A student whose testing experience is minimal has an advantage in habit and a disadvantage in fluency: they will not need to unlearn confirmatory practice, but may struggle to design any test at all. Countermeasures: fading across the three claims; a supplied falsification strategy for claim 2; and the fact that the object under test is their own two-day-old code. Residual risk: their claims at step 1 may be too vague to attack, and the countermeasure is that the worked claim 1 establishes the required specificity.

**Phase 3 is too easy.** Groups often predict the first defect's catchers correctly. The second defect is chosen to be caught by a different subset, which reliably breaks the pattern groups have just formed. Fallback if a group predicts both correctly: ask them to construct a third defect that suite B misses, which is hard and which establishes that even a claim-directed suite is a bounded hypothesis.

**Affective failure.** Two. The first is **defensiveness**, from students whose testing practice is being devalued; countered by locating the failure in the practice's structure and by phase 6's industry-standard case. The second and more insidious is **nihilism about testing** — if passing establishes so little, why test at all. This is the same fatalism risk as S007's and it is more dangerous here, because the nihilistic conclusion is superficially consistent with the day's argument. Countermeasure: phase 4 point 2's asymmetry cuts both ways and is stated as such — failing is *strong* evidence, so a well-designed suite that passes has survived genuine attacks and that is worth a great deal. The specification clause requiring an explanation when nothing failed exists partly for this reason: it forces the student to articulate why survival was informative.

**The second executed defect is not noticed.** Phase 3's payload depends on the room reading a References list they have been told about twice, on a day whose subject is that people look in the wrong place. Countermeasure: no group is told which defect was introduced, and the instructor waits. A room that concludes *the model got worse* has produced the day's finding in its most useful form and is allowed to hold that conclusion for several minutes before the list is opened. **If the executed half cannot run, the day states that the four prompt sets were demonstrated rather than operated and the specification does not assess elements 2, 4 or 6's third answer**; step 6 falls back to two answers, which is the pre-revision day and is described as weaker.

## 7. Assessment and evidence generated

No entrustment evidence.

One specification item in CF101, requiring the full chain from prose claims through falsification-directed tests to the not-entitled-to-conclude statements and the claim-or-code-or-scope decisions. Nine elements are checked, each binary, and the list ends *nothing else is assessed*; the treatment of that sentence on a pre-[S041](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S041.md) day is argued at [WP-006](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-006.md) §7.

The distinctive clause: **a suite in which nothing failed, with no explanation, fails the specification.** This is not a requirement that tests fail; it is a requirement that survival be accounted for. A student whose three claims survive three genuine attacks passes by explaining why the attacks were genuine. The clause exists because the alternative — accepting an all-green submission at face value — would reinstate exactly the practice the day is displacing, at the point where the institution's own signal is strongest.

Element 2 requires the attack on the customization claim to be a **prompt file with a review date**, runnable by name. This is a reuse of the structure [S008](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S008.md) step 6 introduces, and per the amended instrumentation rule 5 it is recorded as **contingent on S008's reported outcome**: S008's prompt file is a proposed remedy that has not run, and if S008 reports that the structure failed, this reuse is retracted at the same moment, along with every other contingent reuse of it. The reason for reusing an untested structure here is stated at §8 objection 3 and is economic rather than pedagogical.

Programme instrumentation: phase 1 item 1 responses are the cohort's overstatement baseline, re-probed at S026 and S050; step 3 prediction accuracy is the calibration series' third point; step 5 specification failure is the entitlement-statement baseline carried to S050; the phase 7 divergence rate measures whether test design is being treated as design; and **the rate at which students reach step 6's third answer — the file was not in force — is the module's closing measure of whether the second register landed.** Its branch under instrumentation rule 4 is at [S029](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S029.md): a cohort that does not reach the third answer has S029's instrument-validation task extended to require a scope check before any output is examined, and S029's artefact carries the element that consumes the result, per rule 6.

Dispositions: **integrity** in step 4 and step 6, where the temptation to adjust a claim after seeing a result is direct and unobserved.

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: the day replaces one ritual with another.**

At its strongest: students currently perform "write tests until coverage is adequate." After this day they will perform "state three claims, write three attacks, add a not-entitled-to-conclude sentence." That is a form, it is assessable, and forms that are assessable get satisfied. Within a term, students will be producing three perfunctory claims and three perfunctory attacks, and the not-entitled sentence will be boilerplate — and this ritual will be worse than the old one, because it comes with the vocabulary of rigour attached, which makes its emptiness harder to detect.

The rebuttal concedes the mechanism and disputes the equivalence. Ritualisation is a real and probably inevitable pressure, and the design does not claim immunity. What it claims is that the two rituals fail differently. A coverage ritual can be satisfied without ever considering what the program claims, because coverage is a property of execution. A claim-and-attack ritual cannot be satisfied without stating claims, and a stated claim is inspectable, arguable and attackable by someone else — as phase 7 demonstrates. The degenerate version of the new ritual still produces an artefact that a reviewer can dispute, whereas the degenerate version of the old one produces a number that a reviewer can only accept.

The concession stands nonetheless: the design has no mechanism for detecting perfunctory claims at scale, and the phase 7 comparison is a within-day check, not an assessment-time one. Whether claim quality degrades across the programme is Prediction 4 in §9 and the design does not know the answer.

**Objection 2: attacking your own work conflicts with how students are assessed everywhere else, including in this programme.**

At its strongest: the day asks students to search actively for evidence that their own work is wrong, and then submits that work for assessment. Every incentive students have — here and in every other course they have taken — rewards work that appears sound. Asking a student to document the strongest available attack on their own claim, two days before a module assessment, is asking them to act against their interest, and the specification clause that rewards honest failure does not change the fact that a submission full of failures reads worse than one full of successes to any human assessor, whatever the rubric says.

This objection the design does not fully answer, and it is the more serious of the two. The countermeasures are real but partial: the specification is binary and pass/fail against stated criteria rather than comparative; tokens make resubmission cheap; the error-clinic firewall means nothing disclosed in phase 6 reaches assessment; and the module has now twice rewarded evidenced wrongness over unevidenced rightness, at S007 and here. But the design's own assessment literature is clear that assessors reason from narrative and that stated rubrics do not fully govern judgement, which is precisely the mechanism the objection invokes. The design cannot claim its assessors are immune to a bias it has documented elsewhere in this wiki.

The honest position is that the tension is structural and unresolved. The day asks for a behaviour the surrounding system incentivises against, and mitigates rather than removes the conflict. Whether the mitigation suffices is Prediction 3 in §9.

**Objection 3: testing a specification is unaffordable, and the day does not price it.**

This is the objection that bites on the practical turn, and unlike the first two it is about economics rather than psychology. Testing a program is close to free. The suite is deterministic, it runs in seconds, an assertion compares an observed value with an expected one, and the result is a colour. **None of that is true of a prompt.** Each attack is slow, consumes a metered resource, produces a different result on a second run, and yields an artefact that a person has to read and judge — there is no assertion, no expected value and no green bar. The day therefore asks students to adopt a discipline whose cost per test is orders of magnitude higher than the one it replaces, whose output is a judgement, and which cannot be put in a pipeline. The predictable consequence is not that students disagree with the argument; they will agree with it, complete the specification item, and never test an instructions file again outside this programme, because nobody has the budget. A practice that survives only where it is assessed is a practice the programme has failed to teach, and it is worse than the coverage ritual it replaced, because coverage at least ran on every commit.

**The design concedes this and the concession is not small.** The economics are as stated. The day cannot make prompt-testing cheap, it does not have a continuous-integration story for it, and — candidly — the programme's own answer does not arrive until [ME301](/archive/bsc-programme/program/06-Course-Catalogue.md), two years later, in a course most of this cohort has not chosen yet.

**The cheap fix is named and is now in the specification.** Element 2 requires the attack on the customization claim to be written as a prompt file with a review date, runnable by name, by somebody who is not its author. That costs one file. It does not make a run cheaper, but it changes what the run is: a named, versioned, reviewable procedure that survives the session, rather than a sentence typed once into a chat box and lost. **The thing that made prompt-testing unaffordable was partly that it was unrepeatable**, and repeatability is the half of the problem a file can solve. The reuse of S008's structure is recorded as contingent on S008's reported outcome, per instrumentation rule 5 as amended, and is retracted with every other contingent reuse if that outcome is bad.

The residue is the half a file cannot solve. Every run still costs, every result still needs a reader, and the judgement is still a judgement. The design's position is that this is a real cost of a real practice rather than a defect in the teaching — characterising an instrument has always been more expensive than using it, which is why [S029](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S029.md) exists and why so few teams do it — but that position is an argument, not evidence, and Prediction 5 in §9 is the only instrument the design has for whether the practice survives contact with a term in which nobody is checking.

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** A majority of students will fail phase 1 item 3, and observed search behaviour will show them adding tests in the style of the existing suite rather than examining what the suite claims. *Instrument:* item 3 outcome plus a brief observation protocol coded for search mode.

**Prediction 2.** In phase 3, group prediction accuracy will be high for the first defect and substantially lower for the second. *Instrument:* per-defect prediction accuracy. **If accuracy is equally high for both, the second defect is not doing its job of breaking the just-formed pattern and the phase needs reconstruction.**

**Prediction 3.** Submissions will contain fewer documented failures than students' phase 5 step 4 in-session results, comparing the same students' session outcomes against their submitted artefacts. *Instrument:* in-session step 4 results against the CF101 submission. **This is the §8 objection 2 test: a systematic gap is evidence that the incentive conflict is operating and that the mitigations are insufficient.**

**Prediction 4.** Claim specificity in the equivalent step at S029 will not be lower than at S009, when rated blind by the same rubric. *Instrument:* blind-rated claim specificity at S009 and S029. **This is the ritualisation test from §8 objection 1; declining specificity across the programme is the signature of a form being satisfied.**

**Prediction 5.** Prompt files written at S009 element 2 will be re-run, by their authors or by anyone, on fewer than one in ten of the repositories that still contain them by the end of Q2. *Instrument:* prompt-file invocation records in student repositories at the Q2 boundary, against the files present. **This is the §8 objection 3 test and it is a prediction of failure.** If the rate is that low, repeatability was not the binding constraint, the cheap fix addressed the wrong half, and the correct response is to stop claiming the practice transfers and to teach it explicitly as something the programme does and industry currently does not.

**Prediction 6.** Fewer than a quarter of students will reach step 6's third answer — that the file was not in force — unprompted, despite having met the failure in phase 3 the same morning. *Instrument:* step 6 responses coded for which of the three answers was given, against the phase 3 group that found the second defect. **If the phase 3 group does no better than the rest, the executed demonstration is not transferring even at a four-hour gap**, which is a finding about the whole executed-cases turn and not only about this day.

## What a student should take from this paper

**What the design is claiming.** That a suite written by the person who believed the code was right tests the model that produced the code, and that defects live where that model is wrong — so a confirmatory suite and an informative one are both green and cannot be told apart by inspection. Passing is weak evidence; failing is strong. The same applies to an instructions file that has never produced non-conforming work.

**What the evidence licenses.** The evidential asymmetry, construct validity and entitlement-of-conclusion positions are **class 1**, verified here. The assessor-narrative finding the paper turns against its own mitigations is **class 1** as well. Confirmation bias and the optimisation of measurable proxies are **class 2**, direction and mechanism only. The customization analyzer is **class 4** and is taught as an uncharacterised instrument, because **no precision, recall or false-positive rate is published for it**, and no measure exists of how reliably a conforming file produces conforming output.

**What to do with it.**

- **State the claim first, then choose the input most likely to falsify it** — not merely one capable of failing.
- **Account for survival.** A suite in which nothing failed, with no explanation, fails. Say why the attacks were genuine.
- **Carry three answers, not two:** the claim was wrong, the code was wrong, or the file was never in force. Only the References list settles the third.
- **Never let the system under test supply the evidence.** Asking the agent whether its own output conformed collapses the asymmetry and fails the specification: **untrusted content is data and never authority**, and a self-report is an unsupported inference rather than an observed result. See the [Agent Configuration Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/13-Agent-Configuration-Standard.md).

**The concession worth remembering.** The paper concedes that it asks you to document the strongest available attack on your own work days before that work is assessed, that every incentive you have runs the other way, and that **the design cannot claim its assessors are immune to a bias it has documented elsewhere in this wiki.** The tension is structural and unresolved. It concedes separately that its own prediction about whether prompt-testing survives outside this programme is a prediction of failure.
