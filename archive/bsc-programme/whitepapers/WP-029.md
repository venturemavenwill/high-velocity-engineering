## In plain terms

**What this paper argues, before the formal version.**

Three days have taken things away from you. Your instrument may not measure what you are using it for. Most of your error will not shrink no matter how much data you collect, and one kind of error cannot be diagnosed at all. Your number describes the population you sampled and no other. **All three are true and not one of them tells you what to do**, which is why a student holding all three and nothing else is worse off than one holding none: the first hesitates over everything, and the second at least produces something.

The diagnostic apparatus only pays for itself in one place, and it is *before the instrument exists.* That is what this day is.

The reframing that makes it work is small and it changes everything downstream: **start from the decision, not from the question.** How good a measurement has to be is set entirely by what will change depending on its answer. An instrument better than the decision needs is waste; an instrument worse than the decision needs is a hazard. **Neither of those errors is visible if you start from the question**, because a question carries no quality requirement with it — which is why so many instruments are either gold-plated or quietly inadequate, and why nobody notices either.

The day's second point is the one almost nobody arrives holding. Instruments get validated when they are built and then trusted forever, and the failure mode is silent: an instrument that was correct and stopped being correct goes on emitting perfectly plausible numbers. **So the question is not whether your instrument is right. It is what would count as evidence that it is working, and what you will do when that evidence stops arriving.** An instrument with no answer to that is unfinished, however elegantly it is designed.

**What this paper concedes, and it is a real concession.** The day tells students that nobody publishes how well these judged instruments agree with human judgement, so the only calibration that will ever exist is one they produce — and then has them produce it by hand-scoring a small held-back set themselves and counting the disagreements. **That is not a calibration.** It establishes that the instrument and the student differ; it does not establish which of them is right, and the day's language strongly implies the student. The research this programme rests on says the opposite is entirely possible. **So the day teaches students to distrust one unvalidated judged instrument and hands them a second one — themselves — and treats its output as ground truth.** The repair adds a second rater, and the paper concedes in the same paragraph that two raters on a handful of cases give a disagreement rate rather than a reliability estimate: a smaller version of the same problem, not a solution to it.

## Abstract

This paper defends organising the module's fourth day as a constructive procedure that begins from the decision rather than from the question, and defends assessing it on whether the instrument carries a validation criterion rather than on whether it is well designed. The first argument is that a measurement's required quality is determined by what will change depending on its answer, that instruments are routinely built to a standard nobody needed or to one nobody checked, and that a design sequence anchored at the decision makes both errors visible before construction begins. The second argument is that the module's three diagnostic days identify failures which are cheap to prevent and expensive to repair, so their value is realised at design time and nowhere else — which makes this day the module's payoff rather than its summary. The paper additionally defends requiring the validation to be *produced* rather than described, on the ground that no vendor publishes agreement data for any judged evaluator and a calibration the student does not make is a calibration nobody has; and it extends the same treatment to the prompt files students already own, which are instruments with no review date and no failing case. It records that the day carries an explicit obligation, stated in three preceding whitepapers, to discharge the module's accumulated fatalism risk. It concedes that the design cannot demonstrate the obligation is met, that the day's instruments enter operational use on a basis the programme has not made safe, and that the validation it now requires is itself uncalibrated.

## 1. The instructional problem this day must solve

Three consecutive days have subtracted. S026 established that an instrument may not measure the construct it is used for. S027 established that most error does not shrink with sampling and that one class cannot be diagnosed at all. S028 established that a number describes the population it sampled and no other, and that a correctly computed interval can be confidently wrong.

Every one of those findings is true and none is actionable on its own. A student who holds all three and nothing else is worse equipped than one who holds none, because the first will hesitate over every measurement and the second will at least produce something. The module's diagnostic apparatus has value only when it is applied *before* an instrument exists, which is what this day does.

The reframing that makes this work is at point 1: **start from the decision, not the question.** How good a measurement must be is determined by what will change depending on its answer. An instrument better than the decision requires is waste; an instrument worse is a hazard; and neither error is visible if the design begins from the question, because a question has no quality requirement attached. Phase 3's late-revealed decision context is built to produce this as a finding rather than as a maxim.

The day's second problem is the module's most-skipped step. Instruments are validated at construction and then trusted indefinitely, and the failure mode — an instrument that was correct and silently stopped being correct — produces no signal at all, because the instrument continues to emit plausible numbers. Item 3 asks what would count as evidence that the instrument is working, and it is answered by very few, which is the pretest's finding and the specification's target.

The third problem is one the design created and must now discharge. [WP-026](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-026.md) §5, [WP-027](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-027.md) §5 and [WP-028](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-028.md) §5 each identified a cumulative fatalism risk and each asserted that it resolves at S029. WP-028 stated explicitly that if it does not, the module has a structural problem rather than three local ones. Phase 8's direct question — name one thing you could not have established four days ago and now can — is the design's instrument for that claim, and it is a weak one.

## 3. Theoretical basis for the design

### 3.1 Load profile and element interactivity

**High, and different in kind from S028's.** S028's load was inferential; this day's is architectural. The six-step sequence at point 2 is a chain in which each step constrains the next, so a poor decision statement at step 1 propagates through all six — and the propagation is not visible until the instrument is built.

Management:

- **The sequence is enumerated and worked** on a different question before the independent task.
- **Validation forms are supplied as a menu** at step 4, because inventing a validation criterion from nothing is the day's hardest demand and its most-skipped.
- **Phase 3 supplies four built instruments,** so groups evaluate rather than construct.
- **The question is new but small,** and staff supply the domain context, so no comprehension load is added late in the module.
- **One run only,** at step 6. Repeated measurement is S027's work and would displace the design.

The load the design accepts is at step 5. Building an instrument in a portion of eighty-five minutes is only possible because the substrate supplies the plumbing, and the design depends on that.

### 3.2 Desirable difficulties selected, and those deliberately not

**Selected.**

- *Designing before instruction,* item 1, so the day's sequence lands against an attempt already made.
- *Classifying your own weakness by module day,* item 4.
- *The late-revealed decision context,* phase 3.
- *Stating what the instrument cannot detect in the honest form,* step 3 — what I checked for and could not detect, not what it misses.
- *The dual-defeat construction,* phase 7, which must beat both the limitation statement and the validation.
- *Costing the instrument at operating frequency,* step 7.

**Deliberately not selected.**

- *Building the measurement plumbing.* Supplied by the substrate.
- *Repeated measurement and error estimation.* S027's, deliberately excluded so the day stays a design day.
- *Formal validation theory.* A menu of forms, not a theory.
- *Withholding the design sequence.* Point 2 states it.

**Scope, per the [Whitepaper Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md).** Point 2's six-step sequence is presented as *a* sequence, not *the* sequence: it omits the negotiation of the question with the decision-maker, which is S039's material, and it assumes the decision is known, which is frequently false. Point 8 applies the scope rule reflexively — an instrument's own limitation statement is always incomplete and the day requires it to say so, which is the first time in the programme a taught artefact is required to bound itself.

**The platform instance, scoped in the strong form.** The day builds a custom Foundry evaluator and then asserts at point 9 that the same treatment applies to a prompt file. Both readings need bounding.

*Safe.* Concluding that an instrument without a validation criterion is unfinished; that a criterion which cannot fail is not a criterion; that the first thing to do with a new judged instrument is feed it something known to be bad; that a review date and a withdrawal condition are part of the artefact; and that a prompt file has every one of these properties and usually none of these attributes. All of that follows from the instrument's structure and is independent of vendor, model and decade.

*Unsafe.* Two readings. **First, treating the prompt-file analogy as exact.** A custom evaluator has a declared threshold, a declared scale and a machine-readable output; a prompt file has none of those, so the validation forms transfer and the tooling does not. A student who concludes that their prompt file can be validated the way an evaluator can will look for a threshold parameter and not find one, and the correct response is to construct the held-back set anyway and score it by hand. **Second, and more consequential, treating a produced disagreement rate as an accuracy.** It is not. It is the rate at which one instrument and one student differed on a set the student chose, and it establishes that the instrument can fail and where — which is worth having and is not a calibration. §8 records this as the day's third objection and applies the available repair.

The boundary is stated to students at point 11 rather than left to this paper, and step 4's requirement to report the disagreement rate **with its own second rater** is where a student treating it as an accuracy would fail.

### 3.3 Engagement and motivational mechanism

The day runs on **producing something that gets used**.

The phase 5 instrument answers a question staff actually want answered, it is submitted with the student's name attached, and it enters the substrate's documentation with its limitation statement. This is the first student output in the programme that is adopted rather than assessed, and the effect on effort is the strongest the design has observed.

The second mechanism is relief, and it is deliberate. After three subtractive days, a day whose output is an instrument that works is affectively different, and the design leans on that ordering. Phase 3's four instruments are all things somebody actually built and all are usable for some decision — none is a cautionary tale — which is a departure from the module's prior contrasting-cases construction and is intentional.

Phase 8's direct question is the day's most exposed moment. Asking the room to name something they could not have established four days ago and now can is a request that can fail publicly, and the design asks it anyway because a module that cannot answer it has not worked.

The competence risk is at step 5: a student whose instrument does not run has produced nothing to submit. The fallback is that a designed and costed instrument that was not built still meets the specification's other elements, and this is stated in advance.

## 4. Phase-by-phase design and its warrant

**Phase 1 (25 min).** A design attempt before instruction, then four questions about it. Item 3 is the day's diagnostic; item 4 is the module's integration check placed before the day's content rather than after it, so that the classification is made with the apparatus students actually hold. **Item 5 is the sealed Copilot prediction** and its expected answer is the same omission item 3 exposes — the agent produces a working evaluator with no way to tell whether it is right — which makes the day's argument reachable from two directions before any instruction occurs.

**Phase 2 (20 min).** Cumulative retrieval with a synthesis item whose answer is the day's thesis: construct errors are cheapest to prevent at design time and most expensive to repair afterwards. Delivering the thesis as a retrieval question rather than as a claim is the design's standard move for a proposition students can derive.

**Phase 3 (45 min).** Four real instruments, a ranking, then the decision context. **Three are executed and the fourth is demonstrated.** The construction deliberately repeats S025's late-criterion structure, and the repetition is the point — the design wants the move recognised as a class. Instrument C's property is now observed rather than asserted: it measures the construct well under conditions that rarely occur, and the students watch it produce nothing useful when retrieval fails, because it has no context to judge against. Instrument D discharges S026's deferred measurement-effects topic and is **demonstrated rather than operated**, because continuous evaluation is preview and requires production traffic and a connected Application Insights resource the programme does not hold for a student project; it is excluded from assessment. Its measurement effect is sharper than the design originally had: the judge's own explanations are redacted by default, and retaining them writes reasoning about the conversation into telemetry, so observing changes what is *stored* about the observed rather than merely what it costs.

**Phase 4 (50 min).** Eleven points organised as a procedure. Points 3, 4 and 5 map the module's three diagnostic days onto design decisions, which is the module's assembly. Point 7 is the most-skipped step and is given its own segment; its platform content is the load-bearing sentence of the whole module, which is that **no agreement figure is published for any built-in evaluator, so the only calibration that will ever exist for one is the one the engineer produces.** **Point 9 is the [Copilot Practice Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md)'s commitment for this day** and is reachable from instrument A, which the students have just watched behave like a prompt file because it is one. Point 8 applies the scope rule to the artefact itself, point 10 names the platform's chosen defaults, and point 11 separates durable claim from instance.

**Phase 5 (85 min).** Seven steps at class 2 producing an adopted artefact. Step 4 is the specification's weighted element and its second half — produce the held-back set, hand-score it, report the disagreement — is where the day's work concentrates and where its §8 objection-3 repair sits. Step 7's operating cost is what converts an instrument into a proposal.

**Phase 6 (40 min).** An instrument that silently stopped being correct, then an agent failure. The attention to whether anything would have caught it — nothing did, for months — is the phase's content and is the direct argument for step 4. The agent case is an evaluator that returned `pass` on every input for four months and is chosen because **an evaluator that never fails is the failure signature students are least likely to read as a failure**, and because the agent did nothing wrong: it built what was asked, and what was asked did not include a way to be wrong.

**Phase 7 (30 min).** The dual-defeat construction, which is [WP-025](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-025.md) §8's identified repair implemented: the partner's break must defeat both the limitation statement and the validation, which calibrates the attack. The forced trade at the end — extend the validation or extend the statement, both costly — is the day's only genuine decision under constraint.

**Phase 8 (20 min).** The pretest resolved, the sealed Copilot predictions opened, then the module's constructive claim tested directly and in public, then submission with the disagreement rate and review date attached.

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**Students with more relevant prior knowledge.** A student with instrumentation or experimental background will design fluently. Predicted failure: they build to a standard the decision does not require, because their training rewards rigour independent of use, and step 7's operating cost then makes the instrument unaffordable at the required frequency. Countermeasure: step 1 requires the decision and its owner, and step 7 forces the frequency calculation; an instrument that cannot be run as often as the decision needs has failed a stated requirement rather than an aesthetic one.

**Students with less relevant prior knowledge.** Building anything in eighty-five minutes is demanding for a student who has only used supplied apparatus. Countermeasures: the substrate's plumbing; a worked sequence; a validation menu; and the stated fallback that a designed, costed, unbuilt instrument passes the remaining specification elements. Residual risk: their instrument may be adopted and be poor, which is a substrate risk rather than a student one and is handled by staff review before adoption.

**Phase 3 is too easy.** Initial ranking is straightforward; the re-ranking is where the difficulty is, and instrument D reliably survives the first ranking and fails the second for a reason groups struggle to articulate. Fallback if a group handles all four: ask which instrument they would choose if the decision were made once a year rather than daily, which usually reverses their answer and makes the frequency dependence explicit.

**Affective failure.** The day's assigned task is to *repair* an affective failure rather than to avoid one, which is unusual and is worth stating plainly. The module's cumulative fatalism risk has been asserted three times to peak and resolve here. The countermeasures are the day's whole structure: a constructive procedure, four usable instruments, an adopted artefact, and a public claim at phase 8.

The design cannot demonstrate that this works. Phase 8's question is not a measurement — it is a group of students, at the end of a day, asked to say something positive, in a setting where saying nothing is socially awkward. A room that answers it enthusiastically is weak evidence and a room that cannot answer it is strong evidence of failure, which is an asymmetric and unsatisfactory instrument. §9's Prediction 4 is the better one and it is delayed by a quarter.

## 7. Assessment and evidence generated

No entrustment judgement — the module's is at S030.

One specification item in ME101, now carrying a produced validation and the three [Copilot Practice Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/12-Copilot-Practice-Standard.md) elements. **An instrument with no validation criterion fails, however well designed.** This is the module's most consequential specification clause: a well-designed unvalidated instrument is precisely the artefact that produces phase 6's failure, and it is the artefact a competent student will otherwise produce — and, since the revision, it is the artefact Copilot produces by default, which the day's own phase 1 item 5 demonstrates.

**An intervention record with no entries fails**, unless the artefact demonstrates that the design could have detected an intervention-worthy error — step 4's held-back set is such a demonstration, provided the instrument actually disagreed with the student somewhere. A student whose instrument agreed with them on every case has produced a set that discriminates nothing, which is phase 6's failure in miniature and is the correct thing to say to them.

Instrument D at phase 3 is demonstrated rather than operated and is not assessed.

Programme instrumentation: the phase 1 item 3 answer rate is the cohort's validation-awareness baseline, re-probed at S047; item 4's self-classification accuracy is the module's integration measure; **phase 8's public claim is recorded verbatim across cohorts** as the fatalism-resolution evidence, with its weakness noted; the adopted instruments' subsequent operational lifetime is tracked and reported to the following cohort; **the two disagreement rates from step 4 are collected across cohorts and are the programme's only data on how hard its own substrate constructs are to judge**; and item 5's sealed prediction accuracy is the day's frontier-calibration datum, read by the following year's cohort through the [Durable and Perishable Register](/archive/bsc-programme/program/09-Durable-and-Perishable-Register.md), per instrumentation rule 9.

The frontier statements are a sample and the day says so, per instrumentation rule 3: one cohort, one substrate, one question set, on a task where the absence of a validation step was known in advance to be the available failure.

Dispositions: **integrity**, at step 3, where the honest-form limitation statement invites a student to disclose what they could not check.

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: the day cannot demonstrate that it discharges the obligation three whitepapers placed on it.**

At its strongest: WP-026, WP-027 and WP-028 each deferred a known affective risk to this day, and this day's evidence that it resolved is a question asked to a room at the end of a session. That is not an instrument by any standard the module itself has taught — the construct is students' beliefs about what they can establish, the instrument is a public verbal response, the sampling is a self-selected subset of a cohort in a social setting, and the measurement effect is severe. S029 is, on its own terms, an example of the failure S026 exists to prevent.

The rebuttal is that the objection is correct and the design says so in §5 and in the seminar page. What is available in mitigation is the asymmetry: the instrument cannot establish success and can establish failure, since a room that cannot answer the question at all is informative. Weak instruments with informative failure modes have a legitimate place, provided their asymmetry is stated.

The concession is that stating the asymmetry does not make the deferral defensible. Three whitepapers deferred a risk to a day that was always going to lack the means to verify its resolution, and none of them checked whether S029 could bear the claim before making it. That is a planning failure in the design rather than a defect of this day, and it is the same class of failure the [Whitepaper Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md)'s scope rule addresses for claims: an assertion was made repeatedly without its verification conditions being examined. **The scope rule covers enumerations, normative claims and criteria; it does not cover deferrals, and on this evidence it should.** That extension is not made.

**Objection 2: student instruments enter operational use on a basis the programme has not made safe.**

At its strongest: phase 8 submits each instrument for staff use, with the student's name and limitation statement attached, into the substrate's documentation. These instruments were designed in eighty-five minutes by second-year students who have never built one before, validated against a menu of forms, and reviewed — the seminar page says — by staff. The programme's own S026 material establishes that a construct-instrument gap is invisible in the data, and its S029 phase 6 disclosure establishes that instrument decay is invisible for months. So the substrate is acquiring measurement instruments whose failures are, by the programme's own account, undetectable by inspection.

This objection the design does not fully answer. The safeguards are staff review before adoption and the attached limitation statement, and neither is adequate to the risk the module itself has documented. Review catches design errors; it does not catch construct gaps, which is the whole of S026's argument. The limitation statement travels with the instrument; nothing ensures it travels with the *numbers*, which is exactly phase 6's failure mode.

Two repairs are available and neither is made. The first is an expiry date: every adopted student instrument carries a review date after which it is withdrawn unless revalidated, which converts an indefinite risk into a bounded one. The second is that the instrument's output should carry its limitation statement mechanically rather than by convention, which the substrate's tooling could enforce. Both are cheap. Their absence means the programme is exposing its own operational system to a hazard it teaches students to recognise, which is the sharpest version of a self-refutation the design has yet produced.

**Both repairs have since been made elsewhere in the module and this paragraph is left standing.** The expiry is now stated at [S030](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S030.md) point 7 as an automatic withdrawal rather than a reviewable one, and the mechanical coupling of number to limitation is [S028](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S028.md)'s step 8 build. That the repairs arrived from adjacent days rather than from the day that identified them is itself an instance of instrumentation rule 5's propagation problem, running for once in the helpful direction, and it is recorded rather than tidied.

**Objection 3: the validation the day now requires is itself uncalibrated, and the day calls it calibration.**

This is the objection that bites on the practical turn and it is the most uncomfortable of the three, because it is the module's own argument applied to the module's own repair.

At its strongest: point 7 tells students that no agreement figure is published for any built-in evaluator, so the only calibration that will exist is one they produce — and step 4 then has them produce it by hand-scoring twenty cases themselves and computing how often the instrument disagrees. **A disagreement rate between one instrument and one student is not a calibration.** It establishes that the two differ; it does not establish which of them is right, and the day's rhetoric strongly implies that the student is. The research this programme rests on says the opposite is entirely possible: rater idiosyncrasy accounts for a large share of variance in human judgement and is unrelated to expertise, which is the finding [S030](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S030.md)'s two-assessor discipline exists to handle. So the day teaches students to distrust an unvalidated judged instrument and then hands them a second unvalidated judged instrument — themselves — and treats its output as ground truth. **The word "calibration" is doing work the artefact cannot support**, and using it is exactly the construct-instrument slippage S026 exists to prevent, committed in the module's own specification.

The objection is conceded in full. The rebuttal available is narrow and is worth stating: the held-back set's primary function is not to establish truth but to establish that the instrument *can* fail and to locate where, and for that function a single rater is sufficient. An evaluator that returns `pass` on every case in a set containing cases the student built to fail has told the student something unambiguous regardless of whether the student's scores were right.

That rebuttal covers the phase 6 failure mode and covers nothing else. It does not cover the case the day actually produces most often, which is a moderate disagreement rate that the student then interprets.

**The cheap fix is a second rater and it is made.** Step 4 now requires the student's phase 7 partner to independently hand-score ten of the same held-back cases, and the artefact reports **two** numbers: the instrument's distance from the student, and the partner's distance from the student. If the second is as large as the first, the finding is not that the instrument is faulty but that the construct is hard to judge, which is a different finding with a different remedy — tighten the construct, not the evaluator. This costs one swap, it uses a pairing the day already has, it converts an uninterpretable number into an interpretable one, and it is the same move [S030](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S030.md) makes with two assessors two days later, which means the module now teaches the same discipline twice in two registers.

What remains unfixed is recorded. Two raters give a disagreement rate and not a reliability estimate, ten cases is too few to support one, and the day has no apparatus for the difference — S049 does, two quarters away. Until then the artefact carries two numbers whose relationship is interpreted qualitatively, and the design is stating plainly that this is a smaller version of the same problem rather than a solution to it.

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** Fewer than a quarter of students will produce a substantive answer to phase 1 item 3, and the rate will be uncorrelated with prior measurement experience. *Instrument:* item 3 coded for substantive validation criteria, split by the admission background survey.

**Prediction 2.** In phase 3, instrument D will be ranked in the top half before the decision context is revealed and in the bottom half afterwards, for a majority of groups. *Instrument:* paired rankings.

**Prediction 3.** Students with instrumentation backgrounds will produce instruments whose step 7 operating cost exceeds the decision's required frequency at a higher rate than other students. *Instrument:* step 7 costs against stated frequencies, split by background. **This is the over-engineering failure stated as a prediction.**

**Prediction 4.** At S046, students will begin instrument design from the decision rather than from the question, unprompted, at a rate above half. *Instrument:* an S046 protocol coded for decision-first framing. **This is the better test of whether the module's constructive turn took, and it is delayed by a quarter — which is itself the answer to §8 objection 1's complaint about phase 8's instrument.**

**Prediction 5.** Fewer than a fifth of item 5 sealed predictions will name the absence of a validation mechanism; the modal prediction will concern the quality of the evaluator's rubric wording. *Instrument:* item 5 responses coded by predicted failure register against what each agent produced. **The design's claim is that students predict the agent will write a bad rubric and not that it will write an uncheckable one, and that the two are different classes of defect — the first is visible on reading and the second is not, which is why only the second survives review.**

**Prediction 6.** In a substantial minority of step 4 artefacts the partner's disagreement with the student will be comparable to the instrument's, and students in that group will produce step 3 limitation statements naming construct ambiguity at a higher rate than students whose partner agreed with them. *Instrument:* the two disagreement rates against step 3 statements coded for construct-ambiguity content. **This is the §8 objection-3 repair's own check. If the partner rate is near zero throughout, the substrate's constructs are easier to judge than the design assumes and the second rater is ceremony; if it is high throughout, the module has been assessing construct statements it could not itself have scored consistently, which would be a finding about the module rather than about the students.**

## What a student should take from this paper

**What the design is claiming.** That you design an instrument from the decision rather than from the question, because how good a measurement has to be is set by what will change depending on its answer — and that an instrument carrying no validation criterion is unfinished however well it is designed.

**What the evidence licenses.** Instrument design, measurement effects, validation and drift sit in **class 1**; the finding that model-generated evaluators must themselves be validated against human grades, and the finding that rater idiosyncrasy is substantial and unrelated to expertise, are cited for direction and mechanism only, at the depth their notes record. **The three-parameter account of a judged instrument, the disclosure rule that replaces the calibration nobody publishes, and the reason a limitation list is always incomplete are also class 1** as of 30 July 2026, when the practitioner source behind them was read in full. The evaluator machinery sits in **class 4**, read on a stated date: a threshold is a required initialisation parameter, a code-based evaluator must name a model deployment it never calls, several tool integrations carry limited support with the failure mode undocumented, and **no agreement figure is published for any built-in evaluator anywhere.** The configuration corpus is cited at a pinned commit and supplies two specimens, neither assessed. **The decision-first principle and the second rater are class 3.** You may say no calibration is published. **You may not call the disagreement rate you produced an accuracy.**

**What to do with it.**

- **Start from the decision and its owner.** An instrument better than the decision requires is waste and one worse is a hazard, and neither error is visible if you start from the question.
- **Feed a new judged instrument something you know is bad, first.** An evaluator that never fails is the failure signature least likely to be read as one.
- **Give the instrument a review date and a withdrawal condition.** Decay emits no signal; the instrument goes on producing plausible numbers.
- **A gate's authority comes from the tier it runs in, not from the check.** The [Agent Configuration Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/13-Agent-Configuration-Standard.md)'s corpus contains a check that runs advisory in one place and authoritative in another, which puts an instrument's consequences outside the instrument.

**The concession worth remembering.** The paper concedes that the validation it requires is itself uncalibrated and that the day called it calibration: a disagreement rate between one instrument and one student establishes that the two differ, not which is right, and the day's rhetoric implies the student. **Hold the design to that.** The second rater added in repair yields a disagreement rate and not a reliability estimate — a smaller version of the same problem rather than a solution to it.
