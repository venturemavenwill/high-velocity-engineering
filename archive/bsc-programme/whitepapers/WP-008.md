> ## What this paper does not yet do
>
> **It defends a theorem and an ordering. The theorem needs no defence, and the ordering has none.**

Everything else on the day is empirical and unsourced. §8 objection 1's whole rebuttal is that "there is a general and well-supported position that making an expert's implicit structure explicit is one of the more reliable instructional moves available" — **no study, no design, no population, no moderator, no meta-analytic status, and no note in this repository.** §8 objection 2's premise, that unverified self-report of a socially desirable behaviour is close to worthless, is in exactly the same condition, and it is the premise on which the paper's largest concession stands.

## In plain terms

**What this paper argues, before the formal version.**

Most people write recursive code by recognising the shape. They have seen a factorial and a tree walk, they produce something with the same silhouette, and they check it on a few small examples. It works often enough to be reinforced, and it fails in one characteristic way: right on small inputs, wrong or endless on the inputs nobody thought to try.

The real problem is that the checking method is not available when you need it. Ask someone why their recursive procedure is correct and they trace it. Tracing to depth *n* costs *n* steps of attention, and it runs out of road at very small *n* — so for any input where correctness is genuinely in doubt, the trace cannot be done. That leaves a way of writing recursion and no way of checking it, which is how you end up back at trying examples.

**The fix is that recursion and induction are the same thing in different handwriting.** If the base case is right, and the step is right *given* that the recursive call is right, and the input gets smaller in a way that cannot go on for ever, then the procedure is correct. Three questions, and they cost the same three questions whether the input has ten elements or ten million. That is not a teaching analogy; it is the same theorem.

The day then asks you to write the argument *before* the code, which is the reverse of what everyone does. An argument written after working code is a story about a result rather than a derivation of one, and a story is perfectly compatible with not understanding why the thing works.

One more loop matters, and it is the reason the day is urgent rather than merely correct. **You are now writing loops you do not implement.** An agent calls a tool, reads what came back and calls again, and your only control over that is the stopping condition you wrote in a sentence. There is no trace and no debugger, and the failures you will actually meet are the two the day already teaches.

**What this paper concedes, in the same plain terms.** It hands you a proof technique and, in the same session, a place where the technique is not valid — and the invalid place is the one you will use next week. An agent's step is not a function of its input: the same prompt against the same state can do something different, so a measure that shrinks on most steps shrinks on most steps and proves nothing. **There is no termination proof for an agent loop to give you.** The honest object is a cost bound, and it holds only because something outside the model is counting. The paper also concedes that its argument-before-code rule cannot be enforced or measured, and that writing the discipline into a file fixes the order of the *request* and never the order in which you thought.

## Abstract

This paper defends teaching recursion as a notational variant of induction rather than as a programming technique, and defends the argument-before-code ordering that structures the day's main task. The first argument rests on the observation that recursion's standard debugging method — tracing — has cost linear in input size and is therefore unavailable exactly when it is needed, leaving students with a construction method they cannot check; the inductive correspondence supplies a constant-cost check. The second argument is that writing the argument first is the only ordering under which the argument does any work, since an argument written after working code is a narration of a result rather than a derivation of one, and narration is compatible with not understanding why the code works. The paper additionally defends supplying at S008 the structural variability that S005 could not, closing a gap that WP-005 conceded, and defends adding a fourth structure that is not a data type: **an agent's tool-calling loop, which has a base case, a hypothesis and an ordering, and whose non-termination costs money rather than a stack.** It concedes that the argument-first discipline is unverifiable and that the day asks students to self-report it anyway, that the design's central pedagogical claim may be true of experts and false of novices, and — the objection that bites on the practical turn — that the correspondence it teaches as complete acquires a fourth part exactly where the day now applies it.

## 1. The instructional problem this day must solve

Students write recursive procedures by imitation. They have seen a factorial, a list length and a tree traversal; they recognise the shape; they produce a new instance of the shape and check it against examples. This works often enough to be reinforced, and it fails in a characteristic way — the procedure is correct on small inputs and wrong or non-terminating on inputs the student did not think to try.

The deeper problem is that the student's checking method is unavailable. Asked why a recursive procedure is correct, the student traces. Tracing a recursion of depth $n$ costs $n$ steps of attention and exceeds working memory at very small $n$; for any input where correctness is actually in doubt, the trace is impossible. So the student has a construction method and no verification method, and falls back on testing examples, which is the ritual S009 exists to replace.

The inductive correspondence solves this exactly. If the base case is correct and the recursive step is correct *given* that the recursive call is correct, and the input measure is well-founded, then the procedure is correct — and checking those three things costs a constant amount of attention regardless of input size. This is not a pedagogical analogy. It is the same theorem.

The day's second problem is the one WP-005 conceded and did not fix. S005 presented three inductive surfaces in fifty minutes and practised one, which is thin variability for a claim about generality. S008 practises all three, at a two-week gap from S005, which is the spacing the schedule permits and roughly what the design would have chosen anyway.

The third problem is disciplinary and belongs to the module. S006 required an invariant before an implementation; S007 required a bet before a change; S008 requires an argument before code. All three are the same demand — commit to a claim before obtaining the result that would make the claim easy — and the module is deliberately repetitive about it, because the demand is unnatural and will not survive a single exposure.

The fourth problem is the one that makes the day urgent rather than merely correct. **Students are now writing loops they do not implement.** An agent in agent mode calls a tool, reads what came back, and calls again, and the student's only control over that loop is the stopping condition they wrote in a sentence. They have no trace — the trace is a chat transcript of unbounded length — and no debugger, and the two failures they will actually meet are exactly procedure B and procedure C: a base case the loop cannot evaluate, and a measure that decreases without approaching the base. **This is the first loop in the programme whose non-termination has a price attached**, and it is being written by people who have never been asked to state a termination argument for anything. The correspondence is the cheapest available intervention, it costs three questions, and the day is where it fits.

## 2. Entry state and prior knowledge assumptions

- **The three obligations from S005,** two weeks old, retrieved four times since.
- **The S006 precondition vocabulary,** which phase 4 point 6 uses directly.
- **Universal recursion exposure of uneven quality.** Nearly every student can write a recursive procedure; almost none can justify one. This asymmetry is the phase 1 diagnostic.
- **A strong and wrong intuition** that a decreasing measure implies termination. Procedure C in phase 3 targets exactly this.
- **No experience of writing an argument before code.** Two prior days have demanded analogous commitments, which is the extent of the preparation.

## 3. Theoretical basis for the design

### 3.1 Load profile and element interactivity

**High, but lower than it appears.** The correspondence at phase 4 point 1 has three parts and they are largely independent — base, hypothesis, ordering can each be checked without the others. That independence is what makes the day tractable, and it is the design's principal claim about why the inductive method is cognitively cheaper than tracing: tracing has interactivity proportional to depth, while the argument has interactivity of three.

The genuine load concentration is at phase 4 point 2 — the permission to assume the recursive call is correct. This is a single idea with very high subjective difficulty, because it feels circular to students, and it does not decompose. It gets its own segment, a worked instance on each of the three structures, and no competing content during it.

Load management elsewhere:

- **Phase 3 removes construction entirely.** Four procedures supplied; groups analyse only.
- **Phase 5's computation is trivial** and identical across all three structures, so all variation is in the structure and none in the task. This is the same construction as S003 phase 3 and for the same reason.
- **Complete fading across the three structures:** worked, completion, independent.
- **No notation.** Prose arguments throughout.

### 3.2 Desirable difficulties selected, and those deliberately not

**Selected.**

- *Argument before code,* phase 5 steps 1 and 2. The day's defining discipline and the reverse of students' established practice.
- *Finding the input that exhibits procedure C's failure,* phase 3. Hard, because the measure genuinely decreases.
- *The weakest sufficient ordering,* phase 7's second half. First appearance of an optimisation demand rather than a sufficiency demand.
- *Three structures with only the last independent,* which is variability under fading.
- *A two-week spaced retrieval of S005,* with a bridging item most students fail.

**Deliberately not selected.**

- *Withholding the correspondence.* Phase 4 point 1 states it completely. It is a theorem, not a discovery task, and students who generate it themselves generate it wrongly.
- *Interesting computations.* Explicitly refused; the computation is trivial so that the structure is the content.
- *Mutual or non-structural recursion.* Deferred. Both are genuine and both would move the difficulty away from the correspondence.
- *Tracing as a permitted method.* Not forbidden, but phase 4 point 3 argues against it explicitly, and phase 5's task is sized so that tracing is impractical for the tree case.
- *Building an agent.* Refused. The day runs four agent tasks and writes one prompt file; it does not construct an orchestration, which is [S073](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S073.md)'s work and would replace the correspondence with a systems-design problem.
- *Comparing models.* Refused for the reason given at [WP-006](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-006.md) §3.2.
- *Assessment stakes on the same day.*

**The strong-form scope statement, and where it binds.** The correspondence is taught as exact, and it is exact for the three data structures, because a recursive call is a function of its input: same input, same result, and a decreasing well-founded measure therefore guarantees termination. **It is not exact for an agent loop, and the unsafe condition is precise: the step is not a function of its input, so a well-founded measure makes non-termination unlikely rather than impossible.** A termination argument for an agent loop is a bound on cost, not a proof, and where a proof is needed the loop must carry an external counter — an iteration cap, a token budget, a wall clock — that does not depend on the model's cooperation. The boundary is stated at phase 4 point 1 rather than only at point 9, so that it travels with the slogan students will actually retrieve, and it is bound to the artefact: **an artefact claiming that an agent loop terminates without naming the external bound fails the specification.**

### 3.3 Engagement and motivational mechanism

The day runs on **a gap the students produce themselves in the first thirty minutes**.

Phase 1 items 3 and 4 are the mechanism. Item 3 — write the procedure — is answered by nearly everyone, quickly and confidently. Item 4 — say why it is correct — is answered by almost nobody. The two items are adjacent, on the same procedure, written by the same student, four minutes apart. The gap is not a claim made by an instructor; it is a document in the student's own handwriting.

That construction is the day's strongest motivational asset because it converts the day's content from an imposition into an answer to a question the student now has. The design uses the same structure at S026 and S046, and this is its first appearance.

Competence is protected asymmetrically. Item 3's success is real and is acknowledged: students *can* write recursion, and the day does not pretend otherwise. What they cannot do is justify it, which is framed as a missing tool rather than a deficiency — and phase 4 supplies the tool in one session, which is a rare instance in this programme of a difficulty being fully resolved on the day it is raised.

Phase 7's second half introduces a different engagement: finding the weakest sufficient ordering is a puzzle with a right answer and a competitive quality, and it is the phase students report enjoying most.

## 4. Phase-by-phase design and its warrant

**Phase 1 (25 min).** Five items, with 3 and 4 as the paired diagnostic. Item 2 separates termination from correctness, retrieving S005 point 5, and is answered wrongly in a specific way: students say a non-terminating procedure is "wrong" rather than "correct if it terminates, which it does not," which is the conflation the day inherits.

**Phase 2 (20 min).** Free recall with a bridging item that a minority answer. The design deliberately alternates answerable and unanswerable bridges across S006, S007 and S008, so that the final item retains its instructional value without becoming either routine or hopeless.

**Phase 3 (45 min).** Four procedures, three obligations, one deep structure; then the same three obligations run as four agent tasks. Procedure C is the written half's payload: a decreasing measure that never reaches the base is counterintuitive enough that groups argue about whether it is possible before they find the input. Procedure D imports S006 into the recursion and is the connection the module needs for S009. **Run C is the executed payload and is chosen because its base case is unreachable for a reason the room can verify rather than take on trust**: the Learn MCP Server serves published documentation, no false-positive rate for the customization analyzer is published anywhere, and the search therefore narrows forever. Run D is the silent-ignore path, which is procedure D with the platform supplying the missing guarantee. All four runs are operated; the prerequisite is a Copilot subscription, a workspace folder and network access to a public, unauthenticated endpoint.

**Phase 4 (50 min).** Nine points. Point 1 is complete and short, and now carries its own boundary clause rather than deferring it. Point 2 is the conceptual obstacle and gets a full segment with three worked instances. Point 3 is the practical argument for the whole approach and is stated as a cost claim, which makes it checkable. Point 4 delivers the variability S005 promised. Points 7 and 8 consolidate the four runs, and point 8 is the day's perishable content and its named default — a token budget that applies to one tool and not the other, and a documented retry with no documented bound. Point 9 is the strong-form scope statement.

**Phase 5 (90 min).** One trivial computation, three structures, seven steps, complete fading. Steps 1 and 2's ordering is the discipline; step 3's well-foundedness argument is where procedure C's lesson is applied; step 4 is S006 inside a recursion; step 5 is the break, now the module's established discriminator. **Steps 6 and 7 are the build**: the discipline written as a reviewable procedure with a stopping condition and a bound, and then the same three obligations stated about the procedure itself. Step 7 is where the fourth part of the correspondence becomes the student's problem rather than a phase 4 clause.

**Phase 6 (40 min).** The instructor's non-terminating recursion, with attention to why every test input happened to be well-founded. That detail is the phase's content and it is a direct set-up for S009: the tests were not chosen to falsify anything.

**Phase 7 (30 min).** Find the input, then find the weakest sufficient ordering. The second half is the first optimisation demand in the programme and is kept as discussion rather than artefact so that it stays exploratory.

**Phase 8 (20 min).** The item 3/4 distribution shown. Then the unverifiable self-report on whether the argument-first discipline held — asked because the asking is itself the intervention, and because the aggregate is informative even if individual responses are not.

## 5. Predicted failure modes and countermeasures

**Students with more relevant prior knowledge.** A student who has taken a functional-programming course may already hold the correspondence, in which case phases 1, 3 and 4 are revision. The predicted failure is skipping the argument-first discipline on the grounds that they already know the answer — which is exactly the failure the discipline exists to prevent, and which is undetectable in their artefact because their code will be correct. Countermeasure: phase 7's weakest-ordering demand is not served by prior exposure, and these students are assigned procedure C variants in phase 3 as adjudicators. Residual: the discipline cannot be enforced on them, only requested.

**Students with less relevant prior knowledge.** A student whose recursion experience is limited to copying examples will pass item 3 by imitation and stall at step 1, where there is nothing to imitate. Countermeasures: the natural-number case is worked through steps 1 to 3 with the room, the list case supplies the argument's skeleton, and only the tree case is independent. Residual risk: the tree case may be unreachable within the time, and the fallback is that they complete the list case fully and attempt the tree argument without implementing it — which preserves the day's actual objective, since the argument is the objective.

**Phase 3 is too easy.** Procedures A, B and D are separable quickly by many groups. The difficulty is concentrated in C and in the input-finding demand, which is robust. Fallback: ask the group to construct a procedure whose measure decreases, reaches the base case, and is still incorrect — which forces them to separate the ordering obligation from the hypothesis obligation, and which several groups find genuinely hard.

**Affective failure.** The characteristic risk is the **circularity objection experienced as a trick**. A student who does not accept phase 4 point 2 does not merely fail to use it; they conclude that the entire method is sleight of hand, and this belief is stable and hard to dislodge, because from inside it every worked example looks like question-begging. Countermeasures: the point gets its own segment; it is grounded explicitly in S005's induction, which the student already accepted in a mathematical setting where it did not feel circular; and the instructor is briefed to invite the objection openly rather than to pre-empt it, since the unvoiced version does not get resolved. This is the day's most consequential affective risk and the countermeasure is a conversation, which is weak.

**Run C does not stop.** The executed payload is a loop the design intends to run long and then interrupt, and an interrupted demonstration in front of thirty groups has a cost. Countermeasure: each group sets a token budget on the endpoint and an explicit tool-call cap before run C begins, which is the day's own lesson applied to the day's own exercise and is stated as such. **The instructor's fallback if the endpoint is unavailable is a recording, in which case the day states that runs A to D were demonstrated rather than operated and the specification does not assess elements 6 and 7**; the prompt file falls back to a written procedure with its stopping condition argued rather than run, which is a weaker day and is described as one.

## 7. Assessment and evidence generated

No entrustment evidence.

One specification item, jointly in CF101 and MS101 — the module's second joint item. Ten elements are checked, each binary, and the list ends *nothing else is assessed*; the treatment of that sentence on a pre-[S041](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S041.md) day is argued at [WP-006](/archive/bsc-programme/whitepapers/WP-006.md) §7.

The specification contains an unusual integrity clause: **an argument written after the code passes if it is disclosed as such, and fails if it is presented as prior and the artefact contradicts that.** The clause exists because the design would rather have accurate reports of a violated discipline than inaccurate reports of an observed one, and because the module's whole assessment posture is that honest reporting of an unfavourable result is rewarded — established at S007 and repeated here.

Element 7 is the artefact binding for the strong-form scope statement: the student states the three obligations for their own prompt file and names which of them depends on the model's cooperation. **A claim that an agent loop terminates, with no external bound named, fails.** This is the only place in the module where a student is required to say that their own argument is a bound rather than a proof, and the design regards it as the most transferable sentence on the day.

Programme instrumentation: the phase 1 items 3 and 4 gap is the cohort's construct-versus-justify baseline and is re-probed at S014; the phase 8 self-report aggregate is the argument-first compliance estimate, treated as a lower bound; the phase 3 procedure C resolution rate is the well-foundedness diagnostic; **phase 1 item 5 — whether the student names a stopping condition the agent can evaluate — is the cohort's agent-termination baseline**, and its branch under instrumentation rule 4 is at [S073](/archive/bsc-programme/seminars/S073.md), where the same students specify a tool boundary and the artefact requires the stopping condition to be restated. Per instrumentation rule 6, S073 is the named later occasion and carries the element that consumes the result.

## 8. Objections and rebuttals

**Objection 1: the correspondence is an expert's reconstruction and is not how anyone learns recursion.**

At its strongest: experienced programmers describe recursion inductively because they already understand it, and the inductive account is a post-hoc rationalisation of a competence acquired by other means — pattern exposure, tracing small cases, building intuition about self-similarity. Teaching the expert's reconstruction to novices supplies the vocabulary of understanding without the understanding, and may actively interfere, because the student who is told not to trace loses the one method that was building their intuition. The day's phase 4 point 3 explicitly argues against tracing, which on this account is removing the ladder.

The rebuttal accepts the premise about experts and rejects the inference. It is likely true that many experts acquired recursion by exposure and reconstructed the inductive account afterwards. That says nothing about whether the reconstruction is teachable, and there is a general and well-supported position that making an expert's implicit structure explicit is one of the more reliable instructional moves available. More decisively, the alternative has a specific and observable failure: students who acquire recursion by exposure produce procedures they cannot check, and the design's whole case is that the checking method is the thing worth teaching.

The objection's point about tracing is partly conceded and the day's position is narrower than phase 4 point 3 makes it sound. Tracing small cases to build intuition is not discouraged and is in fact how the phase 4 worked examples proceed. What is argued against is tracing as a *verification* method, which is a different use. That distinction is in the design and is not stated clearly enough in the phase 4 script.

**Objection 2: the argument-first discipline is unverifiable, and the day knows it and asks anyway.**

At its strongest: nothing in the artefact distinguishes an argument written first from an argument written after working code and presented as prior. Phase 8 asks students to self-report, which is asking them to report a violation with no verification and no consequence — and self-report of a socially-desirable behaviour with no verification is close to worthless as measurement. Worse, the specification's integrity clause creates a situation where a student who violated the discipline and reports it honestly passes, and a student who violated it and reports it dishonestly also passes, since the artefact cannot distinguish them. The clause therefore has no teeth and the discipline is, in practice, optional.

This objection the design does not fully answer, and the concession is substantial. The discipline is unenforceable and the design has chosen not to construct an enforcement mechanism — a plausible one exists, requiring the argument to be submitted separately and timestamped before the code, and it was rejected as ceremony that would displace the intent it was meant to protect. What remains is the request, the stated rationale, and the aggregate self-report, and none of these is measurement.

The design's actual position is that the discipline is a **norm under construction rather than a rule under enforcement**, and that norms are built by repetition and visible institutional seriousness rather than by verification. That position is coherent but it is not evidence, and the honest statement is that the design does not know whether the discipline is being followed by anyone. Prediction 3 in §9 is a weak indirect instrument and is the best available.

**One thing has changed and it is smaller than it looks.** Step 6 writes the discipline into a prompt file, which fixes the order in which the *request* is made and makes that order reviewable by somebody other than its author. It does not fix the order in which the student thought, which was always the unobservable part. The concession above is therefore narrowed rather than closed, and the narrowing is worth exactly one sentence: the design now has an artefact that a colleague can read and disagree with, where before it had a self-report.

**Objection 3: the correspondence is taught as complete and is incomplete precisely where the day now applies it.**

This is the objection that bites on the practical turn. Induction's guarantee depends on the recursive call being a function of its input — same input, same result — and that is what makes a decreasing well-founded measure sufficient for termination. **An agent's step is not a function of its input.** The same prompt against the same state can produce a different tool call, so a measure that decreases on most steps decreases on most steps, and nothing in the argument rules out the case where it does not. The day nonetheless teaches point 1 as a three-part correspondence with no fourth part, spends fifty minutes making that memorable, and then asks students to apply it to a loop where a fourth part exists and is load-bearing. The predictable result is a cohort that writes *this terminates because the budget decreases* with the confidence the day has just installed, having been told that this style of argument is the checking method that replaces tracing. That is not a small pedagogical slip: it is the day handing students a proof technique and a domain in which the technique is invalid, in the same session, and the invalid domain is the one they will use next week.

**The design concedes this in full, and the cheap fix is one clause.** Phase 4 point 1 now states its own boundary — *there is no fourth part, for a procedure; for an agent loop there is one, and it is named at point 9* — so the qualification travels with the slogan rather than arriving eight points later. This is specification rule 2 applied to the design's own teaching material: a qualification must travel with the quantity it qualifies, in the same field, and a boundary stated at point 9 for a claim made at point 1 will be separated from it, because point 1 is the sentence students retrieve. The cost is one clause and a slightly less clean slogan.

What the fix does not do is give students a termination proof for an agent loop, because there is not one to give. The residue is stated at phase 4 point 9 and bound to the artefact at element 7: **the honest object is a cost bound, and it holds only because something outside the model is counting.** The design regards that as the correct thing to teach and notes that it is a weaker claim than the one the day's title implies.

## 9. Falsifiable predictions and instrumentation

**Prediction 1.** The phase 1 item 3 success rate will substantially exceed the item 4 success rate, and the gap will be larger for students with more programming experience than for those with less. *Instrument:* paired item scoring against the admission background survey. **The interaction is the interesting part: if experienced students show a smaller gap, recursion justification is acquired by exposure and §8 objection 1 gains force.**

**Prediction 2.** Fewer than half of groups will find procedure C's exhibiting input within phase 3, and groups that do will have first questioned whether the procedure can fail at all. *Instrument:* phase 3 resolution rate and observed group discussion coded for the possibility-questioning move.

**Prediction 3.** Students who report at phase 8 that the argument-first discipline held will produce step 3 well-foundedness arguments rated higher than those who report it did not, and the difference will exceed what their phase 1 item 4 performance predicts. *Instrument:* step 3 ratings against phase 8 self-report, controlling for item 4. **This is the indirect discipline instrument from §8 objection 2; a null result means either the discipline does not matter or the self-report is uninformative, and it cannot distinguish the two.**

**Prediction 4.** At S014, students will apply structural recursion to an unfamiliar tree problem without being prompted to consider the inductive structure, at a higher rate than they applied it at S008 step 5. *Instrument:* S014 observation protocol coded for unprompted structural reasoning.

**Prediction 5.** In element 7, students will identify the stopping condition as model-dependent far more often than they identify the *ordering* as model-dependent, even though both are. *Instrument:* element 7 responses coded for which obligations were marked as depending on the model's cooperation. **This is the §8 objection 3 test.** If the ordering is systematically missed, the point-1 clause is not doing the work claimed for it, and the correct response is to move run C's reconciliation from phase 3 into phase 5 so that students meet a decreasing-but-unreliable measure in their own artefact rather than in a supplied one.

**Prediction 6.** Fewer than a third of the cohort will write a phase 1 item 5 stopping condition that an agent could evaluate, and the rate will not differ by prior programming experience. *Instrument:* item 5 responses coded as *evaluable* or *not*, against the admission background survey. **If experienced students do markedly better, the competence is acquired by exposure and the day's necessity is weaker than claimed**; if nobody does well, the item is a good baseline and the S073 branch in §7 is the response.

## What a student should take from this paper

**What the design is claiming.** That recursion is induction in different notation, not a programming trick; that tracing costs attention proportional to depth and is therefore unavailable at exactly the sizes where correctness is in doubt; and that checking base, hypothesis and ordering costs the same three questions however large the input.

**What the evidence licenses.** The correspondence itself is a theorem, not an empirical claim, and it is exact for the three data structures. Fading, element interactivity, variability of practice and the unreliability of unverified self-report on socially desirable behaviour are **class 2** — direction and mechanism only. The Learn MCP Server material is **class 4**: a documented refresh-and-retry recovery **with no stated bound**, no published default for the token-budget parameter, no retrieval accuracy, and a silent-ignore path for a named-but-absent tool. That argument-first produces better work is **class 3**, and the paper states plainly that the design does not know whether anyone follows it.

**What to do with it.**

- **Check three things instead of tracing one path:** the base case, the permission to assume the recursive call, and a well-founded measure.
- **Do not carry the correspondence into an agent loop unqualified.** The step is not a function of its input, so the honest object is a cost bound, not a proof.
- **Name the external bound — an iteration cap, a token budget, a wall clock — that does not depend on the model's cooperation.** A stopping condition the model is merely asked to honour is an unsupported inference; one counted outside it is an executable check. See the [Agent Configuration Standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/13-Agent-Configuration-Standard.md).
- **Disclose an argument written after the code.** Disclosed, it passes; presented as prior when the artefact says otherwise, it fails.

**The concession worth remembering.** The paper concedes in full that it teaches a proof technique and, in the same session, a domain in which that technique is invalid — and that the invalid domain is the one you will use next week. It concedes separately that the argument-first discipline is unenforceable and unmeasured, that the prompt file fixes the order of the *request* and never the order in which you thought, and that **there is no termination proof for an agent loop to give you.**
