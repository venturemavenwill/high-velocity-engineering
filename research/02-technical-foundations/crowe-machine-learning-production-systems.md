## What this source is
This is a practitioner survey of everything in a production machine learning system that is not the model. Its organising claim, stated in Chapter 1, is that "a model usually contains only about 5% of the code that is required to put an ML application into production", and the remaining twenty-two chapters are an inventory of the other ninety-five per cent: data collection, labelling and validation; feature engineering and selection; data lineage and storage; model resource management; analysis, interpretability and fairness; serving patterns and infrastructure; model management, versioning and delivery; monitoring and drift; privacy and legal exposure; pipeline orchestration; and generative AI.

The authors are explicit about the register they are writing in. The Preface says the book "is intended in many cases to only introduce you to the topics involved and give you enough background to know when you need to dig deeper", and that "nearly all of these topics have entire books written about them". It is a map, not a treatise, and it says so. The stated audience is people with the job title of ML engineer or something adjacent, including those for whom production deployment is only an occasional or collaborative concern.

Two things follow from the authorship that a reader must hold throughout. First, the book's default toolchain is TensorFlow Extended and the surrounding Google stack — TFDV, TFMD, TF Transform, TFMA, MLMD, TF Serving, TF Lite, Vertex AI, Kubeflow, Apache Beam and Dataflow — and both worked example pipelines are TFX pipelines. Alternatives are named, usually briefly and usually in a list at the end of a section. Second, and more usefully, the book is a legible account of **how one large organisation actually operates production ML**, written by people who built the tooling. Read as a description of a working practice it is strong evidence; read as a comparative evaluation of practices it is not evidence at all.

## The argument in full
Chapter 1 sets the frame by contrasting research ML with production ML across five axes. Research ML uses a static dataset; production ML uses real-world data that is "dynamic and usually shifting". Research ML has one design priority, usually accuracy over the training set; production ML has several at once — "fast inference, fairness, good interpretability, acceptable accuracy, and cost minimization". Research ML terminates in a single optimal result; production ML "requires continuous monitoring, assessment, and retraining". Interpretability and fairness are described as important everywhere but crucial here. The chapter then lists the benefits the authors claim for automated pipelines — freeing data scientists from maintaining existing models, preventing a specific class of bug in which preprocessing changes after training, creating a record for debugging and reproduction, and standardising onboarding — and closes with the honest qualification that "not every data science project needs a pipeline", the threshold being whether the model has users.

Chapter 2 opens the data argument with two anonymous industry quotations, one from "an ML practitioner at Uber" and one from "an ML practitioner at Gojek", asserting respectively that "Broken data is the most common cause of problems in production ML systems" and that "No other activity in the machine learning lifecycle has a higher return on investment than improving the data a model has access to". The chapter then does the useful work: data must have "feature space coverage that is close to that of the prediction requests you will receive"; measurement heterogeneity, integer-versus-float confusion and missing-value encoding are named as concrete defect classes, with the memorable example of an elevation feature where a zero may mean sea level or may mean that no reading was received; and where one model's output is another model's input, the authors flag "the potential for errors to propagate over time".

Responsible collection is treated as part of data engineering rather than as a separate ethics module. The chapter distinguishes data security from data privacy, names four fairness failure modes — representational harm, opportunity denial, disproportionate product failure and harm by disadvantage — and applies them to human labelling, where the controls are rater pool diversity, rater context and incentives, rater tooling, cost and data freshness. Labelling strategy is then made a function of how fast ground truth moves: months or years permits crowd-based curated datasets; weeks brings declining performance into the retraining trigger; days, hours or minutes forces direct feedback or weak supervision.

The chapter's most transferable contribution is its taxonomy of dataset difference. **Drift** compares successive iterations of one source over time and splits into data drift, a change in the statistical properties of the input features, and concept drift, a change in the statistical properties of the labels which "can invalidate the mapping found during training". **Skew** compares two different sources, characteristically training against serving, and splits into schema skew, feature skew and distribution skew. The chapter then walks TensorFlow Data Validation as the instrument: generate summary statistics, infer a schema, curate that schema by hand because "the expectation is that users will tweak or curate the generated schema", then detect anomalies against it. Great Expectations and Evidently are named as alternatives.

Chapter 3 treats feature engineering as the discipline of extracting the most predictive signal at the least compute cost, opening with an Andrew Ng epigraph: "Coming up with features is difficult, time-consuming, and requires expert knowledge." The chapter's central production concern is **training–serving skew**, which it defines as the difference between preprocessing done at training and preprocessing done at serving, and describes as "a very common source of problems in production systems" whose errors "are often difficult to find" because the model still returns plausible answers. The mechanism the authors propose is structural rather than procedural: separate transformations into instance-level and full-pass, compute full-pass statistics such as mean, minimum, maximum and standard deviation once during training, capture them as constants in an exported transform graph, and prepend that graph to the served model so that "the same logic (implementation) that is used to transform the training and evaluation data is applied to transform the new data points during prediction serving". The chapter's self-deprecating anecdote about forgetting to normalise and then debugging the model for hours is a deliberate teaching move.

The second half of Chapter 3 covers feature selection as cost control. Feature space coverage is defined against a training feature space and a serving feature space, with the argument that the former should cover and slightly exceed the latter, and that density matters as much as count because "if there are many duplicates or near duplicates in your data, your model is unlikely to be improved by more data". Filter, wrapper and embedded methods are described with their computational cost. The section closes by noting that for large language models the equivalent problem is **example** selection rather than feature selection, since such datasets have enormous size but very few features.

Chapter 4 makes provenance the organising idea. A data journey runs from raw features and labels through every transformation to the model results; **lineage** is the sequence of artefacts created along that path; and tracking it supports debugging, comparison across training runs, reproduction, and compliance with data-protection regimes that require an organisation to know where personal data came from and where it now is. ML Metadata is presented as the mechanism, recording artefacts, executions and contexts along with the relationships between them — events, attributions and associations — so that questions such as "finding all models trained from a given dataset" or "examining how a given artifact was created" become queries rather than archaeology. The chapter then covers schema development and schema environments, including the worked case where a label feature is required in training and absent in serving, and finishes with a comparison of feature stores, data warehouses and data lakes. Feature stores get the most attention as the interface between feature engineering and model development, including the **time travel** hazard of training on data that would not have been available at the moment the prediction is made.

Chapter 5 addresses labelling cost directly, quoting Jeff Dean to the effect that hand-labelling "is effective, but very labor and time intensive". Semi-supervised labelling propagates labels from a small human-labelled set into a large unlabelled one on the assumption that label classes cluster in the feature space — an assumption the authors describe as "typically—but not always—a good assumption". Active learning selects which unlabelled examples are worth paying to label; margin sampling picks the points nearest the decision boundary, and the authors report that it reaches a given accuracy with fewer labelled examples than random sampling before random sampling eventually converges to it. Weak supervision, exemplified by Snorkel, replaces deterministic labels with noisy probabilistic ones produced by expert-written heuristics, and then learns how much to trust each heuristic. Data augmentation is covered with the necessary warning that "if you add invalid examples, you run the risk of learning the wrong answer". The chapter closes with time series preprocessing, where windowing and sampling are shown to be the levers, and where the offset between history and prediction target must be designed so that training does not use data from after the moment of prediction.

Chapter 6 is about the money. Dimensionality is attacked first, with the corrective observation that although neural networks do learn to suppress uninformative features by driving weights toward zero, "the result is not an efficient model" — the unused capacity still consumes storage and compute at every inference, and every retained feature still requires collection, storage, monitoring and repair "for the lifetime of the product or service". The curse of dimensionality is then explained through distance concentration and the Hughes effect, followed by principal component analysis and the trade it makes: lower dimensionality against the loss of interpretable axes. Quantisation, pruning and knowledge distillation follow. The authors are careful that quantisation "will always reduce model accuracy", that the loss is "specific to the model and data you're optimizing and are challenging to foresee", and that post-training quantisation and quantisation-aware training sit at different points on the accuracy-versus-effort curve. Pruning is given its history from "Optimal Brain Damage" through the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis, including the honest note that the hypothesis "does not perform well for larger-scale problems and architectures". Knowledge distillation is explained through softmax temperature, the teacher's "dark knowledge", and the combination of distillation loss against soft targets with student loss against hard labels.

Chapter 7 covers distributed training, data and model parallelism, synchronous versus asynchronous updates and fault tolerance, then efficient input pipelines as an extract-transform-load problem in which the goal is to keep expensive accelerators busy through software pipelining, prefetching, parallel interleaving, parallel mapping and caching. It closes on pipeline parallelism and micro-batching for models too large to fit on one accelerator.

Chapter 8 is the model analysis chapter and is one of the two strongest in the book. Its core claim is that top-level metrics conceal subgroup failure: "a model with good performance 'on average' may still exhibit failure modes that are not apparent by looking at an aggregate metric". Slicing is therefore mandatory, and which slices matter is a domain-knowledge question, not a statistical one — the authors note that slicing on every feature produces more slices than anyone can analyse, and that slicing on metadata not used by the model can be more informative than slicing on model features. Model debugging is then distinguished explicitly from fixing exceptions: it is "an emerging discipline focused on finding and fixing problems in models and improving model robustness", drawing on model risk management, diagnostics and software testing. Three instruments are given — benchmark models as a sanity baseline, sensitivity analysis, and residual analysis, with the note that residual analysis needs ground truth and so is often unavailable online. Robustness is defined as consistency of results under drastic feature change, and must be measured on data the model never saw. Attacks are then taken seriously, with the Future of Privacy Forum's split into informational harms — membership inference, model inversion, model extraction — and behavioural harms — poisoning and evasion. Fairness closes the chapter, with the Fairness Indicators library and, critically, the statement that "achieving equality across groups with Fairness Indicators doesn't guarantee that your model is fair", plus the instruction to consult social scientists, sociolinguists, cultural anthropologists and affected communities, because "you will probably not get answers unless you ask questions".

Chapter 9 covers interpretability, opening with the admission that "interpretability itself does not have a mathematical definition" and that "the distinction between interpretability and explainability is not well-defined". The taxonomy given is intrinsic versus post hoc, model-specific versus model-agnostic, and local versus global. Intrinsically interpretable architectures are discussed, including the argument that monotonic features aid interpretability because they match domain intuition, and TensorFlow Lattice is presented as an architecture that admits domain constraints while approaching neural-network accuracy — with the frank concession that "dimensionality is their kryptonite" because lattice parameters grow exponentially in the number of input features. Partial dependence plots, permutation feature importance, LIME, Shapley values, the SHAP library, Testing with Concept Activation Vectors and cloud feature-attribution services follow. Throughout, the recurring caveat is correlated features, which break the independence assumption on which several of these methods depend.

Chapters 10 through 14 are the automation and serving block. Neural architecture search is presented as hyperparameter tuning generalised to architecture, decomposed into search space, search strategy and performance estimation strategy, with cloud AutoML services surveyed and a recommendation to use them as a fast baseline rather than as an end state. Chapter 11 makes the serving trade-offs explicit — latency, throughput and cost — with the general rule that "shorter latency equals higher cost", and warns against two bad reasons for model complexity: the impulse to use new architectures "because, well, they're pretty cool", and "a sort of lazy impulse to include more and more features on the assumption that more is better". The recommended discipline is to fix the serving infrastructure, name an optimising metric and a gating metric, and add complexity only while the gating metric holds. Chapter 12 separates batch from real-time inference by their optimisation targets — throughput and cost for batch, latency for real-time — and states that in online serving "it is always better to use simpler models ... if and only if an acceptable level of prediction accuracy can be achieved", because "latency that is too long can result in a model that is simply not usable". Ensembles, model routers for cost control in generative AI, real-time preprocessing and postprocessing, edge and browser inference, and federated learning follow. Chapter 13 covers model servers, horizontal and vertical scaling, containerisation and orchestration, and reliability through redundancy, where the recommendations are conventional site-reliability practice applied to models: define service-level objectives and error budgets, instrument for observability and evolve that instrumentation "based on what you learn from outages", eliminate single points of failure across failure domains, design services to degrade rather than fail under overload, measure user-facing quantities such as query success ratio rather than only CPU, and "ensure that every change can be rolled back" and test the rollback periodically. Chapter 14 is a hands-on walkthrough of TF Serving, the TensorFlow Profiler and TorchServe.

Chapter 15 is the other strongest chapter. It begins with experiment tracking, arguing that ML debugging "is often fundamentally different from debugging in software engineering, because it's often about a model not converging or not generalizing instead of some functional error such as a segmentation fault or stack overflow", and that the things which must be versioned are code, hyperparameters, execution environment, metrics **and data**, because "your data reflects a snapshot of the world at the time when the data was gathered, and, of course, the world changes". Notebooks are treated sympathetically and then bounded: notebook code "is usually not directly promoted to production and is often not well structured". The chapter then contrasts data scientists and software engineers as professional types — the former optimising model metrics, prototyping in notebooks, with "model size, cost, latency, and fairness ... often not a central focus of their work"; the latter focused on cost, stability, scalability, maintainability and schedule but tending "to view their work product as basically static" — and positions the ML engineer as the hybrid, "often not as deep in either specialization" but valuable precisely for spanning them. MLOps is then defined against DevOps by two additions: continuous integration must validate data, schemas and models rather than only code, and continuous delivery ships a pipeline that produces a prediction service rather than shipping a binary. The maturity model runs level 0, manual and script-driven with a handoff between research and operations that "opens the door for potential training–serving skew"; level 1, automated continuous training with pipeline triggers, automated data and model validation, a feature store and a metadata store; and level 2, full CI/CD for the pipeline itself — which the authors mark honestly as "still somewhat speculative". Model versioning is then discussed with the admission that "there is no uniform standard that is widely accepted across the industry", four schemes are compared, and model registries and model lineage are introduced. Delivery closes the chapter: unit testing extended to engineered features and to model behaviour on slices, infrastructure validation as an early warning before deployment, then blue/green and canary deployment, and live experimentation through A/B testing, multi-armed bandits and contextual bandits. The justification given for live experimentation is the sharpest sentence in the book's operational argument — model metrics "are usually not exact matches for the business objectives", the recommender example being that click-through is labelled but profit is wanted.

Chapter 16 covers monitoring. It splits functional or ML monitoring, which watches predictive performance and the distribution of serving features, from non-functional or system monitoring, which watches queries per second, failures, latency and resource utilisation — and notes that the second matters regardless of the first, since "if the system is down, it really doesn't matter how good your model is". ML monitoring is argued to be harder than software monitoring because of two additional components, the data and the model, and two additional sensitivities: **entanglement**, where "changing one thing changes everything", and **configuration**, where hyperparameters, versions and feature lists live in config and "the slightest error here can cause radically different model behavior that won't be picked up with traditional software tests". Observability is imported from control theory with its governing asymmetry — "You can only control a system to the extent that you can observe it" — and is operationalised as alertability and actionability. Logging, log-processing tooling, distributed tracing in the Dapper style, and the point that log data is also the next training dataset all follow. Model decay is then decomposed into data drift and concept drift, with covariate shift and prior probability shift as the formal counterparts, and detection is split into supervised techniques requiring labels — statistical process control, sequential analysis by linear four rates, error distribution monitoring by adaptive windowing — and unsupervised techniques not requiring them — clustering for emerging concepts, per-feature distribution monitoring, and margin density monitoring near the decision boundary. Mitigation covers deciding which old data is still valid, choosing between fine-tuning from a checkpoint and full retraining, and setting a retraining policy. The authors' treatment of scheduled retraining is a model of honest practitioner writing: it is "what many people do because it's simple to understand and in many domains it works fairly well", but it "can ... incur higher training and data gathering costs than necessary, or alternatively it can allow for greater model decay than might be ideal", and "it also assumes that change in the world happens at a fairly steady rate, which is often not the case". The chapter ends with the position that "no model lives forever".

Chapter 17, the contributed privacy chapter, is the most self-limiting in the book and the better for it. Its opening position is that "at the time of this writing, there is always a cost to privacy: increasing privacy for our users comes with a cost in model accuracy, computation time, or both", with the two endpoints being no collection and no utility, or full knowledge and no privacy. Personal identifiable information and sensitive data are distinguished, with the warning that PII appears in free text and support transcripts and not only in fields explicitly asking for it. The harms taxonomy from Chapter 8 is reused. The controlling recommendation is data minimisation — do not collect what you do not need, prefer an identifier to a name, obtain consent before collection and record the response — followed by the observation that web-scraped generative AI datasets make this harder and that the legal position "is not settled". The GDPR and CCPA are summarised, the right to be forgotten is worked through including the consequence that a deletion may force a retrain and that good lineage is what makes this tractable, and pseudonymisation, de-identification and anonymisation are placed on a spectrum. Differential privacy is then explained through randomised response, the local-versus-global distinction, and the epsilon-delta framework, with the practical instruction to tune for "the most private parameters possible while retaining acceptable data utility for the business problem". DP-SGD, PATE and CaPC learning are covered, then federated learning with its five preconditions and the Gboard example, then homomorphic encryption and secure multi-party computation with their honest performance costs — homomorphic encryption "causes several orders of magnitudes of slowdown". The chapter's closing concession is that "there is always a substantial additional engineering effort involved in adding privacy to an ML pipeline".

Chapters 18 through 21 cover orchestration and two worked pipelines. Orchestration is motivated by ordering dependencies and by caching, so that "only the failing component reruns", and pipelines are modelled as directed acyclic graphs. Four orchestrators are compared — interactive TFX in a notebook, Apache Beam, Kubeflow Pipelines and Vertex Pipelines — with a clear warning that interactive notebooks should not deploy production models because "hidden states of Jupyter Notebooks can interfere with the reproducibility of your ML pipeline", and with five non-TFX alternatives named. Chapter 19 covers advanced TFX including conditional execution, exit handlers, warm-starting and four ways to write custom components, with the pragmatic ordering advice to extend an existing component rather than build from scratch. Chapters 20 and 21 build a cat-versus-dog image classifier and a BERT text classifier end to end, and in both the interesting engineering is in preprocessing: images are ingested unresized so that the resize can live in the transform graph and therefore run identically at serving; BERT tokenisation, control tokens, padding, input masks and type identifiers are all expressed as TensorFlow operations so that they parallelise and so that they travel with the model.

Chapter 22 covers generative AI and opens by conceding its own perishability — "any discussion about GenAI in a book such as this one is somewhat doomed to rapid obsolescence". It covers modality-based model types, pretraining and masked self-supervised objectives, embeddings, fine-tuning versus transfer learning, parameter-efficient fine-tuning with LoRA and S-LoRA, human alignment through RLHF, RLAIF and DPO, prompting, chaining, retrieval augmented generation, ReAct, evaluation by human raters, autoraters and reference-overlap metrics, cross-model benchmarking, LMOps, jailbreaks and prompt injection, and Responsible GenAI. Two observations are worth carrying forward. The production argument against fine-tuning is operational rather than statistical: serving multiple fine-tuned models means either holding several in memory or swapping them, and prompting plus a smaller number of less-specialised models may be the better system design. And the argument for fine-tuning is data governance — that with a hosted model "none of your data will be shared with external parties", which matters for regulated industries and for users who have not consented to third-party sharing.

Chapter 23 closes with three positions. The first is the book's real thesis, stated late: "we should think in terms of machine systems rather than only ML models", with the diagnostic questions being how users interact with the model, whether it is integrated into the interface, whether users can report misclassifications, and whether that feedback is used to retrain. The second is that large language models are moving the ML role "away from model creators and toward ML consultants, or model adapters", which the authors call "a good trend". The third is that privacy pressure is increasing, and that pipeline artefacts should be encrypted at rest and in transit.

## Key concepts and practices
- **A production ML system is mostly not the model.** The authors state that a model usually contains only about five per cent of the code required to put an ML application into production.
- **Production ML differs from research ML on data, priorities and lifetime.** Research ML uses a static dataset, one design priority and a single terminal result; production ML uses shifting real-world data, several competing priorities, and continuous monitoring, assessment and retraining.
- **Training data must cover the feature space of the prediction requests the system will actually receive**, and coverage density matters as much as example count because duplicated or near-duplicated examples add no information.
- **Encoding ambiguity is a data defect with the same status as a missing value.** The authors' example is an elevation reading of zero that may mean sea level or may mean that no measurement arrived.
- **Where one model's output is another model's input, errors propagate over time**, which makes upstream model monitoring a dependency of downstream model correctness.
- **Drift and skew are different comparisons and should not be conflated.** Drift compares successive iterations of one data source over time; skew compares two different sources, characteristically training against serving.
- **Data drift is a change in the distribution of the input features; concept drift is a change in the relationship between inputs and labels.** Concept drift invalidates the mapping the model learned rather than merely moving the inputs.
- **Skew decomposes into schema skew, feature skew and distribution skew**, which are detected by different mechanisms and have different remedies.
- **The rate at which ground truth changes determines the labelling strategy.** Change over months or years permits crowd-based curated datasets; change over days or hours forces direct feedback or weak supervision.
- **Training–serving skew is caused by performing different preprocessing at training and at serving**, and the authors describe it as a common production failure that is hard to detect because the model continues to return plausible answers.
- **The structural remedy for training–serving skew is to export the preprocessing as a graph and attach it to the served model**, so that full-pass statistics computed once at training are applied element-wise at serving by the same code.
- **Transformations divide into instance-level and full-pass**, and only the former can be computed from a single serving request, so full-pass results must be stored as constants and shipped with the model.
- **Aggregate metrics conceal subgroup failure, and which slices matter is a domain-knowledge question rather than a statistical one.** Slicing on metadata the model does not consume can be more informative than slicing on model features.
- **ML systems are sensitive in two ways ordinary software tests do not catch: entanglement, where changing one feature changes the whole model, and configuration, where a small error in hyperparameters, versions or feature lists silently changes behaviour.**
- **Observability in ML is imported from control theory, where a system can only be controlled to the extent that it can be observed**, and is operationalised through alertability, meaning metrics and thresholds that make failure unambiguous, and actionability, meaning enough signal to support root cause analysis.
- **Monitoring splits into functional monitoring of predictive performance and serving-data distributions, and non-functional monitoring of latency, failures and resource use**, and the second matters independently of the first because a system that is down serves no predictions at all.
- **Drift detection splits into supervised techniques that require fresh labels and unsupervised techniques that do not**, the latter being the practical default because labelling is slow and expensive.
- **Retraining policy is a design decision with no correct answer.** The authors report that scheduled retraining is common because it is simple and often adequate, while noting that it assumes a steady rate of change in the world which frequently does not hold.
- **Latency, throughput and cost trade against each other and against accuracy**, and the authors state as a general rule that shorter latency costs more.
- **For online serving, latency rather than accuracy is usually the binding requirement**, so a simpler model that clears the accuracy bar is preferred to a more accurate model that does not clear the latency bar.
- **Model complexity should be added against a fixed serving infrastructure and a declared gating metric**, so that complexity stops when an operational constraint such as latency or model size is breached rather than when improvement stops.
- **Optimisation techniques trade accuracy for resource cost, and the size of the loss is specific to the model and data and hard to predict in advance.** Quantisation, pruning and knowledge distillation each reduce size or latency at some accuracy cost that must be measured rather than assumed.
- **Neural networks do learn to suppress uninformative features, but the suppressed capacity is still stored, still traversed at inference and still requires the feature to be collected and maintained for the life of the product.**
- **MLOps extends DevOps in two specific ways: continuous integration must validate data, schemas and models rather than only code, and continuous delivery ships a pipeline that produces a prediction service rather than shipping a model.**
- **The authors describe MLOps maturity as levels 0, 1 and 2 defined by how much of the workflow is automated**, and mark level 2 as still somewhat speculative at the time of writing.
- **There is no industry-standard scheme for versioning models.** The authors propose a major, minor and pipeline scheme and describe several incompatible alternatives in current use.
- **Lineage is what makes a model reproducible, comparable across runs, and deletable on request.** Recording artefacts, executions and contexts turns provenance questions into queries rather than investigations.
- **Live experimentation exists because model metrics are not business metrics.** A recommender trained to maximise click-through is optimising a labelled proxy for a profit objective that was never in the training data.
- **Equal fairness metrics across groups do not establish that a system is fair**, and fairness evaluation must be repeated through development and after launch rather than treated as a release gate.
- **Interpretability has no mathematical definition and is not cleanly separable from explainability**, so the working definition the authors adopt requires only that a human can understand the model's operations through introspection or a produced explanation.
- **Privacy costs accuracy, computation time, or both**, and the cheapest control is not collecting the data in the first place.
- **A deletion request may force a retrain**, which makes data and model lineage a precondition for satisfying data-protection obligations rather than an optional engineering nicety.
- **Pipeline orchestration is required because components have ordering dependencies and because expensive completed steps must be cached so that a failure reruns only the failing component.**
- **Not every project needs a pipeline.** Pipelines are unnecessary for pure experimentation and become necessary once a model has users and therefore requires continuous update.
- **The book's closing position is to reason about ML systems rather than ML models**, the diagnostic questions being how users interact with the model, whether they can report misclassifications, and whether that feedback reaches retraining.

## Method and evidence base
**This is practitioner guidance from a commercial technology publisher, not research.** It warrants what its authors do and recommend; it does not warrant that the practices it recommends outperform the alternatives it does not discuss. There is no study design, no comparison group, no sampling frame for the experiences reported, and no measurement of whether a team adopting these practices produces better outcomes than a team that does not. The book does not claim otherwise, and its Preface is explicit that it is an introduction to a very broad range of topics rather than a treatment of any of them.

The evidence actually offered is of four kinds, and they are not interchangeable. First, **the authors' own operating experience**, which is the book's real contribution and is credible on its own terms: three of the four authors work at Google on the tooling described, and the account of what breaks and what the guardrails are is an insider description of a working practice. Second, **anonymous practitioner testimony** — the data chapter's foundational claim rests on two unattributed quotations from "an ML practitioner at Uber" and "an ML practitioner at Gojek", which cannot be checked, weighted or followed up. Third, **secondary summaries of published research**, including the Transformer paper, GPipe, LoRA, DPO, Constitutional AI, the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis, PATE, DP-SGD and adversarial-example work; these are reported accurately at the level of mechanism but are summarised rather than assessed, and the reader who needs the strength of a result must go to the paper. Fourth, **vendor and first-party benchmarks**, including NVIDIA's GPU-versus-CPU inference comparison and Google's own TFDV and TPU figures; these are the weakest evidence in the book because the party reporting the improvement also sells the thing being improved.

**Where the book states numbers, treat them as properties of a particular system.** This folder is the `ai-systems` namespace, and its warrant standard is that [mechanism is verified while magnitudes are properties of a particular model after a particular training run and do not transfer](/concepts/namespaces.md). Every quantity in this book falls under that rule: the accuracy, latency and size figures for quantised MobileNet and Inception variants are measurements of those checkpoints on that hardware with that toolchain version; the reported speedups from moving between numeric precisions are architecture- and hardware-dependent; the distillation and DistilBERT figures are properties of those teacher-student pairs on those benchmarks; the parameter and memory reductions attributed to LoRA are properties of the configurations in the cited paper; the cost reduction attributed to model routing is a property of that router on those benchmarks. **None of these numbers should be reused as a planning assumption, quoted as an expected result, or carried into teaching material as an effect size.** What transfers is the direction and the mechanism: quantisation reduces size and usually latency at some accuracy cost; distillation can move much of an ensemble's advantage into a single smaller model; routing between a cheaper and a more capable model can reduce cost where quality tolerances permit. The magnitudes do not transfer.

Two further honesty notes. The book's organisational claims — that pipelines raise job satisfaction and retention, that shared field experience aligns teams, that the ML engineer role is the productive hybrid — are stated without any supporting evidence and are the authors' professional judgement. And the forward-looking claims in Chapter 22, including the remark that most observers place artificial general intelligence less than five years away, are opinion recorded at a moment in 2024 and should be cited as such if they are cited at all.

## Vocabulary the source introduces
- **Data drift:** change over time in the statistical properties of the input features, moving prediction requests away from the training distribution.
- **Concept drift:** change over time in the relationship between features and labels, invalidating the mapping the model learned.
- **Emerging concept:** a pattern in the data distribution that was not present in the training set at all, requiring new labels rather than retraining on old ones.
- **Schema skew, feature skew and distribution skew:** the three ways training and serving datasets can differ — incompatible types, differing values for the same identified example, and shifted value distributions.
- **Training–serving skew:** the difference between preprocessing performed at training and preprocessing performed at serving, and the family of silent errors it causes.
- **Instance-level versus full-pass transformation:** transformations computable from one example versus transformations requiring statistics over the whole dataset.
- **Transform graph:** the exported computation graph carrying both the element-wise transformation logic and the full-pass statistics as constants, attached to the served model.
- **Data journey and data provenance:** the flow of data from raw collection to model results, and the linkage that lets any instance be traced back to the process that created it.
- **Artefact and lineage:** any object produced by a pipeline component, and the ordered sequence of such objects through a run.
- **Feature store:** a shared, documented, access-controlled repository of curated features serving both training and inference.
- **Time travel:** including in training data information that would not have been available at the moment the prediction is made.
- **Data swamp:** a data lake that has become unusable because retrieval and identification were never designed.
- **Entanglement:** the property that changing one feature in an ML system changes the behaviour of the whole model.
- **Alertability and actionability:** designing metrics and thresholds so failure is unambiguous, and providing enough signal to identify a cause and a remedy.
- **Model decay:** degradation of a deployed model's usefulness as the world moves away from its training data.
- **Optimising metric and gating metric:** the metric being maximised, such as accuracy, and the operational constraint that must hold, such as latency or model size.
- **Servable, loader, source, aspired version and manager:** TF Serving's abstractions for the unit clients compute against, its lifecycle, its discovery, the set of versions that should be loaded, and the component that loads and unloads them.
- **MLOps levels 0, 1 and 2:** manual handoff, automated continuous training, and full continuous integration and delivery of the pipeline itself.
- **Blue/green and canary deployment:** switching all traffic between two complete environments, versus shifting traffic incrementally while monitoring.
- **Multi-armed and contextual bandits:** live experimentation that reallocates traffic toward better-performing variants during the test, with the contextual form conditioning that choice on request context, and **regret** as the measure of reward lost while exploring.
- **Softmax temperature and dark knowledge:** the parameter that softens a teacher's output distribution during distillation, and the information in the relative probabilities of the non-predicted classes.
- **Differential privacy, local and global:** noise added per individual before collection, versus noise added to a transformation over the whole dataset, with **epsilon** as the strength of the privacy guarantee and **delta** as the probability it does not hold.
- **Postprocessing immunity:** the property that no downstream processing of a differentially private result can weaken its privacy guarantee.
- **Informational and behavioural harms:** attacks that extract information from a model — membership inference, model inversion, model extraction — versus attacks that change its behaviour — poisoning and evasion.

## What to remember
- Budget for the system, not the model. The model is a small fraction of the code and a smaller fraction of the lifetime cost.
- Decide early which slices of the data matter, because aggregate metrics will not tell you when a subgroup is being failed.
- Keep drift and skew separate in your vocabulary; they are different comparisons, detected differently and fixed differently.
- Make the preprocessing travel with the model. Anything else is a standing invitation to training–serving skew.
- Compute full-pass statistics once, store them, and apply them element-wise at serving; you will not have the dataset when the request arrives.
- Version the data alongside the code, the hyperparameters, the environment and the metrics, because the data is a snapshot of a world that moves.
- Record lineage before you need it. Reproduction, comparison and deletion requests all depend on it and none of them can be reconstructed later.
- Treat the retraining trigger as a design decision and state which assumption it rests on, because a schedule assumes a steady rate of change.
- Choose an optimising metric and a gating metric before adding complexity, and stop when the gate closes rather than when improvement stops.
- Prefer the simplest model that clears the accuracy bar for online serving, because latency failures make a model unusable and accuracy shortfalls only make it worse.
- Measure the accuracy cost of every optimisation on your own model and data; the published figures are properties of other systems.
- Do not treat equal fairness metrics as proof of fairness, and do not treat fairness evaluation as a one-time gate.
- Collect less. Every field you do not collect is one you do not have to protect, monitor, explain or delete.
- Expect that a deletion request may force a retrain, and design the metadata that makes that tractable before the request arrives.
- Ship pipelines, not models, once a model has users; and do not build a pipeline for an experiment that has none.
- Remember that the model's training metric is a proxy for a business outcome, which is why live experimentation exists.

## Why it matters for the course
The repository's technical foundations folder is otherwise composed entirely of component papers: attention, paged attention, inference scaling, BM25, HNSW, DiskANN, product quantisation, instruction tuning, preference optimisation, retrieval benchmarking and planning limits. Each explains one mechanism inside or beside a model. **None of them covers the system that surrounds the model**, which is where a forward-deployed engagement actually fails. The nearest existing analogue is [the RAG failure taxonomy](/research/02-technical-foundations/barnett-rag-failure-taxonomy.md), which is an experience report about one compound architecture; this book is a lifecycle inventory across the whole production surface, and it supplies the vocabulary a student needs to name a defect before diagnosing it.

The following are **course-design inferences**, not claims the authors make about forward-deployed or hypervelocity engineering:

- The chapter 1 contrast between research and production ML is the cleanest available statement of why a course on shipping differs from a course on modelling, and can carry an opening seminar without modification.
- The drift and skew taxonomy is a diagnostic instrument. A student who can say "this is distribution skew between training and serving, not concept drift" has converted a complaint into a locatable defect, which is the same move the RAG failure taxonomy teaches for retrieval.
- Training–serving skew is the single best teaching example in the book of a defect that produces no error, no exception and no obviously wrong output. It is worth an entire lab because it teaches that silent failure is the characteristic failure mode of data systems.
- The optimising-metric and gating-metric pair is a transferable design discipline that generalises well beyond ML: declare the constraint before you start adding capability, and stop at the constraint rather than at the point of diminishing returns.
- The MLOps maturity levels are useful as an assessment scaffold precisely because the authors admit level 2 is speculative. A student asked to place a real system on the scale and defend the placement is doing engineering judgement, not recall.
- The observation that model metrics are not business metrics is the justification for every evaluation and live-experimentation practice the course teaches, and it is stated here more plainly than in the measurement literature the repository already holds.
- The chapter 15 contrast between data scientists and software engineers is a legible account of a real professional boundary and of the hybrid role that spans it. It should be taught as a description of how teams are currently constituted, not as a claim that the hybrid is optimal, because the book offers no evidence for the latter.
- The privacy chapter's opening trade — that privacy costs accuracy, computation or both — belongs in the course's governance strand as a designed constraint rather than a compliance afterthought, and its data-minimisation argument is the cheapest control the course can teach.
- The book's own perishability is a teachable object. Chapter 22 concedes it will date; Chapter 15 concedes there is no versioning standard; Chapter 10 concedes that generative approaches to architecture search were not yet robust. A source that dates itself in public is a good model for how the course should handle its own platform layer.

## How to teach it
- **Skew hunt lab.** Give students a pipeline with a seeded training–serving skew — a normalisation constant computed at training and recomputed at serving from the request batch. The model returns plausible outputs and throws no errors. Students must locate the defect from logs and slice metrics alone, then fix it by moving the transformation into the exported graph. Assess the written diagnosis, not the fix.
- **Slice design exercise.** Give a domain brief and a trained model. Students propose the slices that matter, justify each from the domain rather than from the feature list, and must include at least one slice on metadata the model does not consume. Grade whether the justification is domain reasoning or feature enumeration.
- **Drift-versus-skew classification drill.** Present a set of short incident descriptions and require each to be classified as data drift, concept drift, schema skew, feature skew, distribution skew or none of these, with the detection instrument named. This is fast, repeatable and directly assessable.
- **Gating metric negotiation.** Students receive a serving budget and an accuracy target that cannot both be met. They must declare an optimising metric and a gating metric, choose between model simplification, quantisation, distillation and infrastructure spend, and defend the choice orally. Reject any answer that assumes a published speedup figure without measuring.
- **Retraining policy defence.** Each project team writes a retraining policy stating the trigger, the assumption about the rate of world change that the trigger rests on, the cost of retraining too often and the cost of retraining too late. Assess the stated assumption, not the policy.
- **Lineage-to-deletion exercise.** Give a deletion request under a right-to-be-forgotten regime and a system with partial metadata. Students must identify every artefact and derived model affected, state what they cannot determine from the available lineage, and specify the metadata that should have been recorded. This links the data chapter to the privacy chapter and makes provenance concrete.
- **Fairness limits seminar.** Students compute fairness metrics on a supplied model, find them approximately equal across groups, and must then argue whether the system is fair. The intended outcome is the book's own position that equal metrics do not settle the question.
- **Evidence-class audit.** Students take one chapter and classify every claim in it as author operating experience, anonymous practitioner testimony, secondary summary of published research, or vendor benchmark. This teaches the repository's own evidence discipline using a source that mixes all four.

## Limits and cautions
- **This is a survey, and it says so.** No topic is treated to the depth of the primary literature. Use it for the map and the vocabulary; go to the papers this repository already holds for the mechanisms.
- **The toolchain is not neutral.** Three of four authors work at Google, the book was inspired by a Google-authored course and a prior TFX book, the acknowledgements thank the TFX, TFDV, TFT, TFMA, MLMD, TF Serving and Vertex teams, and both worked pipelines are TFX. Alternatives appear but are not evaluated against the default. Pair this note with the repository's [Microsoft platform notes](/research/06-microsoft-platform/apim-ai-gateway-docs.md) rather than treating either vendor account as the general case.
- **No comparative evidence is offered for any recommended practice**, so nothing here supports a claim that these practices outperform others. It supports a claim about what one experienced group does and why.
- **Every number is a property of a particular system.** See the evidence section. Do not carry any accuracy, latency, size, speedup or cost figure from this book into teaching material as an expected result.
- **The vendor benchmarks are the weakest evidence in the book** and should not be cited at all where a claim about hardware or platform advantage is being made.
- **The generative AI chapter is the most perishable content**, dates itself explicitly, and contains forward-looking opinion — including a remark about the timeline to artificial general intelligence — that is not a finding and should not be repeated as one.
- **The legal content is not legal advice and is jurisdiction- and date-bound.** The GDPR and CCPA summaries, the release and consent examples and the position on scraped training data reflect a United States practitioner view as of 2024, and the book itself notes that the legal position on scraped generative AI data "is not settled".
- **Several organisational claims are unevidenced professional judgement**, notably that pipelines improve job satisfaction and retention, and that the ML engineer hybrid is the valuable role. Teach them as descriptions of current practice, not as findings.
- **The platform-specific chapters will decay fastest.** The TFX API surface, the Kubeflow installation procedure, the Vertex console flows, the container image tags and the pinned library versions in Chapters 14 and 18 through 21 are perishable on a months-to-quarters horizon. The durable content is the conceptual half: lineage, skew, drift, orchestration as a directed acyclic graph, and the reasons the components exist.
- **Do not confuse this with Kaestner and Kang's *Machine Learning in Production*** (MIT Press, open access), which the repository lists separately and has not read in full. The two books cover overlapping ground from different positions — one a university course text with published design rationale, the other an industry practitioner survey — and citing one for the other would misattribute both the claim and its warrant.
