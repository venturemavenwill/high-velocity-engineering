## What this source is
A product family, and a bet about how organisations will end up governing AI agents. The bet is stated without hedging in the Foundry integration page: Agent 365 "treats agents as first-class Microsoft Entra identities through Entra Agent ID", and it is "Microsoft's enterprise control plane for AI agents." Everything else follows from that sentence. If an agent is an identity, then the entire apparatus an organisation already owns for governing identities — directory objects, conditional access, access packages, access reviews, audit logs, data-loss prevention, threat detection — can be pointed at agents without inventing a parallel stack. The claim is not that agents are like users. It is that the *governance machinery* built for users is the right machinery for agents, once the identity model is corrected.

There are really three sources here wearing one name, and keeping them apart matters because they carry different weight and decay at different rates.

**Microsoft Entra Agent ID** is the identity layer. It is a genuine addition to the directory — a new object type with new semantics, new audit vocabulary and a new administrative model. It is the most durable and most interesting part of this material, because the design reasoning behind it survives the product.

**Microsoft Agent 365** is the control plane and the commercial product: a registry in the Microsoft 365 admin center, an observability pipeline, an SDK and CLI, and a set of admin surfaces. It is licensed, per user, and it is where the perishability concentrates.

**Purview and Defender integration** is the reuse of existing security products against the new object type. It is the part with the largest gap between what is claimed and what is evidenced, and the part whose coverage boundaries are the most important thing to read.

One framing note before anything else. **These pages establish what Agent 365 is. They are not evidence that using it reduces risk.** Not one detection rate, false-positive rate, coverage measurement, incident-reduction figure or availability target appears anywhere in the material read for this note. The documentation is unusually candid about its own coverage boundaries — more candid than most first-party material — and completely silent on whether the covered part works. Both facts should be taught.

## The argument in full

### The identity argument, which is the good one
The Entra material makes an argument that would be worth teaching even if the product vanished. Its premise is that neither of the two existing identity models fits.

Human user accounts do not fit because agents do not authenticate the way humans do, and because pointing human machinery at them fails in specific, named ways: "Conditional Access policies built for humans don't apply correctly to agents, ID Protection detections are degraded, and identity governance processes can incorrectly remove agent access." That last one is the sharpest — an identity-governance process designed to deprovision leavers will happily deprovision a working agent.

Application service principals do not fit for a different reason, and the reason is **lifespan**. Service principals "were designed for services built and maintained by organizations" and "carry the expectation of long-term stability, known ownership, and managed lifecycle." Agents do not. The documentation states the operational reality plainly: an agent "might exist for minutes during a specific task, or might be created and destroyed thousands of times per day as part of an automated workflow." An identity model that assumes permanence, applied to a population that is ephemeral, produces orphaned credentials and stranded permission assignments at a rate nobody can clean up by hand. **The identity model is designed for scale and ephemerality rather than permanence.** That is the argument, and it is correct independently of who implements it.

The mechanism is a three-object model that repays careful reading, because its security properties are not obvious.

An **agent identity** is "a special service principal in Microsoft Entra ID", modelled as a single-tenant service principal with a new "agent" subtype. The crucial property: **it does not hold credentials of its own.** An **agent identity blueprint** is the template and, more importantly, the authentication foundation: "The blueprint holds credentials and uses them to acquire tokens on behalf of all agent identities created from it." An **agent identity blueprint principal** is the object recording that a blueprint is present in a given tenant, so that the blueprint's own actions — creating agent identities, managing lifecycle — are themselves attributable.

The consequence is stated in one sentence that should be on a slide: "This separation means the blueprint holds the authentication credentials while the agent identity holds the permissions and the audit identity. A compromise of the blueprint's credentials affects all agent identities under it, **which is why blueprint count is a security boundary decision.**" That is a real architectural decision with a real trade-off — fewer blueprints means simpler governance and a wider blast radius; more blueprints means the reverse — and the documentation names it rather than hiding it. Managed identity is supported, but the documentation is careful about what it is: "a credential *on the blueprint*, not a replacement for the agent identity itself."

Two operating patterns are distinguished, and they map onto two token flows. **Interactive agents** act on behalf of a signed-in user through delegated permissions and the on-behalf-of flow; the token's subject is the user and the *actor* is the agent identity. **Autonomous agents** act on their own behalf using the client-credentials flow; the token's subject is the agent identity. Separately and optionally, an **agent's user account** can be paired one-to-one with an agent identity when the agent needs to touch something that requires a user object — a mailbox, a Teams channel, a directory entry with a manager. The documentation is explicit that this is an addition, not a substitute: "An agent's user account doesn't replace the agent identity — both must exist."

### Sponsorship, which is the strongest single idea here
The administrative model separates three roles, and the separation is the most transferable governance idea in the entire source. **Owners** are technical administrators: they manage credentials, edit configuration, add other owners, and can re-enable, restore soft-deleted and hard-delete identities. **Sponsors** hold business accountability: they "understand the business purpose of the agent, and they can determine whether an agent is still needed or requires access", and their powers are deliberately limited to non-destructive lifecycle operations — enable, disable, soft-delete, modify sponsors. They cannot change application settings. **Managers** sit in the organisational hierarchy for an agent's user account and can request access packages for agents reporting to them.

The load-bearing rule is this: **owners are optional, and sponsors are not.** "A sponsor is required when creating an agent identity or agent blueprint." Every agent identity, at the moment of creation, has a named human — or a permitted group — who is accountable for whether it should continue to exist. That is a constraint enforced by the directory rather than by policy documentation, and it is the difference between a governance intention and a governance mechanism.

It is reinforced twice more. **Sponsorship survives departure:** "If a sponsor leaves the organization, sponsorship automatically transfers to their manager, ensuring continuous human oversight." And sponsorship is the anchor for everything downstream — Lifecycle Workflows route sponsor-leaving notifications, access-package expiry escalations go to the sponsor, and entitlement-management approvers rely on the sponsor relationship to validate continued access. The Zero Trust assessment material puts it exactly right: **"Sponsorship is the entry point for identity governance."**

Some constraints are worth carrying: an agent identity or blueprint may have up to **100 sponsors, no more than 5 of which are groups**; an agent's user account up to **5**. Permitted sponsor group types are dynamic-membership groups and assigned-membership Microsoft 365 groups; **role-assignable groups and assigned-membership security groups are not permitted.** On delegated creation with a user context, the calling user becomes sponsor by default unless other sponsors are named — but users holding Agent ID admin roles are *not* auto-assigned, deliberately, "to avoid unintentionally overburdening admins with direct responsibility for individual agents." Somebody thought about the failure mode where all accountability collects on the administrator, and designed against it.

### Audit, and the trap in it
Agent activity is logged, but not under a new log type. "Agent activity is logged under the base identity type from where the activity originates" — blueprint activity appears as *application events*, agent identity activity as *service principal events*, and agent user account activity as *user events*. To know that an event involved an agent at all, you must inspect the **`agentType`** property on `initiatedBy`, `performedBy` or `targetResources`. Its values are `notAgentic`, `agenticApp` (a blueprint), `agenticAppInstance` (an agent identity), `agentIdentityBlueprintPrincipal` and `agentIDuser`. **A value other than `notAgentic` indicates agent involvement.**

This is a trap for anyone with an existing detection or reporting estate. Every query written before agent identities existed will silently fold agent activity into service-principal and user activity. The events are there; the attribution is a property nobody is currently filtering on. **An audit pipeline that does not know about `agentType` is not wrong, it is blind in a way that produces confident, complete-looking output.**

A second trap sits alongside it. Agents predating the platform — including "earlier versions of Microsoft Copilot Studio and Azure AI Foundry" — were secured with standard application service principals. Those objects "appear alongside agent identity objects in the Microsoft Entra admin center" and "behave identically to other application service principals and don't have the agent-specific behaviors described in this article." So the directory contains two populations that look adjacent and are governed differently, distinguishable only by filtering. **Any statement of the form "every agent in this tenant has enforced sponsorship" is false for the older population and the documentation says so.**

### The registry, and what it can and cannot see
The registry is the observe pillar and the thing most practitioners will touch first. It lists agents in four publisher categories — **Microsoft agents, external partner-built agents, published by your org (line-of-business), and shared by creator** — and surfaces three tenant-wide counts: **Total agents, Agents without owners, Unmanaged agents**, the last defined as "agents created or managed outside of Agent 365, without its risk protection and observability." Filters cover status, publisher type, **channel** (Copilot, Teams, Outlook, Microsoft 365 apps, SharePoint), **platform**, and **data source** (embedded knowledge, fine-tuned models). Actions include refresh, **export to CSV**, add agent by uploading a manifest ZIP, manage pinned agents, customise view and search. Ten agent types are enumerated — three Copilot Studio variants, three Foundry variants, Agent Builder, SharePoint, Agents Toolkit, and *agent instance*, the last meaning an agent extended with the Agent 365 SDK.

Ownerless agents get purpose-built handling: a dashboard count, a one-click filter, real-time update "when you hard delete a user from the organization", and per-agent block or delete. Shared agents "become ownerless when you delete the user who created them from the organization" — which is to say the common cause of an ungoverned agent is ordinary staff turnover, not malice.

**Now the boundaries, which the documentation states and which matter more than the features.**

Draft agents are only partly visible: "Currently, you can only view draft agents from Copilot Studio. Support for draft agents from other platforms, such Agent Builder, Foundry, and SharePoint, aren't currently available." The overview card shows only the **top 5** platforms; the full list requires the registry tab. Foundry analytics "only support Microsoft Foundry V2 agents". The **Data & tools** tab — which is where you learn what an agent can read and which tools it can invoke — "is read-only and shows metadata reported by the agent platform", and "Currently, Microsoft 365 admin center doesn't have data and tools information for all agents." And the overview page concedes a consistency limit outright: metrics come from several systems, so "Minor variances may occur due to differences in ingestion timing, update frequency, and system-specific processing."

Every 30-day metric — active users, agent run-time, active users over time, trending agents — **begins at licence activation and is not backfilled.** For the first month, the numbers are structurally incomplete, and each of those metrics carries its own note saying so.

**Registry sync**, which extends the registry to non-Microsoft platforms, is **in preview** and covers exactly four: **Amazon Bedrock, Google Vertex AI, Salesforce Agentforce, Databricks Genie.** Two properties deserve attention. First, authentication is by long-lived secret — an AWS access key ID and secret access key, a Google Cloud service-account key — with a permission list that includes `bedrock:DeleteAgent`. **Centralising agent governance therefore requires depositing standing, deletion-capable credentials for two other clouds into a Microsoft admin surface.** That is a defensible trade and it is a trade, and no page read for this note discusses it. Second, synchronisation is **manual**: an admin presses **Sync agents**, and "You can configure future synchronizations to occur on a scheduled basis, **in a future release**." A registry refreshed by hand is a registry whose accuracy is a function of somebody remembering.

There is also a naming collision to note. Microsoft Entra shipped its own agent registry in preview, and the two are being merged: "Microsoft is converging these registry experiences under Agent 365 to provide a simpler and more consistent management experience", after which "**Agent 365** becomes the unified registry and control plane" while "**Microsoft Entra** continues to provide the identity foundation through Agent ID." Anything written against the Entra registry is on a migration path.

### The risk taxonomy, which is the most teachable artefact in the source
The registry's **Agents at risk** surface publishes a table that is, in effect, a control map: ten named risk types, each with a severity, a named signal source, a stated trigger and a remediation resource. Reproduced here because its structure is the lesson.

| Risk type | Severity | Signal source | Trigger |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shadow agent | Critical | Entra, M365 admin center | No registry entry, no owner, or no Entra Agent ID |
| No owner assigned | Critical | Entra, M365 admin center | No owner or sponsor on record |
| Excessive permissions | Critical | Entra, Defender | Access rights exceed declared function (least-privilege violation) |
| Security misconfiguration | High | Defender | Exploitable attack path detected via Security Exposure Management |
| Prompt injection | High | Defender, Entra SSE | AI Prompt Shield flags or blocks injection attempt at runtime |
| Sensitive data access | High | Purview | Labelled data accessed without a matching DLP policy exception |
| Conditional access violation | High | Entra | Access attempted outside defined conditions |
| Pending approval | Medium | M365 admin center | Agent may be running before IT governance review completes |
| Operational exceptions | Medium | M365 admin center | Errors or failures in agent conversation or tool execution |
| Compliance/retention gap | Medium | Purview | Agent interactions missing required audit trail or retention policy |

Three of the four *Critical* entries are governance failures rather than attacks: no registry entry, no owner, permissions exceeding declared function. That ordering is an argument, and the argument is that **the most severe agent risk is administrative neglect, not adversary action.**

Two qualifications attach, both stated: "The **Risks** column only shows high severity risks... Zero risks is an indication that there are no high risks presently for the agent. However, there could be other types of risks, such as low or medium, in the respective security platforms." And: "**There might be a delay of up to an hour** for the risks count in the Microsoft 365 admin center when compared to what is shown in the security portals." A dashboard reading zero means *no high-severity risk was known here up to an hour ago*.

Then there is the role matrix for the **Review** link, which is the single most consequential operational fact in this note and appears in a table nobody would think to read:

| Role | Investigate in Defender | Investigate in Purview | Investigate in Entra |
|---|---|---|---|
| AI Administrator | No | No | Partial |
| AI Reader | No | No | No |
| Security Reader / Security Administrator / Global Reader | Yes | No | Yes |
| Reports Reader / User Experience Success Manager | No | No | No |

**The AI Administrator — the role that can approve agents, assign owners and grant tenant-wide tool consent — cannot open the Defender or Purview evidence behind the risks it is being asked to act on.** Nobody in the table can investigate Purview risks; that requires separate Insider Risk Management Analyst or Investigator roles inside Purview. A single-operator runbook for agent risk triage cannot be written against these roles. Any real process needs at least two people, and the documentation, to its credit, gives you enough to work that out.

### Purview: what is automatic, and what silently is not
Eleven Purview capabilities are listed against Agent 365. Ten are supported. **The one that is not is "Encryption without sensitivity labels".**

The distinction that matters is between what happens by default and what requires configuration. On creating an agent instance, it "is automatically enabled for audit, detection of sensitive data with data classification, and included with the Assessments for AI regulations from Compliance Manager." Everything else — DLP, Insider Risk Management, communication compliance, retention, eDiscovery — requires you to "include the agent instance in policies **as you would a user**." Three words carrying a large amount of work: every policy in the estate must be revisited, and an agent omitted from a policy is not covered by it, silently.

Audit scope is genuinely broad and worth quoting: "Supported interactions: **All agent-to-human, human-to-agent, agent-to-tools, and agent-to-agent interactions.**" Agent-to-agent is captured. That is more than most observability stories offer.

**DLP scope is not broad, and the contrast is the point.** "Supported interactions: Block or audit **agent-to-human and human-to-agent** for Microsoft Teams, OneDrive or SharePoint, and emails." Agent-to-tool and agent-to-agent appear in the audit list and **not** in the DLP list. You can see an agent hand data to another agent. You cannot, through this control, stop it.

And then the sentence that ought to be read aloud in any session on agentic controls: **"Because an agent instance is unaware of the block action, the agent owner must actively monitor a DLP policy that uses this configuration and understand the impact to subsequent workflows."** The agent is not told it was blocked. It proceeds. Whatever it does next is built on a step that did not happen, and only the owner watching the policy will know. Compare this with the Purview DSPM material's caution about removing sharing links — the safest control is the most disruptive — and note that here the disruption is *invisible to the thing being disrupted*.

Sensitivity labels bring three constraints that will surprise people. Files "must be explicitly **shared** with" the agent instance. Where a label applies encryption, it "must explicitly grant the agent instance VIEW and EXTRACT usage rights", and — stated outright — a label configured for "Add all users and groups in your organization" or "Add any authenticated users" **is not sufficient**. And, most consequentially: **"Newly created content from Agent 365 doesn't inherit sensitivity labels from the source items. As a result, that content isn't automatically labeled or encrypted."**

That last one is a laundering path stated in first-party documentation. An agent reads labelled, encrypted material and emits a summary that is neither. The protection was attached to the container, the agent's output is a new container, and the label did not follow. No attack is required. It is the default behaviour.

The observability surface is the **AI observability** page under the current DSPM, showing agents active in the last 30 days ranked by Insider Risk Management risk level, with three named risky-activity categories — **oversharing, exfiltration and unethical behaviour** — and per-agent detail covering Entra-enabled status, created date, owner, agent user ID and parent agent. One version note is emphatic and easy to get wrong: **"Data Security Posture Management (classic)... doesn't support Agent 365."** The classic page is precisely the one this repository's existing Purview note was built on.

### Defender: named threats, real coverage limits, and a trade nobody advertises
Defender contributes three things: posture management, real-time protection, and detection and hunting.

**Detection is in public preview** — the page carries the banner. The threat classes named are *jailbreak attempts*, *XPIA attempts* (cross-prompt injection), *malicious content propagation*, *secrets and credentials leak*, *evasion techniques*, *suspicious user access*, "and more." Real-time protection rules are scoped by four selectable detection types: **secret exfiltration, malicious content propagation, evasion techniques, unsafe email domain.** These are agent-native failure modes, not repackaged endpoint categories, and the vocabulary is worth adopting.

**Real-time protection** inspects the agentic loop and blocks before execution. Coverage is stated with unusual precision, and the boundary is hard: for Agent 365 agents it works by evaluating tool invocations through **Work IQ MCP**, "including invocations of customer MCP tools onboarded to Agent 365", and then — "**Agents that rely on unsupported tools or don't integrate with Work IQ MCP aren't covered.**" Copilot Studio agents are covered by a different path that does not depend on Work IQ MCP. Local agents are covered through Defender for Endpoint, which "must run in active mode", and are "onboarded separately from cloud agents."

There are two rule kinds. A built-in **Default** rule audits all agents without stopping anything. **Custom** rules block. And here is the trade, stated once and easy to miss: "Near-real-time detections continue to surface as alerts only in audit mode. **When a blocking rule covers an agent, near-real-time alerts aren't generated for that agent.**" Turning on blocking turns off alerting for that agent; the events become behaviours in the `BehaviorInfo` table, queryable but not surfaced. **A team that enables blocking and keeps watching its alert queue will observe the queue go quiet and may reasonably read that as success.** No page read for this note explains the reasoning behind the trade.

Investigation runs through Advanced Hunting over six tables — `AlertInfo`, `CloudAppEvents` (Agent 365 observability data), `AgentsInfo` (inventory, identity, platform, ownership), `AlertEvidence`, `BehaviorInfo`, `BehaviorEntities`. The older `AIAgentsInfo` table "will be deprecated" in favour of `AgentsInfo`, "powered by Microsoft Agent 365 as the single source of truth for AI agent inventory."

**Prompt evidence collection is enabled by default.** Alerts include snippets of user prompts and agent responses. "Sensitive data and secrets are redacted; however, customer conversations might still be sensitive in nature." A default-on setting that puts conversational content in front of security analysts is a privacy decision, and it has been made for you.

A dated migration is already booked. From **1 July 2026**, Copilot Studio threat-detection alerts in Defender for Cloud Apps are deprecated and equivalent alerts run over Agent 365 observability logs; Foundry agent-specific alerts in the Defender for AI Services plan are likewise deprecated. This is the same transition the existing Purview and Defender note in this repository records from the other side, where agent-level discovery and posture move behind an Agent 365 licence.

### Shadow AI, local agents, and the honest edge of the estate
The hardest problem — agents nobody registered — is addressed by preview features under the **Frontier** programme, and their coverage is a published list rather than a capability.

The **Shadow AI** page detects seven named desktop applications: **OpenClaw, ChatGPT Desktop, Ollama Desktop, Poe Desktop, Claw/ZeroClaw, OpenCode, Claude Desktop.** Only **OpenClaw** can be blocked. The **Local Agents** page detects nine developer tools including **Claude Code, Gemini CLI, GitHub Copilot CLI**, and several VS Code extensions — **detection only, no blocking.** Blocking "currently apply only to managed Windows devices enrolled with Microsoft Intune", works by generating an Intune policy named `A365 - Block OpenClaw`, and "Depending on how Intune is configured in your organization, this Intune policy update could take anywhere from **15 minutes up to 8 hours** to apply."

Global Secure Access adds a network-layer path that classifies agents as managed or shadow "based on whether it's registered with Microsoft Entra Agent ID", and several Shadow AI fields "will only be populated with data if Global Secure Access (GSA) has been enabled."

Read together, the honest summary is this. **Shadow-agent discovery is a named-application list plus optional network inspection on managed Windows endpoints.** An agent that is not on the list, or runs somewhere Intune and GSA do not reach, is not discovered — and no page read for this note states what proportion of a real estate that leaves uncovered, because no such measurement is published.

### How it relates to the rest of the platform
**Copilot Studio** is the tightest integration: agents built there "automatically integrate with Microsoft Agent 365. They appear in the agent registry, send telemetry to Agent 365, and automatically receive Entra Agent IDs." No developer action.

**Foundry** connects two ways. Published Foundry agents "automatically appear in the Agent 365 registry when subscribed." And Foundry Hosted agents can be published as **autopilots** — "an agent that acts autonomously on behalf of a user and receives its own Microsoft Entra Agent ID" — which after admin approval can be wired to Teams and other Microsoft 365 surfaces. Support is uneven and tabulated: a **Prompt agent** supports registry sync, autopilot publishing and activity data collection; a **Hosted agent** supports the first two, with activity data requiring the Agent 365 SDK plus explicit Entra permissions for the observability service.

Enablement has two gates that are easy to trip over: the tenant needs a licence, and a global administrator must accept the terms of service and select users or groups. The note attached is emphatic — "**even if the Azure Resource Manager properties on a Foundry resource are set to enabled for A365**", no data flows until both are done. A configuration flag set to true that does nothing is a good way to lose an afternoon.

**Data residency differs between the two platforms and the difference is architectural, not administrative.** Foundry follows "the **Azure region** you select when creating the Foundry resource." Agent 365 follows "the **storage location of the Microsoft Entra tenant**." When activity data flows from Foundry into Agent 365 "it moves from the Azure region-based residency model to the Entra tenant residency model", and processing "may happen across geographical regions." Per-resource opt-out exists through `agent365Config`. For a regulated customer this is a governance question that must be answered before the first agent ships, not after.

**Everything else** arrives through the **Agent 365 SDK**, whose self-description is admirably clear about what it is not: it "does not create or host agents. Instead, it enhances agents you've already built - regardless of the underlying stack." The layering is published as three tiers — enterprise capabilities from the Agent 365 SDK; agent logic in your own code; LLM orchestrator runtime from whatever framework you chose. Named compatible stacks include Microsoft Agent Framework, Microsoft 365 Agents SDK, **OpenAI Agents SDK, Claude Code SDK and LangChain**, hosted "on any endpoint, be it Azure, Amazon Web Services (AWS), Google Cloud Platform (GCP), or any other cloud provider." What the SDK adds is Entra-backed identity, notification participation in Teams, Outlook and Word, **OpenTelemetry-based observability**, and governed MCP tool access.

Which yields the structural fact underneath the whole product. **Governance for a non-Microsoft agent is opt-in and requires a code change.** Foundry and Copilot Studio agents are governed because they were built inside; four external platforms can be inventoried through preview registry sync; seven desktop applications can be spotted by name. Everything else is governed if, and only if, somebody adopted the SDK. The control plane is genuinely broad in ambition and its automatic reach ends at the Microsoft boundary.

### Licensing, availability and preview status, stated plainly
- **Generally available since 1 May 2026**, "for the Commercial segment on a per user basis."
- **Licensed per human, not per agent.** The announcement states it directly: "Each Agent 365 license covers an individual who manages or sponsors agents, or uses agents to do work on their behalf." Standalone at **USD 15 per user per month**, or included in **Microsoft 365 E7**.
- **"Microsoft Agent 365 works best when using Microsoft E5 as a pre-requisite. At least one user must be licensed with a qualifying Microsoft Agent 365 license to enable Agent 365."** The words "works best" are doing unspecified work; no page read for this note enumerates what degrades without E5.
- Standalone routes exist for parts of the Entra surface: **Conditional Access for agents** needs Entra ID P1; **ID Protection for agents** needs P2; **ID Governance for agents** needs P1; **network controls** need Entra Internet Access.
- **Generally available:** agents acting on behalf of users with delegated access; agents operating with their own access behind the scenes; Entra network controls extended to Copilot Studio agents and endpoint-resident agents.
- **Public preview:** agents participating in team workflows with their own access; **registry sync** with Amazon Bedrock and Google Cloud; **Defender AI agent detection and investigation**; **agent identity governance** in Entra ID Governance; the **Entra agent registry**; **Windows 365 for Agents** (United States only).
- **Frontier preview** — a distinct, more restricted programme: the **Shadow AI** page; the **Local Agents** page; the **AI teammate** agent type. Foundry-to-Agent 365 activity data has been documented as requiring Frontier enrolment.
- **Announced with a future date, therefore unavailable when written:** Defender asset-context mapping per agent — devices, MCP servers, associated identities and reachable cloud resources — "**Starting in June 2026**"; policy-based controls, runtime blocking and alerts for local agents "in Agent 365 through Intune and Defender public preview **in June 2026**"; scheduled registry synchronisation, "**in a future release**"; lifecycle governance actions over synced external agents — "and, **soon**, perform basic lifecycle governance—for example, start, stop, delete agents." **The June 2026 items are past their stated date and were not re-verified for this note; treat their status as unknown rather than as shipped.**
- **Dated migration: 1 July 2026** — Copilot Studio alerts in Defender for Cloud Apps and Foundry agent-specific alerts in Defender for AI Services deprecated in favour of alerts over Agent 365 observability logs. The companion fact, from the Defender for Cloud side and recorded in this repository's existing Purview and Defender note, is that agent discovery and posture for Foundry and third-party cloud agents require an Agent 365 licence from the same date.

## Key concepts and practices
- **Agent 365 is a control plane over three pillars — observe, govern, secure — and five capabilities: registry, access control, visualisation, interoperability, security.**
- **GA 1 May 2026**, commercial segment, **per user**, **USD 15 per user per month** standalone or included in **Microsoft 365 E7**. E5 named as the preferred prerequisite in the words "works best".
- **A licence covers a human who manages, sponsors or is served by agents.** Cost scales with people, not with agent count.
- **An agent identity is a single-tenant service principal with an "agent" subtype, and holds no credentials of its own.**
- **The blueprint holds the credentials and impersonates the agent identity**; the agent identity holds the permissions and the audit identity.
- **"A compromise of the blueprint's credentials affects all agent identities under it, which is why blueprint count is a security boundary decision."**
- **A sponsor is required at creation for every agent identity and blueprint. Owners and managers are optional.**
- **Sponsorship transfers automatically to the sponsor's manager on departure.**
- **Sponsors act; they do not configure.** Enable, disable, soft-delete, change sponsors. No application settings. Owners re-enable, restore and hard-delete.
- Limits: **100 sponsors** per agent identity or blueprint, **max 5 groups**; **5** per agent user account. Role-assignable and assigned-membership security groups are **not** permitted as sponsors.
- **Interactive agents** use delegated permissions and the on-behalf-of flow; **autonomous agents** use client credentials. An **agent's user account** is optional, paired 1:1, and additive rather than substitutive.
- **Service principals and regular user accounts are both explicitly not recommended**, with named failure modes for the latter: Conditional Access misapplied, ID Protection degraded, identity governance removing agent access.
- **Agent activity logs under the base identity type.** Detection requires the **`agentType`** property; anything other than `notAgentic` means an agent was involved.
- **Pre-platform agents still run on ordinary application service principals** and lack every agent-specific behaviour, including enforced sponsorship.
- **Registry counts: total agents, agents without owners, unmanaged agents.** Ownerless is usually caused by staff departure, not misconduct.
- **Ten named risk types**, of which three of the four Critical entries are governance failures rather than attacks.
- **The Risks column shows only high-severity risks, and may lag the security portals by up to an hour.**
- **The AI Administrator cannot investigate Defender or Purview risks. Nobody in the published role table can investigate Purview risks** without separate Insider Risk Management roles.
- **Purview audit covers agent-to-agent and agent-to-tool. Purview DLP does not.**
- **"Because an agent instance is unaware of the block action, the agent owner must actively monitor a DLP policy..."** — the agent is not told it was blocked.
- **"Newly created content from Agent 365 doesn't inherit sensitivity labels from the source items."**
- **Encryption without sensitivity labels is the single unsupported Purview capability.**
- **DSPM for AI (classic) does not support Agent 365.** Use the current DSPM's **AI observability** page.
- **Only audit, data classification and Compliance Manager AI assessments are automatic on agent creation.** Everything else must name the agent in a policy "as you would a user".
- **Defender detection is in public preview.** Named threats: jailbreak, XPIA, malicious content propagation, secrets and credentials leak, evasion techniques, suspicious user access.
- **Real-time protection rule detection types:** secret exfiltration, malicious content propagation, evasion techniques, unsafe email domain.
- **"Agents that rely on unsupported tools or don't integrate with Work IQ MCP aren't covered."**
- **Enabling blocking suppresses near-real-time alerts for that agent**; events become behaviours in `BehaviorInfo`.
- **Six Advanced Hunting tables**; `AIAgentsInfo` deprecating in favour of `AgentsInfo`.
- **Prompt evidence collection is on by default**, redacting secrets but not conversational sensitivity.
- **Shadow AI detection is a list of seven named applications; only OpenClaw can be blocked**, only on Intune-managed Windows, with policy application taking **15 minutes to 8 hours**.
- **Registry sync is preview, four platforms, manual trigger, long-lived third-party credentials including delete permissions.**
- **Copilot Studio agents are governed automatically. Foundry agents sync automatically and may publish as autopilots. Everything else requires the Agent 365 SDK — a code change.**
- **Data residency differs: Foundry follows the Azure region, Agent 365 follows the Entra tenant.** Data crosses models on ingestion.
- **All 30-day metrics start at licence activation and are not backfilled.**
- **Draft agents are visible only for Copilot Studio.**

## Method and evidence base
First-party product documentation across five documentation sets, plus one first-party announcement blog. **The authority is definitional throughout and evidential nowhere.**

The documentation is better than most at stating its own boundaries. It says which agents real-time protection does not cover; it says the Risks column shows only high severity; it says the admin centre may lag the security portals by an hour; it says the Intune policy may take eight hours; it says label inheritance does not happen; it says the agent does not know it was blocked; it says registry sync is manual. This is genuinely useful and the coverage boundaries are the most reliable content in the source.

What it does not contain is any measurement at all. Not one detection rate. Not one false-positive rate. No statement of what fraction of agents in a representative tenant the registry actually discovers. No measured latency from agent creation to registry appearance. No SLA, availability target or uptime figure. No before-and-after incident data. No evaluation of whether enforced sponsorship is maintained in practice or quietly satisfied by pointing every agent at one long-suffering group. The strongest empirical claim anywhere in the material read is a customer testimonial in the announcement blog.

The announcement blog is marketing and should be read as such. It supplies chronology, pricing and the GA-versus-preview split, and those are the only things it should be cited for. Its framing sentence — "you can't govern what you can't see, and you can't secure what you don't understand" — is a good argument and not a result.

**The correct posture is to trust these pages completely on what the objects are and what the controls cover, and to treat every question of efficacy as open and unanswered by this source.** The efficacy questions are also, unlike the Learn MCP Server's, **not cheaply testable locally** — they need a licensed tenant, an agent estate and time. That asymmetry should shape how the material is taught.

## Vocabulary the sources introduce
- **Agent 365** — Microsoft's enterprise control plane for AI agents, across observe, govern and secure.
- **Entra Agent ID** — the identity platform providing agent identities and blueprints; available to all Entra customers.
- **Agent identity** — a single-tenant service principal with an "agent" subtype, holding permissions and audit identity but no credentials.
- **Agent identity blueprint** — the template and authentication foundation; holds credentials, acquires tokens on behalf of its agent identities, and carries the policies they inherit.
- **Agent identity blueprint principal** — the object recording a blueprint's presence in a tenant, making the blueprint's own actions attributable.
- **Agent's user account** — an optional Entra user paired 1:1 with an agent identity, giving it a UPN, mailbox, manager and directory presence.
- **Sponsor** — the human or group holding business accountability for an agent's purpose and lifecycle. Required at creation. Transfers to the sponsor's manager on departure.
- **Owner** — the technical administrator. Optional. Holds the destructive powers a sponsor does not.
- **Manager** — the hierarchical relationship on an agent's user account.
- **`agentType`** — the audit-log property distinguishing `notAgentic` from `agenticApp`, `agenticAppInstance`, `agentIdentityBlueprintPrincipal` and `agentIDuser`.
- **Agent registry** — the tenant-wide inventory in the Microsoft 365 admin center, and the intended single source of truth after convergence with the Entra registry.
- **Registry sync** — preview connectors inventorying agents from Amazon Bedrock, Google Vertex AI, Salesforce Agentforce and Databricks Genie.
- **Shadow agent** — an agent with no registry entry, no owner, or no Entra Agent ID. Rated Critical.
- **Unmanaged agent** — created or managed outside Agent 365, "without its risk protection and observability".
- **Agent instance** — an agent extended with the Agent 365 SDK, and therefore holding Entra-backed identity, observability and governed tooling.
- **Autopilot** — a Foundry Hosted agent published to Agent 365 that acts autonomously on behalf of a user with its own Agent ID.
- **AI teammate** — a Frontier-preview agent type with its own mailbox, Teams presence and manager relationship.
- **Work IQ MCP** — the governed MCP tool layer through which Defender evaluates Agent 365 tool invocations; the boundary of real-time protection coverage.
- **Agent 365 SDK** — the library adding identity, observability, notifications and governed tooling to an agent built on any stack. It does not create or host agents.
- **Behaviour** — a real-time protection audit or block event recorded as queryable telemetry in `BehaviorInfo`, as distinct from an alert.
- **XPIA** — cross-prompt injection attack, named as a detected threat class.
- **Frontier** — the early-access programme gating Shadow AI, Local Agents and the AI teammate type.

## What to remember
- The claim is that agents are identities, and that the governance machinery built for identities is the right machinery for agents. Everything else in the product follows from accepting it.
- Service principals assume permanence; agents are ephemeral. That mismatch, not novelty, is why a new object type exists.
- **The blueprint holds the credentials; the agent identity holds the permissions and the blame.** Blueprint count is a blast-radius decision.
- **Sponsorship is required at creation and survives the sponsor leaving.** A governance rule enforced by the directory beats the same rule written in a policy document.
- Owners configure and can destroy. Sponsors decide whether the thing should exist. Separating those two is the transferable idea.
- **Agent activity hides inside existing log types.** Every pre-existing query is blind to `agentType` and will look complete while missing agents entirely.
- Three of the four Critical risk types are administrative neglect. The most likely cause of an ungoverned agent is that its creator left the company.
- **A zero on the risk dashboard means no high-severity risk, known up to an hour ago.** Read the caveats before reading the number.
- **The role that governs agents cannot see the evidence behind their risks.** Agent triage is a two-person process whether or not anyone planned it that way.
- **Audit covers agent-to-agent. DLP does not.** Knowing is not the same as being able to stop.
- **The agent is not told it was blocked.** It continues from a step that did not happen, and only a human watching the policy will notice.
- **Agent output does not inherit the labels of its input.** That is a laundering path present by default, documented by the vendor, requiring no attacker.
- **Enabling blocking suppresses the alerts.** A quiet queue after enforcement is a design consequence, not evidence of safety.
- Real-time protection covers agents that go through the sanctioned tool layer. **Agents that do not are not covered, and the documentation says so.**
- **Shadow-agent discovery is a list of named applications on managed Windows endpoints.** Anything not on the list is not found.
- Governing a non-Microsoft agent requires adopting an SDK. **Automatic reach ends at the platform boundary; the rest is opt-in and needs a code change.**
- Licensing is per human, not per agent. Adding a thousand agents does not change the bill; adding people does.
- **Definitional authority is not evidential authority.** These pages describe the controls precisely and say nothing about whether they work.

## Why it matters for the course
Three reasons, in ascending order of importance.

The narrow one: **Agent 365 currently appears nowhere in the 90 sessions**, and the sessions that teach agent identity, estate hygiene, control mapping and handover ownership are resting on older surfaces. That is a factual gap with a factual fix, and this note is the evidence a fix would rest on.

The larger one: this material supplies something the programme's assure, operate and transfer stages otherwise lack — **a worked example of governance implemented as mechanism rather than as documentation.** Enforced sponsorship at creation, automatic transfer on departure, blueprint-inherited policy, and a risk taxonomy that names the signal source for each entry: these are governance ideas with teeth, and they can be assessed as design reasoning without any student touching the product. The sponsor-versus-owner separation in particular is a genuinely good answer to a question every engagement faces — *who decides whether this should still exist, and is that the same person who can change it?* — and the answer is *no, and the directory enforces it*.

The most important one: **this source is an unusually clean specimen of a control surface that documents its own coverage boundaries and publishes nothing about its efficacy.** It tells you exactly what real-time protection does not inspect, exactly which interaction types DLP omits, exactly how long a policy takes to land, and exactly that the agent is not informed when it is blocked — and it tells you nothing at all about how often any of it catches anything. A student who can read this source correctly can read most vendor security documentation correctly, because the shape recurs. The skill is separating *what is covered* — reliably stated, and the useful part — from *how well it works* — never stated, and the part everyone assumes.

## How to teach it
- **Lecture:** teach the identity argument with no product attached. Why does an ephemeral, bulk-created, autonomously-acting principal break both the human-user model and the application model? Have students derive the requirements before showing them the answer.
- **Lecture:** separate the sponsor from the owner and ask which one an engagement usually names, and which one it usually needs. Most projects name a technical owner and no accountable decider.
- **Lecture:** teach coverage boundaries as a first-class artefact. Read the four sentences — Work IQ MCP coverage, DLP interaction scope, the block the agent cannot perceive, the labels that do not inherit — and ask what each one lets an adversary or an accident do.
- **Lab:** hand students the ten-row risk table with the severity column blanked and ask them to rank. Then reveal that three of the four Criticals are governance failures, and discuss why the vendor ranked them above prompt injection.
- **Lab:** give students the role matrix and the sentence "investigate the risk" and ask them to write a runbook. Let them discover it cannot be completed by one person.
- **Lab:** trace the label-laundering path on paper — labelled source, explicit share, VIEW and EXTRACT, agent output, no label — and ask what control would close it. There is no control in this source that does.
- **Lab:** design a blueprint topology for a fictional estate of thirty agents, and defend the number of blueprints on blast-radius grounds alone.
- **Lab, if a tenant is available:** export the registry to CSV and produce the ownerless list. Whatever the number, the exercise is the artefact.
- **Assessment:** require any claim that an agent estate is governed to state the discovery mechanism, its coverage boundary, its stated latency, and the population it cannot see. A claim without those four is not a claim.
- **Assessment:** ask what evidence would be needed to say these controls reduce incidents, and note that none of it is published. The answer is the assessable part.
- **Assessment:** present the enabling-blocking-suppresses-alerts trade and ask for a decision with a monitoring plan that survives it.
- **Framing:** the most perishable material in the research set. GA is under three months old; large parts are preview or Frontier-preview; a migration is dated 1 July 2026; three announced capabilities carry a June 2026 date and were not re-verified. **Assess the reasoning. Never assess the portal path, the preview status or the price.**

## Limits and cautions
- **Extremely perishable, and newer than anything else in this research set.** General availability was 1 May 2026 and this note was written on 2026-07-31. Re-verify before every offering. Nothing here should be taught as stable.
