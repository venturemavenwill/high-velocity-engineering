# Cliff Notes — Narrative Assessment Literature as an Accreditation Exemplar

## What this source is
Two empirical studies of assessor cognition — what evaluators actually do when they judge complex performance evidence, rather than what the instruments are designed to measure. The 2013 study uses think-aloud protocols with supervisor-raters judging trainee performance; the 2018 study uses the same method with assessors reading competency portfolios. In this folder they serve a specific accreditation function: they are the empirical answer to the reviewer who asks why a programme is relying on narrative judgement instead of scores, and they are also the empirical warning about what that reliance costs if it is not designed carefully.

## The argument in full
The 2013 study establishes the problem that any judgement-based assessment scheme must confront before an accreditation panel. Workplace-based assessment grew in response to competency-based curricula and demands for accountability, but "although there is general agreement that WBA is useful for formative assessment, its usefulness for summative assessment is not undisputed." The reason is stated quantitatively and it is not a small number: "idiosyncratic rater effects account for substantial variance in performance ratings, ranging from 29% to over 50%." Somewhere between a third and a half of the variance in a performance rating is attributable to who is doing the rating rather than to what is being rated. Any programme claiming that its rubric scores measure student attainment is, on this evidence, claiming more than it can support.

The instinctive institutional response is to standardise — better rubrics, rater training, anchored scales. The paper reports that such attempts "have met with mixed success at best." Studying 18 experienced and 16 inexperienced supervisor-raters through think-aloud procedures and verbal protocol analysis, the authors find that raters use different schemas to judge the same performance; that "levels of rater idiosyncrasy were substantial and unrelated to rater expertise"; that experienced raters make more use of task-specific performance schemas, indicating more differentiated mental models rather than more uniform ones; and, most consequentially, that "most raters started to develop person schemas the moment they began to observe trainee performance." Raters do not observe neutrally and then judge. They begin constructing an impression immediately. The conclusion is that raters "have implicit performance theories, which may diverge from those specified by the organization" — which is exactly why standardising the instrument does not standardise the judgement.

The 2018 study carries this into the portfolio setting and produces the finding this folder most needs. Eighteen assessors appraised one of three mock portfolios while thinking aloud, followed by semi-structured interviews. The result: "assessors mainly relied on narrative feedback to inform their judgment, because this provided meaningful and detailed information about the student's development, strengths and weaknesses, as well as specific suggestions for improvement… Grades and qualifications were merely used to confirm impressions based on narratives." This inverts the usual defensive posture. A programme does not adopt narrative evidence because numbers are unavailable; assessors are already using narrative as the primary evidence and treating the numbers as confirmation. A scheme that collects only numbers is not more rigorous — it has simply discarded the data the judgement actually runs on.

The study also documents how the judgement forms, and here the finding is a warning rather than a warrant. Assessors reached judgements through "a 3-phase cyclical cognitive process of acquiring, organizing, and integrating evidence", and having completed a cycle "reviewed the remaining portfolio evidence to look for confirming or disconfirming evidence." But "assessors were inclined to stick to their initial judgments even when confronted with seemingly disconfirming evidence." Final judgements were more elaborate and detailed than preliminary ones, but "not substantially different." The additional evidence produced better articulation of the same conclusion, not revision of it. Any assessment design that assumes more evidence yields a more accurate judgement is assuming something this study does not support.

The most important result for accreditation purposes is that agreement on outcomes concealed disagreement in reasoning: "Although assessors reached similar final (pass–fail) judgments of students' professional competence, they differed in their information-processing approaches and the reasoning behind their judgments." Three sources of divergence are identified. **Assessment beliefs** concern which evidence is trustworthy — some assessors began with workplace narratives as the most authentic record, others with self-evaluations checked for justification; some trusted physicians as content experts, others preferred nurses and fellow students who "had more opportunities to directly observe them". **Performance theories** concern what competence is, and the paper notes these "tended to deviate from the formal assessment criteria" — one group treated growth and effective use of feedback as sufficient even where standard was not met, another measured competence by reflective self-awareness. **Inferences** concern what a specific piece of evidence means about a specific person, and the same insufficient rating was read by one assessor as a knowledge deficit and by another as insecurity. This is the finding a programme must not conceal from a review panel, because it means an inter-rater agreement statistic on final outcomes can be high while the underlying construct being assessed varies from assessor to assessor.

The remedies the authors propose are structural rather than instrumental, and they are the ones a programme should be able to point to. High-stakes decisions "should not be made individually, but should result from group discussions", because deliberation surfaces the divergent reasoning that identical scores conceal. Discussing judgement policies within a group builds shared mental models over time. Assessor training "should focus on raising assessors' awareness of their own beliefs, performance theories, and inferences" rather than on instrument calibration, which the 2013 paper had already shown to have limited effect. And "portfolios should be judged by multiple assessors who should, moreover, thoroughly substantiate their judgments" — substantiation being the mechanism that makes divergent reasoning visible and therefore discussable. A design implication follows: portfolios should support navigation through evidence, and captions matter because they "summarize the context in which the competency feedback was provided."

## Key claims and specifics
- Idiosyncratic rater effects account for **29% to over 50%** of variance in performance ratings.
- Rater idiosyncrasy is **substantial and unrelated to rater expertise**. Experience makes mental models more differentiated, not more uniform.
- Attempts to fix this by standardising instruments and training raters "have met with mixed success at best."
- **Most raters begin forming person schemas the moment observation starts.**
- Assessors rely primarily on **narrative feedback**; grades and qualifications are used only to confirm impressions already formed from narratives.
- Judgement follows a **three-phase cyclical process** of acquiring, organising and integrating evidence.
- Assessors **stick to initial judgements even when confronted with disconfirming evidence**; later judgements are more elaborate but not substantially different.
- **Similar pass/fail outcomes rest on materially different reasoning**, diverging along assessment beliefs, performance theories and inferences.
- Assessors' performance theories "tended to deviate from the formal assessment criteria."
- Recommended remedies: group decision-making for high-stakes judgements; multiple assessors; mandatory substantiation of judgements; training aimed at self-awareness rather than calibration; portfolio design that supports navigation and context captions.

## Method and evidence base
Both studies use think-aloud protocols and verbal protocol analysis with qualitative thematic analysis — a method well suited to reconstructing reasoning but not to estimating population effects. Sample sizes are small by design (18 assessors in the 2018 study; 34 raters in the 2013 study), both are set in Dutch medical education, and the 2018 study uses mock portfolios rather than real high-stakes decisions, which removes genuine consequence from the judgement being observed. The 29-50% variance figure is reported from prior quantitative literature rather than generated by these studies. These constraints should be stated when the work is cited to a review panel; they do not undermine the central findings, which concern the structure of reasoning rather than its distribution.

## Vocabulary the source introduces
- **Idiosyncratic rater effect** — the share of rating variance attributable to the identity of the rater.
- **Implicit performance theory** — an assessor's own, often unstated, model of what good performance is, which may diverge from the published criteria.
- **Person schema** — the impression of the individual that an assessor begins constructing as soon as observation starts.
- **Assessment beliefs** — an assessor's view about which evidence sources are trustworthy.
- **Shared mental models** — the convergence in judgement policy that group deliberation is intended to build.
- **Substantiation** — the requirement that an assessor articulate the reasoning behind a judgement, making divergence visible.

## What to remember
- Numbers do not escape subjectivity. A third to a half of rating variance is the rater, and rubric standardisation does not fix it.
- Assessors already run on narrative. Collecting only scores discards the evidence the judgement uses and keeps the part it merely checks against.
- Agreement on outcomes is not agreement on construct. High inter-rater agreement can coexist with assessors measuring different things.
- Anchoring is real: first impressions form immediately and later evidence tends to elaborate rather than revise them.
- The defensible remedies are structural — multiple assessors, group deliberation, mandatory substantiation — not instrumental.
- Assessor training should target self-awareness about beliefs and inferences, because calibration training has a poor record.
- These are small qualitative studies in medical education. Cite them for mechanism, not for magnitude.

## Why it matters for the course
This literature is what allows a programme to answer the hardest accreditation question about a judgement-based scheme — "how do you know this is reliable?" — without either overclaiming or retreating to numbers that are no more objective. The honest answer it supports is: rater subjectivity is unavoidable and is present in numeric schemes too, at 29-50% of variance; we therefore do not claim to have eliminated it; we have instead designed the structural controls the evidence supports, namely multiple assessors, documented substantiation, and group deliberation on consequential decisions. That answer is stronger than a claimed reliability coefficient because it is defensible under follow-up questioning. It also supplies the direct warrant for ABET's own permission, in its definition of assessment, to use "indirect" and "qualitative" measures — the qualitative measure is not a concession, it is where the information is.

## How to use it in course design
- Make narrative the primary assessment record and treat scores as a summarising convenience, not as the evidence.
- Require assessors to substantiate every consequential judgement in prose, and retain those substantiations as the auditable artefact for the self-study.
- Make high-stakes decisions in a group. Two assessors deliberating with written substantiations is a defensible minimum at course scale.
- Counter anchoring deliberately: have assessors record a preliminary judgement, then review remaining evidence specifically looking for disconfirmation, and record whether the judgement changed.
- Train assessors on their own beliefs and inferences — run a calibration session on a shared example where assessors surface *why* they judged as they did, not merely whether they agreed.
- Design the evidence portfolio for navigation, and caption every piece of feedback with the context in which it was given.
- In the self-study, state the reliability position explicitly and cite the variance figure. Pre-empting the question is more persuasive than answering it under pressure.

## Limits and cautions
- Small qualitative samples in Dutch medical education. Generalisation to computing education is an argument, not a finding.
- The 2018 study used mock portfolios; real consequence may change assessor behaviour in either direction.
- The 29-50% variance figure comes from prior quantitative literature cited by these papers, not from these studies themselves, and should be attributed accordingly.
- Narrative assessment is more informative but substantially more expensive in assessor time, and it requires writing skill from assessors that cannot be assumed.
- Group deliberation surfaces divergence but does not resolve it; it can also produce convergence through social pressure rather than through better reasoning, which these studies do not rule out.
- The most-cited Govaerts works on programmatic assessment validity are paywalled and were not read; conclusions here rest on two open-access studies and should not be presented as a full survey of the literature.
