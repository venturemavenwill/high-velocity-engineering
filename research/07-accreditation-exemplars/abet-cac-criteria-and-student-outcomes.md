# Cliff Notes — ABET CAC Criteria and Student Outcomes

## Citation and access
- **Author:** ABET, Computing Accreditation Commission
- **Title:** Criteria for Accrediting Computing Programs, 2025 – 2026
- **Venue and year:** ABET, Baltimore, Maryland; criteria in force for the 2025-2026 review cycle
- **Source consulted:** `https://www.abet.org/accreditation/accreditation-criteria/criteria-for-accrediting-computing-programs-2025-2026/` (PDF download at `https://www.abet.org/2025-2026_cac_criteria/`)
- **Access status:** publicly published standard, free to read on the ABET site; text is ABET copyright, so this note is a synthesis with short quoted definitions only

## What this source is
This is the operative accreditation standard for computing degree programmes reviewed by ABET's Computing Accreditation Commission. It is a short, prescriptive document — a definitions section, then eight numbered General Criteria at baccalaureate level, parallel sets for associate and master's level, then discipline-specific Program Criteria for computer science, cybersecurity, data science, information systems and information technology. It closes with a section of proposed changes that have been through public comment and are expected to take effect in the 2026-27 review cycle. For a course designer it functions less as reading and more as a constraint list: whatever the curriculum does, it must be mappable onto these criteria.

## The argument in full
The document opens by conceding that institutions may use whatever terminology they prefer, but insists that ABET volunteers and staff need a single shared vocabulary. That framing matters, because the whole logic of the criteria rests on four defined terms that are easy to conflate in ordinary academic speech. *Program educational objectives* are "broad statements that describe what graduates are expected to attain within a few years after graduation" and are "based on the needs of the program's constituencies" — they are about alumni, not students, and about the labour market, not the syllabus. *Student outcomes* "describe what students are expected to know and be able to do by the time of graduation" and "relate to the knowledge, skills, and behaviors that students acquire as they progress through the program." The inclusion of behaviours in that sentence is the hinge on which a great deal of subsequent curricular argument turns.

The document then separates *assessment* from *evaluation*, and it is worth being precise about the distinction because programmes routinely collapse it. Assessment is "one or more processes that identify, collect, and prepare data to evaluate the attainment of student outcomes"; it "uses relevant direct, indirect, quantitative and qualitative measures as appropriate to the outcome being measured", and "appropriate sampling methods may be used as part of an assessment process." Evaluation is "one or more processes for interpreting the data and evidence accumulated through assessment processes"; it "determines the extent to which student outcomes are being attained" and "results in decisions and actions regarding program improvement." Assessment gathers; evaluation interprets and acts. A programme that collects rubric scores but never uses them to change anything has done assessment and skipped evaluation, and Criterion 4 is written specifically to catch that.

Criterion 3 is the heart of the standard. A programme must have documented and publicly stated student outcomes that include five specified items plus anything imposed by the applicable Program Criteria, and it may add its own. Graduates must have an ability to: analyse a complex computing problem and apply principles of computing and other relevant disciplines to identify solutions; design, implement, and evaluate a computing-based solution to meet a given set of computing requirements in the context of the programme's discipline; communicate effectively in a variety of professional contexts; recognise professional responsibilities and make informed judgments in computing practice based on legal and ethical principles; and function effectively as a member or leader of a team engaged in activities appropriate to the programme's discipline. Note what is and is not there. Three of the five are not primarily technical. Communication, professional responsibility and teamwork sit alongside analysis and design as first-class, separately assessed outcomes. A curriculum that can only produce evidence about the first two is not a compliant curriculum, however technically demanding it is.

Criterion 5 sets the curricular envelope and does so in a deliberately permissive way: "The curriculum requirements specify topics, but do not prescribe specific courses." The programme must include mathematics, statistics and science appropriate to the discipline, plus at least 30 semester credit hours of up-to-date coverage of fundamental and advanced computing topics providing both breadth and depth. Three topic areas are named as mandatory within that coverage: techniques, skills and tools necessary for computing practice; principles and practices of security and privacy in computing; and local and global impacts of computing solutions on individuals, organisations and society. Again the mix is instructive — a tooling requirement, a security requirement, and a societal-impact requirement, all three at the same level of obligation.

The Program Criteria for computer science, for which CSAB is the lead society, then layer on discipline specifics. They add a sixth student outcome: "Apply computer science theory and software development fundamentals to produce computing-based solutions. [CS]" They require at least 40 semester credit hours of computer science, with substantial coverage of algorithms and complexity, computer science theory, concepts of programming languages, and software development; substantial coverage of at least one general-purpose programming language; exposure to computer architecture and organisation, information management, networking and communication, operating systems, and parallel and distributed computing; the study of computing-based systems at varying levels of abstraction; and "a major project that requires integration and application of knowledge and skills acquired in earlier course work." They also require at least 15 semester credit hours of mathematics and statistics including discrete mathematics, probability and statistics, with rigour at least equivalent to introductory calculus, plus coursework that develops and applies the scientific method in a non-computing area. Some full-time faculty must hold a PhD in computer science.

The most consequential material for a new course, however, is in section V, the proposed changes approved by the ABET Computing Area Delegation on 25 October 2024 for a 180-day review, with comments accepted until 15 June 2025 and adoption expected after the autumn 2025 Delegation meeting, first applied in the 2026-27 review cycle. Two proposals stand out. First, a new definition is introduced: "Professional dispositions are behaviors desired in the workplace." Second, Criterion 5 gains a fourth mandatory computing topic: "A comprehensive project or experience appropriate to the discipline which: 1) builds on technical knowledge and skills acquired in prior advanced course work, and 2) enables the application of appropriate professional dispositions." That second clause is the important one. It is not enough for the capstone to be technically integrative; it must be structured so that workplace behaviours can actually be exercised, which in practice means it must be structured so that they can be observed and evidenced. Alongside these, the proposed CS Program Criteria add software engineering and artificial intelligence to the substantial-coverage list, add specialised platforms, graphics and interactive techniques, human-computer interaction and sustainable computing to the exposure list, and rename "information management" to "data management". Criterion 2 also shifts from "program constituents" to "all constituencies identified by the program", with a new definition of programme constituencies as groups "that have a common interest in the program and can provide meaningful input regarding the program educational objectives."

## Structure of the original
- Definitions (programme educational objectives, student outcomes, assessment, evaluation, respectful environment, general criteria, programme criteria)
- I. General Criteria for Baccalaureate Level Programs — Criteria 1 Students, 2 Program Educational Objectives, 3 Student Outcomes, 4 Continuous Improvement, 5 Curriculum, 6 Faculty, 7 Facilities, 8 Institutional Support
- II. General Criteria for Associate Level Programs — same eight criteria, lower thresholds (16 semester credit hours; outcomes phrased around "a broadly defined problem" and "a member of a team")
- III. General Criteria for Master's Level Programs — stand-alone (MS1-MS8) and integrated baccalaureate-master's (MI1-MI8) variants
- IV. Program Criteria — Computer Science; Cybersecurity (associate and baccalaureate); Data Science and Data Analytics; Information Systems; Information Technology (associate and baccalaureate)
- V. Proposed Changes to the Criteria — programme constituencies definition, Criterion 2 wording, Criterion 5 comprehensive project and professional dispositions, proposed CS Program Criteria

## Key claims and specifics
- There are **five** general baccalaureate student outcomes, not six. The sixth commonly quoted outcome is added by the Program Criteria and is discipline-tagged: [CS], [CY], [DS], [IS] or [IT].
- Baccalaureate minimum: **30 semester credit hours** of computing topics, plus mathematics, statistics and science appropriate to the discipline.
- Computer science Program Criteria minimum: **40 semester credit hours** of computer science plus **15 semester credit hours** of mathematics and statistics.
- Cybersecurity baccalaureate: **45 semester credit hours** of computing and cybersecurity coursework plus **6** of mathematics; eight named areas (Data, Software, Component, Connection, System, Human, Organizational and Societal Security) and six crosscutting concepts (confidentiality, integrity, availability, risk, adversarial thinking, systems thinking).
- Data science baccalaureate: **45 semester credit hours**; lifecycle topics are data acquisition and representativeness, data management, data preparation and integration, data analysis, model development and deployment, and visualisation and communication.
- Master's level (MS5): minimum **30 semester credit hours** beyond the baccalaureate, and "a project or research activity resulting in a set of deliverables that demonstrates both the mastery of the subject matter and a high level of communication skills."
- Master's outcome wording differs subtly from baccalaureate: graduates "elaborate solutions" rather than "identify solutions", and function "as a member **and** leader of a team" rather than "member **or** leader".
- Criterion 8 requires the institution to "create and foster a respectful environment", defined as one that "supports, values, and treats all members fairly and with dignity."
- Program criteria overlap is not double-counted: "Any overlapping requirements need be satisfied only once."

## Method and evidence base
This is not a research document and carries no evidence base in the empirical sense. Its authority is procedural: criteria are drafted by the Computing Accreditation Commission, approved by the ABET Computing Area Delegation, released for a 180-day public comment period, revised in light of comments, and then adopted with a stated first-application review cycle. The proposed-changes section is published alongside the operative text with live comment links, so the direction of travel is visible before it becomes binding. Programmes are judged against the criteria by trained programme evaluators drawn from member societies; for computer science, cybersecurity, data science, information systems and information technology the lead society is CSAB.

## Vocabulary the source introduces
- **Program educational objectives** — what graduates attain a few years after graduation, driven by constituency needs.
- **Student outcomes** — what students know and can do at graduation, spanning knowledge, skills and behaviours.
- **Assessment** — identifying, collecting and preparing data on outcome attainment.
- **Evaluation** — interpreting that data and deciding what to change.
- **Program constituencies** (proposed) — groups with a common interest in the programme who can give meaningful input on objectives.
- **Professional dispositions** (proposed) — "behaviors desired in the workplace".
- **Comprehensive project or experience** (proposed) — a capstone that builds on prior advanced coursework and enables the application of professional dispositions.
- **General Criteria vs Program Criteria** — universal requirements vs discipline-specific ones implied by the programme title.

## What to remember
- Communication, professional responsibility and teamwork are mandatory outcomes on equal footing with analysis and design. They cannot be treated as soft extras.
- Assessment and evaluation are different obligations. Collecting rubric data is not enough; the programme must show it acted on the interpretation.
- The criteria specify topics, not courses, which leaves genuine freedom in syllabus architecture as long as coverage and credit-hour thresholds are met.
- A major integrative project is already required by the CS Program Criteria and is being elevated into the General Criteria as a "comprehensive project or experience".
- The proposed 2026-27 changes make professional dispositions an explicit, defined object of curricular design for the first time.
- Security and privacy, and societal impact, are non-negotiable topic areas at baccalaureate level.
- The proposed CS criteria add artificial intelligence and software engineering to substantial coverage, which directly legitimises an AI-systems course as core rather than elective.

## Why it matters for the course
This is the external authority that makes an evidence-heavy, project-anchored, orally defended assessment model the safe choice rather than the eccentric one. A course built around observed professional behaviour maps cleanly onto outcomes 3, 4 and 5 and onto the proposed comprehensive-project clause, whereas a course assessed largely by written examination can only produce evidence for outcomes 1, 2 and 6. The proposed professional-dispositions definition is particularly useful, because it gives an accreditation-sanctioned name to precisely the thing the course is trying to assess and which is otherwise hard to defend to a curriculum committee. The addition of artificial intelligence to the proposed CS substantial-coverage list closes the remaining argument about whether this material belongs in the core.

## How to use it in course design
- Build an explicit outcomes map: for each of the five general outcomes plus [CS] outcome 6, name the specific artefact in the course that generates direct evidence, and say whether it is direct or indirect evidence.
- Design the capstone against the proposed Criterion 5 item 4 wording rather than the current one, so the course does not need reworking in the 2026-27 cycle.
- Keep an assessment-and-evaluation loop that is visibly closed: rubric data in, a documented interpretation meeting, a recorded change to the syllabus, and the next cycle's data. Store the artefacts, not just the conclusions.
- Use the assessment definition's licence for "indirect" and "qualitative" measures to justify narrative and oral evidence, and its licence for sampling to keep the workload tractable.
- Track the security-and-privacy and societal-impact topic obligations explicitly; in an AI-systems course these map naturally onto agent security, data governance and deployment-harm material rather than needing bolt-on ethics weeks.

## Limits and cautions
- Section V is *proposed*, not adopted. The professional-dispositions definition and the comprehensive-project clause should be described as expected, not current, until ABET confirms adoption; the stated plan was adoption after the autumn 2025 Delegation meeting with first application in 2026-27.
- ABET sets outcome expectations and coverage thresholds; it does not prescribe pedagogy, sequencing, or a single syllabus shape. Nothing here tells you how to teach.
- Credit-hour thresholds are programme-level, not course-level. A single course cannot satisfy them and should not claim to.
- The criteria are US-centric and expressed in semester credit hours; equivalences need care in other systems.
- "Behaviors" appears in the student-outcomes definition but is not itself defined in the operative text — the definition only arrives with the proposed professional-dispositions language.
