# Cliff Notes — EPA Framework as an Accreditation Exemplar

> Companion note. The full source reading of ten Cate's primer, with the complete supervision scale, the EPA description template and the empirical implementation figures, is at [research/03-measurement-evaluation/ten-cate-epas.md](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/ten-cate-epas.md). This note reads the same source through the accreditation lens: what a professional discipline did when it decided that time served was not evidence of readiness, and what a computing programme can borrow from that precedent when facing ABET.

## What this source is
A primer by the originator of the concept, written thirteen years after he proposed it, describing what an entrustable professional activity is, how it relates to competency frameworks, how entrustment decisions are made and defended, and how to build a workplace curriculum around them. For the purposes of this folder it is not primarily an assessment paper. It is a case study in how an entire regulated profession moved from time-based to evidence-based certification, and in the machinery that had to be invented to make that move survive external scrutiny.

## The argument in full
The historical framing at the start of the primer is the part that matters most for accreditation work. Ten Cate describes how nineteenth-century national regulation of medicine gave way to explicit conceptions of competence, how postgraduate training and educational objectives developed after the Second World War, and how outcome-based education was embraced in the 1990s through frameworks such as CanMEDS and the ACGME competencies. The pressure that produced EPAs came from the Royal Colleges, who "became uneasy with the model in which a fixed time in training would automatically lead to a license to practice, rather than a rigorous evaluation of the competence of the trainee." The 2010 Carnegie Report reinforced the direction by proposing fixed standards with flexible pathways. That is precisely the structural argument ABET makes in computing when it insists that programmes evidence outcome attainment rather than course completion, and medicine's experience shows both that the argument wins and what it costs to implement.

The second lesson is about the failure mode of competency frameworks, and it is a warning worth heeding before writing any outcome map. Ten Cate reports that competency frameworks drew criticism from clinician educators and educational scholars for being abstract. A list of domains — communicator, collaborator, professional, scholar — does not tell a supervisor what to let a trainee do tomorrow morning. This is exactly the complaint a computing faculty makes about ABET student outcome 3, "communicate effectively in a variety of professional contexts", or outcome 5 on functioning as a member or leader of a team. The outcome is real, the obligation is real, and the statement is nonetheless too abstract to assess directly. EPAs were proposed in 2005 as the bridge, and by 2018 "hundreds of publications have appeared."

The bridge works by inverting what gets described. "EPAs constitute the description of work and are independent of persons. They operationally define a profession," whereas "competencies describe persons." The line ten Cate uses to fix the distinction is worth carrying into accreditation conversations verbatim: "Professionals can possess competencies; they can never possess EPAs." The two are joined by an integration requirement — "EPAs require multiple competencies in learners that must be applied in an integrative fashion. Even a simple task as taking a patient's history combines several domains of competence." The primer demonstrates this with a matrix crossing CanMEDS domains against six example activities, marking each cell as necessary or merely useful. That matrix is the mechanism a computing programme needs. An outcome map that claims "outcome 5 is assessed in the group project" is an assertion; a matrix that names each concrete professional activity in the course and marks which outcomes it necessarily recruits is evidence, and it is auditable in a way the assertion is not.

The third lesson concerns what accreditors can reasonably be shown. Ten Cate distinguishes ad-hoc entrustment decisions, which happen constantly and informally whenever a supervisor "feels the skills of the learner at that time match the complexity of the patient and the risks in doing this are acceptable", from summative entrustment decisions, which "have the nature of certification" and are "decisions taken for the future responsibilities of the learner from that moment on", sometimes formalised as a Statement of Awarded Responsibility. Because summative entrustment is granted per activity at whatever moment the evidence supports it, "EPA-based curricula can be truly competency-based and not just time-based." The accreditation-relevant point is the rigour attached to the summative variety: it "should include an agreement between multiple observers that this entrustment is justified, to avoid subjective bias, and multiple observations", drawing on "multiple direct observations, longitudinal observations, case-based discussions, and product evaluations", supplemented by knowledge and skills examination scores. Multiple observers, multiple observations, multiple evidence types, documented. That is a defensible evidence standard, and it is the standard a computing programme should aim at when it claims to have assessed a behavioural outcome.

Ten Cate is also candid about the residual risk, which is the honest thing to put in front of a review committee rather than hide from it: "an entrustment decision implies the acceptance of risk, as it can never be fully predicted what the student will encounter." His mechanism for probing that risk is the entrustment-based discussion — "short focused conversations usually after an EPA, [that] should probe the student's deep understanding of what was done, but should include 'what-if' questions, to grasp whether the learner would know what to do what unexpected or familiar situations would arise." That is the intellectual justification for oral defence, stated by a professional discipline whose regulators accept it, and it is a stronger warrant than any argument a computing programme could construct from first principles.

Finally, the primer supplies sizing guidance that keeps the model from becoming unimplementable. Activities "should be not too small and not too big", with "a suitable number [being] 20 to 40 EPAs for a complete program" — a whole degree programme, not a single course. Targeted supervision levels are calibrated to programme stage: "for most EPAs this may be Level 3 in medical school and Level 4 for postgraduate programs." A course sits well below that granularity and should claim two or three activities, not twenty.

## Key claims and specifics
- EPAs were introduced in **2005**; "a little over 10 years later hundreds of publications have appeared."
- The driver was regulatory dissatisfaction with time-based licensure, reinforced by the 2010 Carnegie Report's fixed-standards-flexible-pathways proposal.
- Competency frameworks were criticised for abstraction; EPAs were designed as the concrete bridge to daily work.
- "Professionals can possess competencies; they can never possess EPAs."
- The EPA–competency matrix crosses framework domains against concrete activities, marking each cell necessary or merely useful.
- Summative entrustment requires agreement between multiple observers and multiple observations, across direct observation, longitudinal observation, case-based discussion and product evaluation.
- Five supervision levels: observe only; execute with direct proactive supervision in the room; execute with the supervisor out of the room but quickly available; work unsupervised; supervise more junior learners.
- Entrustment-based discussion is defined as a short post-activity conversation that must include what-if questions about unencountered situations.
- Programme sizing: **20 to 40 EPAs** for a complete programme; targeted level 3 in medical school, level 4 postgraduate.
- Adoption extends beyond medicine into nursing, physician assistant training, veterinary medicine and midwifery, with interest reported from teacher training.

## Method and evidence base
This is a conceptual and practical primer written by the concept's originator, not an empirical study, and it should be cited as such. Its empirical content — the anaesthesiology reliability finding, the Weller et al. entrustability scale, the five-factor analysis of ad-hoc entrustment, the physician-assistant cohort data — is reported from prior work and cited rather than generated. Its authority for accreditation purposes rests on adoption: EPA-based programmes exist across multiple specialties and multiple countries and are accepted by the bodies that certify practitioners. That adoption record is the evidence a curriculum committee will actually respond to.

## Vocabulary the source introduces
- **Entrustable Professional Activity** — a unit of professional practice with a beginning and an end, entrusted only to sufficiently trained individuals; describes work, not persons.
- **Entrustment decision** — the decision to transfer a responsibility to a learner.
- **Ad-hoc vs summative entrustment** — daily contextual delegation vs certification-like forward-looking permission.
- **Statement of Awarded Responsibility** — the formal record of a summative entrustment decision.
- **Supervision level** — the ordinal scale on which entrustment is expressed, from observation to supervising others.
- **Entrustment-based discussion** — the short, what-if-inclusive post-activity conversation used to probe understanding and expose residual risk.

## What to remember
- Medicine faced the same problem computing accreditation now poses — evidence of readiness rather than time served — and the EPA framework is the machinery it built to solve it.
- Abstract outcome statements are the shared failure mode. Concrete activities are what make them assessable.
- The activity-by-outcome matrix converts an outcome-mapping assertion into an auditable artefact.
- Multiple observers plus multiple observations plus multiple evidence types is a defensible standard for a behavioural judgement, and it is achievable at course scale in reduced form.
- Oral defence has a principled name and a stated purpose here: probing understanding and unencountered situations, not catching cheats.
- Entrustment explicitly accepts residual risk. Saying so is more credible than claiming certainty.
- Sizing matters: 20-40 activities is a whole programme. A course claims two or three.

## Why it matters for the course
This is the precedent that makes an observation-based, orally defended, narratively justified assessment model look like established professional practice rather than local invention. Presented to a curriculum committee, the argument is not "we would like to try oral defence" but "the model used to certify physicians in six specialties across multiple countries, and adopted since by nursing, veterinary medicine and midwifery, is built on exactly this evidence structure." It also supplies the vocabulary — activity, entrustment, supervision level, entrustment-based discussion — that lets the course describe what it is doing in terms a reviewer from outside computing will recognise. Read alongside the proposed ABET professional-dispositions definition and the CS2023 vision statement, it completes a three-part warrant: CS2023 says dispositions belong in computing curricula, ABET is moving to require a project that enables them, and the EPA literature supplies a tested method for evidencing them.

## How to use it in course design
- Name two or three concrete professional activities for the course — not competencies, not learning objectives, but bounded pieces of work with a beginning and an end that a supervisor could delegate.
- Build the activity-by-outcome matrix against ABET's six computer science student outcomes, marking each cell necessary or merely useful, and use it as the outcome-mapping artefact in the self-study.
- Specify, per activity, the target supervision level at course completion, and be conservative — level 3 is an honest target for an undergraduate course.
- Require multiple evidence types per activity, mirroring the primer's four sources: direct observation, longitudinal observation, a discussion, and a product evaluation.
- Structure the oral component explicitly as an entrustment-based discussion with mandatory what-if questions, and document the question shape so it is reviewable.
- Do not build twenty activities. Sizing guidance is programme-level; over-decomposition at course level produces bookkeeping, not evidence.
- State the residual risk in the course documentation rather than claiming the assessment is predictive.

## Limits and cautions
- This is a clinical model. Medicine has patients, supervisors with legal responsibility, and a licensure endpoint; a computing course has none of these, and the translation is not free.
- The primer is guidance from an advocate, not an evaluation. It reports adoption breadth, not effectiveness evidence.
- The full model is expensive. Multiple trained observers making documented, deliberated summative judgements is a staffing profile most computing courses do not have; the course must adopt a defensible reduced version and say so.
- Supervision levels presuppose a supervisor and real work with consequences. A course has to construct both artificially, and the construction is the hard part.
- Entrustment language can be misread by committees as informality or as lowered rigour. It needs to be introduced with the multiple-observer evidence standard attached, or it will be heard as "the instructor's impression".
- Sizing guidance is for whole programmes; quoting the 20-40 figure in a single-course context invites an obvious objection.
