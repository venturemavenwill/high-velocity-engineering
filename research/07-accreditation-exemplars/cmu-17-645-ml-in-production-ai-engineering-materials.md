## What this source is
This is the full public syllabus and materials set for the most established production-AI engineering course in a research university. It is unusually complete as an exemplar because everything is public: not just the topic list but the grading distribution, the grade boundaries, the token economy, the peer-rating procedure, the AI-tool policy, the academic-integrity language, every lab, every assignment, past exams, and video recordings of the lectures. It is, in effect, a course design published as a reproducible artefact, and the authors explicitly invite reuse: "We would be happy to see this course or a similar version taught at other universities."

## The argument in full
The course opens with a rhetorical move that is worth studying because it defines a gap rather than a topic. "Plenty of courses teach you to beat a benchmark in a notebook, and plenty more teach you to pitch a demo, hackathon style. But a demo has no real customer, no real deployment, and no real stakes: Nobody depends on it, nothing breaks when it fails, nobody needs to maintain it. This CMU course is about the hard part that comes next: turning that prototype into a real AI-powered software system that provides benefits to actual customers, that you deploy in the real world, and that keeps running, keeps getting updated, and doesn't go up in flames with security holes, safety incidents, or silent quality collapse." The gap is not knowledge; it is consequence. Every structural decision downstream follows from the attempt to manufacture consequence inside a semester.

The scope statement is deliberately catholic across model types: students "learn AI engineering end to end, working across classic ML, LLMs, and increasingly AI agents that take actions in the real world." The organising thread is named explicitly: "Because models make mistakes, and agents act on those mistakes, managing risk runs through the entire course, alongside MLOps and responsible AI (safety, security, explainability, fairness)." Risk, not accuracy, is the spine. That reframing is what allows safety, security, fairness, explainability, monitoring and requirements to sit in one course without feeling like a survey.

The audience framing is equally deliberate and is directly relevant to a forward-deployed-engineering curriculum. The course names its target roles: "AI Engineer, ML Engineer, MLOps Engineer, AI Product Manager, Forward Deployed Engineer, or Applied Scientist." It then argues that building AI-powered systems "is a team effort across software engineers, data scientists, and product managers. Each brings different expertise and priorities, and they succeed only with a shared understanding of one another's roles, concerns, and goals." The course therefore deliberately speaks to all three audiences at once, and the prerequisite structure reflects this: there are no formal prerequisites, basic ML exposure and basic programming are expected, and software engineering experience is explicitly *not* required. To handle the resulting heterogeneity the course publishes a Google Form prerequisite knowledge check — ten machine-learning questions, anonymous and ungraded, which returns targeted readings and exercises based on the answers. That is a low-cost, high-value pattern: it triages a mixed cohort without gatekeeping it.

The assessment architecture is where the course earns its status as an exemplar. Grading is 35% individual assignments, 30% group project, 25% midterms and participation, 10% labs, and there is no final exam. All parts are graded pass/fail for the points indicated, with no partial credit — specification grading. The syllabus is candid about why this works: "We strive to provide clear specifications and clear point breakdowns for all homework to set clear expectations and take the guessing out of homework… Clear specifications and point breakdowns allow you to intentionally decide to skip parts of assignments with clear upfront consequences." Specification grading of this kind is normally brittle, because a small miss produces a large deduction. The course solves that with two coupled mechanisms rather than with partial credit. The first is a token economy. The second is high grade boundaries: A+ ≥99%, A ≥96%, A- ≥94%, B+ ≥91%, B ≥86%, B- ≥82%, C ≥75%, D ≥60%, with no rounding. The syllabus states the causal link openly — "Since we give flexibility to resubmit assignments, we set grade boundaries fairly high."

The token economy deserves description in full because it is the most transferable single mechanism here. Every student receives eight individual tokens and every team independently receives eight team tokens, and the two pools are strictly separate: "Individual tokens and team tokens are entirely separate; it is not possible to use individual tokens for teamwork or vice versa." One individual token buys one day of lateness on an individual homework with no penalty; without tokens the penalty is 15% per started day. Three tokens buy a resubmission of an individual homework, with 90% of the improvement credited — the syllabus works the arithmetic: "if the original assignment was 80 points and the resubmitted one was 100 points, the resulting grade is 80+(100-80)*.9 = 98 points." One token allows a lab to be completed or redone late by showing the work to a TA in office hours. One token each waives penalties for late or missing team-formation and peer-assessment surveys. Unspent tokens convert to participation days. Team tokens work the same way for milestones: one per day of extension except for the final presentation, three for a milestone resubmission with the same 10% tax. Crucially, the syllabus names its own loophole rather than hiding it: a student "can blow the original deadline (no submission necessary, receiving 0 points initially) and then resubmit the homework arbitrarily late for three tokens and a 10% penalty." Naming the loophole is a design choice — it converts an exploit into a sanctioned, budgeted option and removes the incentive to negotiate privately with staff.

The second mechanism worth extracting is the oral component, which is woven through rather than bolted on. "Some parts of the grading rubric will require you to verbally explain your solution to a member of the course staff or verbally answer their questions -- usually in person during any office hours within one or two weeks of submitting the assignment." Labs go further: the grade is earned in the room. "You receive a grade by showing your work to the TA during that week's lab session… Typically showing your work involves showing source code, demoing executions, and verbally answering a few questions to demonstrate your understanding. The TA may ask a few questions to probe your understanding." And the collaboration policy for labs is startlingly permissive precisely because the oral check backstops it: "We encourage collaboration on labs… While we do not recommend it, you may look at and even copy other solutions. However, you will have to present and explain your solution to the TA on your own." Labs are framed as low stakes — "this is your first practical engagement with the material and mistakes are a normal part of the learning process" — and if a solution does not meet expectations the student may keep working during the session until it does. This is a clean demonstration of a general principle: once you can verify understanding orally, you can relax nearly every other integrity control on the written artefact.

That principle also underwrites the AI-tool policy, which is among the most permissive published by a serious course and is coherent for exactly that reason. "Given the nature of this course, we are open to using AI tools for completing work. We actually strongly recommend it for many tasks." There are no restrictions on ChatGPT, Claude, Claude Code, Copilot or Cursor, and code may be reused from StackOverflow or tutorials without acknowledgment. The conditions are shifted onto responsibility and consequence: "you will be solely responsible for the correctness of the solution", content generation tools "often create plausible-looking but incorrect answers, which will not receive credit", and "Using code generation tools without understanding the generated code will likely create challenges when answering TA questions for labs or assignments and will likely create teamwork problems." Two settings are strictly off limits: "(1) exams and any on-paper quizzes we designate as strictly offline, and (2) live conversations with course staff, whether over Zoom or in person. Using any form of AI to generate answers in these settings is considered cheating." The course also warns students to budget $20-100 per month for AI subscriptions, "roughly comparable to the cost of traditional textbooks." Notably, the same permissiveness does not extend to peer work: "While the use of AI content generation tools is okay… using the work from other students is not."

The project is the course's other defining feature. It is an extended group project "building, deploying, evaluating, and maintaining a robust and scalable movie recommendation service under realistic 'production' conditions serving 1 million active users." The syllabus insists on the difference from a demo: "This is not a toy: your system runs live for several weeks, is graded on how it holds up under real load and changing environment conditions." Teams are 3-6 students, assigned by the instructor rather than self-selected, each with a TA mentor, and each team must schedule a thirty-minute meeting with that mentor in the week after every milestone "to discuss their solution, debrief on teamwork, and explore possible strategies to improve teamwork." Peer rating is performed on "team citizenship (i.e., being active and cooperative members)" following a procedure adapted from a Barbara Oakley article, with a preview form so students can see expected grade adjustments before the fact. Milestones are M1 Modeling and First Deployment, M2 Infrastructure Quality, M3 Monitoring and CD, and M4 Fairness, Security and Feedback Loops, with a final presentation in the registrar's exam slot.

Participation is handled with an opt-in mechanism that is elegant and rarely seen. Students choose whether participation counts: if they opt in, midterms are worth 15% and participation 10%; if they do not, midterms are worth 25% and participation is ungraded. The bands are explicit — 100% for active participation in most lectures with four lectures waived and no questions asked, 80% for two thirds, 60% for over half, 30% for one quarter, 0% below one quarter — and the course guarantees the opportunity by running "at least one breakout session in every single lecture." Inauthentic participation, such as asking others to cover for you, is treated as an academic integrity violation.

## Key concepts and specifics
- 12 units; lectures Tuesday and Thursday 15:30-16:50 in person; labs Friday across five sections.
- Grading: 35% individual assignments, 30% group project, 25% midterms and participation, 10% labs. No final exam. Two midterms; the second is not comprehensive.
- Grade boundaries, no rounding: A+ ≥99, A ≥96, A- ≥94, B+ ≥91, B ≥86, B- ≥82, C ≥75, D ≥60.
- Individual assignments: I1 ML Product / LLM features, I2 Requirements (risks), I3 MCP and Security (agent security), I4 Explainability. The PhD-level 17-745 number replaces two homeworks with a mandatory research project.
- Project milestones: M1 Modeling and First Deployment, M2 Infrastructure Quality, M3 Monitoring and CD, M4 Fairness, Security and Feedback Loops, plus final presentation.
- Tokens: 8 individual, 8 team, strictly non-fungible. 1 token = 1 day late; 3 tokens = resubmission at 90% of the improvement; 1 token = late or redone lab via office hours; 1 token = waived survey penalty; leftovers count as participation days. Without tokens, 15% per started day.
- Time budget: 12 hours per week — roughly 3 in class, 1-2 for labs, 7 on assignments.
- AI subscription cost expectation: $20-100 per month.
- Tools taught: Apache Kafka, GitHub Actions, Prometheus, Grafana, DVC, Weights and Biases, Docker, Kubernetes.
- Textbook: *Machine Learning in Production*, MIT Press, free online at `https://mlip-cmu.github.io/book/`; the course does not assign its own chapters but maps each lecture to one.
- Weekly external readings include "Making data science systems work", "Who validates the validators?", "The ML Test Score", "Data Cascades in High-Stakes AI", "How Engineers Operationalize Machine Learning", "Supabase MCP can leak your entire SQL database", "Hidden Technical Debt in Machine Learning Systems", "Hello AI: Uncovering the Onboarding Needs…", "Human Perceptions of Fairness in Algorithmic Decision Making", and "Improving Fairness in Machine Learning Systems".
- Auditing is not permitted due to demand, but all materials are online and visitors may sit in.
- Student work is analysed for academic research, with email opt-out, analysis only after final grades, de-identified and aggregated.

## Method and evidence base
The course has been offered regularly since 2019, with every prior semester archived and publicly linked, which is itself a form of evidence: the mechanisms described here have survived many iterations and two instructors. The design rationale is published separately as "Teaching Software Engineering for AI-Enabled Systems" (arXiv:2001.06691), and the underlying literature is catalogued in an annotated bibliography at `https://github.com/ckaestne/seaibib`. The course is also its own research site, with student work analysed post hoc under an opt-out protocol. What is not available is outcome evidence — there is no published claim that graduates of this course perform better in industry, and none is made.

## Vocabulary the source introduces
- **Specification grading** — deliverables graded pass/fail against published specifications with no partial credit.
- **Tokens** — a fixed per-student and per-team budget of flexibility, spendable on lateness or resubmission.
- **Resubmission tax** — the 10% of improvement not credited on a resubmitted assignment.
- **Team citizenship** — the object of peer rating: being an active and cooperative member, distinct from technical contribution.
- **Team mentor** — the TA assigned to each team, with a mandatory post-milestone debrief.
- **Opt-in participation** — student-chosen reweighting between midterms and participation.
- **Prerequisite knowledge check** — an anonymous, ungraded diagnostic that returns targeted remediation rather than a gate.

## What to remember
- Specification grading is only humane when paired with a flexibility budget. Tokens and high grade boundaries are one mechanism, not two.
- Oral verification is the enabling control. Once understanding is checked in the room, lab collaboration, code copying and unrestricted AI use can all be permitted without collapsing the assessment.
- Name your loopholes. Publishing the deadline-skip exploit converts it into a budgeted option and removes private negotiation.
- Assigned teams plus a TA mentor plus a mandatory post-milestone teamwork debrief treats collaboration as taught content, not as an assumed capability.
- Opt-in participation grading respects students who cannot or will not perform in class without abandoning the pedagogical case for discussion.
- Realistic scale — a live service under load for weeks — is what separates the project from a demo, and it is what makes the risk framing land.
- Forward Deployed Engineer is named explicitly as a target role by an established CMU course; that is a useful external validation of the role's curricular standing.

## Why it matters for the course
This is the closest existing exemplar to what the course is trying to build, and it demonstrates that the awkward parts are feasible at scale in a research university. It shows that oral defence can be operated at course scale by distributing it into labs and office hours rather than staging it as a single high-stakes event. It shows that a permissive AI policy is compatible with rigour if verification moves to conversation. It shows that specification grading survives contact with real students when paired with a token economy. And it shows that risk, rather than accuracy, is a workable spine for an AI systems syllabus. Because all materials are Creative Commons licensed, the mechanisms can be adopted directly rather than reinvented.

## How to use it in course design
- **Lecture:** use the demo-versus-system opening as the course's own framing, and adopt the risk-as-spine organisation rather than a tool-by-tool tour.
- **Lab:** copy the show-your-work grading model — deliverables graded pass/fail in the room by demonstration and verbal questioning, with permission to keep working during the session until the deliverable passes.
- **Assessment:** adopt tokens with the same non-fungible individual/team split, publish the resubmission arithmetic worked through with a numeric example, and raise grade boundaries to compensate.
- **Assessment:** distribute oral defence across office hours in a one-to-two week window after submission rather than concentrating it into a single viva.
- **Policy:** adopt the two-exception AI policy verbatim in shape — permissive everywhere, prohibited in exams and in live conversations with staff — and state the subscription cost expectation so it is not a hidden barrier.
- **Onboarding:** run an anonymous, ungraded prerequisite diagnostic that returns targeted readings, and use it to triage rather than to gate.
- **Teamwork:** assign teams, attach a mentor, mandate a post-milestone debrief, and preview peer-rating consequences with a form before ratings are collected.

## Limits and cautions
- The oral-verification model is staff-intensive. It works here because of a sizeable TA cohort across five lab sections and generous office hours; a course without that staffing cannot simply copy it.
- The token economy transfers grading effort into bookkeeping. Someone must track sixteen token balances per team-and-student pair and adjudicate resubmissions until the final presentation.
- The 1-million-user movie recommendation infrastructure is a substantial standing engineering investment maintained by the course, not something a new course gets for free.
- The permissive AI policy depends entirely on the oral check. Adopting the policy without the verification mechanism would be reckless.
- Grade boundaries this high are only defensible where the flexibility mechanisms genuinely exist and are known; imported in isolation they would simply be harsh.
- This is one course at one institution with a specific student population. The published schedule is Fall 2026 and will drift; cite the semester explicitly when quoting details.
