## What this source is
Two think-aloud studies of assessor cognition from the Maastricht group: one of supervisors judging trainee performance in the workplace, one of assessors reading competency portfolios. Together they describe what evaluators actually do — which evidence they use first, how quickly they commit, what happens when later evidence contradicts them, and how far their private models of competence diverge from the published criteria. Read as a design manual, they specify the failure modes an entrustment-based scheme must build controls against, and they name the controls the authors think work.

## The argument in full
The design problem begins with a number. The 2013 paper reports that "idiosyncratic rater effects account for substantial variance in performance ratings, ranging from 29% to over 50%." Between roughly a third and a half of the variance in a performance rating is the rater rather than the performance. This is the baseline any assessment scheme inherits, and it is not specific to narrative methods — it is measured on numeric ratings. A course that replaces rubric scores with entrustment judgements is not introducing subjectivity; it is choosing how to manage subjectivity that was already there.

The first design implication is that the obvious remedy does not work. Attempts to fix rater variance "by standardizing assessment instruments and rater training… have met with mixed success at best." The 2013 study explains why. Studying 18 experienced and 16 inexperienced supervisor-raters through think-aloud and verbal protocol analysis, the authors find that raters use different schemas to judge the same performance, and that "levels of rater idiosyncrasy were substantial and unrelated to rater expertise." Experience does not converge judgement; experienced raters make more use of task-specific performance schemas, which means their mental models are more *differentiated*, not more uniform. Raters "have implicit performance theories, which may diverge from those specified by the organization." Handing an assessor a better rubric does not replace the theory they already have.

The second design implication concerns timing, and it is the most actionable finding in either paper: "most raters started to develop person schemas the moment they began to observe trainee performance." Impression formation is not a stage that follows observation; it begins with it. The 2018 portfolio study shows what this produces downstream. Assessors reached judgements through "a 3-phase cyclical cognitive process of acquiring, organizing, and integrating evidence", then "reviewed the remaining portfolio evidence to look for confirming or disconfirming evidence" — and "assessors were inclined to stick to their initial judgments even when confronted with seemingly disconfirming evidence." Final judgements were more elaborate and detailed than preliminary ones but "not substantially different." More evidence produced better articulation of the same conclusion, not revision of it. Any scheme that assumes accumulating evidence improves accuracy is assuming something these studies do not support; what accumulating evidence reliably improves is the *justification*.

The third design implication is about what evidence assessors actually use, and it inverts the usual defensive framing. In the 2018 study, "assessors mainly relied on narrative feedback to inform their judgment, because this provided meaningful and detailed information about the student's development, strengths and weaknesses, as well as specific suggestions for improvement… Grades and qualifications were merely used to confirm impressions based on narratives." Narrative is not the soft supplement to the scores. Narrative is the evidence; the scores are the check. A scheme that collects only scores has therefore discarded the informative half of its own data.

The fourth implication is the one that should determine the shape of the summative procedure. "Although assessors reached similar final (pass–fail) judgments of students' professional competence, they differed in their information-processing approaches and the reasoning behind their judgments." Agreement on the outcome concealed disagreement about the construct. Three sources of divergence are identified, and each maps to a specific control. **Assessment beliefs** concern which evidence is trustworthy: some assessors began with workplace narratives as the most authentic record, others with self-evaluation and reflective writing, checking afterwards whether claims were justified; source credibility was actively contested, with some trusting physicians as content experts and others preferring nurses and fellow students who "had more opportunities to directly observe them", and one assessor naming the selection problem in self-chosen feedback directly — "you can be quite selective in who you ask… that does give you, I think, the most truthful answers." **Performance theories** concern what competence is, and the paper notes these "tended to deviate from the formal assessment criteria": one group treated active engagement in learning and effective use of feedback as sufficient, holding that "the student's competence did not necessarily have to be up to standard as long as there was enough evidence that the student had sufficiently improved over time", while another measured competence by reflective self-awareness, one assessor stating that "when other people feel that you have weaknesses in a given competency and you yourself disagree, I think that is – that is a scary person." Assessors also differed in which competency domains they weighted, most scanning past the less well-defined domains while others examined them deliberately because they discriminated among strong students. **Inferences** concern what a specific datum means about a specific person: the same insufficient rating was read by one assessor as a knowledge deficit and by another as insecurity, and one assessor read sustained middling ratings as evidence of underperformance, reasoning that "it is very difficult to give a one 1 or a two, so you give a three. And that is why I am cautious when someone only scores threes."

The remedies the authors propose are structural, and they follow directly from the diagnosis. Because divergence is invisible in outcomes, it has to be surfaced in reasoning: high-stakes decisions "should not be made individually, but should result from group discussions", and "portfolios should be judged by multiple assessors who should, moreover, thoroughly substantiate their judgments." Substantiation is the mechanism that makes divergent reasoning discussable; group deliberation is the forum in which discussing it builds **shared mental models** over time. Because instrument standardisation has a poor record, assessor training "should focus on raising assessors' awareness of their own beliefs, performance theories, and inferences" instead. And because assessors navigate evidence selectively, portfolios should be designed to support that navigation, with captions on feedback items because they "summarize the context in which the competency feedback was provided."

## Structure of the original

## Key concepts and specifics
- **29% to over 50%** of performance-rating variance is attributable to the rater.
- Idiosyncrasy is **unrelated to expertise**; experience differentiates mental models rather than aligning them.
- Standardising instruments and training raters has had "mixed success at best."
- **Person schemas form at the moment observation begins.**
- Judgement proceeds through a **three-phase cyclical process**: acquiring, organising, integrating.
- Assessors **anchor**: later evidence elaborates rather than revises the initial judgement.
- **Narrative is the primary evidence**; grades merely confirm impressions already formed from it.
- **Same outcome, different construct**: identical pass/fail decisions rest on divergent beliefs, performance theories and inferences.
- Assessors' performance theories "tended to deviate from the formal assessment criteria."
- Source credibility is contested — proximity of observation versus content expertise; self-selected feedback is recognised as selection-biased by assessors themselves.
- The same evidence supports opposite inferences about the same person.
- **Remedies:** group decision-making on high-stakes judgements; multiple assessors; mandatory thorough substantiation; training on self-awareness rather than calibration; navigable evidence with contextual captions.

## Method and evidence base
Think-aloud protocols with verbal protocol analysis and qualitative thematic analysis. Samples are small and purposive by design — 18 assessors in the 2018 study, 34 raters in the 2013 study — both in Dutch medical education, and the 2018 study used mock portfolios, which removes real consequence from the judgement being observed. The 29-50% variance range is reported from prior quantitative literature rather than generated by these studies and should be attributed accordingly. These are studies of the structure of reasoning, not of its distribution; they support claims about mechanism and about what controls to build, not about effect sizes.

## Vocabulary the source introduces
- **Idiosyncratic rater effect** — the share of rating variance attributable to rater identity.
- **Implicit performance theory** — an assessor's private model of good performance, often divergent from published criteria.
- **Person schema** — the impression of the individual formed from the moment observation begins.
- **Assessment beliefs** — an assessor's view of which evidence sources are trustworthy.
- **Inference** — the step from a datum to a claim about the person, where the same datum supports opposite readings.
- **Substantiation** — the required articulation of reasoning that makes divergence visible.
- **Shared mental models** — the convergence in judgement policy that repeated group deliberation is meant to build.

## What to remember
- Subjectivity is not a property of narrative methods. It is present at 29-50% of variance in numeric ratings, and the choice is how to manage it, not whether to admit it.
- Better rubrics do not fix it. Assessors bring theories that a rubric does not displace.
- Assessors commit early and then elaborate. Design the procedure to interrupt that, not to assume more evidence corrects it.
- Narrative carries the signal; scores confirm it. Collect the narrative.
- Outcome agreement is not construct agreement. Two assessors passing the same student may be assessing different things.
- The effective controls are structural — multiple assessors, written substantiation, group deliberation — not instrumental.
- Assessor training should aim at self-awareness of beliefs and inferences, because calibration training has a poor record.
- Feedback needs context captions, or later readers will infer from it wrongly.

## Why it matters for the course
This is the assessor-side half of the entrustment design. The EPA framework says what to assess and on what scale; this literature says what will go wrong when human assessors apply that scale, and which procedural controls are worth their cost. Three findings shape the course's procedure directly: narrative must be the recorded evidence rather than a comment field, because that is what assessors actually reason from; consequential entrustment decisions must involve more than one assessor with written substantiation, because outcome agreement hides construct divergence; and the procedure must actively interrupt anchoring, because assessors will otherwise elaborate their first impression rather than test it. It is also the source that keeps the design honest — it is the only material in this folder that documents what judgement-based assessment does badly, and a scheme that cites only its enthusiasts is weaker than one that names its own failure modes.

## How to teach it
- **Record:** make the narrative the assessment record. Any supervision-level rating must be accompanied by prose that states what was observed and what was inferred from it; a level with no substantiation is not usable evidence.
- **Sequence:** require assessors to record a preliminary judgement explicitly, then review remaining evidence with the specific instruction to look for disconfirmation, then record whether the judgement changed and why. Making the anchoring step visible is the only available control on it.
- **Panel:** decide summative entrustment with at least two assessors who have written independent substantiations before discussing. Independent-then-deliberate is the order; discussing first collapses the divergence the panel exists to surface.
- **Calibration:** run one session per term on a shared example in which assessors surface *why* they judged as they did, not whether they agreed. Target beliefs, performance theories and inferences by name.
- **Sources:** state in the scheme which evidence sources count and with what weight, because assessors will otherwise resolve this privately and differently. Note explicitly that student-selected feedback is selection-biased.
- **Captions:** require every piece of recorded feedback to carry a context caption — what task, what conditions, what the assessor could and could not see.
- **Inference discipline:** ask assessors to state the alternative reading they considered and rejected, since the studies show the same datum routinely supports opposite inferences.
- **Honesty:** publish the reliability position to students and reviewers, including the 29-50% figure, rather than implying that the scheme has eliminated subjectivity.

## Limits and cautions
- Small qualitative samples in Dutch medical education; transfer to computing education is an argument, not a finding.
- Mock portfolios in the 2018 study; real stakes may change assessor behaviour in ways not observed.
- The 29-50% figure is second-hand, reported from prior quantitative work rather than measured by these studies.
- Narrative assessment costs assessor time and requires writing skill that cannot be assumed of every TA.
- Group deliberation surfaces divergence but may resolve it through social pressure rather than better reasoning; these studies do not rule that out, and seniority effects in a teaching team are a real risk.
- The recommended remedies are the authors' inferences from qualitative findings, not evaluated interventions.
- The paywalled canon on programmatic assessment validity was not read; this note is not a literature survey.
