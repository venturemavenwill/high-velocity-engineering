# Cliff Notes — EPA Framework as an Assessment Design Manual

## What this source is
The originator's own primer on entrustable professional activities, containing the operational detail needed to build one: how to write an EPA description, how to express entrustment on a supervision scale, what evidence a summative entrustment decision requires, and how to sequence a curriculum around the resulting units. This folder reads it as a design manual. The question here is not whether EPAs are a good idea but what exactly must be written down, observed and decided if the course is to run an entrustment-based assessment.

## The argument in full
The first design decision is granularity, and ten Cate is specific about it. EPAs "should be not too small and not too big", and "a suitable number is 20 to 40 EPAs for a complete program" — a whole degree or residency, not a single course. Activities are "the units of professional practice that constitute what clinicians do as daily work", they are "activities with a beginning and an end", and they "are only entrusted to trained personnel". The test for correct granularity is therefore behavioural rather than conceptual: could a supervisor delegate this whole thing to a learner and walk away, and would the learner know when they had finished it? A unit that fails either half of that test is the wrong size. His worked examples span the training arc and illustrate the range — an ICU resident evaluating a deteriorating patient after a nurse's night call; a junior obstetrics resident entrusted with an uncomplicated delivery; a senior medical student examining a patient with a known chronic condition, ordering tests and preparing follow-up medication with only a sign-off remaining; and a junior medical student given "small but significant tasks that do not have to be checked if the student has been trained well."

The second design decision is what to write down, and here the primer supplies a template concrete enough to fill in directly. An EPA description should occupy one or two pages and contain, in order: **(1) Title**, which must be recognisable to practitioners and learners, may be a noun or include a verb, "should not contain an adjective", and "should not be phrased as an educational objective—it is just an activity". **(2) Specification and limitations**, capturing "no more and no less than what is exactly meant", with explicit restrictions of the form "only applicable with hemo-dynamically stable patients". **(3) Link with the prevailing framework of competencies**, which is where the activity is joined to the outcome framework the institution is accountable to. **(4) Required knowledge, skills, attitude, and experience.** **(5) Sources of information to support entrustment decisions** — direct observations, longitudinal observations, case-based discussions and product evaluations. **(6) Targeted level of supervision**, where "for most EPAs this may be Level 3 in medical school and Level 4 for postgraduate programs". **(7)** Optionally an **expiry date**, because if a learner "has not practiced the EPA for a long time, the entrustment decision may expire."

Three of those seven items are the ones most often skipped and most consequential. The prohibition on adjectives in the title is not stylistic fussiness — an adjective smuggles a quality standard into the name of the activity, which belongs in the specification and the supervision level instead. The specification-and-limitations section is what stops an activity from silently expanding to cover cases nobody intended to certify. And the expiry date acknowledges that entrustment decays, which most academic assessment schemes never admit.

The third design decision is how entrustment is expressed, and the answer is an ordinal supervision scale rather than a score. Five levels run from **level 1**, present and observing only; **level 2**, executing with direct proactive supervision in the room; **level 3**, executing without a supervisor in the room but quickly available, which is indirect reactive supervision; **level 4**, working unsupervised; to **level 5**, supervising more junior learners. The primer also gives an expanded scale with sub-levels that make the middle of the range usable: level 2a distinguishes co-activity with the supervisor from 2b, where the supervisor is merely in the room ready to step in; level 3 splits into 3a with all findings double-checked, 3b with key findings double-checked, and 3c with the supervisor distantly available and findings promptly reviewed; and level 4 splits into 4a with remote monitoring and 4b without. That sub-level structure is where most of a course's actual progression happens — the distance between "I check everything you did" and "I check the important parts" is a semester's worth of development, and a five-point scale cannot see it.

The primer also reports what happens when a scale is phrased in supervisors' own language rather than in framework language. Citing Weller and colleagues' nine-point anaesthesiology scale: "I'm not comfortable leaving the operating room; I can take a brief coffee break; I can take a lunch break; I have to check in regularly; I can wait until asked for help; I can limit to preview everything with the trainee; I can be off site; and the trainee can work as a consultant." Ten Cate's comment is the design lesson: "it illustrates excellently the core idea of entrustability scales: to align assessment with clinical practice." Mapped onto the nine-point mini-CEX scale, such entrustability phrasing "proved to increase the reliability of assessment." Writing the scale in the assessor's own idiom is not informality; it demonstrably improves the measurement.

The fourth design decision is what evidence licenses a decision, and the primer separates two decision types with different standards. **Ad-hoc entrustment** happens continuously and informally, whenever a supervisor "feels the skills of the learner at that time match the complexity of the patient and the risks in doing this are acceptable". **Summative entrustment** "has the nature of certification" and is a decision "taken for the future responsibilities of the learner from that moment on", sometimes recorded as a Statement of Awarded Responsibility. Summative decisions "should include an agreement between multiple observers that this entrustment is justified, to avoid subjective bias, and multiple observations", drawing on the four evidence types named in the template and supplemented by examination scores. The five factors that in practice drive ad-hoc entrustment are worth knowing precisely because they will operate on a course's assessors whether or not they are acknowledged: perceived learner features; the supervisor's own propensity to delegate; the complexity of the activity; the context; and the nature of the relationship between supervisor and learner. Within learner features sit three professionalism characteristics ten Cate names explicitly — **integrity**, "truthfulness in reports to a supervising clinician and benevolence"; **reliability**, "stable, predictable behavior and a conscientious way of working"; and **humility**, "the ability to observe own limitations and willingness ask for help when needed". Those three are assessable dispositions stated in behavioural terms, and they translate to engineering work with almost no modification.

The fifth design element is the mechanism for probing understanding, and it is the direct ancestor of oral defence. The **entrustment-based discussion** is defined as a short focused conversation, usually after the activity, that "should probe the student's deep understanding of what was done, but should include 'what-if' questions, to grasp whether the learner would know what to do what unexpected or familiar situations would arise." The what-if requirement is what distinguishes it from a walkthrough. Retrospective explanation shows that the learner understood the case they met; counterfactual questioning is the only available evidence about the cases they did not meet, and ten Cate is explicit that this matters because "an entrustment decision implies the acceptance of risk, as it can never be fully predicted what the student will encounter."

The primer closes with a five-step method for building the curriculum: identify the EPAs; write full EPA descriptions; determine assessment rules; establish individualised portfolio-based pathways; and allow flexibility in the length or breadth of training. He reports implementation data from a physician-assistant programme that has been EPA-based since 2010: across **101 graduates** of a **2.5-year** programme, students began by targeting about **seven EPAs** (6.8 on average), completed slightly fewer (6.6 on average), and made **1.5 altered EPAs** on average. Those numbers are the closest thing available to a realistic per-learner load, and they suggest that even in a programme designed around the model, the number of activities actually carried through to entrustment per learner is small.

## Key concepts and specifics
- **Granularity test:** bounded work with a beginning and an end that a supervisor could delegate whole. Programme sizing 20-40 activities; per-learner completion in the reported cohort averaged 6.6.
- **EPA description template, one to two pages:** title (no adjectives, not an objective); specification and limitations; link to the competency framework; required knowledge, skills, attitude and experience; sources of information; targeted supervision level; optional expiry date.
- **Five supervision levels:** observe only; direct proactive supervision in the room; indirect reactive supervision, supervisor quickly available; unsupervised; supervising others.
- **Expanded sub-levels:** 2a co-activity / 2b supervisor present; 3a all findings double-checked / 3b key findings double-checked / 3c distant supervisor, prompt review; 4a remote monitoring / 4b none.
- **Targeting guidance:** level 3 for undergraduate, level 4 for postgraduate, for most activities.
- **Four evidence sources:** direct observation, longitudinal observation, case-based discussion, product evaluation.
- **Summative standard:** multiple observers in agreement, multiple observations, supplemented by examination scores.
- **Five drivers of ad-hoc entrustment:** learner features; supervisor propensity to delegate; activity complexity; context; supervisor-learner relationship.
- **Three professionalism characteristics:** integrity (truthfulness and benevolence); reliability (stable, predictable, conscientious); humility (recognising limits, willing to ask for help).
- **Entrustment-based discussion:** short, post-activity, probes deep understanding, must include what-if questions.
- **Entrustability scales phrased in supervisors' own language** — Weller et al.'s nine points from "I'm not comfortable leaving the operating room" to "the trainee can work as a consultant" — mapped onto the mini-CEX scale "proved to increase the reliability of assessment."
- **Five-step build method:** identify activities; write descriptions; determine assessment rules; establish individualised portfolio pathways; allow flexibility in training length or breadth.
- **Implementation data:** 101 graduates, 2.5-year physician-assistant programme, 6.8 EPAs targeted, 6.6 completed, 1.5 altered on average.
- **Expiry:** entrustment can lapse if the activity has not been practised for a long time.

## Method and evidence base
A conceptual and practical primer by the concept's originator, synthesising a decade of literature. It is guidance, not evaluation. The empirical items it contains — the anaesthesiology reliability finding, the Weller scale, the five-factor analysis of ad-hoc entrustment, and the 101-graduate cohort figures — are reported from prior studies and cited, not generated here. For design purposes this is the right kind of source: it tells you what to build and what the builders learned, without claiming to have measured the result.

## Vocabulary the source introduces
- **Entrustable Professional Activity** — a bounded unit of professional practice that can be entrusted to a sufficiently trained individual; describes work, not persons.
- **Ad-hoc entrustment decision** — the continuous, informal, contextual delegation judgement.
- **Summative entrustment decision** — a certification-like decision about future responsibilities, requiring multiple observers and observations.
- **Statement of Awarded Responsibility** — the formal record of a summative entrustment decision.
- **Supervision level** — the ordinal scale, with sub-levels, on which entrustment is expressed.
- **Entrustability scale** — a supervision scale phrased in the assessor's own practical idiom rather than in framework language.
- **Entrustment-based discussion** — the short, what-if-inclusive post-activity conversation.
- **Expiry** — the lapse of an entrustment decision through disuse.

## What to remember
- The unit of assessment is bounded work, not a competency and not a learning objective. If it does not have an end that both parties would recognise, it is the wrong unit.
- Titles carry no adjectives. Quality standards live in the specification and the supervision level.
- The sub-levels are where a course's progression is actually visible. A bare five-point scale is too coarse for one semester.
- Phrase the scale in the assessor's own words. There is reported evidence that doing so improves reliability, not merely comfort.
- What-if questioning is the whole point of the oral component. Retrospective walkthrough is not entrustment evidence.
- Multiple observers, multiple observations, multiple evidence types — that is the summative standard, and anything less should be described as ad-hoc.
- Integrity, reliability and humility are stated behaviourally and are assessable. They are also the best available operationalisation of "professional dispositions".
- Entrustment expires. Assessment schemes that assume permanence are claiming more than the model does.
- Even in mature programmes, learners complete fewer than seven activities. Two or three in a course is the realistic figure.

## Why it matters for the course
This is the spine of the course's assessment design. It supplies the unit of assessment (a bounded professional activity), the scale (supervision level with sub-levels), the evidence standard (multiple observers, multiple observations, four evidence types), the oral mechanism (entrustment-based discussion with mandatory what-if questions), and the dispositions vocabulary (integrity, reliability, humility) that maps directly onto what ABET's proposed criteria call professional dispositions. Where CMU supplies the operational machinery for running such a scheme inside a semester, and Govaerts supplies the warning about assessor subjectivity, this source supplies the object being assessed and the form the judgement takes. Every other assessment decision in this repository is downstream of getting the activity definitions right.

## How to teach it
- **Design:** define two or three activities for the course, each bounded, each something a supervisor could hand over whole. Test each against the beginning-and-end criterion before writing anything else.
- **Design:** write each activity to the seven-part template on one to two pages. Enforce the no-adjectives rule on titles and take the specification-and-limitations section seriously — that section is what prevents scope creep in assessment.
- **Design:** adopt the expanded sub-levels, not the bare five, and set the course target at level 3b or 3c rather than at 4. Undergraduate targeting guidance in the primer is level 3.
- **Design:** rewrite the scale anchors in the assessors' own idiom — what a supervising engineer would actually say about how much they need to check — following the Weller precedent.
- **Assessment:** require all four evidence types per activity: a direct observation, a longitudinal record, a discussion, and evaluation of the produced artefact.
- **Assessment:** structure the oral component as an entrustment-based discussion, with a documented requirement that what-if questions be asked, and record the answers as evidence.
- **Assessment:** make the summative decision with more than one assessor and record the agreement. Anything decided by one observer on one occasion should be labelled ad-hoc and weighted accordingly.
- **Assessment:** assess integrity, reliability and humility explicitly using ten Cate's behavioural definitions, rather than leaving professionalism implicit.
- **Design:** set an expiry convention if entrustment is to carry across terms, and state it.
- **Calibration:** brief assessors on the five drivers of ad-hoc entrustment, particularly the supervisor's own propensity to delegate and the effect of the relationship, so that these operate consciously rather than invisibly.

## Limits and cautions
- A clinical model. Medicine has patients, legally responsible supervisors and a licensure endpoint; a course has to construct the stakes artificially, and the construction is the hardest part of the transfer.
- The primer is guidance from an advocate. It reports adoption, not effectiveness.
- The full evidence standard — multiple trained observers producing documented, deliberated summative judgements — is expensive. A course must adopt a reduced version and say honestly that it is reduced.
- Supervision levels presuppose that someone is actually supervising real work. Simulated supervision produces simulated entrustment evidence.
- Writing good activity descriptions is genuinely difficult and iterative; the reported cohort data shows learners altering 1.5 activities on average even in an established programme.
- Entrustment language can be misread as lowered rigour. It should always be introduced with the multiple-observer standard attached.
- The three professionalism characteristics are defined but not operationalised into observable indicators; that work has to be done locally.
