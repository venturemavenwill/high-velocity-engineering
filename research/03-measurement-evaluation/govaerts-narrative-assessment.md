## What this source is
Two empirical studies of what assessors actually do when they judge performance, rather than of what assessment instruments are supposed to measure. The 2018 study observes assessors reading competency portfolios and reconstructs their reasoning; the 2013 study observes supervisors judging trainee performance and characterizes the mental models they bring. Together they supply the evidence base for treating narrative comments as primary assessment data and for requiring group deliberation on high-stakes decisions.

## The argument in full
The 2013 paper establishes the problem. Workplace-based assessment has grown in response to competency-based curricula, demands for physician accountability, and concerns about health care quality, and "although there is general agreement that WBA is useful for formative assessment, its usefulness for summative assessment is not undisputed." The reason is that "the idiosyncratic nature of (untrained) rater judgments results in large differences between performance ratings, low inter- and intra-rater reliabilities and questionable validity of WBA." The magnitude is stark: the paper reports that "idiosyncratic rater effects account for substantial variance in performance ratings, ranging from 29% to over 50%." Between a third and a half of the variance in a performance rating is attributable to who is doing the rating. Attempts to fix this by standardizing instruments and training raters "have met with mixed success at best."

Rather than treating this as noise to be suppressed, Govaerts and colleagues treat it as cognition to be understood, drawing on theoretical frameworks from social cognition and person perception. They study 18 experienced and 16 inexperienced supervisor-raters in general practice residency training using think-aloud procedures and verbal protocol analysis. The findings are that raters use different schemas to judge the same performance; that a normative performance theory comprising seventeen inter-related performance dimensions can be reconstructed from their reasoning; that "levels of rater idiosyncrasy were substantial and unrelated to rater expertise"; that experienced raters make significantly more use of task-specific performance schemas, suggesting more differentiated mental models; and — the finding with the sharpest implication — that "most raters started to develop person schemas the moment they began to observe trainee performance." Raters do not observe neutrally and then judge; they begin constructing an impression of the person immediately. Raters "have implicit performance theories, which may diverge from those specified by the organization," which explains why standardizing the instrument does not standardize the judgment.

The 2018 study takes this into the portfolio setting, where the assessor is not watching a performance but reading an accumulated evidentiary record. Eighteen assessors appraised one of three mock portfolios while thinking aloud, followed by semi-structured interviews. The finding that gives this note its title is unambiguous: **"assessors mainly relied on narrative feedback to inform their judgment, because this provided meaningful and detailed information about the student's development, strengths and weaknesses, as well as specific suggestions for improvement… Grades and qualifications were merely used to confirm impressions based on narratives."** The numerical ratings in the portfolio were not the evidence. They were a check on a conclusion already formed from the prose.

The study also reconstructs the process. Assessors reached judgments through "a 3-phase cyclical cognitive process of acquiring, organizing, and integrating evidence." Having completed one cycle, "assessors reviewed the remaining portfolio evidence to look for confirming or disconfirming evidence" — and here the paper reports a genuine warning: "assessors were inclined to stick to their initial judgments even when confronted with seemingly disconfirming evidence." Their final judgments were more elaborate and detailed than their preliminary ones but "not substantially different." The additional reading produced better articulation of the same conclusion, not revision of it.

The most consequential finding is that convergence on the outcome concealed divergence in the reasoning. "Although assessors reached similar final (pass–fail) judgments of students' professional competence, they differed in their information-processing approaches and the reasoning behind their judgments." The paper identifies three sources of that divergence. **Assessment beliefs** concern which evidence is trustworthy: some assessors began with workplace-based assessment narratives as the most authentic record, while others began with self-evaluations and reflective writing and then checked whether the claims were justified. Source credibility was actively contested — some assessors trusted physicians as content experts, while others preferred feedback from fellow students and nurses who "had more opportunities to directly observe them," and some valued longitudinal feedback from a single assessor for pattern detection while others preferred anonymous multi-source feedback specifically to counter the selection bias one assessor described directly: "you can be quite selective in who you ask… that does give you, I think, the most truthful answers."

**Performance theories** concern what competence is, and the paper notes these "tended to deviate from the formal assessment criteria." One group defined competence as active engagement in learning and effective use of feedback, reading the portfolio chronologically to establish growth, and held that "the student's competence did not necessarily have to be up to standard as long as there was enough evidence that the student had sufficiently improved over time." A second group measured competence by reflective self-awareness — one assessor stated that "when other people feel that you have weaknesses in a given competency and you yourself disagree, I think that is – that is a scary person." A third difference concerned which competency domains carried weight: most assessors targeted medical expert, manager, and communicator and scanned the rest, while others deliberately examined the less well-defined domains such as health advocate and scholar precisely because they discriminated among above-average students.

**Inferences** concern what a given piece of evidence means about this particular person. The same insufficient rating was read by one assessor as a knowledge deficit and by another as insecurity. One assessor read sustained middling ratings as evidence of underperformance, reasoning that "it is very difficult to give a one 1 or a two, so you give a three. And that is why I am cautious when someone only scores threes." Some assessors began with the curriculum vitae to contextualize the student as a person, with one stating a preference plainly: "I'd rather have people who travel, play music and sports, look around them, speak foreign languages, read three newspapers, and pass by the narrowest margin. Than someone who receives outstanding grades but does not leave his room."

The practical conclusions follow directly from the finding that identical outcomes rest on different reasoning. High-stakes decisions "should not be made individually, but should result from group discussions," because group deliberation surfaces the divergent reasoning that identical scores conceal. Discussing judgment policies within a group builds **shared mental models**. Assessor training "should focus on raising assessors' awareness of their own beliefs, performance theories, and inferences" rather than on instrument calibration, which the 2013 paper had already shown to have limited effect. And the papers converge on the recommendation that "portfolios should be judged by multiple assessors who should, moreover, thoroughly substantiate their judgments" — substantiation being what makes divergent reasoning visible and therefore discussable. A design implication follows too: portfolios should facilitate the selection of and navigation through evidence, and captions matter because they "summarize the context in which the competency feedback was provided."

## Key claims, findings, and specifics
- **"Assessors mainly relied on narrative feedback to inform their judgment… Grades and qualifications were merely used to confirm impressions based on narratives."** (2018)
- Assessors judge through a **three-phase cyclical process: acquiring, organizing, and integrating evidence**, then re-entering acquisition to seek confirming or disconfirming evidence. (2018)
- **Anchoring is real:** "assessors were inclined to stick to their initial judgments even when confronted with seemingly disconfirming evidence"; final judgments were more elaborate but not substantially different from initial ones. (2018)

## Method and evidence base
Both studies use think-aloud protocols — assessors verbalize their reasoning while performing a real judgment task — followed in the 2018 study by semi-structured interviews, with audio recordings transcribed verbatim and analyzed thematically. This method is the point: it captures reasoning rather than outcome, which is precisely where the two studies find the variation that outcome-level agreement statistics hide. The 2018 study controlled the stimulus by constructing three mock portfolios with deliberately different competency profiles, built by a team comprising a psychologist, two educationalists, and a veterinarian, with six assessors per portfolio. Assessors were selected by maximum variation sampling from family medicine and surgical and non-surgical specialties, with 18 of 24 invited participating, and theoretical saturation was reached after the twelfth transcript. The 2013 study adds a quantitative layer, measuring rater idiosyncrasy numerically and testing for expertise effects alongside the qualitative schema analysis. The 2018 authors state their limitations candidly: a single institution and portfolio type; the inherent inability of think-aloud methods to capture automatic or fast processing and the risk that participants confabulate explanations for the researcher; and the fact that assessors here judged on the portfolio alone whereas real mentors also know the student personally — though they note this supports separating formative and summative assessor roles.

## Vocabulary the source introduces
- **Assessment beliefs** — an assessor's convictions about which kinds of evidence are most valuable and trustworthy for judging competence.
- **Performance theory** — an assessor's own conceptualization of what professional competence and competent performance consist of, which may diverge from the formal framework.
- **Inference** — the conclusion an assessor draws about a student as a person and future professional from a given piece of evidence; the same evidence supports different inferences.
- **Person schema** — a mental model of the individual being assessed, which the 2013 study finds raters begin building immediately upon observation.
- **Task-specific performance schema** — a differentiated mental model of what good performance looks like on a particular activity, used more by experienced raters.
- **Rater idiosyncrasy** — the portion of rating variance attributable to the individual rater rather than to the performance.
- **Shared mental models** — the convergent understanding of judgment standards that develops when assessors discuss their policies as a group.
- **Three-phase cyclical process** — acquiring, organizing, and integrating evidence, repeated until the assessor has sufficient information.

## What to remember
- Narratives are the evidence and scores are the confirmation. Assessors reading real portfolios used the prose to form judgments and the numbers only to check them.
- Between 29 and over 50 percent of rating variance comes from the rater, and this does not improve with experience. Assessor variability is structural, not a training deficiency.
- Agreement on the outcome does not mean agreement on the reasoning. Two assessors can both pass a student for incompatible reasons, which is invisible in the scores.
- Assessors anchor early and then seek confirmation. Additional evidence tends to elaborate the initial judgment rather than overturn it.
- The remedy is group deliberation with substantiated judgments, not better rating scales. Standardization and rater training have had mixed success at best.
- Assessor training should target self-awareness of one's own beliefs, performance theory, and inferences — not calibration to an instrument.

## Why it matters for the course
This is the empirical foundation for making narrative a required assessment artifact rather than an optional courtesy, and it is the necessary companion to the EPA material. Ten Cate supplies the assessment architecture; Govaerts and colleagues supply the evidence about what happens inside the assessor's head when that architecture is used, and the news is sobering. For a course assessing ambiguous professional work in AI systems engineering, three implications are direct. First, rubric scores alone will not carry a high-stakes decision, because the assessors will be reasoning from the prose anyway — so the prose should be required, structured, and treated as data. Second, single-assessor decisions are indefensible given the measured magnitude of rater idiosyncrasy; the course needs a committee process, and it needs assessors to write down their reasoning so that divergence becomes discussable. Third, and most useful pedagogically, the finding that assessors' personal performance theories deviate from the formal criteria means the course must invest in calibration conversations among its own staff before it can claim its assessments mean anything consistent. These papers also give students something valuable in its own right: a demonstration that human judgment has documented, measurable biases — anchoring, confirmation seeking, immediate person-schema formation — which is exactly the critical stance the course asks them to take toward LLM judges. The symmetry is worth making explicit.

## How to teach it
- **Lecture point:** lead with the finding that grades merely confirmed impressions formed from narratives. It reframes what a rubric is for.
- **Lecture point:** the 29-to-50-percent idiosyncrasy figure, together with the finding that it is unrelated to rater expertise. This forecloses the assumption that more experienced graders are more consistent.
- **Lecture point:** parallel the human rater biases with the LLM judge biases from Zheng et al. Anchoring and confirmation seeking in human assessors sit alongside position and verbosity bias in model judges — both are systematic, both are measurable, neither is fixed by good intentions.
- **Lab exercise:** a think-aloud replication. Give several students the same portfolio or project artifact and have each verbalize their reasoning while judging it. Compare outcomes and then compare reasoning. The gap between the two is the lesson.
- **Lab exercise:** have students articulate their own performance theory for a course EPA before assessing peer work, then compare across the cohort against the formal criteria.
- **Assessment idea:** require every assessment decision in the course to include a written substantiation, and make the quality of the substantiation itself a criterion.
- **Assessment idea:** run high-stakes progression decisions through a committee that discusses before deciding, and document where assessors initially diverged. Use those records to build shared mental models among course staff over time.
- **Assessment idea:** structure portfolios so evidence is navigable and each item carries a caption describing the context in which the feedback was given, following the 2018 paper's design recommendation.

## Limits and cautions
- Both studies are from medical education at Maastricht, single-institution, with a specific portfolio type and specific competency framework. Transfer to an AI systems course is an argument, not a given, and should be made explicitly.
- The 2018 study used mock portfolios and assessors who did not know the students. Real assessors bring prior relationships, which the authors note is a difference and which cuts both ways.
- Think-aloud methods cannot capture fast or automatic processing, and participants may construct plausible explanations for the researcher rather than reporting actual reasoning. The authors state this limitation themselves.
- Narratives are not self-validating. Unstructured comments are hard to compare across students and can carry bias more easily than numbers — the CV-based inference quoted in the 2018 study is a clear example of an assessor importing a personal preference about what kind of person a good professional is.
- These findings describe how assessors reason; they do not establish that narrative-based judgment is more valid than score-based judgment. They establish that it is what actually happens and that it therefore needs to be designed for.
- The note substitutes two open-access studies for Govaerts' most-cited paywalled works. The substitution is reasonable but the canonical statements of programmatic assessment and of validity in work-based assessment have not been consulted here.
