## What this source is
A practitioner book on building applications on top of foundation models, by the author of *Designing Machine Learning Systems*, to which it refers back at several points (pp. 205, 375, 380). Its function in this folder is structural: it is the one source here that treats evaluation as an engineering problem embedded in a running system rather than as a statistical problem or a human-judgement problem, and it gives evaluation **two of its ten chapters** — Chapter 3 on evaluation methods and Chapter 4 on evaluating the systems built from them.

The book states its own position on evaluation three times, and all three are worth having exactly, because they are pitched at different strengths. Chapter 3 opens with the weakest and most defensible: "For some applications, figuring out evaluation can take up the majority of the development effort" (p. 113). Chapter 4 closes with a middle form: "Not having a reliable evaluation pipeline is one of the biggest blocks to AI adoption" (p. 208). In between she states the strong form and marks it as an opinion rather than a finding: "I believe that evaluation is the biggest bottleneck to AI adoption. Being able to build reliable evaluation pipelines will unlock many new applications" (p. 161). Teaching material should quote whichever of the three it can carry, and should not upgrade the hedge.

## The argument in full
Four claims carry the book. Only the first is the one the placeholder emphasised, and the fourth is the one it inverted.

**AI engineering differs from ML engineering in three enumerated ways, and the third is evaluation.** She does not argue that it is a separate discipline; she argues nearly the opposite. "AI engineering evolved out of ML engineering" (p. 36), the two roles "have significant overlap" (p. 36), and "much of what ML engineers have learned and shared over the last decade is still applicable" (p. 39). The differences are listed at pp. 39–40. First, "Without foundation models, you have to train your own models for your applications. With AI engineering, you use a model someone else has trained for you. This means that AI engineering focuses less on modeling and training, and more on model adaptation." Second, the models are larger and slower, so "there's more pressure for efficient training and inference optimization." Third, "AI engineering works with models that can produce open-ended outputs. Open-ended outputs give models the flexibility to be used for more tasks, but they are also harder to evaluate. This makes evaluation a much bigger problem in AI engineering." Her own compression: "AI engineering differs from ML engineering in that it's less about model development and more about adapting and evaluating models" (p. 40).

**Evaluation criteria are defined before the application is built.** She names this and attributes the name: "Before investing time, money, and resources into building an application, it's important to understand how this application will be evaluated. I call this approach *evaluation-driven development*. The name is inspired by *test-driven development* in software engineering, which refers to the method of writing tests before writing code. In AI engineering, evaluation-driven development means defining evaluation criteria before building" (p. 160). She then concedes the objection to it in her own voice, which is worth teaching alongside the idea: "focusing only on applications whose outcomes can be measured is similar to looking for the lost key under the lamppost (at night). It's easier to do, but it doesn't mean we'll find the key. We might be missing out on many potentially game-changing applications because there is no easy way to evaluate them" (p. 161).

**No single method suffices, and the reason given is a property of the measurement rather than of the tooling.** "No perfect evaluation method exists. It's impossible to capture the ability of a high-dimensional system using one- or few-dimensional scores. Evaluating modern AI systems has many limitations and biases. However, this doesn't mean we shouldn't do it. Combining different methods and approaches can help mitigate many of these challenges" (p. 209). The operational form is a costed mixture rather than a hierarchy: "you might have a cheap classifier that gives low-quality signals on 100% of your data, and an expensive AI judge to give high-quality signals on 1% of the data. This gives you a certain level of confidence in your application while keeping costs manageable" (p. 204).

**The evaluation pipeline must be more stable than the thing it evaluates.** This is the claim the placeholder got backwards, and it is the one that most changes how the material should be taught. "An application evolves over time, but the way it's evaluated ideally should be fixed. This way, evaluation metrics can be used to monitor the application's changes. However, AI judges are also AI applications, which means that they also can change over time" (p. 143). She gives the failure mode concretely: a coherence score moving from 90% to 92% between months is uninterpretable unless you know the judge was identical, and "if the application and the AI judge are managed by different teams… the application team might mistakenly attribute the changes in the evaluation results to changes in the application, rather than the changes in the judges" (p. 143). At the close of the pipeline chapter she allows iteration and immediately bounds it: "While iteration is necessary, you should be able to expect a certain level of consistency from your evaluation pipeline. If the evaluation process changes constantly, you won't be able to use the evaluation results to guide your application's development" (p. 208).

Underneath these sits the taxonomy the chapters are actually organised by, which is not the one the placeholder assumed. Chapter 3 divides methods into **exact** and **subjective**: "Exact evaluation produces judgment without ambiguity… As you'll see in the next section, AI as a judge is subjective. The evaluation result can change based on the judge model and the prompt" (p. 125). Exact evaluation covers functional correctness and similarity against reference data; subjective evaluation covers AI-as-a-judge and comparative ranking. Human evaluation sits outside the split and is treated as a live option rather than a fallback: "Use automatic metrics as much as possible, but don't be afraid to fall back on human evaluation, even in production" (p. 204).

Finally, the surrounding engineering is genuinely first-class rather than appended. "Cost and latency" is one of the four criteria buckets an application is supposed to define up front (p. 161), inference metrics get their own treatment in Chapter 9, and observability, gateways, caching, guardrails and user feedback get Chapter 10. Her one-line warning about skipping evaluation for speed is characteristically short: "Some teams decide to skip evaluation in the hope of reducing latency. It's a risky bet" (p. 208).

## Key claims, findings, and specifics
This is a `measurement`-namespace source, so each method below is recorded with the conditions the book attaches to it. Where a figure originates with someone else, the originating work is named, because in almost every case the book is reporting rather than measuring.

### Why evaluating foundation models is harder — the four reasons, in her order (pp. 114–115)
1. "the more intelligent AI models become, the harder it is to evaluate them", with the corollary that "evaluation can be so much more time-consuming for sophisticated tasks. You can no longer evaluate a response based on how it sounds. You'll also need to fact-check, reason, and even incorporate domain expertise."
2. "the open-ended nature of foundation models undermines the traditional approach of evaluating a model against ground truths", because "for an open-ended task, for a given input, there are so many possible correct responses. It's impossible to curate a comprehensive list of correct outputs to compare against."
3. Models are black boxes, "either because model providers choose not to expose models' details, or because application developers lack the expertise to understand them."
4. Scope expansion: with general-purpose models "evaluation is not only about assessing a model's performance on known tasks but also about discovering new tasks that the model can do."

Separately she records **benchmark saturation** as an observation with four dated instances: "A benchmark becomes saturated for a model once the model achieves the perfect score. With foundation models, benchmarks are becoming saturated fast" — GLUE (2018) saturated "in just a year", necessitating SuperGLUE (2019); NaturalInstructions (2021) replaced by Super-NaturalInstructions (2022); MMLU (2020) "largely replaced by" MMLU-Pro (2024) (p. 115).

And she records the state of practice bluntly: "When asked how they are evaluating their AI applications, many people told me that they just eyeballed the results… You might be able to get away with this ad hoc approach when getting a project off the ground, but it won't be sufficient for application iteration" (p. 117). The under-investment claim is borrowed from DeepMind and quoted as such: "developing evaluations has received little systematic attention compared to developing algorithms" (Balduzzi et al., quoted p. 116).

### AI as a judge — method, with its conditions
The method: prompt a model to score an output by itself, against a reference response, or against a competing response (three naive prompt templates given, pp. 138–139). A judge prompt should state the task, the criteria, and the scoring system.

**Adoption and correlation, as reported by her:**
- "As of this writing, AI as a judge has become one of the most, if not the most, common methods for evaluating AI models in production" (p. 137). Supporting figure: LangChain's *State of AI* 2023 reported "58% of evaluations on their platform were done by AI judges" (p. 137).
- Zheng et al. (2023) on MT-Bench: "the agreement between GPT-4 and humans reached 85%, which is even higher than the agreement among humans (81%)" (p. 137). AlpacaEval authors (Dubois et al., 2023) "found that their AI judges have a near perfect (0.98) correlation with LMSYS's Chat Arena leaderboard" (p. 137). **Both are reported second-hand and both are benchmark-conditional**; see [zheng-mt-bench-chatbot-arena](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/zheng-mt-bench-chatbot-arena.md).

**The conditions she attaches:**
- **A judge is a system, not a model.** "An AI judge is not just a model—it's a system that includes both a model and a prompt. Altering the model, the prompt, or the model's sampling parameters results in a different judge" (p. 140). Boxed rule at p. 143: "Do not trust any AI judge if you can't see the model and the prompt used for the judge."
- **Scores are not portable between judges.** "It's essential to remember that AI as a judge criteria aren't standardized" (p. 139). She demonstrates it with a table (p. 142) showing MLflow, Ragas and LlamaIndex all shipping a built-in `faithfulness` criterion on scales of 1–5, 0/1, and YES/NO respectively, and asks: "If, given a (context, answer) pair, MLflow gives a faithfulness score of 3, Ragas outputs 1, and LlamaIndex outputs NO, which score would you use?"
- **Consistency is buyable and does not buy accuracy.** "Zheng et al. (2023) showed that including evaluation examples in the prompt can increase the consistency of GPT-4 from 65% to 77.5%. However, they acknowledged that high consistency may not imply high accuracy—the judge might consistently make the same mistakes." The cost is recorded in the same paragraph: "including more examples in their prompts caused their GPT-4 spending to quadruple" (p. 142).
- **Scoring-system shape matters, and she hedges the evidence.** "It's been reported that AI judges work better with classification than with numerical scoring systems. For numerical scoring systems, discrete scoring seems to work better than continuous scoring. Empirically, the wider the range for discrete scoring, the worse the model seems to get. Typical discrete scoring systems are between 1 and 5" (p. 140, boxed). The hedges — "it's been reported", "seems to" — are hers and should not be dropped.
- **Named biases, with figures attributed:** self-bias, where in Zheng et al.'s 2023 experiment "GPT-4 favors itself with a 10% higher win rate, while Claude-v1 favors itself with a 25% higher win rate"; first-position bias, "the opposite of that of humans", who show recency bias; verbosity bias, where Wu and Aji (2023) "found that both GPT-4 and Claude-1 prefer longer responses (~100 words) with factual errors over shorter, correct responses (~50 words)" (pp. 144–145). She notes GPT-4 is less prone to verbosity bias than GPT-3.5, "suggesting that this bias might go away as models become stronger" — a hedge, not a prediction.
- **Cost arithmetic.** "If you use GPT-4 to both generate and evaluate responses, you'll do twice as many GPT-4 calls, approximately doubling your API costs. If you have three evaluation prompts… you'll increase your number of API calls four times" (p. 144). Mitigations: weaker judges, and spot-checking, with its stated trade: "Spot-checking means you might fail to catch some failures. The larger the percentage of samples you evaluate, the more confidence you will have in your evaluation results, but also the higher the costs" (p. 144).
- **The standing caveat.** "AI judges should be supplemented with exact evaluation methods and/or human evaluation" (p. 145). Note the disjunction: *and/or*, not *and*.

One sentence is worth quoting whenever the method is being defended: "Even when AI judgments aren't as good as human judgments, they might still be good enough to guide an application's development and provide sufficient confidence to get a project off the ground" (p. 137).

### Comparative evaluation — method, with its conditions
Used for ranking rather than scoring. "For responses whose quality is subjective, comparative evaluation is typically easier to do than pointwise evaluation" (p. 149). Three stated limitations:
- **Scalability.** "The number of model pairs to compare grows quadratically with the number of models. In January 2024, LMSYS evaluated 57 models using 244,000 comparisons… this averages only 153 comparisons per model pair" (p. 152). Ranking algorithms lean on transitivity, and "it's unclear if this transitivity assumption holds for AI models" (p. 152).
- **Quality control.** "Among 33,000 prompts published by LMSYS Chatbot Arena in 2023, 180 of them are 'hello' and 'hi', which account for 0.55% of the data" (p. 154). "Simple prompts are easy to respond to, making it hard to differentiate models' performance."
- **It does not convert to absolute performance.** "Comparative evaluation tells us which model is better. It doesn't tell us how good a model is or whether this model is good enough for our use case" (p. 154). She gives the worked case: a model resolving 70% of support tickets, against a challenger that wins 51% of head-to-heads, and no way to convert the win rate into a resolution rate — "Several people have told me that in their experience, a 1% change in the win rate can induce a huge performance boost in some applications but just a minimal boost in other applications" (p. 155).

The compensating property is stated as a durable one: "Unlike benchmarks that become useless when model performance achieves perfect scores, comparative evaluations will never get saturated as long as newer, stronger models are introduced" (p. 155). This is the same ranking apparatus critiqued in [singh-leaderboard-illusion](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/singh-leaderboard-illusion.md), and the two should be read against each other.

### Public benchmarks — what they can and cannot do
- Scale: "Google's BIG-bench (2022) alone has 214 benchmarks"; EleutherAI's `lm-evaluation-harness` "supports over 400 benchmarks" and OpenAI's `evals` "approximately 500" (p. 191).
- Leaderboards are compute-constrained, not comprehensive: HELM Lite "left out an information retrieval benchmark (MS MARCO…) because it's expensive to run"; Hugging Face "opted out of HumanEval due to its large compute requirements". "A small set of benchmarks is not nearly enough to represent the vast capabilities and different failure modes of foundation models" (p. 192).
- Running them yourself is costly: "Stanford spent approximately $80,000–$100,000 to evaluate 30 models on their full HELM suite" (p. 197).
- Contamination is assumed, not suspected: "they are likely contaminated" (p. 197). Two detection heuristics are given with their trade — n-gram overlap is "more accurate but can be time-consuming and expensive to run… It's also impossible without access to the training data", while perplexity is "less accurate but much less resource-intensive" (pp. 198–199). Evidence: OpenAI, analysing GPT-3, "found 13 benchmarks with at least 40% in the training data" (Brown et al., 2020, reported p. 199). And Schaeffer's 2023 satirical paper *Pretraining on the Test Set Is All You Need*, in which "his one-million-parameter model was able to achieve near-perfect scores and outperformed much larger models on all these benchmarks" (p. 197).
- The conclusion she draws is narrow and quotable: "Public benchmarks will help you filter out bad models, but they won't help you find the best models for your application" (p. 200). And: "Just because a model can achieve high performance on bar exams doesn't mean it's good at giving legal advice" (p. 198).
- On multiple-choice benchmarks specifically: "In April 2024, 75% of the tasks in Eleuther's `lm-evaluation-harness` are multiple-choice", and "a model's performance on MCQs can vary with small changes in how the questions and the options are presented" (p. 162).

### The evaluation pipeline — three steps, with the sample-size arithmetic
**Step 1, evaluate every component.** "You should evaluate the end-to-end output and each component's intermediate output independently… If you don't evaluate each component independently, you don't know exactly where your system fails" (p. 200). Then per-turn and per-task: "Given that what users really care about is whether a model can help them accomplish their tasks, task-based evaluation is more important", with the stated difficulty that "it can be hard to determine the boundaries between tasks" (p. 201).

**Step 2, write the guideline.** "Creating a clear evaluation guideline is the most important step of the evaluation pipeline. An ambiguous guideline leads to ambiguous scores that can be misleading. If you don't know what bad responses look like, you won't be able to catch them" (p. 202). She reports LinkedIn's finding that "the first hurdle was in creating an evaluation guideline. *A correct response is not always a good response*", with their Job Assessment example: "You are a terrible fit" is correct and useless (p. 202). LangChain's *State of AI 2023*: "on average, their users used 2.3 different types of feedback (criteria) to evaluate an application" (p. 202). Rubrics must be human-tested: "Validate your rubric with humans: yourself, coworkers, friends, etc. If humans find it hard to follow the rubric, you need to refine it to make it unambiguous" (p. 203).

The step also requires tying evaluation metrics to business metrics, with her illustrative mapping (p. 203): factual consistency of 80% automates 30% of customer support requests, 90% automates 50%, 98% automates 90%. **These numbers are hers as an illustration of the shape of such a mapping, not as measurements**, and must not be reproduced as findings. The transferable idea is the *usefulness threshold*: "what scores must an application achieve for it to be useful?"

**Step 3, methods and data.** Slice the evaluation data — by tier, traffic source, usage, known-failure sets, out-of-scope sets — partly to avoid Simpson's paradox, for which she supplies a worked table (p. 205). "If you care about something, put a test set on it" (p. 206).

On how much data: the reliability check is a bootstrap. "Draw 100 samples, with replacement, from the original 100 evaluation examples", evaluate, repeat; "If you get 90% on one bootstrap but 70% on another bootstrap, your evaluation pipeline isn't that trustworthy" (p. 206). For detecting a difference between two systems she reproduces OpenAI's rough table (p. 207) — ~10 samples for a 30% difference, ~100 for 10%, ~1,000 for 3%, ~10,000 for 1% — with the rule "for every 3× decrease in score difference, the number of samples needed increases 10×", and with the condition stated first: "In theory, a statistical significance test can be used to compute the sample size needed for a certain level of confidence… However, in reality, it's hard to know the true score distribution." Reference points in the same passage: the median benchmark in `lm-evaluation-harness` has 1,000 examples and the mean 2,159; the Inverse Scaling prize organisers "suggested that 300 examples is the absolute minimum and they would prefer at least 1,000" (p. 207). This is the same problem [miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md) treats properly, and the two belong together.

**Evaluate the evaluation pipeline.** Four questions (pp. 207–208): is it giving the right signals; how reliable is it on repeat runs, for which "if you use an AI judge, make sure to set your judge's temperature to 0"; how correlated are the metrics, since "if two metrics are perfectly correlated, you don't need both of them"; and what does it cost. Plus experiment tracking: "log all variables that could change in an evaluation process, including but not limited to the evaluation data, the rubric, and the prompt and sampling configurations used for the AI judges" (p. 208).

### Criteria, and how factual consistency is actually measured
Four buckets: "domain-specific capability, generation capability, instruction-following capability, and cost and latency" (p. 161). Factual consistency is split into **local** ("The output is evaluated against a context") and **global** ("The output is evaluated against open knowledge") (p. 165), with the honest difficulty stated: "Often, the hardest part of factual consistency verification is determining what the facts are" (p. 165). Methods named: general AI judges; self-verification via SelfCheckGPT, which "works but can be prohibitively expensive, as it requires many AI queries to evaluate a response"; knowledge-augmented verification via DeepMind's SAFE; and specialised entailment scorers such as a 184-million-parameter DeBERTa model (pp. 166–169). One boxed caution belongs in any teaching use: "How well a model performs depends on the quality of its instructions… When a model performs poorly, it can either be because the model is bad or the instruction is bad" (p. 173).

### Retrieval, inference and production measurement
- **Retrieval.** Context precision ("Out of all the documents retrieved, what percentage is relevant to the query?") and context recall ("Out of all the documents that are relevant to the query, what percentage is retrieved?"), with the practical asymmetry recorded: "In production, some RAG frameworks only support context precision, not context recall", because recall requires annotating every document in the database against every query (p. 264). A RAG system should be evaluated in three places — retrieval quality, final outputs, and the embeddings themselves (p. 266). BEIR is named as the retrieval evaluation harness (p. 266); see [thakur-beir](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/thakur-beir.md).
- **Inference.** Latency decomposes as "TTFT + TPOT × (number of output tokens)" (p. 413). "Because latency is a distribution, the average can be misleading" — her worked example has nine requests near 100 ms, one at 3,000 ms, and a mean of 390 ms — so "it's more helpful to look at latency in percentiles", typically p50, p90, p95, p99 (p. 414). *Goodput* "measures the number of requests per second that satisfies the SLO" (p. 415). She is scathing about the default utilisation metric: "actively processing tasks doesn't mean doing so efficiently… `nvidia-smi`'s GPU optimization metric is, therefore, not very useful" (p. 416), and offers MFU and MBU instead, with the closing condition: "A higher utilization rate means nothing if the cost and latency both increase" (p. 419).
- **Production.** Tracing is a design requirement, not an add-on: "Ideally, you should be able to trace each query's transformation step-by-step through the system. If a query fails, you should be able to pinpoint the exact step where it went wrong" (p. 470). She cites Shankar et al. (2024) for the criteria-drift mechanism directly: developers' "perceptions of what constitutes good and bad outputs change as they interact with more data" (p. 470); see [shankar-evalgen](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/shankar-evalgen.md). Three drift classes are named: system prompt changes, user behaviour changes, and "underlying model changes", for which she reports Voiceflow's "10% performance drop when switching from the older GPT-3.5-turbo-0301 to the newer GPT-3.5-turbo-1106" alongside GoDaddy's improvement on the same migration (pp. 196, 472). LinkedIn is cited as manually evaluating "up to 500 daily conservations [sic] with their AI systems" (p. 204).
- **User feedback biases**, which bear directly on any human-rating scheme: leniency bias, with Uber's 2015 average driver rating of 4.8 and deactivation risk below 4.6; randomness; position bias; and preference bias including verbosity and recency (pp. 490–491). Plus degenerate feedback loops and sycophancy, citing Sharma et al. (2023): models trained on human feedback "are more likely to present user responses matching this user's view" (p. 492). And the constraint that limits all of it: "user feedback is incomplete. You only get feedback on what you show users" (p. 491).

### Data quantity, for the finetuning decision
"In the vast majority of cases, you should see improvements after finetuning with 50–100 examples", and "If no improvement is observed with small data, a bigger dataset will rarely do the trick" — though she immediately qualifies that hyperparameters, data quality and prompts can all be the cause instead (p. 374). The transferable technique is the performance-gain curve: finetune on 25%, 50% and 100% subsets and read the slope (p. 375). Diminishing returns are stated as typical, not universal (p. 376).

## Method and evidence base
This is a practitioner synthesis, but it is a **heavily citational** one, and the placeholder undersold that. The epilogue gives the accounting: "more than 150,000 words, 160 illustrations, 250 footnotes, and 975 reference links" (p. 495). The preface gives the interview base: "During the process of writing this book, I used notes from over 100 conversations and interviews, including researchers from major AI labs (OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, …), framework developers… executives and heads of AI/data at companies of different sizes, product managers, community researchers, and independent application developers" (p. xii). Those interviews are unattributed and unquotable, and a large number of the book's most useful sentences begin "several people have told me" or "many teams" — which is testimony, not evidence, and she does not pretend otherwise.

She does present **some original descriptive data**: an analysis of the top 1,000 AI repositories on GitHub by stars, used to show that evaluation tooling lags modelling and orchestration tooling (pp. 38, 116–118), with her method stated in a footnote — keyword search on "LLM", "GPT", "generative" and "transformer" over repositories with at least 500 stars, plus crowdsourced additions (p. 116 n.5). That is a convenience sample of a proxy, and she frames it as one: "Given the prevalence of GitHub, I believe this data is a good proxy for understanding the ecosystem" (p. 38).

Everything else quantitative in the evaluation chapters is **reported from a named third party** — Zheng et al., Wu and Aji, Saito et al., Dubois et al., Chen et al., Brown et al., LMSYS, LangChain, LinkedIn, OpenAI, Stanford's HELM. The practical consequence for this repository is unchanged from the placeholder's conclusion, and the placeholder had this right: **cite the book for framing, vocabulary and the shape of a method; cite the underlying paper for any number.** The difference is that the book now tells us exactly which paper, so the trace is cheap.

One further property is worth recording because it is a virtue rather than a limitation. The book routinely states the objection to its own recommendations in the same breath as the recommendation: evaluation-driven development gets the lamppost objection (p. 161); AI-as-a-judge gets six pages of limitations against four of advantages (pp. 137–148); comparative evaluation gets "From comparative performance to absolute performance" (p. 154). Where the argument is her opinion she marks it — "I believe", "I'm especially excited", "I hope", "I doubt" — and where she is repeating hearsay she marks that too. That is a citable model of the disposition this programme is trying to teach.

## Vocabulary the source introduces
**Attribution caution retained, and now sharpened.** Most of these terms are standard in contemporary practice and not coined by this book. Two exceptions are flagged. The definitions below are the book's, corrected where the placeholder's guesses diverged.
- **AI engineering** — "the process of building applications with readily available foundation models" (jacket copy; developed pp. 12–14, 39–46). Distinguished from ML engineering by adaptation-over-training, compute pressure, and the difficulty of evaluating open-ended output — **not** by being a separate discipline.
- **Evaluation-driven development** — "defining evaluation criteria before building" (p. 160). **She names this herself** and states the analogy to test-driven development. The clearest candidate in the book for a term it originates.
- **AI as a judge / LLM as a judge / AI judge** — "The approach of using AI to evaluate AI… An AI model that is used to evaluate other AI models is called an AI judge" (p. 136). Note that *model-based evaluation*, which the placeholder used, is **not** her term.
- **Model adaptation** — divided into exactly two categories "depending on whether they require updating model weights": prompt-based techniques, which "adapt a model without updating the model weights", and finetuning, which "requires updating model weights" (p. 40). Retrieval and context construction do not update weights, so by that criterion they fall on the prompt-based side of the split rather than on a rung between it and finetuning.
- **Exact versus subjective evaluation** — "Exact evaluation produces judgment without ambiguity" (p. 125); subjective evaluation "can change based on the judge model and the prompt". This is the organising distinction of Chapter 3.
- **Goodput** — "the number of requests per second that satisfies the SLO", explicitly "adapted from networking for LLM applications" (p. 415).
- **Context precision and context recall** — retrieval-quality metrics; context precision "is also called context relevance" (p. 264).
- **Time to first token (TTFT), time per output token (TPOT), time to publish** — latency decomposition for streaming generation; *time to publish* is offered as the honest metric where a system generates intermediate tokens the user never sees (p. 413).
- **MFU and MBU** — Model FLOP/s Utilisation and Model Bandwidth Utilisation, offered against `nvidia-smi`'s GPU utilisation, which "is, therefore, not very useful" (pp. 416–417). MFU is credited to the PaLM paper (p. 417 n.11).
- **Degenerate feedback loop** — "when the predictions themselves influence the feedback, which, in turn, influences the next iteration of the model, amplifying initial biases" (p. 491).

## What to remember
- Evaluation is given two chapters and stated as the bottleneck three times at three different strengths. Use the hedge that matches the claim being made: "can take up the majority of the development effort" (p. 113), "one of the biggest blocks to AI adoption" (p. 208), or "I believe that evaluation is the biggest bottleneck" (p. 161).
- **Evaluation-driven development means defining the criteria before building**, and she supplies the objection to it herself.
- **An AI judge is a model plus a prompt plus sampling parameters.** Change any of the three and it is a different judge, and its scores are no longer comparable with the previous ones — nor with any other tool's score for the same-named criterion.
- **The evaluation pipeline should change more slowly than the application.** Iterate it, but expect stability from it; otherwise the results cannot be used to steer development.
- **Public benchmarks filter out bad models; they do not find good ones for your application**, and contamination should be assumed.
- **Comparative rankings do not convert to absolute performance.** A 51% win rate does not tell you how many tickets get resolved.
- Sample size has arithmetic: roughly 100 examples to detect a 10% difference and 10,000 to detect 1%, with a bootstrap as the cheap reliability check on whatever set you have.
- Cost and latency are evaluation criteria, not deployment concerns — and evaluating with a strong judge roughly doubles inference spend before you have added a second criterion.

## Why it matters for the course
The course needs one text a student can read end to end and come away with a coherent model of the whole system, and the individual research papers cannot do that: Miller is about standard errors, Thakur about retrieval generalisation, Shankar about criteria drift, Singh about leaderboard governance. This book can, and its particular value is that it makes evaluation structural rather than topical — not because it says so, but because it spends two chapters, most of Chapter 10 and returning passages in Chapters 6, 7, 8 and 9 on it, and says explicitly where each of those returns happens (p. 209).

It also earns a second role the placeholder did not anticipate. Because almost every number in it is reported from a named source, **the book is a citation map** for the rest of this folder: Zheng, Thakur, Shankar, Rafailov, Ouyang, Malkov and Yashunin, Jégou, Robertson and Zaragoza are all cited in it, and in several cases the passage citing them is a better one-paragraph statement of why the paper matters than the paper's own abstract. A student who reads the book acquires both the vocabulary and the reasons to read the papers.

The honest division of labour across this folder is unchanged: the book organises, and the papers substantiate. [Husain](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/husain-your-ai-product-needs-evals.md) supplies the consulting-side workflow, [Shankar](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/shankar-evalgen.md) the human-in-the-loop mechanics, [Miller](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md) the statistics the book's sample-size table only gestures at, [Zheng](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/zheng-mt-bench-chatbot-arena.md) the judge methodology whose headline figures the book quotes, [Singh](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/singh-leaderboard-illusion.md) the governance critique of the comparative leaderboards the book treats sympathetically, and [Thakur](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/thakur-beir.md) the retrieval-specific evidence.

One further use is specific to this repository. The book is now the clearest available worked example of an author **stating the strength of her own claims as she makes them** — opinion marked as opinion, hearsay marked as hearsay, figures attributed, objections conceded in the same paragraph. That is precisely the disposition the assessment architecture is trying to produce, and it is easier to show than to describe.

## How to teach it
- **Structural use:** it is a defensible cover-to-cover assignment for the systems half of the course, with Chapters 3 and 4 as the compulsory core and the papers assigned against specific weeks. It is a commercial book, so see the cost caution below.
- **Lecture point:** the three enumerated differences between AI engineering and ML engineering (pp. 39–40). Teach all three, including "AI engineering evolved out of ML engineering", because the exaggerated version — that this is a wholly new discipline — is the one students will meet in marketing material.
- **Lecture point:** an AI judge is a model plus a prompt plus sampling parameters (p. 140), and therefore a versioned artefact. Pair it with the `faithfulness` table at p. 142, which shows three tools shipping incomparable scores under one name.
- **Lecture point:** the stability requirement (pp. 143, 208). Ask what it means for a team that changes its judge prompt in the same sprint as its retrieval strategy.
- **Lab exercise:** run the bootstrap reliability check from p. 206 on an evaluation set the students have built themselves, and have them report whether their set is large enough for the difference they are claiming.
- **Lab exercise:** take one application and write the p. 203 business-metric mapping for it — which score threshold unlocks which automated share — and defend the usefulness threshold. Make clear that her figures are an illustrative shape, not measurements.
- **Lab exercise:** reproduce the criteria-ambiguity finding. Give students one (context, answer) pair and the three `faithfulness` prompts at pp. 142–143, and have them reconcile a 3, a 1, and a NO.
- **Assessment idea:** ask students to write the evaluation section of a design document specifying criteria, scoring rubric with worked examples, methods per criterion, data slices, sample size with its justification, and the cost of running it — that is Chapter 4's Step 2 and Step 3 turned into a specification.
- **Assessment idea:** give students a claim drawn from the book and require them to classify it as her measurement, her opinion, or a figure she is reporting, and to trace the reported ones to the originating paper. The book is unusually good raw material for this because it makes the classification possible.
- **Caution when teaching:** do not lift the illustrative numbers at p. 203, and do not reproduce her reported figures — the 85% GPT-4/human agreement, the 58% AI-judge share, the 10% and 25% self-bias rates — without the originating citation and its conditions.

## Limits and cautions
- **It is a practitioner synthesis and should not be cited as evidence for an empirical claim.** Every number in the evaluation chapters is either reported from a named third party or offered as an illustration. Trace it or drop it.
- **It dates itself deliberately, and quickly.** The phrase "as of this writing" recurs throughout, and the tables are timestamped in their captions — the built-in AI-judge criteria table is labelled "as of September 2024. Note that as these tools evolve, these built-in criteria will change" (p. 139). Treat the tool tables, model comparisons, benchmark lists and API-provider discussion as `platform`-namespace content with a decay measured in months, even though the note as a whole is `measurement`-namespace. See [concepts/namespaces.md](/concepts/namespaces.md).
- **The interview base is unattributed.** "Several people have told me", "many teams", "a friend quipped" — these are the load-bearing citations for several of the book's most quotable lines, including the claim that a 1% win-rate change can mean anything (p. 155). They are testimony. They cannot be checked and should not be presented to students as findings.
- **Breadth is bought at the cost of depth, and the sample-size passage is the clearest instance.** The OpenAI table at p. 207 is a rule of thumb offered without a stated error model, immediately after conceding that the true score distribution is unknown. [Miller](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/miller-adding-error-bars-to-evals.md) does this properly, and the book should not displace it. The same holds for retrieval generalisation against [Thakur](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/thakur-beir.md), for rater cognition against the assessment literature, and for leaderboard governance against [Singh](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/singh-leaderboard-illusion.md), whose critique the book's sympathetic treatment of comparative leaderboards does not anticipate.
- **Illustrative numbers read as measurements.** The factual-consistency-to-automation mapping at p. 203 is the most dangerous passage in the book for this repository's purposes, because its form is exactly that of a finding. It is not one. If it is reproduced anywhere, it must carry the word *illustrative*.
- **As a copyrighted commercial work, nothing beyond fair-dealing quotation with attribution may be reproduced.** The quotations in this note are at that limit; do not extend them.
- **Assigning a book has a cost to students.** If the course cannot require purchase, the openly available sources in this folder carry part of the same load at no cost — but only part. [Husain](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/husain-your-ai-product-needs-evals.md) does not cover inference metrics, benchmark contamination, sample size, or the build-versus-buy analysis, and no free source in this folder covers all four. The book should then be listed as optional with those four gaps named.

#### Contradicted or materially overstated
1. **"AI engineering is a distinct discipline rather than a subset of either software engineering or machine learning research."** The book argues close to the opposite. "AI engineering evolved out of ML engineering" (p. 36); the roles "have significant overlap" (p. 36); "much of what ML engineers have learned and shared over the last decade is still applicable" (p. 39); and there is a section titled "AI Engineering Versus Full-Stack Engineering" (p. 46) whose existence refutes the "neither of the above" framing. Her claim is a *difference in emphasis along three named axes*, not a claim of disciplinary independence. **A lecture built on the placeholder's version would have attributed to her the marketing claim she is implicitly arguing against.**

2. **"the ordering of adaptation techniques by cost and reversibility. Prompt and context engineering come before retrieval augmentation, which comes before finetuning, which comes before anything involving training from scratch."** Three errors in one sentence. (a) The book's taxonomy is **binary**, split on a single criterion: "model adaptation techniques can be divided into two categories, depending on whether they require updating model weights" (p. 40). (b) **Reversibility is not one of her criteria**; hers are cost, data requirement and complexity. (c) **Retrieval is not a rung between prompting and finetuning.** Retrieval does not update weights, so by her own criterion it falls on the prompt-based side of the split rather than between the two sides; and the book carries a section titled "Finetuning and RAG" (p. 316, title read from the table of contents, page not read), which at minimum indicates the relation is treated as a comparison rather than as a fixed order. Training from scratch is outside the book's scope. The one thing the placeholder got right here is the direction: "You should make the most out of prompting before moving to more resource-intensive techniques like finetuning" (p. 211).

3. **"Model-based judges answer whether an open-ended output meets a criterion at a scale humans cannot sustain, but only after being validated against human labels."** The final clause is **not in the book, and the book states the contrary permission explicitly**: "Even when AI judgments aren't as good as human judgments, they might still be good enough to guide an application's development and provide sufficient confidence to get a project off the ground" (p. 137). Her guardrails are entirely different in kind — see the judge's model and prompt (p. 143), fix the sampling temperature at 0 (p. 207), never compare scores across judges (p. 139), supplement with exact **and/or** human evaluation (p. 145), and evaluate the evaluation pipeline itself (pp. 207–208). **This is the most consequential single correction in this note.** The human-validation precondition is [Zheng's](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/zheng-mt-bench-chatbot-arena.md) and [Shankar's](/research/03-measurement-evaluation/shankar-evalgen.md); the placeholder imported it from adjacent notes and attributed it to Huyen. That is the characteristic failure of writing a note about an unread book from its neighbours.

4. **"evaluation moves from a project phase to a continuous requirement"**, and the corresponding takeaway, "Evaluation is not a phase. In this setting it is the only way to know whether any change helped, which makes it continuous." The first half is supported; the word *continuous* inverts her strongest structural claim about pipelines. "An application evolves over time, but the way it's evaluated ideally should be fixed" (p. 143). "While iteration is necessary, you should be able to expect a certain level of consistency from your evaluation pipeline. If the evaluation process changes constantly, you won't be able to use the evaluation results to guide your application's development" (p. 208). What is continuous is **running** the evaluation; what must be stable is **the evaluation**. The placeholder collapsed the two, and a team taught the collapsed version would rewrite its judge prompts as freely as its application prompts — precisely the failure she describes at p. 143.

#### Asserted but not addressed by the book
- **"The system's behaviour is a distribution, and a distribution can only be compared to another distribution through measurement."** This argument does not appear. It is not contradicted; it is simply not hers. Her nearest move is the opposite in spirit — she asks how many samples you need before a difference is real (p. 207) and how to tell whether your own pipeline is trustworthy (p. 206), which treats the measurement as the thing in doubt rather than the thing that settles doubt.
- **"Evaluation infrastructure and production infrastructure are the same infrastructure."** Not stated. She distinguishes experimentation from production evaluation explicitly — "During experimentation, you might have reference data… whereas, in production, reference data might not be immediately available" (p. 204) — while insisting both are required.
- **"students arriving with a research background tend to reach for finetuning first"** — a pedagogical claim with no warrant in the book, which offers "Reasons Not to Finetune" (p. 312) without characterising who needs them. It survives as this note's own judgement, unwarranted.
- **"Model-based evaluation"** as a term of art. Not her vocabulary; she uses *AI as a judge*, *LLM as a judge*, *AI judge* (p. 136).
- **Publisher and year.** "O'Reilly Media, 2025" is defensible from the copyright line but the edition is dated December 2024. Corrected above rather than treated as an error.

#### What this episode says about the placeholder practice
The placeholder did its job: it was labelled, it was quarantined, it told the reader not to trust it, and it specified what would discharge it. Nothing in it leaked into the wiki, because nothing in the wiki cites it. **The practice worked.** But the failure mode it exhibits is worth naming, because it is not "the note was vague" — it was specific, confident and mostly correct, which is what made the four errors survivable long enough to matter. Each error made the book **tidier** than it is: a clean disciplinary boundary where the author draws a fuzzy one, a four-rung ladder where she draws a two-way split, a hard precondition where she offers a permission, and a continuous process where she demands a stable one. A reconstruction assembled from a folder's other notes will inherit that folder's tidiness. **The tell is not implausibility; it is excess coherence.**
