## What this source is
The author's own introduction to the concept he originated, written thirteen years after proposing it and covering the rationale, the relationship to competency frameworks, the theory of entrustment decisions, the supervision scale, and a five-step method for building a workplace curriculum. It is a primer rather than a research report, and it is the best single open-access statement of what an EPA is, what it is not, and how to implement one.

## The argument in full
Ten Cate situates EPAs in a long institutional history. Nineteenth-century national regulation of medicine gave way to explicit conceptions of competence; postgraduate training and educational objectives developed after the Second World War; and in the 1990s and early twenty-first century, outcome-based and competency-based education were embraced through frameworks such as CanMEDS and the ACGME competencies. The pressure that produced EPAs came from the Royal Colleges, who "became uneasy with the model in which a fixed time in training would automatically lead to a license to practice, rather than a rigorous evaluation of the competence of the trainee." The stated goal was to move from time-based to competency-based medical education, and the 2010 Carnegie Report reinforced this by proposing fixed standards with flexible pathways. But competency frameworks drew criticism from clinician educators and educational scholars for being abstract — a list of domains such as communicator, collaborator, and professional does not tell a supervisor what to let a trainee do tomorrow morning.

EPAs were proposed in 2005 as the bridge across that gap, and ten Cate notes that "a little over 10 years later hundreds of publications have appeared." The definition is deliberately concrete: "EPAs are the units of professional practice that constitute what clinicians do as daily work." They "can be conceived of the responsibilities or tasks that must be done in patient care," and "typically EPAs are activities with a beginning and an end and are only entrusted to trained personnel." His examples span the training arc — an ICU resident evaluating a deteriorating patient after a nurse's night call; a junior obstetrics resident entrusted with an uncomplicated delivery; a senior medical student examining a patient with a known chronic condition, ordering tests, and preparing follow-up medication with only a sign-off remaining; and even a junior medical student given "small but significant tasks that do not have to be checked if the student has been trained well."

The distinction between EPAs and competencies is the conceptual core, and ten Cate states it more sharply than most secondary sources do. "EPAs constitute the description of work and are independent of persons. They operationally define a profession." By contrast, "competencies describe persons. Learners who become competent professionals must acquire competencies that include knowledge, skills, and attitudes." The consequence is the line worth memorizing: "**Professionals can possess competencies; they can never possess EPAs.**" The two are related through an integration requirement — "EPAs require multiple competencies in learners that must be applied in an integrative fashion. Even a simple task as taking a patient's history combines several domains of competence." The paper illustrates this with a matrix crossing CanMEDS domains against six example EPAs, marking each cell as necessary or merely useful. The examples are performing a venipuncture, performing an appendectomy, signover at morning report after a night shift, developing and implementing a patient management plan, chairing a multidisciplinary meeting, and requesting an organ donation. The pattern the matrix demonstrates is that different activities recruit different combinations of competence: venipuncture draws on medical expert and communicator, while chairing a multidisciplinary meeting draws on collaborator, leader, health advocate, and professional.

Assessment in this framework takes the form of an entrustment decision: "the decision to transfer a responsibility to a learner has been called an entrustment decision." Ten Cate separates two kinds. **Ad-hoc entrustment decisions** happen constantly and informally, whenever a supervisor "feels the skills of the learner at that time match the complexity of the patient and the risks in doing this are acceptable." **Summative entrustment decisions** "have the nature of certification" and are "decisions taken for the future responsibilities of the learner from that moment on," sometimes formalized as a Statement of Awarded Responsibility. Because summative entrustment is granted per EPA at whatever moment the evidence supports it, "EPA-based curricula can be truly competency-based and not just time-based" — and he notes that "programs that deeply invest in training, simulation, supervision and frequent feedback may result is marked shortening of training."

The analysis of what actually drives ad-hoc entrustment is the most transferable part of the paper. Five factors are identified: perceived learner features, the supervisor's propensity to delegate, the complexity of the EPA, the context, and the nature of the relationship between clinician and learner. Learner features include specific skills and experience but also three professionalism characteristics — **integrity**, meaning "truthfulness in reports to a supervising clinician and benevolence"; **reliability**, meaning "stable, predictable behavior and a conscientious way of working"; and **humility**, meaning "the ability to observe own limitations and willingness ask for help when needed" — plus knowing how to handle unfamiliar situations. Supervisors "can differ vastly in how easy they delegate," depending on their own expertise, experience, and personality. Rare and high-risk activities are entrusted later. Context includes "complexity of the patient, facilities that are available, the time of the day or night, the need for workforce but also the rules about supervision, working hour restrictions, and a perceived 9-5 working mentality of learners." And the relationship "must have evolved beyond first impressions to allow critical entrustment decisions," a dynamic he describes as "a dance that takes place between senior resident and supervising clinician when residents approach the completion of training."

Summative entrustment demands more rigour than ad-hoc entrustment. It "should include an agreement between multiple observers that this entrustment is justified, to avoid subjective bias, and multiple observations," with recommended sources being "multiple direct observations, longitudinal observations, case-based discussions, and product evaluations," supplemented by knowledge and skills examination scores. Ten Cate is explicit that "an entrustment decision implies the acceptance of risk, as it can never be fully predicted what the student will encounter." His mechanism for probing that risk is the **entrustment-based discussion**: "short focused conversations usually after an EPA, [that] should probe the student's deep understanding of what was done, but should include 'what-if' questions, to grasp whether the learner would know what to do what unexpected or familiar situations would arise."

The supervision scale gives entrustment its measurement form. Five levels run from observation to supervising others, and the paper also presents an expanded version with sub-levels that distinguish, for example, direct supervision as co-activity from direct supervision with the supervisor merely present, and unsupervised practice with remote monitoring from unsupervised practice without it. Ten Cate reports that in anesthesiology training, entrustability scales "translated to the 9-point scale of the mini Clinical Evaluation Exercise, proved to increase the reliability of assessment," and cites Weller and colleagues' 9-point scale phrased in supervisors' own colloquial language: "I'm not comfortable leaving the operating room; I can take a brief coffee break; I can take a lunch break; I have to check in regularly; I can wait until asked for help; I can limit to preview everything with the trainee; I can be off site; and the trainee can work as a consultant." His comment on it captures why the scale design matters: "it illustrates excellently the core idea of entrustability scales: to align assessment with clinical practice."

The final third is implementation. Ten Cate gives five steps, beginning with identifying EPAs — which "should be not too small and not too big," with "a suitable number [being] 20 to 40 EPAs for a complete program" — and proceeding through writing full EPA descriptions, determining assessment rules, establishing individualized portfolio-based pathways, and allowing flexibility in the length or breadth of training. The EPA description template is specific enough to use directly and is summarized below. The concluding survey reports EPAs documented in psychiatry, pediatrics, internal medicine, family medicine, anesthesiology, and emergency medicine; undergraduate EPAs as "a reality in the United States and in Canada"; and adoption beyond medicine in "nursing, physician assistant training, veterinary medicine, and midwifery," with "even such remote programs as teacher training [having] shown interest."

## Key claims, findings, and specifics
- EPAs were introduced in **2005**; by 2018 "hundreds of publications have appeared."
- Definition: "the units of professional practice that constitute what clinicians do as daily work," with a beginning and an end, entrusted only to trained personnel.
- **"Professionals can possess competencies; they can never possess EPAs."** EPAs describe work and are independent of persons; competencies describe persons.
- **Two kinds of entrustment decision:** ad-hoc (daily, informal, contextual) and summative (certification-like, forward-looking), the latter sometimes formalized as a **Statement of Awarded Responsibility (STAR)**.
- **Five factors driving ad-hoc entrustment:** perceived learner features; the supervisor's propensity to delegate; the complexity of the EPA; the context; the nature of the clinician-learner relationship.
- **Three professionalism characteristics** within learner features: **integrity** (truthfulness and benevolence), **reliability** (stable, predictable, conscientious), **humility** (recognizing one's limits and asking for help).
- **Five levels of supervision:** 1 — present and observing only; 2 — executing with direct, proactive supervision in the room; 3 — executing without a supervisor in the room but quickly available, i.e. indirect reactive supervision; 4 — working unsupervised; 5 — supervising more junior learners.
- **Expanded scale sub-levels** distinguish, among others, level 2a (co-activity with supervisor) from 2b (supervisor in room ready to step in); level 3a (all findings double-checked), 3b (key findings double-checked), and 3c (supervisor distantly available, findings promptly reviewed); and level 4a (remote monitoring) from 4b (no monitoring).
- **Program sizing:** "a suitable number is **20 to 40 EPAs** for a complete program."
- **EPA description template**, six or seven sections, confined to one or two pages: (1) **Title** — recognizable to clinicians and learners, may be a noun or include a verb, "should not contain an adjective," and "should not be phrased as an educational objective—it is just an activity"; (2) **Specification and limitations** — "no more and no less than what is exactly meant," with restrictions such as "only applicable with hemo-dynamically stable patients"; (3) **Link with the prevailing framework of competencies**; (4) **Required knowledge, skills, attitude, and experience**; (5) **Sources of information to support entrustment decisions** — direct observations, longitudinal observations, case-based discussions, product evaluations; (6) **Targeted level of supervision** — "for most EPAs this may be **Level 3 in medical school and Level 4 for postgraduate programs**"; (7) optionally an **expiry date**, because if a learner "has not practiced the EPA for a long time, the entrustment decision may expire."
- **Empirical implementation data** from a physician-assistant program EPA-based since 2010: across **101 graduates** of a **2.5-year** program, students began targeting about **7 EPAs (6.8 on average)**, completed slightly fewer (**6.6 on average**), and made **1.5 altered EPAs** on average.
- **Anesthesiology finding:** entrustability scales mapped onto the 9-point mini-CEX scale "proved to increase the reliability of assessment."
- **Adoption breadth:** psychiatry, pediatrics, internal medicine, family medicine, anesthesiology, emergency medicine; undergraduate EPAs in the US and Canada; nursing, physician assistant training, veterinary medicine, midwifery, and interest from teacher training.

## Method and evidence base
This is a conceptual and practical primer, not an empirical study, and it should be cited as such. Its authority derives from ten Cate's position as the originator of the concept and from its synthesis of a decade of subsequent literature. Empirical claims within it are drawn from other work and cited rather than generated here — the anesthesiology reliability finding, the Weller et al. entrustability scale, the five-factor analysis of ad-hoc entrustment, and the physician-assistant program cohort data are all reported from prior studies. The five-step curriculum-building method is presented as practical guidance grounded in implementation experience rather than as an evaluated intervention. Readers wanting empirical validation of EPA-based assessment must go to the cited literature; readers wanting the concept stated correctly by its author should use this paper.

## Vocabulary the source introduces
- **Entrustable Professional Activity (EPA)** — a unit of professional practice, with a beginning and an end, that constitutes part of the daily work of a profession and can be entrusted to a sufficiently trained individual.
- **Entrustment decision** — the decision to transfer responsibility for an activity to a learner.
- **Ad-hoc entrustment decision** — an informal, in-the-moment judgment that a learner's current skills match the current task's complexity and risk.
- **Summative entrustment decision** — a certification-like judgment about a learner's future responsibilities, made on accumulated evidence by multiple observers.
- **Statement of Awarded Responsibility (STAR)** — a formal record of a summative entrustment decision.
- **Levels of supervision** — the five-point (or expanded) scale from observation only through unsupervised practice to supervising others, used as the assessment scale.
- **Entrustability scale** — a rating scale phrased in terms of supervision required rather than abstract performance quality.
- **Entrustment-based discussion** — a short focused post-activity conversation probing deep understanding, including "what-if" questions about unexpected situations.
- **Expiry date** — an optional attribute of an EPA whereby entrustment lapses if the activity is not practised for an extended period.

## What to remember
- EPAs describe the work, not the worker. Competencies describe the worker. A person can hold competencies but can never hold an EPA — they are entrusted with one.
- The assessment output is a supervision level, not a grade. This is what makes EPA assessment actionable: it answers "what may this person do next?"
- Entrustment is a risk acceptance. Integrity, reliability, and humility are assessed alongside technical skill, and humility is explicitly defined as knowing one's limits and asking for help.
- Summative entrustment requires multiple observers, multiple observations, and multiple evidence types. A single observation is an ad-hoc judgment, not a certification.
- The EPA description template — title, specification and limitations, competency links, prerequisites, evidence sources, target supervision level, optional expiry — is directly reusable.
- Twenty to forty EPAs is the recommended size for a complete program. This is a useful anchor against both over-fragmentation and vagueness.

## Why it matters for the course
This is the assessment architecture the course needs, and it solves a problem that conventional grading cannot. AI systems engineering work is ambiguous, contextual, and consequential in ways that resist decomposition into exam questions: knowing when a retrieval result is untrustworthy, when an eval is underpowered, when to escalate rather than ship. EPAs let the course assess exactly that, because the unit of assessment is a real activity performed under real conditions and the output is a statement about what supervision the student still requires. The five-factor analysis of ad-hoc entrustment maps almost directly onto forward-deployed engineering practice — task complexity, deployment context, and the trust relationship with a client team are precisely the variables that determine what an engineer should be allowed to do unsupervised. The three professionalism characteristics are also unusually well-suited to this domain: integrity in reporting what a system actually does, reliability in following through on measurement discipline, and humility in recognizing when a problem exceeds one's competence are all failure modes with real consequences in AI deployment. Finally, the supervision scale gives the course a graduation criterion that is defensible to an employer in a way that a transcript is not.

## How to teach it
- **Lecture point:** open with the competencies-versus-EPAs distinction and the "can never possess EPAs" line. Students consistently conflate the two, and the distinction is what makes the framework work.
- **Lecture point:** the five levels of supervision as an assessment scale. Contrast with a percentage grade and ask what each communicates to a future employer.
- **Lecture point:** entrustment as risk acceptance, including the integrity–reliability–humility triad, with AI-deployment examples for each.
- **Design exercise:** have students draft an EPA for the course using ten Cate's template, with particular attention to the title rules — no adjectives, not phrased as a learning objective — and to writing meaningful limitations.
- **Design exercise:** build an EPA–competency matrix for the course, crossing candidate EPAs against the course's competency domains, and use the sparsity pattern to check whether the EPA set covers the intended competencies.
- **Assessment idea:** adopt entrustment-based discussions as the oral component. After a student completes a technical activity, a short structured conversation probes their reasoning and includes "what-if" questions about failure modes they did not encounter.
- **Assessment idea:** a portfolio requirement in which each EPA's summative entrustment must be supported by multiple observations from multiple observers across at least two evidence types, mirroring the paper's requirement.
- **Assessment idea:** ask students to write the supervision-level definition for one course EPA in colloquial language, following the Weller et al. anesthesiology example. Making them phrase level 3 as something a real supervisor would actually say is clarifying.

## Limits and cautions
- The framework is from medical education and rests on an apprenticeship structure with continuous supervision by practising professionals. A course does not have an ICU. Any transfer requires deliberate design of an equivalent observation setting, and the mismatch should be stated rather than glossed.
- EPAs do not eliminate subjectivity; they relocate it into an entrustment judgment. The paper's own remedy — multiple observers, multiple observations, agreement to avoid subjective bias — is not optional, and the Govaerts material in this folder documents how variable individual assessor reasoning remains even under such a framework.
- Ad-hoc entrustment is demonstrably influenced by the supervisor's personality and by the supervisor-learner relationship. In a graded course this is an equity risk that must be managed structurally.
- Writing good EPAs is difficult. Ten Cate's warnings about size, adjectives, and objective-phrasing exist because these mistakes are common, and a badly written EPA is worse than no EPA.
- The paper is a primer and carries the authority of its author, not of an empirical study. Effectiveness claims should be sourced to the cited literature.
- This is one of several ten Cate texts. AMEE Guide No. 99 and the 2013 "Nuts and bolts" piece are frequently cited and were not consulted here.
