# Cliff Notes — Adding Error Bars to Evals

## Citation and access
- **Author:** Evan Miller (Anthropic)
- **Title:** Adding Error Bars to Evals: A Statistical Approach to Language Model Evaluations
- **Venue and year:** arXiv preprint 2411.00640, submitted 1 November 2024 (v1); primary category stat.AP, cross-listed cs.CL; 14 pages
- **Source consulted:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00640 and the full HTML text at https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640v1
- **Access status:** open access (full text fetched and read, including all six sections and three appendices)

## What this source is
A short, self-contained statistics tutorial aimed at machine-learning practitioners, arguing that language model evaluations are experiments and should be analyzed as such. It supplies explicit formulas for standard errors, clustered standard errors, paired-difference comparisons, variance reduction, and power analysis, plus concrete recommendations for how eval results should be reported. It is unusual among the sources in this folder in being fully prescriptive and fully derivable: every recommendation comes with an equation and, in the appendices, a derivation.

## The argument in full
Miller opens with a critique of prevailing practice. Evals are run and reported "with a 'highest number is best' mentality; industry practice is to highlight a state-of-the-art (SOTA) result in bold, but not necessarily to test that result for any kind of statistical significance." He notes that Chatbot Arena popularized confidence intervals in its Elo scores, but that "error bars remain noticeably absent from traditional question-and-answer evals," with the Llama 3 technical report a notable recent exception. The diagnosis is that the eval literature has simply not imported the experimental-design literature that other empirical sciences take for granted.

The motivating example is a fictional comparison between two models, "Galleon" and "Dreadnought," on three real evals: MATH, HumanEval, and MGSM. Galleon wins MATH 65.5% to 63.0%; Dreadnought wins HumanEval 83.6% to 87.7% and MGSM 75.3% to 78.0%. A superficial reading gives Dreadnought the win on two of three. Miller carries this example through the entire paper and, by the end, shows that a correct analysis reverses the conclusion: the MATH difference is statistically significant while the HumanEval and MGSM differences are indistinguishable from noise. The pedagogical structure is therefore a single worked example that repeatedly changes answer as each statistical tool is applied.

The analytic framework rests on one conceptual move: suppose the questions in an eval "were drawn at random from a (hypothetical, infinite, unseen) super-population of questions." This "lets us jump 'through the looking glass' of the specific questions that appear in an eval in order to study the underlying skill that the eval is attempting to measure." Each question score decomposes into a conditional mean and a zero-mean random component, which in turn decomposes the total variance of the estimate into the variance of the conditional means (a property of the super-population, and therefore immutable) plus the mean conditional variance (which the practitioner can attack). Under this framing, the Central Limit Theorem gives the standard error directly, and Miller argues that bootstrapping — as used in the OpenAI evals framework — is "unnecessary unless a complicated sampling scheme or estimator is being used." He also flags a concrete error in the Llama 3 report, which "incorrectly estimates all of its standard errors using SE_Bernoulli, even when s_i takes fractional values, such as with an F1 score," making those intervals conservatively too wide, while noting that the UK Inspect framework computes the CLT version correctly.

The clustering section contains the paper's most consequential empirical finding. Many evals draw questions in related groups: DROP, QuAC, RACE, and SQuAD ask multiple questions about the same independently selected passage, and MGSM is the same question translated into many languages. Because question inclusion is non-independent, "a key assumption of the Central Limit Theorem (or a bootstrap) is violated, and so a naive application of Equation 1 will yield inconsistent standard errors." Miller imports clustered standard errors from the social sciences and, critically, reports real numbers on real evals with Anthropic models: the clustered standard error is 3.05 times the naive one on DROP, 1.88 times on MGSM, and 1.10 times on RACE-H. His conclusion is blunt: "failure to adjust standard errors for clustered sampling may lead an unsuspecting analyst to suppose that the measurement of the overall eval score is much more precise than it actually is," and the reading-comprehension confidence intervals in the Llama 3 report "are likely anti-conservative (too narrow)."

Variance reduction gets three strategies and one prohibition. Resampling each question K times reduces the conditional variance by a factor of K, but with diminishing returns once the conditional variance term is small relative to the variance of conditional means; in Miller's worked uniform-difficulty example, going from K=1 to K=2 cuts variance by one third, K=4 by one half, K=6 by five ninths, with an asymptotic ceiling of two thirds. Analyzing next-token probabilities instead of sampled outputs eliminates the conditional variance entirely and immediately attains that two-thirds ceiling, though it only works for evals that do not require chain-of-thought generation. The prohibition is memorably titled "Don't touch the thermostat!" — lowering the sampling temperature to suppress variance is a trap, because it can shift variance from the reducible conditional-variance term into the irreducible variance-of-conditional-means term, or inject bias. His two worked examples show temperature reduction tripling the minimum variance in one case and, in another, both shifting the expected score and increasing the variance of conditional means roughly fivefold.

The comparison section makes the case for paired analysis. When two models are evaluated on the same questions, the unpaired standard error throws away the covariance between them. Because models tend to agree about which questions are easy and which are hard, that covariance is generally positive, so "paired differences represent a 'free' reduction in estimator variance when comparing two models." With a score correlation of 0.5 in his worked example, pairing reduces estimator variance by a third. Miller's reporting recommendation follows directly: technical reports should include not just per-model scores and standard errors but "pairwise differences, pairwise standard errors, and score correlations whenever two or more models are being evaluated," along with the number of questions and, where relevant, the number of clusters. He gives a suggested table format for each.

The final section supplies power analysis, which reframes the entire enterprise from post-hoc reporting to advance planning. Given a significance level, a power level, and a Minimum Detectable Effect, the sample-size formula tells you how many questions an eval needs. Miller's worked example — detecting a 3 percentage-point absolute difference 80 percent of the time at a 5 percent false-positive rate — requires approximately 969 independent questions, from which he draws the practical guideline that "new evals should contain at least 1,000 questions in order to have good signaling ability." Inverting the formula gives the Minimum Detectable Effect for a fixed eval, which lets a practitioner decide in advance whether running the eval can possibly answer the question they care about. A second example shows that with n=198, increasing resampling from K=1 to K=10 reduces the Minimum Detectable Effect from 13.2% to 7.5%. The conclusion states the paper's ambition plainly: that practitioners "will think of their model evaluations as informative experiments rather than a series of contests to produce the largest number."

- 3. Variance reduction — 3.1 Resampling; 3.2 Next-token probabilities; 3.3 "Don't touch the thermostat!"

- 6. Conclusion

- Appendix B: derivation of the sample-size formula
- Appendix C: cluster-adjusted sample-size formula and variance estimation from prior eval data

## Key claims, findings, and specifics
- **The five headline recommendations, verbatim in substance:** (1) compute standard errors of the mean using the Central Limit Theorem; (2) when questions are drawn in related groups, compute clustered standard errors; (3) reduce variance by resampling answers and by analyzing next-token probabilities; (4) when comparing two models, conduct inference on question-level paired differences rather than population-level summary statistics; (5) use power analysis to determine whether an eval or a random subsample can test the hypothesis of interest.
- **Real measured clustering effect:** on Anthropic models, clustered standard errors were **3.05×** the naive value on DROP, **1.88×** on MGSM, and **1.10×** on RACE-H. These are described as non-fictional numbers.
- Evals with clustered questions named explicitly: DROP, QuAC, RACE, SQuAD (multiple questions per passage) and MGSM (one question translated into many languages).
- Bootstrapping for standard errors is "unnecessary unless a complicated sampling scheme or estimator is being used"; the CLT formula suffices.
- The Llama 3 technical report is critiqued twice: its Bernoulli standard errors are too wide for fractional scores such as F1, and its reading-comprehension intervals are too narrow because they are unclustered.
- The UK **Inspect** framework is named as correctly computing the CLT standard error in its `stderr()` metric.
- **Resampling returns:** in the uniform-difficulty example, K=2 reduces variance by 1/3, K=4 by 1/2, K=6 by 5/9, with an upper limit of 2/3.
- **Next-token probabilities** achieve the full 2/3 reduction immediately by eliminating conditional variance, but only apply when no chain-of-thought generation is required.
- **Temperature reduction is explicitly advised against** for variance reduction: one worked example shows it tripling minimum variance from 1/12 to 1/4; another shows it shifting the expected score from 2/3 to 3/4 while increasing the variance of conditional means roughly fivefold.
- **Pairing benefit:** with a between-model score correlation of 0.5, paired differences reduce estimator variance by 1/3 relative to unpaired.
- **Sample-size guideline:** detecting δ = 0.03 at 80% power and α = 0.05 requires ≈ **969 independent questions**, supporting a rule of thumb that **new evals should contain at least 1,000 questions**.
- **Minimum Detectable Effect example:** with n = 198, raising K from 1 to 10 lowers the MDE from **13.2% to 7.5%**.
- **Reporting recommendations:** report standard errors in parentheses beneath means, report the number of questions, report the number of clusters where applicable, and report pairwise differences, pairwise standard errors, and score correlations when comparing models.

## Method and evidence base
This is an analytical paper, not an empirical one. Its claims are established by derivation: Appendix A derives the clustered standard error by treating the eval as a linear regression with question-level fixed effects and applying the standard clustered-covariance sandwich estimator; Appendix B derives the sample-size formula from the Type I and Type II error z-scores; Appendix C extends both to the clustered case and shows how to estimate the required variance components from prior eval data with K greater than one. The illustrative magnitudes throughout are computed from stylized distributional assumptions, most often uniform question difficulty, and are labelled as such. The one genuinely empirical result is Table 4, which reports clustered versus naive standard errors computed on DROP, RACE-H, and MGSM using Anthropic models — this is where the 3.05×, 1.10×, and 1.88× ratios come from, and Miller is explicit that these are non-fictional. The critiques of the Llama 3 report are analytical inferences from that report's stated methodology rather than re-analyses of its raw data.

## Vocabulary the source introduces
- **Super-population** — the hypothetical infinite pool of questions from which an eval's questions are treated as a random draw; the assumption that makes inference about the underlying skill possible.
- **Conditional mean** — the expected score on a specific question, conditional on that question having been selected.
- **Conditional variance** — the variance of a model's score on a specific question, arising from sampling randomness in generation.
- **Variance of the conditional means** — variance attributable to which questions were drawn; a property of the super-population and therefore irreducible except by increasing n.
- **Mean conditional variance** — the average within-question sampling variance; reducible by resampling or by using next-token probabilities.
- **Clustered standard error** — a standard error adjusted for non-independent question draws, acting as a "sliding scale" between treating each cluster as one observation and treating every question as independent.
- **Paired differences** — question-level score differences between two models, which exploit their positive covariance to shrink the standard error at no cost.
- **Minimum Detectable Effect (MDE)** — the smallest true difference an eval of a given size can reliably detect at chosen significance and power levels.
- **Power** — the probability that an experiment detects an effect of a given magnitude when that effect is real.

## What to remember
- An eval is an experiment. Report the standard error next to the mean, report the number of questions, and report the number of clusters when questions come in groups.
- Clustering is not a technicality. On real reading-comprehension evals the correction was over threefold, meaning unclustered intervals can overstate precision by a large factor.
- Always compare two models with paired question-level differences. It is a free variance reduction — roughly a third in the worked example — and it changes conclusions.
- Never lower the sampling temperature to make scores look more stable. It can increase the irreducible variance component and bias the estimate.
- Do the power analysis before running the eval. Roughly 1,000 questions is the floor for detecting a 3-point difference; below that, a null result may mean nothing.
- The Galleon versus Dreadnought example is the lesson in miniature: correct analysis reversed the apparent winner across three evals.

## Why it matters for the course
This is the source that turns evaluation from a reporting habit into a defensible engineering practice, and it is the natural statistical backbone of the course's measurement strand. Husain teaches students to build the eval; Shankar teaches them that the criteria will move; Miller teaches them whether their result means anything. The Galleon–Dreadnought reversal is an unusually effective teaching device because it demonstrates in one worked example that ordinary practice produces the wrong answer, not merely an imprecise one. The recommendations are also directly actionable in a course setting: they are five things a student can put in a report template and be graded against. For a forward-deployed engineer, the power-analysis section is arguably the most valuable, because it converts "we ran the eval and saw no difference" into a statement about what the eval was capable of detecting — which is exactly the conversation a client needs to have before committing to a model change.

## How to teach it
- **Lecture point:** open with the Galleon–Dreadnought table and take a class vote on which model is better. Return to it at the end of the lecture after the paired-and-clustered analysis reverses the answer.
- **Lecture point:** the variance decomposition. Draw the split between variance of conditional means and mean conditional variance, and use it to explain why some interventions help and others cannot.
- **Lecture point:** "Don't touch the thermostat" as a case study in how an intuitive variance-reduction move can silently make things worse.
- **Lab exercise:** give students raw per-question eval data with cluster identifiers. Have them compute naive and clustered standard errors and report the ratio. Reproducing something near the DROP 3× effect is a decisive result.
- **Lab exercise:** paired versus unpaired comparison on the same data, reporting both standard errors and the score correlation, with a written statement of which conclusions change.
- **Lab exercise:** implement the sample-size formula and have each student compute the Minimum Detectable Effect of the eval they are actually using for their project. Many will discover their eval cannot detect the effect they are claiming.
- **Assessment idea:** require every project report to include the paper's recommended table format — question count, cluster count, mean with standard error in parentheses, and for comparisons, the pairwise difference, pairwise standard error, confidence interval, and correlation.
- **Assessment idea:** a critique exercise. Give students a published model card or technical report and ask them to identify which of Miller's five recommendations it violates and estimate the consequence.

## Limits and cautions
- The paper assumes questions are drawn from a well-defined super-population. If an eval's questions were hand-selected, curated for difficulty, or leaked into training data, the inference machinery is valid about the wrong target. This is exactly the failure Singh et al. document, and the two papers should be taught together.
- Statistical validity says nothing about construct validity. A tight confidence interval around a meaningless metric is still meaningless.
- Most illustrative magnitudes rest on stylized assumptions, usually uniform question difficulty. The 2/3 resampling ceiling and the 1/3 pairing benefit are worked examples, not universal constants. The clustering ratios in Table 4 are the exception and are real.
- Next-token-probability scoring is unavailable for chain-of-thought, agentic, and free-generation evaluation, which covers much of what a modern AI systems course actually evaluates.
- The framework addresses comparing scores, not choosing what to score. It does not help with criteria drift, judge bias, or rubric design.
- Power analysis requires variance components estimated from prior data with K greater than one, which a team running an eval for the first time will not have.
