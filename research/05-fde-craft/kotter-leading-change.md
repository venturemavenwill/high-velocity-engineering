## What this source is

> **Correction notice.** The previous version of this note was a synthesis written without reading the book, and on one substantive point it asserted the opposite of what the text says. It stated that Kotter is "specific that this means an honest confrontation with reality rather than manufactured crisis". Chapter 3 contains a section headed "The Role of Crises" in which Kotter writes that "Real leaders often create these sorts of artificial crises rather than waiting for something to happen", and item 1 of his table of ways to raise urgency is "Create a crisis by allowing a financial loss, exposing managers to major weaknesses vis-à-vis competitors, or allowing errors to blow up instead of being corrected at the last minute." Two further old-note claims are corrected below, and all four are quoted and dealt with in `## Verification status`. Anything carried from the earlier note into teaching material should be re-checked against this one.

This is the most widely used framework for organisational change: a diagnosis of eight recurring errors followed by a prescription of eight stages that invert them. It is written as practitioner counsel rather than as research, and the 1996 Preface is explicit about the form: "Unlike my previous books, *Leading Change* is not filled with footnotes and endnotes. I have neither drawn examples or major ideas from any published source except my own writing nor tried to cite evidence from other sources to bolster my conclusions." Kotter describes what follows as "what I've seen, heard, and concluded".

Two things about the book are easy to miss from summaries. First, it is a book about **leadership** that treats the eight steps as a vehicle rather than as the point; the steps exist to concentrate enough leadership to overcome inertia, and Kotter says so repeatedly. Second, its case material is entirely anonymised. Every extended example is a pseudonymous person or an unnamed firm identified only by industry — Adrien in specialty chemicals, Claire in a US bank, "King" Henry in food, Frank the disempowering supervisor, an Australian financial services firm, a European manufacturer. No case in the book can be traced or checked by a reader.

## The argument in full

**Chapter 1, "Transforming Organizations: Why Firms Fail", is the diagnosis.** Kotter opens not with a failure rate but with a qualitative judgement: "in too many situations the improvements have been disappointing and the carnage has been appalling, with wasted resources and burned-out, scared, or frustrated employees." He then names eight errors, each as a numbered heading.

*Error #1: Allowing Too Much Complacency.* "By far the biggest mistake people make when trying to change organizations is to plunge ahead without establishing a high enough sense of urgency in fellow managers and employees." The sentence that follows carries no hedge at all: "This error is fatal because transformations always fail to achieve their objectives when complacency levels are high."

*Error #2: Failing to Create a Sufficiently Powerful Guiding Coalition.* Kotter states that his claim exceeds the usual one about executive sponsorship: "Major change is often said to be impossible unless the head of the organization is an active supporter. What I am talking about here goes far beyond that." The coalition is "the president, division general manager, or department head plus another five, fifteen, or fifty people". "Individuals alone, no matter how competent or charismatic, never have all the assets needed to overcome tradition and inertia except in very small organizations. Weak committees are usually even less effective."

*Error #3: Underestimating the Power of Vision.* Without it, "a transformation effort can easily dissolve into a list of confusing, incompatible, and time-consuming projects". The test is offered as a heuristic and hedged as one: "A useful rule of thumb: Whenever you cannot describe the vision driving a change initiative in five minutes or less and get a reaction that signifies both understanding and interest, you are in for trouble."

*Error #4: Undercommunicating the Vision by a Factor of 10 (or 100 or Even 1,000).* The multiplier is in the heading itself. The substantive claim beneath it is about deeds rather than volume: "Communication comes in both words and deeds. The latter is generally the most powerful form. Nothing undermines change more than behavior by important individuals that is inconsistent with the verbal communication."

*Error #5: Permitting Obstacles to Block the New Vision.* "One well-placed blocker can stop an entire change effort." No hedge. The illustration is "Ralph", the number three executive at a financial services firm, who "paid lip service to his firm's major change efforts but failed to alter his behavior".

*Error #6: Failing to Create Short-Term Wins.* "Most people won't go on the long march unless they see compelling evidence within six to eighteen months that the journey is producing expected results." And the distinction the whole stage turns on: "Creating short-term wins is different from hoping for short-term wins. The latter is passive, the former active."

*Error #7: Declaring Victory Too Soon.* "While celebrating a win is fine, any suggestion that the job is mostly done is generally a terrible mistake. Until changes sink down deeply into the culture, which for an entire company can take three to ten years, new approaches are fragile and subject to regression." The mechanism is a coalition of the sincere and the cynical: "a combination of idealistic change initiators and self-serving change resisters often creates this problem", with the resisters "quick to spot an opportunity to undermine the effort".

*Error #8: Neglecting to Anchor Changes Firmly in the Corporate Culture.* "change sticks only when it becomes 'the way we do things around here'". Two mechanisms are named: making the link between new behaviour and improved performance explicit, because "When people are left on their own to make the connections, as is often the case, they can easily create inaccurate links"; and succession, because "One bad succession decision at the top of an organization can undermine a decade of hard work."

The chapter closes on a hedge worth keeping: "These errors are not inevitable. With awareness and skill, they can be avoided or at least greatly mitigated."

**Chapter 2, "Successful Change and the Force That Drives It", supplies the process and the leadership argument.** Kotter states his sample and his conclusion together: "After watching dozens of efforts to enhance organizational performance via restructuring, reengineering, quality programs, mergers and acquisitions, cultural renewal, downsizing, and strategic redirection, I draw a different conclusion. Available evidence shows that most public and private organizations can be significantly improved, at an acceptable cost, but that we often make terrible mistakes when we try because history has simply not prepared us for transformational challenges." The phrase "Available evidence shows" is not attached to any evidence.

He introduces the model with an unusual disclaimer: "All diagrams tend to oversimplify reality. I therefore offer figure 2–2 with some trepidation." The steps are then named in one sentence: "establishing a sense of urgency, creating the guiding coalition, developing a vision and strategy, communicating the change vision, empowering a broad base of people to take action, generating short-term wins, consolidating gains and producing even more change, and institutionalizing new approaches in the culture." **Two of those names differ from the chapter titles that treat them** — chapter 7 is "Empowering Employees for Broad-Based Action" and chapter 10 is "Anchoring New Approaches in the Culture" — so the canonical wording of steps five and eight is not fixed even within the book.

The phases have a shape: "The first four steps in the transformation process help defrost a hardened status quo... Phases five to seven then introduce many new practices. The last stage grounds the changes in the corporate culture and helps make them stick."

On sequence Kotter is more careful than his reputation suggests, and the hedges are load-bearing: "Successful change of any magnitude goes through all eight stages, **usually** in the sequence shown in figure 2–2. Although one **normally** operates in multiple phases at once, skipping even a single step or getting too far ahead without a solid base **almost always** creates problems." He then makes concurrency his own position rather than a concession: "After getting well into the urgency phase (#1), all change efforts end up operating in multiple stages at once, but initiating action in any order other than that shown in figure 2–2 rarely works well. It doesn't build and develop in a natural way. It comes across as contrived, forced, or mechanistic." The section "Projects within Projects" extends this: a large transformation is composed of smaller projects each running the full sequence, so that at one moment "you might be halfway through the overall effort, finished with a few of the smaller pieces, and just beginning other projects. The net effect is like wheels within wheels."

The management and leadership definitions are given as processes, not as people: "Management is a set of processes that can keep a complicated system of people and technology running smoothly. The most important aspects of management include planning, budgeting, organizing, staffing, controlling, and problem solving. Leadership is a set of processes that creates organizations in the first place or adapts them to significantly changing circumstances. Leadership defines what the future should look like, aligns people with that vision, and inspires them to make it happen despite the obstacles." Then the book's most-quoted number, derived from inspecting two of his own diagrams: "A close look at figures 2–2 and 2–3 shows that successful transformation is 70 to 90 percent leadership and only 10 to 30 percent management."

The chapter's closing position is emphatic and unqualified: "Only leadership can blast through the many sources of corporate inertia. Only leadership can motivate the actions needed to alter behavior in any significant way. Only leadership can get change to stick by anchoring it in the very culture of an organization." He immediately rejects the great-man reading of it: "The solution to the change problem is not one larger-than-life individual who charms thousands into being obedient followers. Modern organizations are far too complex to be transformed by a single giant."

**Chapter 3, "Establishing a Sense of Urgency".** Kotter gives an unsourced participation threshold: "In an organization with 100 employees, at least two dozen must go far beyond the normal call of duty to produce a significant change. In a firm with 100,000 employees, the same might be required of 15,000 or more." He lists nine sources of complacency, of which the notable ones for an engineering audience concern measurement: narrow functional goals, where "Only the CEO was responsible for overall sales, net income, and return on equity"; internal planning and control systems "rigged to make it easy for everyone to meet their functional goals"; performance feedback arriving only through those internal systems; and hostility to people who go and get external data — "when enterprising young employees went out of their way to collect external performance feedback, they were often treated like lepers."

He measures urgency with an instrument he invents in the same sentence: "on an urgency scale of 0 to 100, the average rating among those executives was certainly less than 50." The word "certainly" carries no method.

The counsel is for boldness: "Creating a strong sense of urgency usually demands bold or even risky actions that we normally associate with good leadership." And, contrary to the previous note, he endorses engineered crises. Item 1 of table 3–1 is "Create a crisis by allowing a financial loss, exposing managers to major weaknesses vis-à-vis competitors, or allowing errors to blow up instead of being corrected at the last minute." He gives approving examples: a manager who "decided to accept revenue and cost projections that he knew were unrealistic", producing "The resulting 30 percent plunge in expected income"; "Helen accepted what she believed were unrealistic promises about a major new product introduction and allowed the whole thing to blow up in her face—not an action to be taken by the faint of heart"; and a CEO who "cleaned up a balance sheet, funded a number of new initiatives, and created a loss of nearly $1 billion in the process". His preference ordering is stated plainly: "Even though transformations start more easily with a natural financial crisis, given a choice, it's clearly smarter not to wait for one to happen. Better to create the problem yourself. Better still, if at all possible, help people see the opportunities or the crisislike nature of the situation without inducing crippling losses."

The chapter also contains the material most relevant to a junior practitioner, in "The Role of Middle and Lower-Level Managers". His example is "a middle manager in a large travel-related services company who almost singlehandedly confronted top management with data on the firm's increasingly fragile competitive position", using a routine assignment as cover to commission consultants. But the constraint is stated as absolutely as anything in the book: "If everyone in senior management is a cautious manager committed to the status quo, a brave revolutionary down below will always fail." He adds that in his experience this case does not arise, since "Even in the worst cases, 20 to 30 percent seem to know that the enterprise isn't living up to its potential, want to do something, but feel blocked."

The sufficiency threshold is quantified and hedged: "A majority of employees, perhaps 75 percent of management overall, and virtually all of the top executives need to believe that considerable change is absolutely essential."

**Chapter 4, "Creating the Guiding Coalition".** Kotter opens by naming the belief he is attacking — Iacocca at Chrysler, Walton at Wal-Mart, Gerstner at IBM — and then: "This is a very dangerous belief." The four membership criteria are given as questions under the headings POSITION POWER, EXPERTISE, CREDIBILITY and LEADERSHIP, with a categorical warning: "Never forget: A guiding coalition made up only of managers—even superb managers who are wonderful people—will cause major change efforts to fail." Sizing: "Change often starts with just two or three people. The group in successful transformations then grows to half a dozen in relatively small firms or in small units of larger firms. In bigger enterprises, twenty to fifty may eventually need to be signed up."

Two exclusions are stated: "Two types of individuals should be avoided at all costs when putting together a guiding coalition. The first have egos that fill up a room, leaving no space for anybody else. The second are what I call snakes, people who create enough mistrust to kill teamwork." Trust is the necessary condition: "regardless of the process used, one component is necessary: trust. When trust is present, you will usually be able to create teamwork. When it is missing, you won't." The chapter is honest about its own recommended intervention: of team-building off-sites, "Such team-building outings much too often still fail to achieve results."

**Chapter 5, "Developing a Vision and Strategy".** The definition: "Vision refers to a picture of the future with some implicit or explicit commentary on why people should strive to create that future." Three functions: it clarifies direction, it motivates action that is "not necessarily in people's short-term self-interests", and it aligns people so that they "can figure out for themselves what to do without constantly checking with a boss or their peers." Six characteristics are tabulated — imaginable, desirable, feasible, focused, flexible, communicable — the last defined as "can be successfully explained within five minutes". The five-minute rule recurs as a personal heuristic: "Remember my rule of thumb: If you cannot describe your vision to someone in five minutes and get their interest, you have more work to do."

The sample for this chapter is stated: "Over the past decade, I've closely observed a dozen companies as they tried to create effective visions for change." The process is characterised as slow and unpleasant: "Vision creation is almost always a messy, difficult, and sometimes emotionally charged exercise", and "Vision is never created in a single meeting. The activity takes months, sometimes years." The warning against premature closure is unusually strong: "An ineffective vision may be worse than no vision at all. Pursuit of a poorly developed vision can sometimes send people off a cliff."

**Chapter 6, "Communicating the Change Vision".** "Managers undercommunicate, and often not by a small amount." Seven elements are tabulated: simplicity, metaphor and analogy and example, multiple forums, repetition, leadership by example, explanation of seeming inconsistencies, and give-and-take. The arithmetic behind the "factor of 10 (or 100 or even 1,000)" appears only here, and it is a constructed hypothetical rather than an observation: six repeats over six months in one imagined case, against "twenty-five executives, four times a day, over six months equals more than 12,000 repeats. Six versus 12,000." The generalisation is hedged: "All successful cases of major change **seem to** include tens of thousands of communications."

The strongest claim in the chapter is about behaviour, not volume: "Nothing undermines the communication of a change vision more than behavior on the part of key players that seems inconsistent with the vision." Table 6–1 states the same thing as a principle: "Behavior from important people that is inconsistent with the vision overwhelms other forms of communication." Kotter also requires that unavoidable inconsistencies — corporate jets, refurbished offices, an overseas board meeting during redundancies — be named aloud rather than defended: "If mixed signals can't be eliminated, they are usually explained, simply and honestly." Communication must finally be two-way, and he accepts the cost: "The downside of two-way communication is that feedback may suggest that we are on the wrong course and that the vision needs to be reformulated."

**Chapter 7, "Empowering Employees for Broad-Based Action".** Empowerment here means barrier removal, and the barriers are enumerated: "Four can be particularly important: structures, skills, systems, and supervisors." Structure is illustrated by an Australian financial services firm whose customer-first vision was defeated because "A typical product required people from four different functional organizations to work together seamlessly" and "Strong structural silos undermined the teams in dozens of subtle ways". Training is treated as usually insufficient rather than usually absent: "People are expected to change habits built up over years or decades with only five days of education. People are taught technical skills but not the social skills or attitudes needed to make the new arrangements work." Systems means chiefly the human-resources systems — appraisal, compensation, promotion, succession, recruiting. Supervisors means confronting individuals, which Kotter says is habitually deferred: "If I've heard it once, I've heard it a hundred times: 'I should have dealt with Hal/George/Irene much earlier.'" His recommended method is not political manoeuvre but "honest dialogue". Table 7–2 puts the supervisor case bluntly: "Nothing disempowers people the way a bad boss can."

**Chapter 8, "Generating Short-Term Wins".** The definition is operational: "A good short-term win has at least these three characteristics: 1. It's visible; large numbers of people can see for themselves whether the result is real or just hype. 2. It's unambiguous; there can be little argument over the call. 3. It's clearly related to the change effort." What does not qualify is spelled out: "Having a good meeting usually doesn't qualify... nor does getting two people to stop fighting, producing a new design that the engineering manager thinks is terrific, or sending 5,000 copies of a new vision statement around the company." Timing: "In small companies or in small units of enterprises, the first results are often needed in half a year. In big organizations, some unambiguous wins are required by eighteen months."

Six functions are tabulated, of which the least obvious are testing the vision against reality — "Short-term wins give the guiding coalition concrete data on the viability of their ideas" — and retaining the confidence of superiors. The section headed "Planning versus Praying for Results" is the chapter's core: "Short-term wins don't come about as the result of a little luck. They aren't merely possibilities. People don't just hope and pray for performance improvements. They plan for short-term wins, organize accordingly, and implement the plan to make things happen."

Kotter records a change of position on the short-term versus long-term trade-off: "Ten years ago I might have agreed with this point of view. But I've seen too much recent evidence that contradicts it... In the past decade, I've watched dozens of firms have it both ways." He is candid about the ethics: "To some degree, all management is manipulation—and that includes the production of short-term performance improvements", and he describes an accounting-driven variant before ruling against it: "Short-term wins that support transformation are usually genuine. They aren't the product of smoke and mirrors." The chapter is also where the leadership argument is deliberately balanced: "Transformation is not a process involving leadership alone; good management is also essential."

**Chapter 9, "Consolidating Gains and Producing More Change".** The chapter opens with an awards banquet that destroys urgency, and states the rule: "After watching dozens of major change efforts in the past decade, I'm confident of one cardinal rule: Whenever you let up before the job is done, critical momentum can be lost and regression may follow." "Three years of work can come undone with remarkable speed." The reason is that opposition persists: "Irrational and political resistance to change never fully dissipates... instead of changing or leaving, they will often sit there waiting for an opportunity to make a comeback." And so, "short-term wins are essential to keep momentum going, but the celebration of those wins can be lethal if urgency is lost."

The chapter's substantive contribution is the interdependence argument, developed through an extended image of an office in which the furniture is joined by "a series of ropes, big rubber bands, and steel cables". The conclusion is the reason the process has eight stages: "changing highly interdependent settings is extremely difficult because, ultimately, you have to change nearly everything." Scale and duration follow: "At the extreme, stage 7 can become a decade-long process in which hundreds or thousands of people help lead and manage dozens of change projects." The organisational precondition is delegation: "Running twenty change projects simultaneously is possible if (a) senior executives focus mostly on the overall leadership tasks and (b) senior executives delegate responsibility for management and more detailed leadership as low as possible in the organization." He also recommends deleting interdependencies that history left behind, since "the purging of unnecessary interconnections can ultimately make a transformation much easier."

**Chapter 10, "Anchoring New Approaches in the Culture".** The definition: "Culture refers to norms of behavior and shared values among a group of people." Three reasons for its power: selection and indoctrination, the number of people enacting it, and its invisibility — "all of this happens without much conscious intent and thus is difficult to challenge or even discuss."

This chapter contains the book's clearest recorded change of mind. Kotter states the culture-first theory, then: "I once believed in this model. But everything I've seen over the past decade tells me it's wrong." His replacement claim: "Culture changes only after you have successfully altered people's actions, after the new behavior produces some group benefit for a period of time, and after people see the connection between the new actions and the performance improvement. Thus, most cultural change happens in stage 8, not stage 1." Restated as a diagnostic: "A good rule of thumb: Whenever you hear of a major restructuring, reengineering, or strategic redirection in which step 1 is 'changing the culture,' you should be concerned that it might be going down the wrong path."

He qualifies this carefully — cultural sensitivity is needed early even though cultural change comes late — and distinguishes two situations: grafting new practices onto a compatible core culture, illustrated by a CEO delivering a thirty-minute eulogy over six obsolete procedure manuals stacked on a table beside the lectern; and replacing an incompatible culture, which he says may require changing who is in the organisation. Table 10–1 says so directly: "May involve turnover: Sometimes the only way to change a culture is to change key people", and "Makes decisions on succession crucial". The chapter's own image for the failure mode is the best short summary of the stage: "Shallow roots require constant watering."

**Chapters 11 and 12** extend the argument beyond the episodic case. Chapter 11 argues that "the urgency rate of the winning twenty-first-century organization will have to be medium to high all the time", while conceding that "A higher rate of urgency does not imply ever present panic, anxiety, or fear." It is prefaced with a hedge — "Speculating on the future is always hazardous" — and rests on performance information systems that "provide honest and unvarnished news", flatter structures, and cultures that "value candid discussions far more than they do today". Chapter 12 attacks the trait theory of leadership: "the historically dominant concept takes leadership skills as a divine gift of birth, a gift granted to a small number of people. Although I, too, once believed this, I have found that the traditional idea simply does not fit well with what I have observed in nearly thirty years of studying organizations and the people who run them." Five habits are tabulated: risk taking, humble self-reflection, solicitation of opinions, careful listening, openness to new ideas.

**The 2012 preface qualifies the book in four ways and revises it in none.** It claims increased rather than diminished relevance: "The material in this book is not only still relevant now, sixteen years after it was published, but I believe it is more relevant, and for one simple reason: the speed of change continues to increase." It concedes practitioner learning while denying that the gap has closed: "That does not mean executives have learned nothing in the past few decades. They have. But the challenges have been growing as fast, or faster, than their skills." It hardens chapter 3 to the point of removing the hedge — "I truly believe it is impossible to overstate the severity of the challenges caused by an inadequate or unaligned sense of urgency" — and introduces "unaligned" as a second failure mode, a term that does not appear in the 1996 text. And it restates the management and leadership distinction in its shortest form: "Management makes a system work. It helps you do what you know how to do. Leadership builds systems or transforms old ones."

The preface's one directly practical addition is a criticism of the vehicles organisations actually use, which lands squarely on ordinary engineering delivery structures: "Task forces, 'work-streams,' and project management organizations are still the most common vehicles used to drive significant change efforts. These structures can help, but they have tendencies that can lead toward wrong processes, and they simply don't have sufficient power for an extremely difficult set of tasks."

It also concedes the book's boundaries twice — "I have already published four additional books that drill deeper into these ideas in various ways", and "the increasing speed of change has some profound implications that go beyond this book" — while directing new readers here first. **It corrects no error, retracts no claim, adds no step, and says nothing about the evidence base.** It closes: "Speed of change is the driving force. Leading change competently is the only answer."

## Key concepts and practices
- The eight errors are the diagnosis and the eight stages are their inversion; Kotter presents the diagnosis first, and it is the more transferable half.
- The order matters "usually", and concurrency is expected: all efforts end up "operating in multiple stages at once", and a large transformation contains smaller projects each running the whole sequence.
- Complacency is the default condition and is systemic rather than a defect of individuals; nine specific sources are named, most of them measurement and feedback failures.
- Urgency may legitimately be manufactured. Engineered crises, deliberately accepted unrealistic targets and permitted failures are all endorsed, with a stated preference for opportunity-based routes where they will work.
- A guiding coalition is selected for position power, expertise, credibility and leadership; a coalition of managers only is said to guarantee failure; large egos and "snakes" are excluded outright.
- A usable vision is "explained within five minutes" and gets a reaction showing "both understanding and interest".
- The vision is undercommunicated by orders of magnitude, and inconsistent behaviour by senior people "overwhelms other forms of communication".
- Visible inconsistencies that cannot be removed must be explained aloud rather than defended.
- Empowerment is barrier removal across four named categories — structures, skills, systems, supervisors — with HR systems singled out.
- Short-term wins are planned, not hoped for; they must be visible, unambiguous and clearly attributable, and they are also a test of the vision.
- Wins are dangerous. Celebration is the mechanism by which resisters end change efforts.
- Interdependence is why the process needs eight stages: "you have to change nearly everything".
- Culture changes at the end, only after new behaviour has visibly produced better results and the link has been made explicit; succession and promotion decide whether it holds.
- Management produces consistency and leadership produces change, and transformation is over-managed and under-led; but stage 6 in particular fails without competent management.

## Method and evidence base

**What Kotter says his method is.** The 1996 Preface is the governing statement, and it is a disclaimer rather than a description: "Unlike my previous books, *Leading Change* is not filled with footnotes and endnotes. I have neither drawn examples or major ideas from any published source except my own writing nor tried to cite evidence from other sources to bolster my conclusions. In that sense, this work is more personal than any I've previously published. I'm communicating here what I've seen, heard, and concluded on a set of interrelated topics that appear to be increasingly important." There are no references, no appendix, no methods section and no data tables. Every figure is either a conceptual diagram or is adapted from one of Kotter's own earlier books — *A Force for Change* (1990) for the management and leadership contrast, *Corporate Culture and Performance* with Heskett (1992) for the components of culture and the overmanaged and underled syndrome, and *The New Rules* (1995) for the macroeconomic forces and the lifelong-learning model.

**The sample descriptions he offers.** These are the only quantifications of scope in the book, and they are recollective counts rather than a defined population:

- Preface, of the 1995 article the book extends: "my analysis of dozens of initiatives over the prior fifteen years".
- Chapter 2: "After watching dozens of efforts to enhance organizational performance via restructuring, reengineering, quality programs, mergers and acquisitions, cultural renewal, downsizing, and strategic redirection".
- Chapter 5: "Over the past decade, I've closely observed a dozen companies as they tried to create effective visions for change."
- Chapter 7: "I've seen at least a dozen cases where three or four key players were Frank-like."
- Chapter 9: "After watching dozens of major change efforts in the past decade".
- Chapter 10: "I've seen a dozen cases over the past decade in which the senior VPs of human resources were assigned to 'change the culture'".
- Chapter 12: "nearly thirty years of studying organizations and the people who run them".

There is no sampling frame, no criterion for inclusion, no statement of how success and failure were classified, and no indication of whether the "dozens" in different chapters overlap.

**The passage that comes closest to a tabulated result**, in chapter 1: "In the recent past, I have watched a dozen change efforts operate under the reengineering theme. In all but two cases, victory was declared and the expensive consultants were paid and thanked when the first major project was completed, despite little, if any, evidence that the original goals were accomplished". Three sentences later the denominator changes: "In two of the ten cases, it's hard to find any trace of the reengineering work today." Twelve becomes ten inside one paragraph. This is not a serious flaw in the argument, but it is decisive evidence about the status of the numbers: they are recalled, not counted.

**What each kind of evidence can actually support.**

*Anonymised case narratives* are the bulk of the book and are its real contribution. They can support the claim that these failure modes are recognisable, that practitioners find them familiar, and that they recur across industries. They cannot support any claim about how often the errors occur, how much damage they cause, or whether the eight stages are necessary or sufficient. Because every case is pseudonymised and no organisation is named, none can be re-examined by a reader, and no rival explanation of any case can be tested.

*The 70-to-90-per-cent leadership figure* is the most precise number in the book and has the weakest derivation of any of them: "A close look at figures 2–2 and 2–3 shows that successful transformation is 70 to 90 percent leadership and only 10 to 30 percent management." Figure 2–2 is his eight-stage diagram and figure 2–3 is his management-versus-leadership diagram, adapted from his own 1990 book. The figure is therefore obtained by comparing two of his own conceptual drawings. It measures nothing and should never be quoted as a finding.

*Self-reported stage completion*, chapter 2: twelve division officers judged themselves "about 80 percent finished with stage #1, 40 percent with #2, 70 percent with #3, 60 percent with #4, 40 percent with #5, 10 percent with #6, and 5 percent with #7 and #8." This is participants rating themselves against Kotter's own model. It illustrates the model's use as a diagnostic vocabulary; it cannot test the model, because the model supplies the categories being scored.

*One third-party measurement appears in the whole book*, chapter 6, on the airline CEO who personally answered every customer complaint within forty-eight hours: "An outside research firm finds that 90 percent of the employees can describe the change vision when asked and nearly 80 percent say that they believe senior management is committed to making it a reality." The airline is unnamed, the research firm is unnamed, the instrument is undescribed, there is no baseline and no comparison group, and the causal attribution to the CEO's letters is Kotter's inference.

*One before-and-after business result appears*, chapter 10, for the aerospace division: "over a five-year period divisional revenues had gone up 62 percent, while net income rose 76 percent; comparable figures for the previous five years were 21 percent and 15 percent, respectively." There is no control and no attribution test, and the case is used to argue that the gains subsequently regressed once the general manager retired.

*One formal study is cited*, chapter 12: "a twenty-year study of 115 students from the Harvard Business School class of 1974". This is the only study with a stated *n* anywhere in the book, it is Kotter's own, it is uncited, its result is presented as a figure adapted from *The New Rules*, and its subject is individual career success rather than organisational change. It is not evidence for the eight-stage process.

*Two prominent numbers are arithmetic illustrations built on invented parameters.* The communication multiplier — "twenty-five executives, four times a day, over six months equals more than 12,000 repeats. Six versus 12,000" — assumes both cases. The compounded-growth comparison in chapter 12 — "If they each have 100 units of career-related capability at age thirty, twenty years later, Janice will have 122 units, while Fran will have 321" — assumes a growth rate and a unit of "career-related capability" that has no operational definition.

**A widely repeated figure attributed to Kotter is not in this book.** There is no statement anywhere in *Leading Change* that any percentage of change programmes, transformations or reengineering efforts fail. The familiar claim that "70 per cent of change efforts fail, according to Kotter" has no source in this text. Chapter 1 states the problem qualitatively — "the improvements have been disappointing and the carnage has been appalling" — and chapter 2 states something close to the opposite of a high failure rate as its premise: "most public and private organizations can be significantly improved, at an acceptable cost". The only 70 in the vicinity is the "70 to 90 percent leadership" claim, which concerns the composition of transformation work and not its success rate. That adjacency is a plausible route by which the misattribution arose, but it is offered here as a conjecture and nothing more. What is a finding is the absence: **the failure-rate statistic cannot be cited to this book, and should not be attributed to Kotter on its authority.**

**How he hedges, and where he does not.** The pattern is consistent and worth teaching in its own right. Kotter hedges heavily whenever he generalises from observation — "usually", "normally", "almost always", "rarely works well", "seem to", "perhaps 75 percent", "From what I've seen", "I think". He hedges the model itself: "All diagrams tend to oversimplify reality." He does not hedge his causal architecture. "This error is fatal because transformations **always** fail to achieve their objectives when complacency levels are high." "**Never forget**: A guiding coalition made up only of managers... **will** cause major change efforts to fail." "**One** well-placed blocker **can stop an entire** change effort." "**Only** leadership can blast through the many sources of corporate inertia." "A brave revolutionary down below will **always** fail." "successful transformation **is** 70 to 90 percent leadership." The empirical claims are qualified; the structural claims are not. Since the structural claims are the ones that carry the framework, this asymmetry is the framework's principal evidential weakness.

**Overall standing.** The book is craft knowledge from an unusually well-placed and unusually candid observer, offered as such and labelled as such by its own author. Its diagnostic vocabulary is genuinely useful and its case narratives are recognisable to anyone who has watched a rollout fail. It contains no test of its own claims, no comparison against any alternative model, and no data a reader can inspect. Teach it as a well-argued professional position with high descriptive resonance and enormous adoption, and never as an empirically established process.

## Vocabulary the source introduces
- **Sense of urgency** — the shared belief that the status quo is unacceptable. Kotter's threshold: "A majority of employees, perhaps 75 percent of management overall, and virtually all of the top executives."
- **Complacency** — the systemic condition that defeats change before it starts; nine named sources, most of them measurement and feedback failures rather than attitudes.
- **Artificial crisis** — a deliberately induced visible failure or loss, used to raise urgency when no natural crisis exists.
- **Guiding coalition** — the team assembled for position power, expertise, credibility and leadership; distinguished from a sponsor, a steering committee and a task force.
- **Snakes** — Kotter's term for people who "create enough mistrust to kill teamwork"; excluded from the coalition along with outsized egos.
- **Change vision** — a picture of the future with commentary on why it is worth creating; directs, motivates and aligns; explicable in five minutes.
- **Defrosting** — steps one to four, whose function is to thaw "a hardened status quo" before new practices are introduced.
- **Projects within projects** — the wheels-within-wheels structure by which a large transformation contains smaller efforts each running all eight stages.
- **Short-term win** — a result that is visible, unambiguous and clearly related to the change effort; planned rather than hoped for.
- **Declaring victory too soon** — the specific failure of treating the first major improvement as completion.
- **Anchoring in the culture** — embedding new practice in norms and shared values, chiefly by making the behaviour-to-results link explicit and by controlling succession.
- **Overmanaged and underled** — the condition, produced by past success, in which an organisation has ample management capability and insufficient leadership.
- **Management versus leadership** — sets of processes, not types of person: consistency and control, against direction, alignment and motivation.

## What to remember
- Delivery is not adoption. A technically correct system that nobody uses has failed.
- The diagnosis travels better than the prescription. Teach the eight errors first.
- Urgency comes first, is usually assumed rather than established, and Kotter permits it to be manufactured.
- The sequence is "usual", not rigid, and concurrency is Kotter's own position rather than a criticism of him.
- No individual drives transformation alone, and a coalition made only of managers is stated to guarantee failure.
- Communicate the vision far past the point of boredom, and understand that behaviour outweighs all of it.
- Where an inconsistency cannot be removed, name it out loud.
- Empowerment means removing structures, skill gaps, systems and people that block the vision — HR systems above all.
- Engineer visible, unambiguous, attributable wins into the schedule. Do not wait for them.
- Celebration is a risk. Use wins as credibility for the harder work, not as a finish line.
- Culture changes last, after results, not first.
- The failure-rate statistic everyone quotes is not in this book.

## Why it matters for the course
Engineering students consistently equate shipping with success, and this is the standard corrective. In forward-deployed work the gap between a delivered system and an adopted one is where most of the value is lost, and Kotter supplies both a vocabulary for that gap and a checklist for closing it.

Several stages map onto activities the programme already requires. Establishing urgency is what a discovery finding is for once it is presented back to a sponsor, and Kotter's nine sources of complacency read as a catalogue of the measurement and feedback defects that engineering teams are unusually well placed to fix. The guiding coalition is the stakeholder map made actionable, and it supplies the argument for why an executive sponsor alone is insufficient. Short-term wins are the strongest available argument for slicing delivery so that something visible and attributable lands early, and Kotter's three tests — visible, unambiguous, clearly related to the change effort — are directly usable as acceptance criteria for a first increment. Anchoring in the culture is what a handover plan is actually for, and his insistence that the behaviour-to-results link be stated explicitly makes that a concrete deliverable rather than a sentiment.

The 2012 preface earns its place in the reading for one sentence. Kotter's criticism that "Task forces, 'work-streams,' and project management organizations" are the most common vehicles for change and "simply don't have sufficient power" is a criticism of exactly the delivery structures a student team will be handed. It is worth putting in front of them before they are handed one.

Read against [research/05-fde-craft/hiatt-adkar.md](/research/05-fde-craft/hiatt-adkar.md), the pair covers both levels of the same problem: Kotter says what a change programme must contain, ADKAR says where a particular rollout is stuck. Read against [research/05-fde-craft/beyer-holtzblatt-contextual-design.md](/research/05-fde-craft/beyer-holtzblatt-contextual-design.md), his insistence on external performance feedback and on confronting real conditions is the organisational counterpart of going to where the work happens.

## How to teach it
- Lecture: present the eight errors before the eight stages. The diagnosis is more memorable, more transferable and more honest about what the evidence supports.
- Lecture: teach the hedging pattern explicitly. Put Kotter's hedged empirical claims beside his unhedged structural claims and ask which the framework actually rests on. This is a general skill and this book is an unusually clean specimen.
- Lecture: use the Preface disclaimer as the reading's evidence lesson. An author who states plainly that he cites nothing is more trustworthy, not less, than one who decorates the same material with references — but the reader must then hold the conclusions accordingly.
- Lecture: address the missing failure statistic directly. Ask students to find it in the text. They cannot, and the exercise teaches source-checking better than being told.
- Seminar: run the artificial-crisis section as an ethics discussion. Kotter endorses accepting projections known to be false, and letting a launch "blow up in her face". Ask whether an engineer could do either, and what the honest alternatives are.
- Lab: have teams map their project's real stakeholders onto a candidate guiding coalition and identify which of position power, expertise, credibility and leadership is missing.
- Lab: require a change vision for the system that can be delivered in under five minutes, then have a second team attempt to restate it. Kotter's own test requires both understanding and interest.
- Lab: require one short-term win to be planned into the delivery schedule, with its visibility, unambiguity and attribution criteria written down in advance and checked afterwards.
- Assessment: require an adoption plan alongside the technical deliverable, structured against the eight stages, with honest identification of which stages the team cannot execute from its position and why. Chapter 3's section on middle and lower-level managers is the right reading for that constraint.
- Assessment: pair with ADKAR so students see the organisational and individual levels of the same problem.

## Limits and cautions
- **The evidence base is professional observation with no controls, no comparison group, no sampling frame and no inspectable data.** The author says so himself. Present it as a well-argued position, never as an established process.
- **No figure from this book should be quoted as a measurement.** The 70-to-90-per-cent leadership split is derived from comparing two of the author's own diagrams; the communication multiplier and the compounded-growth units are arithmetic illustrations on assumed parameters; the stage-completion percentages are self-reports against Kotter's own model.
- **Do not attribute a change-failure rate to this book.** No such figure appears in it.
- The framework is directional rather than diagnostic. It says what to do but not how to tell which stage is currently blocking you, which is what ADKAR supplies.
- It is written primarily from a senior vantage point. Chapter 3 does address middle and lower-level managers, and does so usefully, but its conclusion for them is bleak: without at least a substantial minority of receptive senior managers, "a brave revolutionary down below will always fail". Students should be given the qualification and the constraint together.
- The strict-sequence criticism is usually aimed at the wrong target. Kotter already states that efforts operate in several stages at once and that transformations contain sub-projects at different stages. Criticise the claim he does make, which is that *initiating* out of order rarely works.
- The urgency counsel includes methods a student cannot ethically or practically use, and some a professional should not. Teach it with the ethics discussion attached.
- It was developed for large-scale corporate transformation and is routinely over-applied to small, local or purely technical changes. The book's own timescales — three to ten years to reach the culture, up to a decade in stage seven — should be quoted whenever someone proposes running the model over a twelve-week engagement.
- It is culturally situated in large Western corporate practice of the 1980s and early 1990s, and the 2012 preface does not revisit that.
- The 2012 preface adds emphasis, not correction. Do not present the reissue as a revised or updated argument.

2. **Contradicted.** The old note listed among its cautions: *"The strict sequence is contested; real change efforts run steps concurrently and iteratively."* It presented concurrency as an external criticism of Kotter, having earlier written that *"Kotter insists that it is sequential rather than a menu."* Concurrency is Kotter's own stated position: "one normally operates in multiple phases at once", "all change efforts end up operating in multiple stages at once", and the whole "Projects within Projects" section. The claim he actually makes is narrower — that *initiating* out of order "rarely works well" — and that is what a critique should address.

3. **Overstated, and it omitted the decisive fact.** The old note said: *"Kotter's evidence is systematic professional observation of a large number of organisations over an extended period, analysed qualitatively."* The description is directionally right, but "systematic" is not supported: there is no sampling frame, no inclusion criterion and no stated basis for classifying success or failure, and the counts are recollective enough that "a dozen change efforts" becomes "two of the ten cases" within one paragraph of chapter 1. More importantly, the old note did not record the single most important fact about the evidence base, which the author states himself in the Preface: the book cites no external source at all, and draws "neither examples or major ideas from any published source except my own writing".
