# Cliff Notes — Interviewing Users

## Citation and access
- **Author:** Steve Portigal
- **Title:** Interviewing Users: How to Uncover Compelling Insights
- **Publisher and year:** Rosenfeld Media, 2013; second edition published 2023 with added coverage of remote research
- **Access status:** Copyrighted book, not open access. This note is an original synthesis and reproduces no source text.
- **Related public material:** Portigal publishes articles, talks and a podcast on user research practice, and Rosenfeld Media hosts excerpts and summaries.

## What this source is
This is a craft manual for the research interview, written on the premise that interviewing is a learnable skill with identifiable failure modes rather than a natural talent. Where Contextual Design supplies a full methodology, this book supplies the moment-to-moment technique of being in the room with someone and getting a true account out of the conversation.

## The argument in full
The book's opening move is to dismantle the belief that anyone who can hold a conversation can conduct an interview. Ordinary conversation is reciprocal, cooperative and oriented towards agreement; a research interview is asymmetric, deliberately curious and oriented towards learning. Those goals conflict, and the conflict is why untrained interviewers fail in consistent ways. They fill silences, they finish sentences, they signal approval, they explain their own product, and they accept the first answer because pressing further feels rude. The book's project is to replace those social reflexes with trained ones.

The central stance the author prescribes is the deliberate embrace of not knowing. The interviewer's expertise is in the process; the participant is the only expert on their own experience. Adopting a position of genuine, slightly naive curiosity is what gives a participant permission to explain things they would otherwise assume are obvious, and it is precisely those unstated obvious things that contain the design-relevant information. This has an uncomfortable corollary that the book states directly: the more the interviewer knows about the domain, the harder it is to interview well, because expertise produces the assumption that an answer is already understood.

From this stance follow the book's practical rules. Ask about specific past events rather than generalities, because a person recounting last Tuesday is reporting, while a person describing what they usually do is theorising. Avoid leading questions, which encode the expected answer and are readily accepted by a participant trying to be helpful. Use silence deliberately, because the second thing a person says after a pause is usually more considered and less rehearsed than the first. Follow up on anything unclear rather than nodding through it, and use the participant's own words when doing so, since substituting your vocabulary quietly imports your framing. Ask the participant to show as well as tell, since demonstrating a task surfaces steps that narration omits.

The book gives particular attention to contradictions, and treats them as the opposite of a problem. When a participant says one thing and does another, or contradicts an earlier statement, the untrained response is embarrassment and a change of subject. The trained response is to notice the contradiction, name it gently, and explore it, because the gap between stated and actual practice is where the real constraints live. This is one of the book's most transferable ideas for engineering students, who are otherwise inclined to treat inconsistency as noise to be resolved rather than signal to be pursued.

Structurally, the author presents the interview as having an arc rather than being a list of questions. It opens with introductions, consent and low-stakes context-setting that establishes rapport and calibrates the participant to the unusual conversational mode. It moves through a broad tour of the relevant domain, then narrows into deep exploration of specific episodes, which is where the richest material appears. It closes with reflection and a wrap-up that invites the participant to add anything the interviewer failed to ask about. Recognising the arc lets an interviewer place a difficult question at the point in the session where trust is highest rather than asking it too early.

The book also treats the practical scaffolding that determines whether interviews happen at all, and treats it as part of the craft rather than as administration: recruiting and screening participants so the sample matches the question, designing an interview guide that is a flexible instrument rather than a script, handling consent and recording, defining roles when a team is present so that one person leads and another documents, and taking notes in a way that supports later synthesis. On synthesis, the argument is that raw interviews are not findings; the team must review, pattern-find and interpret together, and the book insists this is a collaborative activity because a single analyst reproduces a single set of assumptions. The second edition extends all of this to remote and video-mediated research, where rapport, observation of environment and reading of hesitation all behave differently.

## Structure of the original

- Preparing — research questions, recruiting and screening, logistics, and building the interview guide

- Sharing findings and making them actionable within an organisation
- In the second edition: remote and online interviewing, and its differences from in-person work

## Key concepts and practices
- Interviewing is a skill that is learned and practised, not an innate ability.
- The participant is the expert on their own experience; the interviewer is the expert on the process.
- Domain expertise makes interviewing harder, because it produces assumed understanding.
- Ask about specific recent events rather than habitual or hypothetical behaviour.
- Avoid leading questions; they supply the answer the participant then agrees with.
- Use silence as an instrument; the follow-on statement after a pause is often the valuable one.
- Mirror the participant's own vocabulary rather than substituting your own.
- Treat contradictions as findings to explore, not as errors to smooth over.
- The session has an arc; place sensitive questions where trust is highest.
- The interview guide is a flexible instrument, not a script to be executed.
- Synthesis is a collaborative team activity, not solitary transcription.

## Method and evidence base
The book is a practitioner's distillation of extensive professional field research, supported by worked examples and by contributed accounts from other researchers that illustrate how sessions go wrong and what recovery looks like. It offers craft knowledge rather than experimental evidence, and its claims are best assessed by whether they hold up when students try them. That is convenient pedagogically: every technique in the book can be tested in a fifteen-minute exercise, so the course can treat its claims as hypotheses students verify rather than assertions they accept.

## Vocabulary the source introduces
- **Interview guide** — a prepared but flexible structure of topics and questions, distinct from a script.
- **Rapport** — the working trust that makes candid disclosure possible, built deliberately in the session's opening.
- **Leading question** — a question that encodes its expected answer and therefore produces confirmation rather than information.
- **Probing and follow-up** — pursuing a partial or unclear answer instead of accepting it.
- **Contradiction as signal** — the treatment of inconsistency between stated and observed behaviour as valuable data.
- **Synthesis** — the collaborative process of moving from raw sessions to patterns and insights.
- **The arc of the interview** — the progression from introduction through broad context to deep specifics and reflection.

## What to remember
- Ask about real events, not abstractions; last Tuesday beats usually.
- The interviewer is there to learn, never to persuade, validate or demonstrate.
- Silence is a technique; resist filling it.
- Your own domain expertise is a liability in the room and must be actively suspended.
- Pursue contradictions rather than resolving them politely.
- Use the participant's words; changing the vocabulary changes the data.
- Synthesis is where interviews become findings, and it must be done with others.

## Why it matters for the course
This book supplies the live technique students need for every discovery exercise in the forward-deployed strand, and it addresses the single most common failure in student customer work: turning the interview into a pitch. Engineering students are trained to demonstrate competence, and an interview rewards the opposite behaviour, so the stance has to be taught explicitly and practised under observation. The book also gives the course a language for critiquing interview performance, which is what makes discovery coachable: an instructor can name a leading question, an unfollowed thread or a filled silence as a specific, correctable error rather than offering a general impression.

## How to teach it
- Lecture: demonstrate a deliberately bad interview in front of the class and have students name the errors before teaching the correct technique.
- Lab: run paired practice interviews on a mundane topic with a third student acting as observer, scoring only for leading questions, missed follow-ups and filled silences.
- Lab: require students to record and transcribe one interview and annotate their own errors; self-critique transfers better than instructor critique here.
- Lab: have students rewrite a set of leading questions into behavioural ones and defend each rewrite.
- Assessment: grade the interview guide and the observed session technique separately from the findings, so that process quality is assessed independently of luck in what the participant happened to say.
- Assessment: require the discovery report to identify at least one contradiction encountered and describe how it was explored.

## Limits and cautions
- The book teaches interviewing technique; it does not supply the domain knowledge needed to interpret answers correctly.
- Interviews report perception and recollection, both of which are unreliable; observation and system telemetry are needed as corroboration.
- Enterprise access constraints often prevent the sample the research question requires, and the book cannot solve that.
- Interview data is qualitative and small-sample; presenting it as though it establishes frequency is a misuse.
- Remote interviewing degrades several of the techniques, which is precisely why the second edition addresses it separately.
- Skill decays without practice, so a single lab is insufficient to establish competence.
