## What this source is
This is a practitioner manual for contextual, semi-structured user interviewing across the whole research cycle: deciding when interviews fit, aligning stakeholders, recruiting, constructing a field guide, conducting and documenting fieldwork, recovering from difficult sessions, analyzing the resulting material, and helping an organization act on it. Portigal writes for researchers, designers, engineers, strategists, and marketers who talk with customers in order to make something for them.

The book's central distinction is between ordinary conversation and deliberate inquiry. A relaxed tone does not make an interview casual in its operation. The interviewer manages objectives, rapport, attention, pacing, silence, follow-up, documentation, and team participation while resisting the ordinary conversational pull to speak about oneself, demonstrate expertise, correct the other person, or seek agreement. Technique matters, but Portigal presents the mature practice as a way of being: set aside the team's worldview long enough to enter the participant's, then do the analytic work needed to turn an encounter into an insight rather than a quotation or request.

This is not a complete ethnographic method, a usability-testing handbook, or a claim that interviews answer every product question. The book repeatedly limits interviews: they favor depth over sample size, do not yield statistical significance, are partly artificial even when conducted in context, and do not reliably predict future behavior, purchase intent, or price. Portigal instead shows how interviews can reveal behavior, meaning, language, context, mental models, tensions, and reframes, and how those findings can complement observation, experiments, analytics, surveys, and testing.

## The argument in full
The front matter establishes the book's practical scope and rejects the idea that interviewing is merely talking. Ordinary conversation gives the interviewer many familiar but counterproductive defaults: taking half the airtime, waiting for a turn to speak, sharing a related experience, smoothing over silence, supplying possible answers, and correcting an error. A research interview requires deliberate choices about what to ask, when to ask it, how to listen, and when to say nothing. The FAQ also makes the organizational premise explicit: product makers inhabit a different context, language, and set of expectations from the people who use what they make. Using one's own product does not erase that difference. Research can be a recurring organizational asset rather than a single product milestone, and participation should extend beyond specialist researchers even though leading a study remains skilled work.

Chapter 1, **The Importance of Interviewing in Design**, places interviewing inside a larger design process. The sequence is to study people, preferably in context; explore both behavior and its meaning; interpret and synthesize the material; and use the resulting insight to inform a product, service, strategy, or other response. Portigal avoids a definition fight over whether this is ethnography, contextual research, design research, or user research. The operative question is whether the work produces a deep enough understanding to inform design and business decisions.

The chapter rejects a **scooping model** in which researchers collect ready-made needs and engineers implement them. A reported pain point may be tolerated because changing it costs more than living with it; Portigal uses satisficing to name this acceptance of a good-enough arrangement. Insight therefore requires inference and synthesis, not transcription of requests. One high-value outcome is a **reframe**, a changed model of the problem that reveals opportunities or likely failure points. A reframe should extend rather than silently replace the commissioned question: the research team must answer the brief while making the more consequential interpretation visible.

Interviews can help before a design exists, while hypotheses are being refined, or when an existing offering is being reconsidered. They can also be paired with quantitative or evaluative methods. Portigal is equally clear about what they cannot establish: a small semi-structured sample is not a prevalence estimate; an interview is not fully natural observation; and statements about future use, buying, or price are not dependable predictions. The source claims additional value from team members encountering customers together: shared field experience can align a team and strengthen customer advocacy. Its stronger claims about increasing empathy are practitioner reasoning, supported in a footnote by analogy rather than a direct study of user interviews.

Chapter 2, **A Framework for Interviewing**, supplies the stance from which later tactics follow. First, **check your worldview at the door**. A project begins with stakeholder beliefs, hypotheses, hopes, and disagreements, so Portigal recommends a brain dump that externalizes them without requiring consensus or later scoring people as right or wrong. Immediately before a session, the field team performs a smaller reset: its immediate task is to learn about this person and this setting, not to defend the roadmap or solve the quarter's business problem in the room.

Second, actively embrace how the participant sees the world. Go to the participant's environment when the question depends on artifacts, practices, relationships, and context. Avoid importing corporate cues unnecessarily. Ask apparently naive questions even when the answer seems obvious; the participant's timing, exception, rationale, or interpretation may be the real finding. Treat the participant as expert and the interviewer as novice. This posture is not passive. It requires preparation, full attention, and freedom from avoidable distractions.

Third, build rapport deliberately. Participants may interpret a home interview as hosting guests or a workplace interview as giving a demonstration. The interviewer should respect relevant social rituals without letting small talk or self-disclosure take over. Sharing something about oneself is justified when it gives permission or resolves a barrier, not merely because the interviewer has found common ground. Rapport may arrive only after a long awkward period. Portigal describes a **tipping point** at which short answers become stories; the interviewer cannot schedule it and must patiently create the conditions for it.

Fourth, listen in a form stronger than not interrupting. Questions should demonstrate attention by following what the participant has said, explicitly looping back to an earlier statement, or signaling a topic change. Eye contact, posture, acknowledgments, and the handling of notes all communicate whether the participant has the interviewer's attention. The interview remains an unusual, constructed event; acknowledging its purpose gives both parties permission to investigate mundane details that ordinary conversation would skip.

Chapter 3, **Getting Ready to Conduct Your Interviews**, treats preparation as part of research quality. Objectives are developed through conversations with stakeholders about project history, existing beliefs, business questions, methodological concerns, organizational barriers, prior research, products, and prototypes. The immediate output is a set of research goals that says what the team needs to learn, not yet the literal questions it will ask. This step can expose objectives that interviews cannot answer or a scope too broad for the available work. Portigal includes a contrasting rapid-research case to show that lightweight preparation can work when objectives are clear, experienced interviewers and decision makers are present, the recruitment mechanism fits the live behavior, the technology is familiar, and everyone understands the evidential limits. Speed is conditional, not a license to omit alignment.

Recruiting begins by defining a sample in behavioral and contextual terms and by including different consequential positions in a transaction, not merely a broad demographic label. Criteria are hypotheses to review with the team. A **screener** turns them into qualifying questions and branching rules. Participants may come from customer lists, sales introductions, specialist recruiters, intercepts, or other channels, each with bias and operational cost. Difficulty finding the population is itself data about the team's conception of the market. A superficially eligible participant may still lack the relevant device, task, environment, authority, or relationship at the interview site, so the screener must establish both participant and context.

The **field guide**, also called an interview guide or protocol, translates research goals into possible questions, activities, logistics, and time allocations. It normally moves through introduction and background, a main body organized by inquiry area, later reflection or dream questions, and wrap-up. A shot list can sit beside it. Writing likely wording and mentally rehearsing the session exposes timing and sequencing problems, but the guide remains preparation for flexibility rather than a script. Scheduling must account for participant routines, travel, fatigue, setup, debriefing, and adaptation between sessions; Portigal's numeric examples are contextual heuristics, not universal throughput targets.

Preparation also covers consent, confidentiality, releases, incentives, and legal review. The source's release discussion includes voluntary participation and the right to stop, recording and likeness permissions, incentive terms, confidentiality obligations when unreleased material is shown, and different arrangements for minors or public use. Portigal advises plain language compatible with legal needs and obtaining agreement before substantive interviewing or recording begins. Incentives express appreciation but may be inappropriate or prohibited in professional settings, where food, a donation, or another context-sensitive form may fit better. These are 2013 U.S.-oriented practitioner examples, not current legal advice.

Chapter 4, **More Than Just Asking Questions**, treats the interview as a platform for observation and elicitation. Asking someone to summarize a process reveals memory and interpretation; asking them to demonstrate it reveals steps, artifacts, interruptions, and emotion. Asking the participant to teach the interviewer can make tacit details more explicit. When a natural event cannot be observed on demand, the team can stage it, role-play an interaction, bring equipment, or ask the participant to act out an ideal. Divergence among telling, teaching, and doing is a thread to explore rather than an error to average away.

Participatory design lets participants externalize needs with sketches, objects, or improvised models, but their proposed solution is evidence, not a specification. A requested handle may reveal a need to move the object rather than mandate a handle. Mapping likewise turns a process, relationship network, technical estate, or physical environment into a shared artifact that can be clarified during the interview and analyzed later.

Portigal reframes concept work as elicitation rather than simple concept testing. A deliberately provocative, partial, or nonviable concept can reveal design criteria and the boundaries of desirability. Begin with open reaction before prompting on planned topics, present concepts neutrally, and avoid signaling authorship or emotional investment. When a participant asks the interviewer for product facts, the **interviewer sidestep** returns the question to expectations or importance. Fidelity should match the question: appearance, behavior, content, and physical form are separate dimensions, and polish can introduce unintended assumptions about brand, feasibility, mobility, or completeness.

Other tools include casual card sorts, evocative images, artifacts, and homework. Cards and images give participants material to select, group, annotate, and explain; the explanation matters more than the arrangement alone. Homework can collect examples across time, prompt self-documentation, or accelerate exposure to a product before a follow-up interview. It also primes the participant and therefore changes the state being observed. The team must decide whether that change helps or contaminates the question.

Chapter 5, **Key Stages of the Interview**, joins team management to a session arc. Portigal generally prefers two fieldworkers: a lead who controls the interview and a second interviewer who listens, documents, tracks missed threads, and asks questions when invited. Additional observers increase organizational exposure but also alter the power relationship and may inhibit openness. Novices need explicit rules: stay engaged, hold questions until an appropriate opening, do not correct terminology, use open questions, and trust the lead's management of flow. Experienced peers can hand off more fluidly, but they still agree their operating model before entering.

The on-site arc has seven stages. **Crossing the threshold** includes identification, social expectations, seating, lighting, noise, equipment, paperwork, and preventing small talk from becoming unrecorded research. **Restating objectives** gives a participant-understandable purpose, high-level agenda, timing, and chance to raise constraints. A broad **kick-off question** begins the inquiry without imposing detail. During **awkwardness**, the interviewer accepts discomfort, asks accessible factual questions, and keeps creating small successes. At the **tipping point**, answers become stories and richer follow-ups become possible. **Reflection and projection** then invite larger meanings, ideals, and futures; these are useful expressions of worldview, not forecasts. Finally, the **soft close** recognizes that consequential information may emerge while equipment is being packed or at the door, so attention and recording continue until the encounter has truly ended.

Chapter 6, **How to Ask Questions**, gives the most detailed account of live technique. Set the field guide aside unless it is needed. Ask the shortest clear question and stop. Silence before an answer allows thinking; silence after an answer allows the next paragraph. Do not soften anxiety by adding choices or trailing examples that constrain the response. Silence has different meanings across people and cultures: it can create shared readiness, signal effort, expose confusion, or produce stress. The interviewer must read cues and adjust rather than apply waiting as a mechanical trick.

Most desired answers require a chain of questions. Follow the participant's interpretation when it is revealing, or redirect explicitly when the study needs another path. Signal transitions so a discontinuous question does not feel like the preceding answer was ignored. The interviewer simultaneously tracks time, objectives, new branches, unanswered details, participant energy, and rapport. Threads can be left to recur, noted for later, or triaged by relevance and conversational continuity.

Portigal's question palette includes sequence, quantity, a recent concrete example, exceptions, exhaustive lists, relationships, and organizational structure. To surface what is unsaid, ask for clarification, investigate native terms, notice emotional cues, ask why, probe a coded reference delicately, offer nonpresumptive alternatives, or ask the participant to teach an outsider. Comparisons across processes, people, and time reveal mental models. Future questions are used to loosen current assumptions and expose aspirations, not to predict behavior.

The governing discipline is to use the participant's language and assume apparent inconsistency makes sense within a framework not yet understood. Do not turn vocabulary into a competence test, impose an interpretive frame and count polite agreement as confirmation, or enter lecture mode. Do not correct a mistaken product belief or repair a workflow during data gathering; either action shifts authority to the interviewer and may convert research into support. A genuinely helpful correction can be offered after the interview if it benefits the participant rather than the interviewer's need to be right.

Chapter 7, **Documenting the Interview**, separates the record from the researcher's immediate interpretation. Notes cannot capture everything and can reduce eye contact, but for some people writing supports attention and memory. Typed notes are shareable but introduce screens, sound, power, and mobility problems. Whatever the medium, notes should mark observed or reported material separately from interpretation. The source recommends recording because memory and rough notes will omit or misconstrue details; audio captures speech but loses pointing and demonstrations, while video captures more context at the cost of setup, storage, file handling, and greater privacy exposure.

Documentation is a system, not a device. Test batteries, capacity, microphones, lighting, background noise, framing, recording state, backups, and transfer before fieldwork. Photographing creates a quickly reviewable visual story and can expose details missed in the moment. Sketching can document sensitive screens without reproducing private data and can become a shared interpretive activity. Collecting permitted artifacts preserves context. Consent to record does not remove the need to handle the act of photographing or recording respectfully in the room.

The field team should debrief immediately after each session, recording surprises, likely implications, methodological problems, and changes for the next interview. Speculating about a design for this participant can provoke thinking but is not yet a conclusion. Longer field notes, transcripts, and rapid **field highlights** serve different purposes: a transcript is a detailed source, field notes preserve narrative and context, and highlights quickly bring the wider team into the emerging story. None substitutes for later cross-interview analysis.

Chapter 8, **Optimizing the Interview**, handles breakdowns and variants. A reticent participant may simply have a different rhythm, need more structure or silence, be reacting to the topic, connect better with another interviewer, or feel inhibited by observers or a manager. The team can slow down, simplify, change lead, reduce the room, change topic, disclose something selectively, ask directly about discomfort, or reschedule. Prevention begins in recruiting and expectation-setting, but the field team still has to diagnose what is happening rather than label the person difficult.

A participant who appears wrongly recruited may reveal ambiguity in the screener or in the team's category. Complete the session when it is safe and ethical, learn what is available, then revise criteria and rescreen later participants if needed. A highly talkative participant is not automatically a problem; distinguish the interviewer's loss of control from failure to answer the research question. Allow the story, connect it back to the question, and interrupt only when necessary, with an explanation grounded in limited time.

Discomfort and danger are different. Portigal advises against entering an unsafe setting or interviewing alone in unfamiliar private locations, and directs the team to leave if risk emerges. Discomfort can sometimes be tolerated in service of learning, but the source also recognizes escalating boundary violations and the interviewer's own limits. This judgement is personal and situational, not a requirement to persist.

The first edition's special cases include remote interviewing, facilities, short sessions, professional settings, and multiple participants. Phone or video removes environmental and facial information, changes silence and turn-taking, depends on participant fluency and connection reliability, and benefits from advance technical testing and supplemental photographs or screen sharing. A research facility makes the interviewer host and sacrifices context. A short session can be primed with topics in advance but allows less probing. Professionals may need to continue real work and often frame the encounter as a meeting; requests should specify access, setting, duration, and role. Multiple participants can illuminate interaction but introduce conformity and power effects, so some topics require separate sessions or separate portions.

Improvement is continuous: practice in and outside formal studies, review recordings and transcripts, vary habitual technique, experience being interviewed, observe peers and media interviewers critically, seek critique from the field team and participants, teach others, and exchange failure stories. The aim is not a universal interview persona but a personal style governed by deliberate principles.

Chapter 9, **Making an Impact with Your Research**, distinguishes analysis from synthesis. Analysis breaks interviews into episodes, observations, language, and stories; synthesis combines material across cases into patterns, interpretations, implications, and opportunities. Immediate experience matters, but it is only one pass. Debriefs and highlights feed a provisional set of themes and questions. A **topline report** shares those early impressions while explicitly warning that formal analysis is still to come; it also reveals which findings attract interest or resistance and therefore where deeper evidence and explanation will be needed.

Formal processing returns to documented data. Team members each take enough interviews to understand cases rather than isolated quotations, annotate transcripts, present each participant as a case, capture key points, cluster them, and develop a shared point of view tied to the research objectives. The final findings should articulate implications in a form the receiving team can use. The source sketches this process rather than claiming to be a full analysis or synthesis manual.

The final organizational argument is that research is a leadership activity. A practitioner must understand the organization's strategy, maturity, objections, resource constraints, and previous attempts; find allies; propose a study sized to the decision; involve stakeholders in planning, fieldwork, and synthesis; and make both process and outputs visible. Sometimes direct participation in one interview changes a stakeholder's model more than a polished report. Findings may need to become stories, requirements, prototypes, or engineering constraints to be actionable. This translation brings research close to design, but it should retain the evidence chain from field material to interpretation to proposed action.

The acknowledgments and author biography locate the work in a practitioner community. The book was technically reviewed, contains contributed field accounts from researchers and a lawyer, and reflects Portigal's experience interviewing people across consumer, professional, technical, medical, and financial settings. The consolidated footnotes point mainly to examples, practitioner resources, related methods, and cultural or conversational concepts. They do not add a controlled empirical evaluation of the method.

## Key concepts and practices
- **Interviewing is skilled, asymmetric inquiry.** A conversational tone coexists with deliberate control of purpose, pacing, follow-up, and attention.
- **Check the team's worldview at the door.** Externalize assumptions before fieldwork so they can be noticed without making confirmation the session's job.
- **Study behavior and meaning together.** A sequence without rationale is incomplete; a stated rationale without observed context may be idealized.
- **Reframe without abandoning the brief.** A deeper problem can extend the commissioned question, but the team remains accountable for the original decision need.
- **Use the participant's context and language.** Artifacts, interruptions, social relations, terminology, and misperceptions are part of the evidence.
- **Prepare to improvise.** Objectives, screening, a field guide, roles, logistics, consent, and equipment make responsible flexibility possible.
- **Treat recruiting as research data.** Scarcity, ambiguity, and mismatched contexts can falsify assumptions about the population or workflow.
- **Ask, show, enact, and make.** Demonstration, mapping, role-play, concepts, sorting, and homework expose different layers of a practice.
- **Build rapport through attention.** Follow-ups, silence, body language, selective self-disclosure, and respect for social norms show that the participant matters.
- **Silence is interpreted, not applied.** It can invite continuation, make room for thought, or signal confusion and distress depending on person and culture.
- **Assume contradictions are intelligible.** Explore the missing distinction instead of accusing the participant or averaging the conflict away.
- **Separate record from interpretation.** Notes, recordings, photographs, sketches, transcripts, highlights, and debriefs have different evidential roles.
- **Analyze cases before extracting themes.** Preserve enough context to understand a participant, then synthesize across cases rather than collecting decontextualized quotes.
- **Research impact is designed.** Stakeholder involvement, visible process, appropriate output forms, and an explicit chain from evidence to implication determine whether findings enter decisions.

## Method and evidence base
The source is a first-person practitioner synthesis built from Portigal's field career, training practice, case stories, contributed sidebars, sample artifacts, and concrete examples of successful and failed interviewing. Its examples span homes, workplaces, financial trading, healthcare, technology, media, consumer goods, and organizational research. The method is internally testable in the sense that its recommended behaviors can be observed in a recording and their immediate conversational consequences discussed.

It is not an empirical comparison of interview techniques. It reports no controlled trials, sampling framework for its cases, measured causal effects, validated saturation threshold, or universal staffing and scheduling rule. Some references point to adjacent concepts such as satisficing, conversation analysis, flow, conformity, and rapport, but the book's procedural recommendations remain practitioner claims. In particular, the chapter 1 empathy claim is supported indirectly through an analogy in the footnotes, not by research measuring whether user interviewing increases empathy. The source warrants a craft hypothesis and a diagnostic vocabulary, not an effect size.

## Vocabulary the source introduces
- **Scooping model:** treating research as collection of explicit needs ready to be implemented.
- **Satisficing:** accepting a good-enough arrangement because correction costs more than the problem feels worth.
- **Reframe:** a changed model that reveals a more consequential problem, opportunity, or likely failure.
- **Worldview brain dump:** externalizing stakeholder assumptions and contradictions before fieldwork.
- **Tipping point:** the unscheduled shift from short question-and-answer exchanges into participant stories.
- **Field guide:** the detailed but non-scripted plan for topics, questions, activities, timing, logistics, and closure.
- **Screener:** the branching instrument used to determine whether a potential participant and context fit the study.
- **Interviewer sidestep:** returning a participant's question about a concept to their expectations, priorities, or interpretation.
- **Participant observation:** learning through participation in the activity, not merely watching the participant.
- **Projection or dream question:** a later-session prompt that exposes values and aspirations without being treated as a forecast.
- **Soft close:** the period after formal questions when recording and attention continue because important material may still emerge.
- **Question tree:** the growing set of possible follow-ups an interviewer must remember and triage while time remains linear.
- **Field highlights:** rapid, shareable accounts that preserve the flavor and initial significance of a session without pretending to be full analysis.
- **Topline report:** explicitly provisional themes, stories, and questions shared after fieldwork but before deep processing.
- **Analysis and synthesis:** breaking field material into meaningful parts and recombining material across cases into new interpretations and opportunities.

## What to remember
- Decide what the organization needs to learn and whether interviews can answer it before recruiting anyone.
- Make stakeholder assumptions visible, then enter the session ready to be surprised rather than ready to validate them.
- Recruit for relevant behavior, role, and setting; eligibility without access to the actual context may be unusable.
- Write a detailed guide to enable flexibility, not to force every participant through identical wording and order.
- Obtain consent before substantive research begins and before recording; treat legal templates as context-specific.
- Begin broad and accessible, tolerate the awkward stage, and earn the deeper reflective questions later.
- Ask briefly, wait, and follow the answer. Do not append choices merely to rescue yourself from silence.
- Preserve the participant's words and apparent inconsistencies long enough to learn the framework behind them.
- Use demonstrations and artifacts when remembered summaries are insufficient, but account for staging and priming.
- Keep product expertise out of the session unless a correction at the end genuinely serves the participant.
- Record responsibly, distinguish observation from inference, and debrief before memory from one session blends into another.
- Return to documented cases for synthesis; early impressions and memorable quotations are not findings by themselves.
- Plan how evidence will enter a decision. A report format that the receiving team cannot use is an incomplete research design.

## Why it matters for the course
This source covers a wider operational surface than a question-writing guide. Forward-deployed engineers work inside the conditions Portigal describes: an ambiguous brief, stakeholders with incompatible beliefs, hard-to-reach users, unfamiliar workplaces, product experts who want to correct the customer, prototype pressure, compressed fieldwork, and an organization waiting for an actionable answer. The book provides named controls at every stage, from stakeholder interview and screener to debrief, case review, and findings delivery.

The following are **course-design inferences**, not claims Portigal makes about FDE or hypervelocity engineering:

- Translate the stakeholder brain dump into an **assumption register**. Record business, user, workflow, data, security, and technical beliefs before fieldwork without converting the interview into a validation checklist.
- Translate research goals into an **engagement decision map**: for each goal, name the decision it could change, the stakeholder who owns that decision, and the evidence mechanism appropriate to it.
- Treat recruiting friction as a deployment signal. If the supposed operator, data owner, approver, or exception handler cannot be identified, the workflow or governance model may not exist as described.
- Use demonstrations to reconstruct operational truth: ask a user to perform the last real case, open the actual artifacts, trace handoffs, and explain exceptions. Protect production data, credentials, and third-party information while doing so.
- Use the question tree as a live uncertainty queue. Follow a surprising branch when it can change scope or architecture; park it when it is merely interesting.
- Treat contradiction as a candidate segmentation, policy, incentive, or context distinction. Do not resolve it by choosing the statement that best supports the proposed solution.
- Translate field documentation into an **evidence packet** that separates observation, participant account, researcher interpretation, technical corroboration, and proposed engineering action.
- Pair interview evidence with the right non-interview evidence. Use telemetry, logs, representative data, technical spikes, evaluations, policy review, or controlled workflow trials for claims interviews cannot establish.
- Define hypervelocity as shortening the path from uncertainty to a warranted decision. Faster interviewing without recruiting discipline, debriefing, synthesis, or organizational uptake only produces weak evidence sooner.

## How to teach it
- **Worldview lab:** Give a team an enterprise AI brief and require an anonymized brain dump of user, workflow, data, model, security, and adoption assumptions. Grade whether contradictions and unknowns remain visible rather than being forced into consensus.
- **Objective-to-method lab:** Students interview mock stakeholders, produce research goals, and reject at least one requested question that interviews cannot answer. They must route that question to observation, telemetry, experiment, technical evaluation, or another method.
- **Recruiting and screener lab:** Build a behavioral screener for users, operational owners, approvers, and exception handlers. Inject a participant who technically qualifies but lacks the relevant system or context; students revise the criterion after diagnosing the mismatch.
- **Field-guide review:** Require introduction, consent checkpoint, background, workflow reconstruction, demonstration, exceptions, stakeholder relationships, reflective questions, soft close, timings, and a shot or artifact list. Review it as a flexible coverage instrument, not prose to be recited.
- **Silence and follow-up drill:** In triads, the participant answers in paragraphs, the interviewer must leave a beat before and after each answer, and the observer maps every supplied answer option, interruption, missed cue, and unsupported interpretation.
- **Question-palette circuit:** Students rotate through sequence, quantity, recent example, exception, complete list, relationship, organizational structure, native term, emotion, comparison, and teach-an-outsider prompts against one workflow.
- **Show-versus-tell lab:** Ask one participant to describe a task, demonstrate it, and teach it. Students identify divergence without declaring one account false and state what corroboration would resolve the difference.
- **Concept-reaction lab:** Present concepts with deliberately varied fidelity. Students practice a neutral reveal, collect unprompted reaction before topic prompts, use the interviewer sidestep, and document assumptions introduced by the artifact itself.
- **Difficult-session simulation:** Run reticent, overtalkative, wrong-context, manager-present, remote-connection, and boundary-crossing scenarios. Assessment includes recognizing when to adapt, separate participants, reschedule, or leave.
- **Documentation lab:** Produce descriptive notes with interpretations visibly marked, verify them against a recording, write a rapid field highlight, and conduct an immediate debrief. Grade discrepancies and provenance, not note volume.
- **Synthesis lab:** Assign each student multiple interviews, require case summaries before cross-case clustering, and trace every proposed theme back to documented episodes. A vivid quotation without case context does not pass.
- **Organizational-impact exercise:** Students deliver the same warranted finding as a research narrative, an engineering constraint, a prototype implication, and an executive decision brief, preserving uncertainty and evidence in each form.
- **Assessment packet:** Research goals and method rationale; assumption register; sample and screener; field guide; consent and data-handling plan; role allocation; selected recording or transcript evidence; debrief and field highlight; case summaries; cross-case synthesis; corroboration plan; changed decision; and limitations.
- **Performance rubric:** Score objective-method fit, participant and context fit, consent, rapport, question neutrality, silence, follow-up depth, adaptation, safety judgement, documentation provenance, case-preserving synthesis, evidential restraint, and whether the output changes a named decision.

## Limits and cautions
- Interviews are deep and semi-structured, not statistically representative. Repetition across a small purposive sample does not establish prevalence, market size, or an effect rate.
- Self-report and demonstration are both situated. Recall can be inaccurate, observation changes behavior, staging changes conditions, homework primes attention, and the presence of managers, family, interpreters, or multiple researchers changes what can be said.
- Future, purchase, and price statements expose present mental models more reliably than future behavior. Do not turn aspiration into forecast.
- The source is a 2013 U.S.-centered practitioner book. Its release, NDA, incentive, minor-participation, recording, and workplace examples are not current legal, privacy, employment, accessibility, or research-ethics guidance. Use appropriate review for jurisdiction and domain.
- The book gives limited attention to trauma-informed interviewing, vulnerable populations, disability and communication access, interpreter-mediated research, data minimization, retention schedules, re-identification, indigenous data governance, and harms to people who are affected but are neither users nor buyers.
- Contextual access can expose personal data, credentials, confidential records, bystanders, and customer information. Consent from the scheduled participant may not authorize capture of everyone and everything in view.
- Portigal's advice to persist through discomfort must not override withdrawal, refusal, professional boundaries, or safety. The participant can stop; the researcher can stop; an incentive must not become coercion.
- Native language should be preserved without imitating dialect, claiming insider status, or repeating harmful terminology casually. Clarify respectfully when shared understanding or safety requires it.
- Recording can improve fidelity while increasing privacy, security, and breach risk. Sometimes constrained notes, participant-created artifacts, redaction, or no recording are the warranted choice.
- A concept reaction is shaped by fidelity, framing, facilitator ownership, brand cues, and feasibility assumptions. It is neither a clean preference measure nor a product evaluation by default.
- Collaborative synthesis can broaden interpretation but can also amplify hierarchy and confirmation. Preserve dissent, negative cases, rival explanations, and traceability to source material.
- Stakeholder participation can align an organization while intimidating participants or encouraging premature action on early impressions. The topline must remain visibly provisional.
- Actionability is not permission to overstate. Translating a finding into an engineering requirement introduces design judgement; keep the transition from evidence to inference explicit.
- The source's staffing, timing, cost, and workload figures are historical examples and practitioner heuristics. They should not be taught as universal thresholds.
- The book offers no controlled evidence or effect sizes for the method. Teach its practices as inspectable craft hypotheses, compare them with alternatives, and measure student behavior directly.
