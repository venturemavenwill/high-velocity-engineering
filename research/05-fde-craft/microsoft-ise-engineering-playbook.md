# Cliff Notes — Microsoft ISE Engineering Fundamentals Playbook

## Citation and access

- **Author:** Microsoft Industry Solutions Engineering (ISE), a customer-embedded engineering organisation within Microsoft
- **Title:** Engineering Fundamentals Playbook (commonly referred to as the Code With Engineering Playbook)
- **Source consulted:** <https://microsoft.github.io/code-with-engineering-playbook/>
- **Repository:** <https://github.com/microsoft/code-with-engineering-playbook>
- **Access status:** Open access, published as a MkDocs Material site from a public GitHub repository, open to external contribution via pull request with a CONTRIBUTING guide in the repo
- **Read depth:** thirty-two pages were fetched and read in full on 2026-07-30. The pages are listed in [Verification status](#verification-status), together with every place where the previous version of this note inferred a page's content from its navigation title and inferred it wrongly.
- **Currency — the playbook has no single date, and the previous version of this note was wrong to give it one.** Every page carries its own footer date and the dates are years apart. Observed in the deep read: **26 September 2025** on Definition of Done, Definition of Ready and Team Manifesto; **3 June 2025** on Automated Testing; **17 April 2025** on Test Planning; **7 February 2025** on all five machine-learning pages read; **19 November 2024** on Logs vs Metrics vs Traces; **26 August 2024** and **22 August 2024** across many others; and **7 May 2021** on Creating Good Documentation. **The playbook is maintained unevenly.** Parts of it were revised within the last year and parts have not been touched in over four. Any citation must be pinned to the individual page and that page's own date, never to the site.

## What this source is

This is the working engineering handbook of a Microsoft organisation whose entire delivery model is embedding engineers with customers to build software jointly. It is not a whitepaper or a maturity model; it is the operational standard the organisation holds itself to, published verbatim, and it is valuable to a forward-deployed curriculum precisely because it is the working artefact rather than a description of one.

**It is not, however, one genre of document, and reading it as though it were is the mistake this note previously made.** Read page by page, it resolves into at least four distinct kinds of writing. Some pages are genuinely normative standards with quotable rules and explicit fields — the architecture decision record page, Reviewer Guidance, Automated Testing, Responsible AI in ISE. Some are navigation indexes composed entirely of links, most importantly The First Week of an ISE Project. Some are stubs of two or three sentences that signpost elsewhere — Creating Good Documentation, DevSecOps. One, "Who is ISE?", is recruiting copy. And at least one section index does not exist at all: `/non-functional-requirements/` returns HTTP 404 while the fifteen category pages beneath it remain reachable. **A note that treats the whole site as a uniform standard overstates the depth of roughly a third of it.**

## The argument in full

The playbook opens by defining the engineer rather than the process, and the definition is deliberately obligation-shaped. An engineer working on an ISE project has responsibilities to their team as mentor, coach and lead; knows their playbook, follows their playbook, and fixes their playbook if it is broken; leads by example; and strives to understand how their work fits into a broader context and ensures the outcome. The third clause is the interesting one. A playbook that engineers are expected to repair rather than merely obey is a living standard, and the fact that the repair mechanism is a public pull request against a public repository makes the claim testable rather than rhetorical. The uneven page dates recorded above are the test's result: the repair right is real, and it is exercised on some parts of the document and not on others.

The rationale offered for having a playbook at all is stated in three parts: to increase overall efficiency for both individual team members and the team as a whole, to reduce mistakes and avoid common pitfalls, and to strive to be better engineers by learning from other people's shared experience. This is a knowledge-transfer argument, and it matters in a customer-embedded organisation because engineers are dispersed across engagements and cannot learn by osmosis from adjacent desks.

The general guidance section is the playbook's philosophical core and is worth teaching close to verbatim, because several of its instructions cut against the reflexes engineering students are trained into. It insists on keeping the code quality bar high and on valuing quality and precision over simply getting things done, which is a striking position for an organisation delivering under customer time pressure. It instructs engineers to work diligently on the one important thing, rather than in parallel on several. It recognises the distributed condition explicitly, requiring that context be shared through the wiki, through Teams and through backlog items rather than held in individual heads. It says to make the simple thing work now, and to build fewer features today provided they work amazingly. It forbids adding scope to an existing backlog item, requiring a new item instead, which is a scope-discipline mechanism rather than a bookkeeping preference. It states that the goal is to ship incremental customer value. It requires backlog item details to be kept current, and it requires that product issues be reported with clear, repeatable engineering feedback. It closes with a collective-ownership statement: we all own our code.

**The two entry points are not what their titles suggest, and the difference matters.**

**The First Week of an ISE Project is a navigation index, not an onboarding guide.** Its own stated purpose is to "Organize content in the playbook for quick reference and discoverability". Every item on it is a link to another page; there is no prose, no narrative and no instruction of its own. What it does supply is a *sequence*, and the sequence is genuinely informative because it is counter-intuitive in one place: the page is shaped as a working week — Before Starting, then Day 1 through Day 5 — and **source control is established on Day 2, after sprint planning rather than before it.** A team that reads the ordering as a claim about dependency learns something; a team that expects the page to teach it anything learns nothing.

**The Engineering Fundamentals Checklist is assessable only in part.** It is twelve sections of flat binary items, roughly sixty of them, and it is not uniform in kind. Perhaps two-thirds are mechanically checkable against an artefact — "The default target branch is locked", "A minimum number of reviewers (usually 2) for a PR merge is enforced by policy", "Unit tests cover the majority of all components (>90% if possible)". Several others are not falsifiable as written; "The agile process is clearly defined within team" has no observable that distinguishes a team that satisfies it from one that does not. The Security section also slips out of declarative form partway down and begins asking questions rather than asserting conditions, so items in the same list are doing different grammatical work. **The checklist makes the artefact-bearing part of the playbook assessable. It does not make the playbook assessable.**

The body is organised by engineering discipline. Agile Development covers backlog management, ceremonies and roles, minimal valuable slices, risk management, pair programming, delivery planning, scrum of scrums, and the social contracts a team writes for itself in the form of a team manifesto, a working agreement, a definition of ready and a definition of done. Those three team-agreement pages carry the most recent dates on the site, which is a signal about which parts of the standard the organisation is still actively revising. Automated Testing spans unit testing including test-driven development and mocking, integration testing, end-to-end testing, consumer-driven contract testing, UI testing, smoke testing, shadow testing, synthetic monitoring, fault injection, and performance and load testing with a test-planning process and an iteration template. It also states a hard norm in one sentence: **"We consider code to be incomplete if it is not accompanied by tests."** That is the sharpest single line in the playbook and it is stated as a definition rather than as a recommendation. CI/CD covers continuous integration and continuous delivery, GitOps including deployment workflows and secret management, and a DevSecOps section — though the DevSecOps page itself is two paragraphs and an index to the pages beneath it, not a treatment.

Code Reviews is treated as a first-class discipline, with process guidance separated into author and reviewer responsibilities, material on inclusion in code review, pull request templates, tooling, and language-specific recipes covering Azure Pipelines YAML, Bash, C#, Go, Java, JavaScript and TypeScript, Markdown, Python and Terraform. **Reviewer Guidance is the richest page in the section and the one worth teaching.** It prescribes a two-pass structure — a first pass for shape and intent, a second for detail — and it does not permit sampling: "Read every line changed". It states the norm that makes review survivable socially, "What matters is a bug caught, not who made it, not who found it, not who fixed it", and it closes a common loophole outright: "Tests should always be committed in the same PR as the code itself".

**Evidence and Measures is narrower than a reader would guess from its title.** The evidence it proposes is branch-policy verification — "Verification of the policies on the main branch in Azure DevOps… may be sufficient evidence that a project team is conducting code reviews" — which is evidence that review was *required*, not that it was *done well*. The measures it offers are defect removal efficiency, time spent reviewing, and lines of code reviewed per unit time, and it acknowledges that these are gathered manually, "e.g. in an Excel sheet". The page then closes with an assertion that this repository must not carry forward: it states that **"the cost of doing code reviews is actually negative!"** No evidence, no measurement, no comparison and no source is offered for it anywhere on the page. **It is exactly the class of unwarranted magnitude claim this repository refuses**, and it should be taught as a specimen of the failure rather than repeated as a finding.

Design is the section most directly relevant to forward-deployed work, and it is where the playbook is at its most specific. **The architecture decision record page states its fields explicitly**, which is what makes it directly reusable: an ascending number and a title, a date, a status drawn from "[Proposed/Accepted/Deprecated/Superseded]", a context section that must be "a value-neutral description of the forces at play", a decision section whose sentence begins "We will…", and a consequences section. Records are stored under `doc/adr` in the repository and are "added as pull request in the proposed status", so the record enters review as a proposal rather than arriving as a fait accompli.

**Trade studies are handled more thinly than the previous version of this note implied.** The trade studies page gives a five-step flow and one negative rule — "Trade studies should be avoided if there is a clear solution choice" — and it carries **no required fields of its own**. The fields live on a separate template page. The page tells a team when a trade study is warranted and roughly how to run one; it does not tell them what a completed one contains.

**Engineering Feasibility Spikes is the most operationally prescriptive page in the section.** It opens the spike with a **ninety-minute pre-mortem** framed by one question — "Imagine the project has failed. What problems and challenges caused this failure?" — and then runs **weekly thirty-minute sharing calls with the whole crew attending**, so that findings do not stay with the person who found them. It also carries a warning that constrains how spike output may be used: "the code written for the spikes is not going to serve as the code for the final solution". Design Reviews itself frames its measures as diagnostic questions rather than metrics, and supplies a decision rule for the case where the room does not converge: "Disagree and commit!".

Developer Experience covers the inner loop for client applications, Copilots, cross-platform tasks, dev containers, running pipelines locally, and fake services, along with an onboarding guide template. It also does three things the previous version of this note did not record. It defines a **DevEx Champion** as a named role rather than a shared aspiration. It names two measures — **Time to First E2E Result** and **Time To First Commit** — which are both time-to-first-outcome measures for a newly joined engineer. And it states an **"F5 Contract"**: clone the repository, configure it, press F5, and the thing runs. That contract is a falsifiable claim about a repository and is the most directly testable idea in the section.

Documentation is thinner than its navigation suggests. Creating Good Documentation is **two sentences of links, dated May 2021** — the oldest page encountered in the deep read. The section around it covers establishing and managing documentation, guidance for code, pull requests, REST APIs, work items, projects and repositories, plus DocFx and MkDocs recipes and wiki tooling.

**Engineering Feedback is a bug-report writing template, and nothing more.** The guidance page specifies the parts of a good feedback item: a Title, a Summary, a Categorization block covering the Azure service, the type, the stage of work, the impact, the time frame and a priority on a P0 to P3 scale, then Reproduction Steps and Notes. **There is no routing, no triage, no ownership, no service-level expectation and no closure loop anywhere on it.** It teaches an engineer how to write a report that a product team can act on. It does not describe, and does not claim to describe, what happens to the report afterwards.

The ML and AI Projects section covers agile considerations specific to machine learning work, envisioning and problem formulation with a summary template, feasibility studies, data exploration, model experimentation, the proposed ML process, testing data science and MLOps code, profiling, and TPM considerations. **Responsible AI in ISE is mandatory process rather than stated principle**, and it is the strongest page in the section: "Every ML project in ISE goes through a Responsible AI (RAI) assessment", producing a review document and an impact assessment, structured around fifteen questions of which the first is the one most worth teaching — "Can the problem be solved without AI? Would simpler technology suffice?" The resulting documents "remain living documents" rather than being filed at approval. **Feasibility Studies runs "between 4-16 weeks"** and, unusually for this playbook, explicitly relaxes its own rigour for that window: "not all experimentation requires full test coverage and code review." A standard that names the conditions under which it does not apply is more useful than one that does not.

**The ML Model Production Checklist explicitly declines to be a gate.** It is eleven items, each expanded with worked examples, with no thresholds, no scoring and no stated definition of passing — and it says so: "there might be scenarios where it is not possible to check all the items on this checklist. However, it is advised to go through all items and make informed decisions based on your specific use case." What it supports is a structured discussion before a production decision, item by item, with the decision recorded. What it refuses is being used as a pass condition.

Observability is organised around dashboards, logging, metrics and tracing, plus alerting, correlation IDs, diagnostic tools, profiling, known pitfalls, observability as code, and specific guidance for microservices, machine learning, CI/CD pipelines and Azure Databricks, with tooling coverage of OpenTelemetry, Prometheus, Loki and Kubernetes dashboards. **The Logs vs Metrics vs Traces page draws its distinction by data volume and processing cost rather than by semantics**, which is not how the distinction is usually taught and is the more operationally honest framing: the question it answers is what each instrument costs to keep, not what each one means. It also frames tracing as an optimisation instrument rather than an incident-response one — "Its purpose isn't reactive, but is instead focused on optimization."

Security covers application security analysis with rules of engagement, and threat modelling. **The Threat Modeling page is a signpost rather than a treatment**: four phases at roughly two sentences each, with STRIDE linked out rather than explained and the worked example held on a separate page. Source Control covers component versioning, merge strategies, branch naming, handling secrets in source control, and Git guidance including Git LFS and VFS. A short UI/UX section covers recommended technologies.

**Non-Functional Requirements has no index page.** `/non-functional-requirements/` returns HTTP 404. The fifteen category pages beneath it are nonetheless real and substantive — accessibility, availability, capacity, compliance, data integrity, disaster recovery and continuity, internationalisation and localisation, interoperability, maintainability, performance, portability, privacy, reliability, scalability and usability — so the previous version of this note's claim that the categories are enumerated rather than gestured at survives the deep read. The substantive guidance on *capturing* non-functional requirements is not filed under that path at all; it is at `design/design-patterns/non-functional-requirements-capture-guide/`.

**"Who is ISE?" is recruiting copy.** It is two paragraphs and a link to a blog post, closing with "If you like variety, coding in many languages… then come talk to us." It is not a description of the engagement model, and the previous version of this note's account of the institutional feedback loop rested partly on a claim made there rather than on any operational page.

## Method and evidence base

This is codified organisational practice, not research. Its authority comes from being the working standard of a large customer-embedded engineering group, refined across engagements and maintained in public where it is open to external scrutiny and contribution. It offers no comparative evidence that its practices outperform alternatives, and it must not be taught as though it did.

Three qualifications, all of them from the deep read rather than from the site's self-description.

**First, the maintenance is uneven and the document therefore has no single currency.** Page dates in the read span 2021 to 2025. A citation to "the playbook" is not pinnable; a citation to a page and its footer date is.

**Second, the one place the playbook reaches for a magnitude, it does so without warrant.** The code-review page's closing assertion that the cost of review is negative has no measurement, no baseline and no source attached to it. Nothing else in the read makes a quantitative claim, so the failure is isolated — but it is the kind of sentence that gets quoted, and it should be flagged wherever the page is used.

**Third, the source declines the strongest use the previous version of this note proposed for it.** The ML Model Production Checklist states in its own words that not all its items will be checkable and that teams should make informed decisions instead. That is a refusal of gate semantics, written by the authors, and it must be honoured rather than argued around.

What the playbook does offer, uniquely among the sources in this strand, is specificity about artefacts: field lists, stored locations, review states, time boxes and named roles, at a level of detail a student team can actually produce and be shown against.

## Vocabulary the source introduces

- **Engineering Fundamentals Checklist** — twelve sections of binary items covering what a team is expected to have in place. Assessable where the item names an artefact.
- **The First Week of an ISE Project** — an index of playbook links arranged as a five-day sequence. A sequencing device, not a guide.
- **Working agreement, team manifesto, definition of ready, definition of done** — the explicit social contracts a team writes for itself.
- **Minimal valuable slice** — the smallest increment that delivers customer value.
- **Trade study** — a documented comparison of design options against stated criteria, to be avoided where the choice is already clear.
- **Architecture Decision Record and decision log** — the durable written record of why a design decision was made, entering review in a proposed status.
- **Engineering feasibility spike** — a time-boxed investigation to reduce technical uncertainty, opened with a pre-mortem and closed without contributing its code to the product.
- **Pre-mortem** — a structured session imagining the project has already failed, run before the work rather than after it.
- **Responsible AI assessment** — a mandatory pre-project review producing a review document and an impact assessment that remain living.
- **Engineering feedback** — a structured written report of product friction, with a specified set of fields.
- **Inner loop** — the local develop-run-test cycle, and the target of deliberate optimisation.
- **DevEx Champion** — a named individual accountable for developer experience on a team.
- **F5 Contract** — the commitment that a cloned repository can be configured and run by pressing F5.
- **Time to First E2E Result, Time To First Commit** — the two named developer-experience measures.
- **Observability pillars** — dashboards, logging, metrics and tracing.
- **DevSecOps** — security practices embedded into the CI/CD pipeline rather than applied afterwards.

## What to remember

- The playbook is meant to be fixed, not just followed; a standard nobody may amend is a standard nobody owns. Its uneven page dates show the amendment right being exercised selectively.
- Quality is stated to outrank throughput, in an organisation delivering under customer pressure.
- Code is defined as incomplete without tests, and tests must arrive in the same pull request as the code they cover.
- Distributed and embedded work makes written context mandatory rather than optional.
- Scope discipline is mechanical: new work becomes a new backlog item, never a bigger old one.
- Architecture decision records are the durable evidence of engineering judgement, and their field list is explicit enough to be reused directly.
- A spike opens with a pre-mortem and its code is disposable; those two rules together are what stop a spike becoming an unreviewed prototype in production.
- Responsible AI is a mandatory assessment with a standing first question about whether AI is needed at all.
- **Engineering feedback, as documented, is a writing standard for a single report. It is not an institutional loop, and the playbook nowhere describes one.**
- **The ML Model Production Checklist is a discussion structure that explicitly declines to be a gate.**
- Non-functional requirements are enumerated so that omitting one becomes visible, even though the section has no index page.

## Why it matters for the course

This remains the strongest openly published account of what a customer-embedded engineering organisation requires of its engineers, and the deep read makes it more useful rather than less, because it replaces a general impression of completeness with a specific set of reusable artefacts.

Three things carry the weight. **First, the artefact specifications.** The architecture decision record field list, the spike's pre-mortem and sharing cadence, the Responsible AI assessment's fifteen questions, the engineering feedback field set and the F5 Contract are all concrete enough to be handed to a student team as a specification and marked against. That is worth more to a course than the checklist is, because each of them names an object the team must produce rather than a condition the team must be in. **Second, the design review apparatus** — decision records, decision logs, trade studies, feasibility spikes — gives the course a way to assess engineering judgement in writing rather than inferring it from a finished system, which matters because judgement is what forward-deployed work actually tests. **Third, the placement of Responsible AI inside the engineering process as a mandatory assessment with a standing "could this be solved without AI?" question** is directly reusable, and it is a better model for the course's own production-readiness requirement than the ML Model Production Checklist, which declines that role.

**Two of the previous version's arguments for this source have been withdrawn.** It is not the institutional half of a field-to-product flywheel; the playbook documents how to write one report and says nothing about what happens next, and the flywheel account in this repository rests on [research/05-fde-craft/palantir-fde-practitioner-writing.md](/research/05-fde-craft/palantir-fde-practitioner-writing.md), which describes the loop and — importantly — the governance constraint on it. And the checklists are not a production-readiness rubric; one of them says in its own words that it is not.

**A new reason to hold this note has appeared since the previous version was written.** This folder now also holds [research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-fde-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-fde-delivery-practice.md), **a public paraphrase of an internal Microsoft forward-deployed delivery standard** that is built on this playbook and maps its own item tags back to the Engineering Fundamentals Checklist. That note is filed under a restrictive provenance rule — paraphrased, never linked, never quoted by label — and nothing from it is reproduced here. What matters for this note is the structural relationship: **this playbook is now the substrate of a second, more specific standard.** The later material does not restate the engineering expectations; it sequences them into phases with owners and durations, and attaches an automation status to each. That makes the checklist's genre visible in a way reading the checklist alone does not — it is an inventory of expectations, and a delivery standard is what you get when somebody puts an owner and a date on each line of it. For the course, the pair is more instructive than either alone: the playbook supplies what must exist, the derived standard supplies one organisation's opinion about when and by whom, and the gap between them is exactly the planning judgement a forward-deployed engineer is expected to exercise.

## How to teach it

- **Lecture:** use the general guidance list as a discussion text and ask students which items they would push back on, particularly quality over getting things done and the prohibition on adding scope.
- **Lecture:** put the Evidence and Measures page's closing assertion in front of students without comment and ask what would have to be measured for it to be true. It is a compact, real specimen of an unwarranted magnitude claim inside an otherwise disciplined document, and it is more instructive than a constructed example.
- **Lab:** have each project team complete the Engineering Fundamentals Checklist in week one and again at project close, and grade the delta — **but only on the artefact-bearing items.** Ask the teams to identify, as part of the exercise, which items they cannot honestly answer and why. The unfalsifiable items are the point of that second question.
- **Lab:** use The First Week of an ISE Project as a **sequencing** exercise, not a content one. Give teams the day-by-day ordering and ask them to defend or reject it — in particular, why source control might reasonably be established on Day 2, after sprint planning, rather than before anything else.
- **Lab:** require architecture decision records for every architecturally significant decision, using the playbook's field list verbatim, entered in a proposed status by pull request and stored under `doc/adr`. Assess the reasoning and the value-neutrality of the context section, not the outcome.
- **Lab:** require one trade study with a real decision, real options and stated criteria — **noting that the fields come from the template page, not the trade studies page** — and require teams to justify that the study was warranted at all, against the source's own rule that a study should be avoided where the choice is clear.
- **Lab:** run a spike exactly as specified: a ninety-minute pre-mortem opening on the failure question, weekly thirty-minute sharing calls, and an explicit rule that the spike's code will not be carried into the product.
- **Lab:** use the ML Model Production Checklist as a **structured discussion held before a production decision**, requiring a recorded decision and rationale against each of the eleven items. **Do not score it and do not use it as a pass condition** — the source disclaims that use directly.
- **Assessment:** require a Responsible AI assessment before any project involving a model, using the source's fifteen questions and opening on "Can the problem be solved without AI? Would simpler technology suffice?", and require the resulting document to be updated at least once during the project rather than filed at approval.
- **Assessment:** require a structured engineering feedback item written against a real tool or platform the team used, following the playbook's field set — and teach it explicitly as **a writing standard for a single report**, then ask students what the report's fate would be in an organisation that had not specified one.
- **Assessment:** hold every team repository to the F5 Contract, tested by another team cloning it cold, and record Time To First Commit for that other team as the measure.
- **Assessment:** assign the observability material and require that projects instrument logging, metrics and tracing distinctly, defending the choice of what went into each **on the source's own basis — data volume and processing cost — rather than on semantics.**

## Limits and cautions

- **The playbook is unevenly maintained and cannot be dated as a whole.** Pin every citation to a page and its own footer date. Material dated 2021 sits in the same navigation as material dated 2025 with nothing to distinguish them but the footer.
- **Depth varies far more than the navigation suggests.** Several entries that read as substantial sections are stubs or signposts, and one section index does not exist at all. Confirm a page has content before assigning it.
- **One page asserts a magnitude with no evidence.** The claim that code review costs less than nothing must not be repeated as a finding by anything in this repository.
- **Parts of the Engineering Fundamentals Checklist are not falsifiable as written**, and the Security section changes grammatical form partway through. It is a good inventory and a partial instrument.
- **The ML Model Production Checklist explicitly refuses gate semantics.** Using it as a pass condition contradicts the source.
- The playbook reflects a specific organisational culture and toolchain, with a visible Microsoft and Azure orientation — the code-review evidence guidance assumes Azure DevOps branch policies specifically. Students should extract the principle and not assume the tooling.
- Its scale assumes a professional delivery team over a full engagement, and it will overwhelm a student project if adopted wholesale rather than selectively.
- It is prescriptive practice with no comparative evidence base, and should not be taught as though its recommendations were empirically established.
- Published guidance describes the intended standard, not measured compliance; a course should not treat it as evidence that the practices are universally followed.
- It is strong on engineering discipline and comparatively light on the customer discovery and negotiation craft that the other sources in this strand supply, so it complements rather than replaces them.
