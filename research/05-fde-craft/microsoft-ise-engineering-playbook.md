# Cliff Notes — Microsoft ISE Engineering Fundamentals Playbook

## Citation and access

- **Author:** Microsoft Industry Solutions Engineering (ISE), a customer-embedded engineering organisation within Microsoft
- **Title:** Engineering Fundamentals Playbook (commonly referred to as the Code With Engineering Playbook)
- **Source consulted:** <https://microsoft.github.io/code-with-engineering-playbook/>
- **Repository:** <https://github.com/microsoft/code-with-engineering-playbook>
- **Sitemap:** <https://microsoft.github.io/code-with-engineering-playbook/sitemap.xml>
- **Access status:** Open access, published as a MkDocs Material site from a public GitHub repository, open to external contribution via pull request with a CONTRIBUTING guide in the repo
- **Read depth: complete.** The site's own sitemap enumerates **243 pages**. All 243 were fetched and read in full on 2026-07-30, across eight parallel passes divided by section. Exactly one URL in the navigation returns HTTP 404 — `/non-functional-requirements/`, the section index — and no other page failed to resolve. Section-by-section counts and the substantive-versus-recipe classification are in [Structure of the original](#structure-of-the-original); every correction the full reading forced on the earlier thirty-two-page reading is in [Verification status](#verification-status).
- **Currency — the playbook has no single date, and no citation to it may carry one.** Every page carries its own footer date, and across all 243 pages **those dates run from 7 May 2021 to 26 September 2025**. The oldest date is shared by at least four pages in different sections — Creating Good Documentation, the Engineering Feedback guidance page, End-to-End Testing Methods and Alerting. The newest cluster, 26 September 2025, covers the Agile Development and CI/CD indexes and most of the team-agreement pages; the AI-authorship page under Developer Experience is 19 August 2025; the Automated Testing index is 3 June 2025; the machine-learning pages are uniformly 7 February 2025; and a very large middle band sits on 22 or 26 August 2024, which appears to be a bulk edit rather than a review. **The playbook is maintained unevenly, and the unevenness is not visible in the navigation.** Any citation must be pinned to the individual page and that page's own footer date, never to the site.

## What this source is

This is the working engineering handbook of a Microsoft organisation whose entire delivery model is embedding engineers with customers to build software jointly. It is not a whitepaper or a maturity model; it is the operational standard the organisation holds itself to, published verbatim, and it is valuable to a forward-deployed curriculum precisely because it is the working artefact rather than a description of one.

**It is not, however, one genre of document, and the complete reading makes that far sharper than the partial one did.** Across 243 pages it resolves into six distinct kinds of writing, and they are interleaved without any signal in the navigation that distinguishes them.

1. **Normative practice pages** — quotable rules stated as team commitments or hard prohibitions. Reviewer Guidance, the Automated Testing index, Observability Best Practices, Dependency and Container Scanning, Secrets Management, Evaluate Open Source Software, the Git guidance page, Reliability, Privacy Data Handling.
2. **Artefact templates** — literal skeletons with named headings a team fills in. There are at least ten, and they are the most directly reusable thing on the site: the trade study template, four design-review templates, the pull request template, the onboarding guide template, the non-functional-requirements capture template, the iterative performance-test template, the test-type template and the end-to-end case-study template.
3. **Worked examples** — a real instance rather than a rule: two architecture decision records, a fifteen-row decision log, a GitOps trade study, a threat model of an IoT Edge system, a test-driven-development walkthrough, a working agreement with literal meeting times in Central European Time.
4. **Recipes** — step-by-step vendor procedure pinned to a tool and a version. Roughly a quarter of the site. `detect-secrets` pinned at `v1.4.0` with a workaround for an upstream bug; `actions/checkout@v2`; Terraform `0.12.4`; fifteen screenshots of an Azure DevOps card editor.
5. **Tool catalogues and link lists** — pages whose entire content is pointers outward. `recipes-observability/`, `distributed-system-design-reference/`, `object-oriented-design-reference/`, `cloud-resource-design-guidance/`, `documentation/tools/integrations/`.
6. **Stubs** — two to five sentences under a title that promises a treatment. Creating Good Documentation, DevSecOps, `agile-development/roles/`, `source-control/secrets-management/`, `privacy/`, Static Code Analysis, the Documentation Pull Requests page.

One page, "Who is ISE?", is recruiting copy. One section index does not exist: `/non-functional-requirements/` returns HTTP 404 while the seventeen pages beneath it remain reachable. **Reading the site as a uniform standard overstates the depth of close to half of it — and, symmetrically, understates the parts that are genuinely excellent, because the good pages are not marked either.**

## The argument in full

The playbook opens by defining the engineer rather than the process, and the definition is deliberately obligation-shaped. An engineer working on an ISE project has responsibilities to their team as mentor, coach and lead; knows their playbook, follows their playbook, and fixes their playbook if it is broken; leads by example; and strives to understand how their work fits into a broader context and ensures the outcome. The third clause is the interesting one. A playbook that engineers are expected to repair rather than merely obey is a living standard, and the fact that the repair mechanism is a public pull request against a public repository makes the claim testable rather than rhetorical. The uneven page dates recorded above are the test's result: the repair right is real, and it is exercised on some parts of the document and not on others.

The rationale offered for having a playbook at all is stated in three parts: to increase overall efficiency for both individual team members and the team as a whole, to reduce mistakes and avoid common pitfalls, and to strive to be better engineers by learning from other people's shared experience. This is a knowledge-transfer argument, and it matters in a customer-embedded organisation because engineers are dispersed across engagements and cannot learn by osmosis from adjacent desks.

The general guidance section is the playbook's philosophical core and is worth teaching close to verbatim, because several of its instructions cut against the reflexes engineering students are trained into. It insists on keeping the code quality bar high and on valuing quality and precision over simply getting things done, which is a striking position for an organisation delivering under customer time pressure. It instructs engineers to work diligently on the one important thing, rather than in parallel on several. It recognises the distributed condition explicitly, requiring that context be shared through the wiki, through Teams and through backlog items rather than held in individual heads. It says to make the simple thing work now, and to build fewer features today provided they work amazingly. It forbids adding scope to an existing backlog item, requiring a new item instead, which is a scope-discipline mechanism rather than a bookkeeping preference. It states that the goal is to ship incremental customer value. It requires backlog item details to be kept current, and it requires that product issues be reported with clear, repeatable engineering feedback. It closes with a collective-ownership statement: we all own our code.

**The two entry points are not what their titles suggest, and the difference matters.**

**The First Week of an ISE Project is a navigation index, not an onboarding guide.** Its own stated purpose is to "Organize content in the playbook for quick reference and discoverability". Every item on it is a link to another page; there is no prose, no narrative and no instruction of its own. What it does supply is a *sequence*, and the sequence is genuinely informative because it is counter-intuitive in one place: the page is shaped as a working week — Before Starting, then Day 1 through Day 5 — and **source control is established on Day 2, after sprint planning rather than before it.** A team that reads the ordering as a claim about dependency learns something; a team that expects the page to teach it anything learns nothing.

**The Engineering Fundamentals Checklist is assessable only in part.** It is twelve sections of flat binary items, roughly sixty of them, and it is not uniform in kind. Perhaps two-thirds are mechanically checkable against an artefact — "The default target branch is locked", "A minimum number of reviewers (usually 2) for a PR merge is enforced by policy", "Unit tests cover the majority of all components (>90% if possible)". Several others are not falsifiable as written; "The agile process is clearly defined within team" has no observable that distinguishes a team that satisfies it from one that does not. The Security section also slips out of declarative form partway down and begins asking questions rather than asserting conditions, so items in the same list are doing different grammatical work. **The checklist makes the artefact-bearing part of the playbook assessable. It does not make the playbook assessable.**

The body is organised by engineering discipline, and the complete reading permits a much more specific account of each than the navigation does.

**Agile Development, twenty-one pages, is the section the organisation is still actively revising** — most of it carries the newest footer date on the site. Its index states seven fundamentals as goals rather than methods and says so: "We care about the goal for each activity, but not necessarily about how they are accomplished." The strongest page by a distance is Ceremonies, which is not the procedural summary its title implies — roughly two-fifths of it is estimation theory, with three worked estimation methods and a flat prohibition on converting story points to days. It also carries the section's most interesting judgement, which is that **the dev lead should assign work rather than team members self-assigning**, argued on inclusion grounds: "Self assignment by team members can give a feeling of fairness in how work is split in the team. Sometimes, this ends up not being the case as it can give an advantage to the loudest or more experienced voices in the team." Virtual Collaboration is the section's most original page and is misnamed — it proposes **Red Team Testing**, in which two developers jointly design an interface and then split, one writing the implementation and one writing tests against the public interface without seeing it. That is an alternative to pairing, not a form of it. The Async Collaboration Checklist supplies the only stated service level anywhere on the site: "Mark urgent reviews clearly; otherwise assume a 24-48 hour review window for non-urgent PRs." Against these, `agile-development/roles/` is four sentences and three links to scrumguides.org, whose only original content is a naming preference — "We prefer using 'process lead' over 'scrum master'" — and the only real job description for that role is buried in the Working Agreement page, which is an example instance rather than a template and still contains a redaction where a customer's name was stripped, leaving "All PRs are reviewed by one person from and one from Microsoft".

**Source Control is seven pages and three of them decline to prescribe.** Merge Strategies is three ASCII diagrams and about ten sentences and explicitly refuses to choose: "Agree if you want a linear or non-linear commit history. There are pros and cons to both approaches." Naming Branches gives one convention — `<user alias>/[feature/bug/hotfix]/<work item ID>_<title>` — and immediately disclaims it, and **it is contradicted by two other pages on the site**, which use `feature/123-add-working-agreement` and `user/your_alias/feature_name`. The section's own secrets page is a five-sentence redirect to the CI/CD material. The weight is carried by Git Guidance, a long and genuinely useful tutorial covering merge-conflict resolution, reflog recovery, revert versus reset and submodules, with hard commit rules — "Never commit incomplete code", "Don't mix whitespace changes with functional code changes", a fifty-character subject line and a body wrapped at seventy-two — and a warning that `git reset` will not remove committed secrets from history.

**Code Reviews, nineteen pages, is the section with the best single page and the worst structural padding.** Ten pages are practice; nine are language recipes, and all nine follow one skeleton — style-guide pointer, linter install, formatter, build-validation YAML, closing checklist — in which only the closing checklist is review guidance. **Reviewer Guidance remains the richest page on the site.** It prescribes a two-pass structure, a first pass for shape and intent and a second for detail, and it does not permit sampling: "Read every line changed". It states the norm that makes review survivable socially, "What matters is a bug caught, not who made it, not who found it, not who fixed it", and it closes the commonest loophole outright: "Tests should always be committed in the same PR as the code itself ('I'll add tests next' is not acceptable)." Author Guidance closes the matching loophole from the other side — "If the requested change is within the scope of the task, 'I'll do it later' is not an acceptable reason!" — and requires reviewers **from both organisations** on a code-with engagement. Inclusion in Code Review is unusual and genuinely curriculum-usable: it names three non-inclusive behaviours, treats impostor phenomenon directly, and makes the sharpest empirical observation in the section, that large commits get biased review, the senior engineer's approved unread and the junior engineer's not reviewed at all. **Both Pull Requests and Process Guidance promise a size rule and refuse to give a number**, the latter explicitly: "It's not a matter of setting a code line limit, but rather using common sense."

**Evidence and Measures is narrower than a reader would guess from its title.** The evidence it proposes is branch-policy verification — "Verification of the policies on the main branch in Azure DevOps… may be sufficient evidence that a project team is conducting code reviews" — which is evidence that review was *required*, not that it was *done well*. The measures it offers are defect removal efficiency, time spent reviewing, and lines of code reviewed per unit time, and it acknowledges that these are gathered manually, "e.g. in an Excel sheet". The page then closes with an assertion that this repository must not carry forward: it states that **"the cost of doing code reviews is actually negative!"** No evidence, no measurement, no comparison and no source is offered for it anywhere on the page. **It is exactly the class of unwarranted magnitude claim this repository refuses**, and it should be taught as a specimen of the failure rather than repeated as a finding. The complete reading found that it is not the only one; see [Method and evidence base](#method-and-evidence-base).

**Automated Testing, twenty-eight pages, is the most internally coherent section and the one that states its norms as commitments rather than advice.** Its index is the only place on the site where practice is written in the first person plural as a standard the organisation binds itself to — "We consider code to be incomplete if it is not accompanied by tests", "We write our tests early and block any further code merging if tests fail." The section's strongest page is Fault Injection Testing, which builds properly on the dependability literature, defines the fault-error-failure cycle, separates fault injection from chaos engineering, and requires blast-radius reasoning in advance. Shadow Testing and Synthetic Monitoring are the two genuinely shift-right topics and both are substantive. Consumer-Driven Contract Testing exists to remove the end-to-end rung entirely. Against that, **the section carries two incompatible taxonomies without reconciling them.** One page is a single table with no prose that arranges unit, integration, system and end-to-end tests on a waterfall ladder ordered by scope, environment, data fidelity and timing, and it marks end-to-end testing as **Manual** — while the end-to-end pages, the contract-testing page and the index all assume it is automated, and the End-to-End Testing page itself concedes that "the E2E test is often performed manually as the cost of automating such test cases is too high to be afforded by any organization." System testing is defined in that one table and nowhere else on the site.

**CI/CD is twenty-eight pages of which more than half are recipes, and the substance is concentrated in four places.** Continuous Integration is the longest and densest page in the section, covering build automation, environment dependencies, infrastructure as code, configuration validation, isolated environments and observability of the pipeline, and it states the durable rule plainly: "A single command should have the capability of building the system." Continuous Delivery takes its release strategy from Humble and Farley and defines production-like on operational criteria. **The DevSecOps page itself is a near-stub — three short sections and a six-item link list — and every page beneath it carries the weight.** Two of those are excellent. Evaluate Open Source Software gives eleven criteria for taking a dependency, opening on "The best dependency is the one you don't have." Azure DevOps Service Connection Security is a genuine threat write-up rather than a settings guide: it names the attack in which a YAML pipeline triggered from a temporary branch executes arbitrary commands through a service connection with no review. The secrets material is the section's hardest normative content, and it is unusually honest about its own limits — it concedes that environment-variable techniques do not protect a secret that is already plaintext in memory. The GitOps pages contain the section's best trade study, comparing encrypted-in-repository against external key vault with per-tool advantages, and reaching a verdict; **but one of its sibling recipes then recommends the encrypt-in-repository approach it advises against**, and no page reconciles the two. **Structurally, three pages are misfiled**: static code analysis, credential scanning and secret rotation all sit under `secrets-management/`, and only one of them is about secrets.

**Observability is twenty-four pages arranged around four pillars, and the pillars are wildly uneven.** Logging is the longest and best of them, with roughly thirteen rules including "Log a raised exception only once", a preference for standard output in containers, and a taxonomy of what to log at startup and on incoming and outgoing requests. Metrics is solid, with a cardinality rule — "do not use unique strings or those with high cardinality as the value of a label". **Tracing is about ten lines and does not define a span, a trace context or propagation**; those definitions live on two other pages that are not filed as pillars. Dashboards is filed under Insights on the index while living in the `pillars/` directory. The strongest durable argument in the section is Observability as Code, which requires dashboards, alerts and log queries to be source-controlled and deployed by infrastructure as code, and gives seven reasons, the sharpest of which is that this brings observability configuration into peer review. Pitfalls is short and unusually disciplined: "It is recommended to collect and measure what you need and not what you can", and, as a standing review rule, "any data that is collected but not aggregated or alerted on must be reviewed if it is still required." **The section is also the most rotted on the site.** Its OpenTelemetry page still quotes a roadmap targeting "the first half of 2021" and calls the project "a very recent project"; its Databricks page recommends Ganglia, which has been removed from the runtime, and a monitoring library that is archived; two of the six Kubernetes dashboards it lists are archived projects; and its Loki page recommends Promtail, which has been deprecated in favour of a successor.

**Design is forty-three pages — by far the largest section — and it is two different documents wearing one name.** Nineteen pages sit under `design/` proper and twenty-four under `design/design-reviews/`, and the quality distribution is not the same in the two halves. In `design/` proper, four pages are pure link lists that prescribe nothing, and two of those carry 2023 footer dates and have not been touched since. What is substantive there is specific and worth having: the data-heavy guidance page, which prefers optimistic concurrency to two-phase locking and treats eventual consistency as a last resort rather than a scaling strategy; the Azure network guidance, with hub-and-spoke, a centralised firewall and roughly nine address-allocation rules; the four diagram-type pages, which prescribe scope rather than notation and treat a stale diagram as actively harmful; the non-functional-requirements capture guide; and the sustainability pair, of which the disclaimers page is the most intellectually honest content in the whole section because **it exists to undercut the checklist on the page above it**, row by row.

**`design/design-reviews/` is where the playbook is at its most reusable, because it is where the templates are.** The architecture decision record page states its fields explicitly: an ascending number and a title, a date, a status drawn from "[Proposed/Accepted/Deprecated/Superseded]", a context section that must be "a value-neutral description of the forces at play", a decision section whose sentence begins "We will…" or "We have agreed to…", and a consequences section. Records are stored under `doc/adr` and are "added as pull request in the proposed status", so the record enters review as a proposal rather than arriving as a fait accompli. It also gives a collision rule for parallel authorship: give the record the identifier of the work item it relates to. Alongside it sit **four design-review templates at different scopes** — feature or story, milestone or epic, task, and technical spike — each with an explicit heading list, and each requiring a `[DRAFT/WIP]` prefix in the title while the document is in progress. The procedural core is Async Design Reviews, which puts design documents in a pull-requestable repository directory excluded from continuous-integration triggers, and gives a six-step workflow with one rule worth quoting: "After two round trips of question/response, resort to synchronous communication for resolution". The Engagement Process page attaches all of this to the sprint cadence and states the maintenance rule bluntly: "Artifacts that are not up to date are `bugs` in the design."

**Trade studies are documented across two pages and only one of them is usable as a specification.** The Trade Studies page gives a five-step flow and one negative rule — "Trade studies should be avoided if there is a clear solution choice" — and carries **no required fields of its own**. The template page carries them, in order, and adds a floor: "there should be at least two options compared, otherwise you didn't need a trade study." **The two pages then disagree on the ceiling**: the template says narrow to two or three, the index says pick two to four. And the only worked trade study in the repository, the GitOps comparison, does not follow the template — it omits seven of its required sections and renames another — so a student copying the example would fail the specification.

**Engineering Feasibility Spikes is the most operationally prescriptive page in the section.** It opens the spike with a **ninety-minute pre-mortem** framed by one question — "Imagine the project has failed. What problems and challenges caused this failure?" — and then runs **weekly thirty-minute sharing calls with the whole crew attending**, so that findings do not stay with the person who found them. It also carries a warning that constrains how spike output may be used: "the code written for the spikes is not going to serve as the code for the final solution". The separate Technical Spike page adds the sharper version of the same constraint: "The goal of a spike should be fact-finding, not decision-making or recommendation." Design Reviews itself frames its measures as diagnostic questions rather than metrics — cost of change, reviewer participation, time to potential solutions, time to decisions — and supplies a decision rule for the case where the room does not converge: "Disagree and commit!".

**Developer Experience is ten pages, of which six are recipes, and the index carries almost all of the durable content.** It defines a **DevEx Champion** as a named role rather than a shared aspiration. It names two measures — **Time to First E2E Result** and **Time To First Commit** — both of which are time-to-first-outcome measures for a newly joined engineer. And it states an **"F5 Contract"**: clone the repository, configure it, press F5, and the thing runs. That contract is a falsifiable claim about a repository and remains the most directly testable idea on the site. Two other pages here are worth naming. The onboarding guide template is a genuine six-heading skeleton. And the AI-authorship page, the second-newest page on the site, is the only place the playbook addresses generated code: it distinguishes the agentic case, where the coding agent is the commit author and the supervising human is recorded with a `Co-authored-by` trailer, from the assistant case, where it recommends a team convention using **a deliberately different trailer key**, `Assistant-model:`, on the stated grounds that Git tooling expects `Co-authored-by` values to be email addresses and that authorship should not be confused with assistance. It gives three triggers for applying it, of which the first is that more than half the lines in the commit were generated.

**Documentation is eighteen pages and is the weakest large section on the site.** Eight of the eighteen are stubs, pure redirects or two-link pages, including Creating Good Documentation, which is two sentences of links dated May 2021, and the Pull Requests page, which is four bullets all of which point into the code-reviews section. What survives is worth having. The Documentation index carries an excellent **taxonomy of documentation failure** — non-existent, hidden, incomplete, inaccurate, obsolete, out of order, duplicate, and written as an afterthought. The Projects and Repositories page is the real requirement page and is pure enumeration: per-repository and common documents, README, onboarding guide, contributing guide, branch policy, decision records, trade studies, team agreements, definitions of ready and done. And the Work Items page is genuinely prescriptive, specifying required fields for each work-item type, defining bug priority on a one-to-three scale and severity on a one-to-four scale, and setting a task-size rule: "Each Task should be sized so that they can be completed within a day."

**Engineering Feedback is a bug-report writing template, and nothing more.** The guidance page specifies the parts of a good feedback item: a Title, a Summary, a Categorization block covering the Azure service, the type, the stage of work, the impact, the time frame and a priority on a P0 to P3 scale, then Reproduction Steps and Notes. **There is no routing, no triage, no ownership, no service-level expectation and no closure loop anywhere on it.** It teaches an engineer how to write a report that a product team can act on. It does not describe, and does not claim to describe, what happens to the report afterwards.

The ML and AI Projects section is fourteen pages, all of them dated 7 February 2025 or later, which makes it the most uniformly maintained section on the site. It covers agile considerations specific to machine-learning work, envisioning and problem formulation with a summary template, feasibility studies, data exploration, model experimentation, the proposed ML process, testing data science and MLOps code, profiling, and TPM considerations. **The Proposed ML Process is the section's spine**: six numbered stages — data and problem understanding, responsible AI assessment, feasibility study, baseline model experimentation, model evaluation and experimentation, and model operationalisation — each with a named typical output, and version control as a cross-cutting seventh. It states the reproducibility rule that separates ML engineering from ML research: "While the model outputs can be non-deterministic and hard to reproduce, the process of releasing ML software into production should be reproducible." **Responsible AI in ISE is mandatory process rather than stated principle**: "Every ML project in ISE goes through a Responsible AI (RAI) assessment", producing a review document and an impact assessment, structured around fifteen questions of which the first is the one most worth teaching — "Can the problem be solved without AI? Would simpler technology suffice?" The resulting documents "remain living documents" rather than being filed at approval. **Feasibility Studies runs "between 4-16 weeks"** and, unusually for this playbook, explicitly relaxes its own rigour for that window: "not all experimentation requires full test coverage and code review." A standard that names the conditions under which it does not apply is more useful than one that does not. The section's oddest page is TPM Considerations, which is a programme-management essay aimed at technical programme managers rather than engineers, and which is candid about the shape of the risk — early error-rate gains that flatten, and a cost question the engineering pages never ask: "An 80% 'good enough' rate may save business costs and increase productivity but if going from 80 to 95% would require unimaginable cost and effort. Is it worth it?" It is also the worst-sourced page on the site.

**The ML Model Production Checklist explicitly declines to be a gate.** It is eleven items, each expanded with worked examples, with no thresholds, no scoring and no stated definition of passing — and it says so: "there might be scenarios where it is not possible to check all the items on this checklist. However, it is advised to go through all items and make informed decisions based on your specific use case." What it supports is a structured discussion before a production decision, item by item, with the decision recorded. What it refuses is being used as a pass condition. It is a separate page from the observability section's machine-learning page, which is a comparison of four metric sinks rather than a checklist and should not be confused with it.

**The Logs vs Metrics vs Traces page draws its distinction by data volume and processing cost rather than by semantics**, which is not how the distinction is usually taught and is the more operationally honest framing: the question it answers is what each instrument costs to keep, not what each one means. It also frames tracing as an optimisation instrument rather than an incident-response one — "Its purpose isn't reactive, but is instead focused on optimization."

Security is four pages. **The Threat Modeling page is a signpost rather than a treatment**: four phases at roughly two sentences each, with STRIDE linked out rather than explained. **The worked example, held on a separate page, is however genuine and substantial** — an IoT Edge video, optical-character-recognition and text-to-speech system, with an architecture diagram, an eight-row asset table, a data-flow narrative, twelve enumerated threats and a threat-properties table mapping principle to threat to mitigation. Two features of it are instructive rather than defective: **all twelve threats are marked "Not Mitigated"**, which is what an honest threat model looks like at the point it is first written, and the assumptions are stated separately from the threats. Its defects are clerical — duplicated row numbers, and three of the key artefacts held as images rather than text. UI/UX is two pages, both substantive; the technology page is opinionated and carries one hard security norm about token storage in the browser.

**Non-Functional Requirements has no index page.** `/non-functional-requirements/` returns HTTP 404 — the only 404 in 243 pages — and the sidebar jumps straight from the machine-learning section to Accessibility. Seventeen pages sit beneath the missing index: fifteen category pages, plus a privacy index and a privacy-frameworks page. **The categories are enumerated rather than gestured at, which is the useful property, but they are not uniformly substantive and they do not share a structure.** Only nine of the fifteen use the section's own Characteristics-and-Implementations pairing. Accessibility and Reliability abandon it entirely and are inherited essays — Reliability is the best-written page of the seventeen and states the operating stance directly: "If a user ever makes a mistake, don't ask: 'how could somebody possibly do that?', do ask: 'how can we prevent this from happening in the future?'" Against that, Scalability is two bullets of characteristics and no implementations, Interoperability is four bullets, Usability has broken list rendering, and the privacy index is two sentences. The Privacy Data Handling page is the section's hardest content and states an absolute rule for engagements: "Never directly access production data." **The substantive guidance on *capturing* non-functional requirements is not filed under that path at all**; it is at `design/design-patterns/non-functional-requirements-capture-guide/`, and it is not really a capture process — two-thirds of it is a pre-populated catalogue of thresholds a team could lift straight into a requirements document.

**"Who is ISE?" is recruiting copy.** It is two paragraphs and a link to a blog post, closing with "If you like variety, coding in many languages… then come talk to us." It is not a description of the engagement model, and the previous version of this note's account of the institutional feedback loop rested partly on a claim made there rather than on any operational page.

**A last finding, and one only a complete reading could produce: the playbook contradicts itself in at least a dozen places, and it never notices.** Its testing comparison table says end-to-end tests are manual while three other pages assume they are automated. Its trade-study template says compare two or three options while its own index says two to four. Its sustainability checklist recommends caching, content delivery networks and edge devices as carbon wins while the disclaimers page immediately beneath argues each may be net negative and that cost-optimisation tools do not guarantee carbon savings. Its component, deployment and sequence diagram pages say draw from the start of the engagement; its class diagram page says wait until the code is ten to twenty per cent complete. Its GitOps secrets page recommends an external key vault; a sibling recipe encrypts secrets into the repository. Its branch-naming conventions differ on three pages. Its unit-testing page recommends mocks judiciously while its mocking page recommends avoiding mocking frameworks in favour of hand-written fakes. **None of these are errors of fact; they are the normal residue of a document written by many hands over four years with no reconciliation pass.** They matter here for one reason: a course that presents the playbook as a coherent standard will be caught out by a student who reads two adjacent pages, and the honest teaching move is to hand the contradictions over as material rather than hide them.

## Structure of the original

**The section sizes below are counted from the site's own sitemap and total 243.** They are not what the navigation implies: the largest section is Design, which is bigger than Code Reviews and Documentation put together, and Security — which a reader of the top-level menu would expect to be major — is four pages.

| Section | Pages | Where the substance is | What is recipe, stub or link list |
|---|---|---|---|

**The honest ratio, stated as a judgement of the reading rather than a measurement.** Roughly half the site — on the order of 130 pages — is substantive practice a reader learns something from. Roughly a quarter is recipe or worked example bound to a named tool at a named version, useful as illustration and perishable on the vendor's schedule rather than the playbook's. Roughly a fifth — on the order of 45 to 50 pages — is stub, pure link list, tool catalogue or example instance, and teaches nothing beyond its title. The remainder, about ten pages, is templates, which are the most valuable pages on the site per word and are scattered across five sections with no index that gathers them.

**The distinction is invisible in the navigation and does not track section size.** Documentation is eighteen pages of which eight are stubs; UI/UX is two pages and both are real. A reader who assigns by menu entry will assign badly, and this is the single most useful thing the complete reading produced.

## Key claims and specifics

**From the homepage and general guidance:**

- An ISE engineer knows the playbook, follows it, and fixes it when it is broken; the fix mechanism is a public pull request.
- The playbook exists to increase efficiency, reduce mistakes and common pitfalls, and transfer shared experience.
- Quality and precision are valued above getting things done, stated explicitly.
- Work diligently on the one important thing.
- Context must be shared through the wiki, Teams and backlog items because the team is distributed.
- Make the simple thing work now; build fewer features today but ensure they work amazingly.
- Never add scope to an existing backlog item; create a new one.
- Keep backlog item details up to date, and report product issues with clear, repeatable engineering feedback.
- We all own our code.

## Method and evidence base

This is codified organisational practice, not research. Its authority comes from being the working standard of a large customer-embedded engineering group, refined across engagements and maintained in public where it is open to external scrutiny and contribution. It offers no comparative evidence that its practices outperform alternatives, and it must not be taught as though it did.

Three qualifications, all of them from the complete reading rather than from the site's self-description.

**First, the maintenance is uneven and the document therefore has no single currency.** Page footer dates run from 7 May 2021 to 26 September 2025. A citation to "the playbook" is not pinnable; a citation to a page and its footer date is.

**Second — and this is the finding the complete reading produced that the partial one could not — the playbook's warrant varies enormously by section while its tone does not.** Every page is written in the same flat declarative register, and nothing on the page indicates whether the claim behind it is well grounded, borrowed, or invented on the spot.

The well-sourced end is genuinely well sourced. **Automated Testing carries the densest bibliography on the site**: Fowler on the test pyramid, contract tests and consumer-driven contracts; the Google Testing Blog on end-to-end tests; Kent C. Dodds; the Pact specification; the dependability and failure-mode literature by way of Wikipedia; the Google SRE book; OWASP on fuzzing. **Observability is comparably grounded**, citing the SRE book's service-level-objectives and postmortem chapters, the W3C Trace Context recommendation, B3 propagation and the OpenTelemetry specification-compliance matrix. **Code Reviews cites the two standard external sources** — Google's engineering practices for reviewers and authors, and the SmartBear peer-review study — and Agile Development cites the Scrum Guide with per-section anchors, Tuckman, Cohn and Derby and Larsen.

The poorly sourced end cites nothing at all, in the same voice. **The non-functional-requirements capture guide's threshold catalogue — dozens of specific numbers for uptime, mean time between failures, response time, concurrency, cache hit rate, cyclomatic complexity, coverage and task-completion time — carries no citation of any kind**, and a reader who does not notice will take them for benchmarks rather than illustrations. **The whole of `design/` outside the diagram pages contains a single quantitative claim**, and it is a wide-banded vendor figure from a 2018 Microsoft PDF. **The TPM page's entire reference list is four LinkedIn posts and a Medium article by one named author.** The recipes cite only the documentation of the tool they configure.

**Third, the playbook reaches for a magnitude in at least four places and warrants it in none of them.** The code-review page asserts that "the cost of doing code reviews is actually negative!" with no measurement, baseline or source. The collaboration page asserts that pairing and swarming produce "a higher sprint velocity" with none either. The unit-testing rationale page asserts a six-fold cost multiple for defects found in production, attributing it to a 2008 study **through a dead `ftp://` link that cannot be followed in any current browser**. And the sustainability material carries vendor efficiency figures with bands wide enough to be unfalsifiable, from a document that was seven years old when this reading was made. **This note records all four as unwarranted assertions and adopts none of them.** The previous version of this note said the failure was isolated; the complete reading shows it is a pattern, and the pattern is that the playbook does not distinguish between a practice it has reasoned about and a number it has repeated.

**A fourth qualification, unchanged: the source declines the strongest use the earlier version of this note proposed for it.** The ML Model Production Checklist states in its own words that not all its items will be checkable and that teams should make informed decisions instead. That is a refusal of gate semantics, written by the authors, and it must be honoured rather than argued around.

What the playbook does offer, uniquely among the sources in this strand, is specificity about artefacts: field lists, stored locations, review states, time boxes and named roles, at a level of detail a student team can actually produce and be shown against. **That specificity is concentrated in about ten template pages that the site itself does not index.**

## Vocabulary the source introduces

- **Engineering Fundamentals Checklist** — twelve sections of binary items covering what a team is expected to have in place. Assessable where the item names an artefact.
- **The First Week of an ISE Project** — an index of playbook links arranged as a five-day sequence. A sequencing device, not a guide.
- **Working agreement, team manifesto, definition of ready, definition of done** — the explicit social contracts a team writes for itself.
- **Minimal valuable slice** — the smallest increment that delivers customer value.
- **Trade study** — a documented comparison of design options against stated criteria, to be avoided where the choice is already clear.
- **Architecture Decision Record and decision log** — the durable written record of why a design decision was made, entering review in a proposed status.
- **Engineering feasibility spike** — a time-boxed investigation to reduce technical uncertainty, opened with a pre-mortem and closed without contributing its code to the product.
- **Pre-mortem** — a structured session imagining the project has already failed, run before the work rather than after it.
- **Responsible AI assessment** — a mandatory pre-project review producing a review document and an impact assessment that remain living.
- **Engineering feedback** — a structured written report of product friction, with a specified set of fields.
- **Inner loop** — the local develop-run-test cycle, and the target of deliberate optimisation.
- **DevEx Champion** — a named individual accountable for developer experience on a team.
- **F5 Contract** — the commitment that a cloned repository can be configured and run by pressing F5.
- **Time to First E2E Result, Time To First Commit** — the two named developer-experience measures.
- **Observability pillars** — dashboards, logging, metrics and tracing.
- **DevSecOps** — security practices embedded into the CI/CD pipeline rather than applied afterwards.
- **Crew** — the joint Microsoft-and-customer engineering team, used throughout in preference to "team".
- **Process lead** — the playbook's preferred name for the scrum master role, stated as a deliberate substitution.
- **Red Team Testing** — two engineers jointly design an interface, then split, one implementing and one writing tests against the public interface without seeing the implementation.
- **Shadow testing** — production traffic replicated to a next-version environment whose responses are collected and diffed but never returned to users.
- **Blast radius** — the scope of damage a fault-injection experiment can cause, to be reasoned about before the experiment rather than after.
- **Correlation identifier** — an identifier assigned to a transaction as early as possible and propagated through every downstream component and log.
- **Observability as code** — dashboards, alerts and queries held in source control and deployed by infrastructure as code, so that they enter peer review.
- **Async design review** — a design document reviewed as a pull request in a repository directory excluded from continuous-integration triggers.
- **Operationalization** — the milestone-scope design-review heading that the story-scope template omits; the point at which a design acquires a runtime owner.
- **Assistant-model trailer** — the custom Git trailer proposed for commits whose code was primarily generated by an assistant, deliberately distinct from `Co-authored-by`.

## What to remember

- The playbook is meant to be fixed, not just followed; a standard nobody may amend is a standard nobody owns. Its uneven page dates show the amendment right being exercised selectively.
- Quality is stated to outrank throughput, in an organisation delivering under customer pressure.
- Code is defined as incomplete without tests, and tests must arrive in the same pull request as the code they cover.
- Distributed and embedded work makes written context mandatory rather than optional.
- Scope discipline is mechanical: new work becomes a new backlog item, never a bigger old one.
- Architecture decision records are the durable evidence of engineering judgement, and their field list is explicit enough to be reused directly.
- A spike opens with a pre-mortem and its code is disposable; those two rules together are what stop a spike becoming an unreviewed prototype in production.
- Responsible AI is a mandatory assessment with a standing first question about whether AI is needed at all.
- **Engineering feedback, as documented, is a writing standard for a single report. It is not an institutional loop, and the playbook nowhere describes one.**
- **The ML Model Production Checklist is a discussion structure that explicitly declines to be a gate.**
- Non-functional requirements are enumerated so that omitting one becomes visible, even though the section has no index page and six of the fifteen categories do not follow the section's own structure.
- **About half the site is substantive and about half is recipe, stub or link list, and nothing in the navigation tells you which is which.** That is the practical fact a reader needs before assigning anything from it.
- **The playbook's tone is uniform and its warrant is not.** The best-sourced sections cite Fowler, the Google SRE book and the W3C; the worst cite nothing while sounding exactly the same.
- **It contradicts itself in at least a dozen places and never notices**, which is the normal condition of a multi-author document maintained over four years and is more instructive to show than to hide.

## Why it matters for the course

This remains the strongest openly published account of what a customer-embedded engineering organisation requires of its engineers, and the complete reading makes it more useful rather than less — because it replaces a general impression of completeness with a specific inventory of what is worth taking and a specific list of what is not.

**The single most valuable thing on the site is the set of about ten artefact templates, and the site does not index them.** Gathered, they constitute a complete written apparatus for engineering judgement, at a level of detail a student team can produce and be marked against:

- **The trade study template** — header fields naming who conducted it, against which backlog item, in which sprint, with the decision and the decision makers; then desired outcomes, evaluation criteria, optional key metrics and constraints, an accessibility section, solution hypotheses, and per solution an experiment and its evidence, closing with results, decision and next steps. It also carries the floor — at least two options — and the sibling page carries the negative rule that a study should be avoided where the choice is clear. **That is a complete specification for assessing a comparison, and it is the best artefact on the site.**
- **The four design-review templates at four scopes** — task, feature or story, milestone or epic, and technical spike — which let a course require design writing proportionate to the decision rather than a single essay for everything. The escalation between the story and milestone templates is exactly one heading, **Operationalization**, which is a precise and teachable claim about what changes when a design leaves the story scope.
- **The architecture decision record** — six fields, an explicit status vocabulary, a mandated opening phrase for the decision sentence, a storage location, and the requirement that it enter review as a proposal by pull request rather than arrive as a fait accompli.
- **The pull request template** — nine fields including a six-item checklist and an explicit breaking-change question, all of which are mechanically checkable.
- **The onboarding guide template** — six headings, which pairs with the F5 Contract and the Time To First Commit measure to give a course a testable definition of a repository another team can pick up.
- **The non-functional-requirement capture template** — eight fields, of which **Measurement/Metric** and **Verification Method** are the two that make a non-functional requirement assessable rather than aspirational.

**Second, the design review apparatus as a whole** — decision records, decision logs, trade studies, feasibility spikes, and the async review workflow that puts all of them through pull request — gives the course a way to assess engineering judgement in writing rather than inferring it from a finished system, which matters because judgement is what forward-deployed work actually tests. The spike's ninety-minute pre-mortem, weekly thirty-minute share-out and disposable-code rule are a complete protocol, not a suggestion.

**Third, the placement of Responsible AI inside the engineering process as a mandatory assessment with a standing "could this be solved without AI?" question** is directly reusable, and it is a better model for the course's own production-readiness requirement than the ML Model Production Checklist, which declines that role.

**Fourth — and this is new from the complete reading — the playbook is now the best available specimen of a particular failure mode, which is worth teaching in its own right.** It is a serious, competently written, publicly maintained standard in which about half the pages are excellent and about half are stubs, dead recipes and link lists, and in which the two are indistinguishable from the navigation and from the prose register. Every engineer a course sends into industry will meet a document like this, usually an internal one, and the skill of reading it — checking whether a page has content before relying on it, checking a footer date, noticing that a page cites nothing, noticing that two adjacent pages disagree — is a transferable professional skill this source can teach better than a constructed exercise could.

**Two of the previous version's arguments for this source have been withdrawn.** It is not the institutional half of a field-to-product flywheel; the playbook documents how to write one report and says nothing about what happens next, and the flywheel account in this repository rests on [research/05-fde-craft/palantir-fde-practitioner-writing.md](/research/05-fde-craft/palantir-fde-practitioner-writing.md), which describes the loop and — importantly — the governance constraint on it. And the checklists are not a production-readiness rubric; one of them says in its own words that it is not.

**A new reason to hold this note has appeared since the previous version was written.** This folder now also holds [research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-fde-delivery-practice.md](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-fde-delivery-practice.md), **a public paraphrase of an internal Microsoft forward-deployed delivery standard** that is built on this playbook and maps its own item tags back to the Engineering Fundamentals Checklist. That note is filed under a restrictive provenance rule — paraphrased, never linked, never quoted by label — and nothing from it is reproduced here. What matters for this note is the structural relationship: **this playbook is now the substrate of a second, more specific standard.** The later material does not restate the engineering expectations; it sequences them into phases with owners and durations, and attaches an automation status to each. That makes the checklist's genre visible in a way reading the checklist alone does not — it is an inventory of expectations, and a delivery standard is what you get when somebody puts an owner and a date on each line of it. For the course, the pair is more instructive than either alone: the playbook supplies what must exist, the derived standard supplies one organisation's opinion about when and by whom, and the gap between them is exactly the planning judgement a forward-deployed engineer is expected to exercise.

## How to teach it

- **Lecture:** use the general guidance list as a discussion text and ask students which items they would push back on, particularly quality over getting things done and the prohibition on adding scope.
- **Lecture:** put the Evidence and Measures page's closing assertion in front of students without comment and ask what would have to be measured for it to be true. It is a compact, real specimen of an unwarranted magnitude claim inside an otherwise disciplined document, and it is more instructive than a constructed example. **Then show them the other three** — the sprint-velocity claim, the six-fold defect-cost multiple behind a dead link, and the wide-banded vendor efficiency figures — and ask what the four have in common.
- **Lecture:** hand out four pages from four sections without their footers — one that cites Fowler and the Google SRE book, one that cites the tool's own documentation, one that cites four LinkedIn posts, and one that cites nothing while asserting two dozen numeric thresholds — and ask students to rank them by how much they would rely on each. **The register is identical across all four; only the bibliography differs.** This is the single most transferable lesson the source offers.
- **Lab — read the document before believing it.** Give each team a section of the playbook and require them to classify every page in it as substantive practice, recipe, worked example, template, link list or stub, then defend the classification. Mark the defence, not the classification. This is the exercise that prepares them for the internal wiki they will meet in their first job.
- **Lab:** have each project team complete the Engineering Fundamentals Checklist in week one and again at project close, and grade the delta — **but only on the artefact-bearing items.** Ask the teams to identify, as part of the exercise, which items they cannot honestly answer and why. The unfalsifiable items are the point of that second question.
- **Lab:** use The First Week of an ISE Project as a **sequencing** exercise, not a content one. Give teams the day-by-day ordering and ask them to defend or reject it — in particular, why source control might reasonably be established on Day 2, after sprint planning, rather than before anything else.
- **Lab:** require architecture decision records for every architecturally significant decision, using the playbook's field list verbatim, entered in a proposed status by pull request and stored under `doc/adr`. Assess the reasoning and the value-neutrality of the context section, not the outcome. **Require at least one record to reach `Superseded`**, because neither of the playbook's own worked examples ever does, and a status vocabulary nobody exercises is decoration.
- **Lab:** require one trade study using **the template page's field list in full**, including the accessibility section and a stated experiment and evidence per option — and require teams to justify that the study was warranted at all, against the source's own rule that a study should be avoided where the choice is clear. **Then show them the playbook's own worked trade study and ask them to mark it against the template.** It omits seven required sections. That is a better lesson in specification compliance than any constructed example.
- **Lab:** require design writing at the scope of the decision, using the playbook's four templates — task, feature or story, milestone or epic, spike — and require the `[DRAFT/WIP]` prefix while the document is open. Assess whether the team chose the right template.
- **Lab:** run the async design review workflow as specified: documents in a repository directory excluded from continuous-integration triggers, reviewed by pull request with named reviewers, and **the two-round-trip rule enforced** — after two exchanges of question and response, the thread moves to a synchronous conversation.
- **Lab:** run a spike exactly as specified: a ninety-minute pre-mortem opening on the failure question, weekly thirty-minute sharing calls, and an explicit rule that the spike's code will not be carried into the product. Close it with the spike template — goal, method, evidence, conclusions, next steps — and enforce the constraint that the conclusion is fact-finding rather than recommendation.
- **Lab:** adopt the pull request template verbatim for the whole cohort and enforce the two loophole-closing norms from Reviewer and Author Guidance: tests in the same pull request as the code, and no deferral of an in-scope change to a later one. Both are quotable as written and both are checkable from the pull request itself.
- **Lab:** use the ML Model Production Checklist as a **structured discussion held before a production decision**, requiring a recorded decision and rationale against each of the eleven items. **Do not score it and do not use it as a pass condition** — the source disclaims that use directly.
- **Assessment:** require every non-functional requirement to be written in the playbook's eight-field form, and mark only two of the fields — **Measurement/Metric and Verification Method**. A requirement that cannot name how it would be verified has not been written. Supply the playbook's threshold catalogue as an example bank and **state explicitly that its numbers carry no source**, then require each team to justify its own numbers.
- **Assessment:** require a Responsible AI assessment before any project involving a model, using the source's fifteen questions and opening on "Can the problem be solved without AI? Would simpler technology suffice?", and require the resulting document to be updated at least once during the project rather than filed at approval.
- **Assessment:** require a structured engineering feedback item written against a real tool or platform the team used, following the playbook's field set — and teach it explicitly as **a writing standard for a single report**, then ask students what the report's fate would be in an organisation that had not specified one.
- **Assessment:** require an onboarding guide against the playbook's six headings, then hold every team repository to the F5 Contract, tested by another team cloning it cold, and record Time To First Commit for that other team as the measure. **The guide and the contract are assessed together**, because a guide that is needed to get to F5 is evidence the contract has failed.
- **Assessment:** assign the observability material and require that projects instrument logging, metrics and tracing distinctly, defending the choice of what went into each **on the source's own basis — data volume and processing cost — rather than on semantics.** Require the dashboards and alerts to be held in source control, per the observability-as-code argument, so that they arrive through review.
- **Assessment:** require a threat model against the playbook's worked example as the model of the genre, and **do not penalise a team for marking threats "Not Mitigated"** — the playbook's own example marks all twelve that way, and an honest unmitigated list is worth more than a fabricated mitigated one.

## Limits and cautions

- **The playbook is unevenly maintained and cannot be dated as a whole.** Page footer dates run from **7 May 2021 to 26 September 2025**, and the navigation gives no indication which is which. Material four and a half years old sits beside material four months old with nothing to distinguish them but a footer a reader has to scroll for. **Pin every citation to a page and that page's own date.** Note also that the large 22-and-26-August-2024 cluster has the shape of a bulk repository edit rather than a review, so a 2024 footer is weaker evidence of currency than it looks.
- **About half the site is not what its titles promise, and the proportion is the practical limit on using it.** Roughly a fifth of the 243 pages — on the order of 45 to 50 — are stubs, pure link lists, tool catalogues or example instances, including the DevSecOps index, the Agile Development roles page, three of the seven source-control pages, eight of the eighteen documentation pages and four pages in the design section that prescribe nothing at all. **Confirm a page has content before assigning it.** The named list is in [Structure of the original](#structure-of-the-original).
- **A further quarter is recipe: perishable vendor procedure pinned to a tool at a version, not durable practice.** These pages have the same authority of tone as the practice pages and none of the shelf life. They should be read as illustrations of a principle stated elsewhere, never as the standard itself, and **nothing in this repository should cite a recipe page for anything except the existence of the approach.**
- **The rot is real and it is not visible as broken links.** Only one URL in 243 returns HTTP 404. The decay is in content that still renders: a page recommending a monitoring agent that has been removed from the product it monitors; two of six recommended Kubernetes dashboards that are archived projects; a log shipper deprecated in favour of a successor; a security-analysis extension that has been retired; a Java linter that is dead and superseded; a Go linter the page itself notes is archived; Terraform pinned at 0.12.4; `actions/checkout@v2`; a secrets scanner pinned to work around an upstream bug the page tells you to watch for; an OpenTelemetry status section quoting a roadmap for the first half of 2021. **A page that renders is not a page that is current.**
- **Several links are dead or misdirected in ways a reader will not notice.** A 2008 cost study is cited through an `ftp://` URL no current browser will follow. Two policy documents on the privacy page are internal `aka.ms` shortlinks that resolve externally to a search fallback rather than to the named documents. Three in-page links in the design-review recipes point at `design/agile-development/...`, a path that does not exist because that section is top-level. The documentation requirements page links to a team-agreements path that is likewise broken. One diagram is hotlinked to an individual contributor's personal fork. The Prometheus entry on the metrics pillar links to Azure Monitor documentation instead.
- **The playbook reaches for a magnitude in at least four places and warrants it in none.** The claim that code review costs less than nothing, the claim that pairing raises sprint velocity, the six-fold defect-cost multiple behind a dead link, and the wide-banded vendor efficiency figures **must not be repeated as findings by anything in this repository.** The non-functional-requirements threshold catalogue is a related but distinct case: those numbers are illustrative targets rather than claims, and they carry no source, so they may be used as an example bank and never as a benchmark.
- **The site contradicts itself in at least a dozen places.** End-to-end testing manual or automated; two or three trade-study options or two to four; diagram at engagement start or at ten to twenty per cent completion; secrets in a key vault or encrypted in the repository; three different branch-naming conventions; mocking frameworks recommended and discouraged; caching a sustainability win and a sustainability risk. None is an error of fact and all are the residue of many hands over four years, but a course that presents the playbook as coherent will be caught out.
- **There are rendering defects that destroy content.** The pull-request-versus-review comparison table in the code-review FAQ renders with empty headers; the decision-log template's column headers do not render at all, leaving only the cell descriptions; the JavaScript and TypeScript testing section is truncated mid-sentence; the usability page's lists are mangled into run-on prose; the worked threat model's properties table has duplicated row numbers.
- **Parts of the Engineering Fundamentals Checklist are not falsifiable as written**, and the Security section changes grammatical form partway through. It is a good inventory and a partial instrument.
- **The ML Model Production Checklist explicitly refuses gate semantics.** Using it as a pass condition contradicts the source.
- The playbook reflects a specific organisational culture and toolchain, with a visible Microsoft and Azure orientation — the code-review evidence guidance assumes Azure DevOps branch policies specifically, the work-items page is written against Azure DevOps field names, and one agile page is fifteen screenshots of an Azure DevOps card editor. Students should extract the principle and not assume the tooling.
- Its scale assumes a professional delivery team over a full engagement, and it will overwhelm a student project if adopted wholesale rather than selectively.
- It is prescriptive practice with no comparative evidence base, and should not be taught as though its recommendations were empirically established.
- Published guidance describes the intended standard, not measured compliance; a course should not treat it as evidence that the practices are universally followed.
- It is strong on engineering discipline and comparatively light on the customer discovery and negotiation craft that the other sources in this strand supply, so it complements rather than replaces them.
