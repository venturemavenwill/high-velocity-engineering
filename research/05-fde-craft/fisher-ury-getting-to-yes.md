# Cliff Notes — Getting to Yes

## Citation and access
- **Authors:** Roger Fisher and William Ury, with Bruce Patton from the second edition onward
- **Title:** Getting to Yes: Negotiating Agreement Without Giving In
- **Publisher and year:** Houghton Mifflin, 1981; second edition 1991; third revised edition 2011
- **Origin:** Product of the Harvard Negotiation Project
- **Access status:** Copyrighted book, not open access. This note is an original synthesis and reproduces no source text.
- **Related public material:** The Program on Negotiation at Harvard Law School publishes extensive open articles on principled negotiation and BATNA.

## What this source is
This is the founding text of interest-based, or principled, negotiation. It proposes a method that is neither soft nor hard, on the argument that the usual choice between those two styles is a false one, and it supplies four operational principles plus a concept, BATNA, that has entered general business vocabulary.

## The argument in full
The book opens by diagnosing what it calls positional bargaining, in which each side stakes out a position, defends it, and concedes grudgingly towards a compromise. The authors identify three specific costs. It is inefficient, because ritual concession consumes time that produces no value. It endangers relationships, because the process is adversarial and each concession is experienced as a defeat, which matters enormously when the parties must continue working together afterwards. And it tends to produce unwise agreements, because a settlement reached by splitting the difference between two arbitrary anchors is unlikely to be the arrangement that best serves either party's actual needs.

The authors then close the obvious escape route. Faced with the costs of hard positional bargaining, many people adopt a soft style: concede readily, avoid conflict, preserve the relationship. The book argues this simply loses. The soft negotiator facing a hard one is systematically exploited, and the outcome is both a worse agreement and, often, a damaged relationship anyway through accumulated resentment. The proposal is therefore to change the game rather than to choose a style within it.

The method has four principles. The first is to separate the people from the problem. Negotiators are human beings with egos, fears and a need to be seen to have done well, and those human factors get entangled with the substantive issues so that an attack on a proposal is felt as an attack on a person. The instruction is to treat the relationship and the substance as two problems to be handled separately, dealing directly with perception, emotion and communication rather than trying to trade substantive concessions to manage a relational grievance. The authors' formulation is to be soft on the people and hard on the problem, which is what distinguishes the method from both hard and soft bargaining.

The second principle, and the book's most quoted, is to focus on interests, not positions. A position is what someone says they want; an interest is why they want it. The distinction matters because positions are usually incompatible while interests frequently are not, so reconciling interests opens solutions that position-trading cannot reach. The authors also observe that behind opposed positions lie multiple interests, not one, and that some of the most powerful interests are basic human needs such as security, recognition, control over one's own domain and economic wellbeing. The practical instruction is to ask why, and to ask why not, meaning to work out why the other side has not already agreed to what you propose, which usually reveals an unstated interest.

The third principle is to invent options for mutual gain. The authors identify four obstacles: premature judgement, which kills ideas before they are developed; searching for the single answer, which narrows the field too early; the assumption of a fixed pie, which frames the negotiation as purely distributive; and the belief that solving their problem is their problem, which removes any incentive to look for joint solutions. Their remedy is to separate inventing from deciding, generating options in a session explicitly free of commitment, then broadening those options and looking for differences in interest, priority, risk tolerance and time horizon that permit trades in which both sides give up something they value less for something they value more.

The fourth principle is to insist on using objective criteria. When interests genuinely conflict, the alternative to a contest of wills is agreement on an independent standard by which the outcome will be judged, such as market value, precedent, professional standards, cost, or an established procedure. Agreeing the standard before applying it changes the conversation from whose will prevails to what the standard implies, and it produces agreements both sides can defend to their own constituencies.

The book then addresses three practical objections, and it is here that the concept of BATNA is introduced. What if they are more powerful? The answer is that a negotiator's power comes from their Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement, the course of action they will take if no deal is reached. Knowing your BATNA protects you from accepting terms worse than your alternative and from rejecting terms better than it. Improving your BATNA, which is done away from the table, is the most reliable way to increase your negotiating power. What if they will not play? The answer is negotiation jujitsu: do not push back against attack, redirect it onto the problem, treating an attack on you as an attack on the problem and a positional assertion as an expression of an underlying interest to be explored. What if they use dirty tricks? The answer is to name the tactic explicitly, then negotiate the process itself before continuing on substance.

## Structure of the original
- Part I: The Problem — why positional bargaining is inefficient, endangers relationships and produces unwise agreements, and why the soft alternative fails
- Part II: The Method — separate the people from the problem; focus on interests, not positions; invent options for mutual gain; insist on using objective criteria
- Part III: Yes, But — what if they are more powerful (BATNA); what if they will not play (negotiation jujitsu and the one-text procedure); what if they use dirty tricks
- Later editions add a section of common questions on fairness, dealing with specific people and tactics, and applying the method in practice

## Key concepts and practices
- Positional bargaining is inefficient, endangers relationships and produces unwise agreements.
- The soft-versus-hard choice is false; the method changes the game rather than the style.
- Principle one: separate the people from the problem; be soft on people, hard on the problem.
- Principle two: focus on interests, not positions; ask why, and ask why not.
- Behind opposed positions lie multiple interests, including basic human needs such as security, recognition, control and economic wellbeing.
- Principle three: invent options for mutual gain, separating the act of inventing from the act of deciding.
- Four obstacles to inventing: premature judgement, searching for a single answer, assuming a fixed pie, and assuming their problem is theirs alone.
- Principle four: insist on objective criteria, agreed before they are applied.
- BATNA, the Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement, is the true source of negotiating power and is improved away from the table.
- Negotiation jujitsu redirects attacks onto the problem rather than resisting them.
- Dirty tricks are handled by naming the tactic and negotiating the process explicitly.

## Method and evidence base
The book is the practitioner-facing output of an academic research programme, the Harvard Negotiation Project, and its argument proceeds by analysis and illustrative case rather than by controlled study. Its illustrations are drawn from contexts ranging from domestic disputes to international diplomacy, which is both a strength, in demonstrating generality, and a weakness, in that the reader cannot judge how representative the successful cases are. The framework's durability across four decades and its adoption into mainstream negotiation teaching constitute its practical validation. There is a substantial subsequent literature, some of it critical, arguing that the method underweights situations of genuine distributive conflict and asymmetric power.

## Vocabulary the source introduces
- **Principled negotiation** — negotiation on the merits, as distinct from hard or soft positional bargaining.
- **Position** — the stated demand; **interest** — the underlying need or concern that motivates it.
- **BATNA** — Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement; the yardstick against which any proposed deal is measured.
- **Objective criteria** — independent standards such as market value, precedent or professional norms used to settle conflicting interests.
- **Inventing versus deciding** — the deliberate separation of option generation from option selection.
- **Negotiation jujitsu** — redirecting attack and positional pressure onto the problem instead of resisting it directly.
- **One-text procedure** — having a third party draft and iterate a single document against both sides' criticism rather than exchanging competing proposals.

## What to remember
- Positions are not interests, and the entire method depends on holding that distinction.
- Be soft on the people and hard on the problem; conflating the two is the usual error.
- Ask why, and also why not, to surface the interest that is actually blocking agreement.
- Invent before you decide; judgement during generation destroys the options you needed.
- Agree the standard before you apply it, so the outcome is defensible to both constituencies.
- Your power is your BATNA, and you improve it before the meeting, not during it.
- A conversation you cannot walk away from is not a negotiation.

## Why it matters for the course
Forward-deployed engineering is a negotiation-dense role and this is usually invisible to students. Getting access to production data, agreeing what is in scope for a pilot, obtaining a security exception, securing a subject-matter expert's time, and settling who owns an evaluation criterion are all negotiations conducted under relationship constraints, because the same people must continue working together afterwards. Positional bargaining is therefore especially costly in this setting. The interest-versus-position distinction is also directly transferable to requirements work: a customer's stated requirement is a position, and the engineering job is to find the interest underneath it, which frequently admits a cheaper and better solution. BATNA gives students a way to think clearly about what happens if a blocked request stays blocked, which is a more productive question than how to argue harder.

## How to teach it
- Lecture: teach the position-interest distinction with a low-stakes example first, then immediately map it onto a data-access refusal, which is the case students will actually meet.
- Lecture: make explicit that a stated requirement is a position, and that eliciting the interest behind it is a design activity rather than a negotiation trick.
- Lab: role-play a data-access negotiation with a security officer and a business sponsor holding genuinely different interests, then debrief on which interests were never surfaced.
- Lab: have students write down their BATNA before a simulated negotiation and compare the outcomes of those who did with those who did not.
- Lab: run an inventing session under a strict no-evaluation rule to demonstrate how much premature judgement suppresses.
- Assessment: require project teams to document, for one real constraint they encountered, the stated position, the inferred interest, the objective criterion proposed, and their BATNA.

## Limits and cautions
- The method assumes some good faith and some room for joint gain; purely distributive negotiations with a genuinely fixed pie fit it poorly.
- It is a framework, not a substitute for authority, budget or organisational standing.
- Severe power asymmetry limits what the method can achieve, and the BATNA answer, while honest, is cold comfort when the alternative is genuinely bad.
- The approach is culturally situated in norms of direct discussion and explicit interest articulation, which do not hold universally.
- Critics argue the framework understates the role of hard bargaining and of relationship-independent leverage.
- Students may over-apply it, treating every disagreement as a negotiation when some are simply decisions belonging to someone else.
