# Cliff Notes — ADKAR

## What this source is
ADKAR is a model of change at the level of the individual person rather than the organisation. Hiatt states the premise in the first paragraph of Chapter 1: "The secret to successful change lies beyond the visible and busy activities that surround change. Successful change, at its core, is rooted in something much simpler: How to facilitate change with one person." The book is "a framework for understanding change at an individual level" that "is then extended to show how businesses, government agencies and communities can increase the likelihood that their changes are implemented successfully." Chapter 7 restates it flatly: "By its nature, ADKAR is an individual change management model."

It is a practitioner model, written by an engineer, and it says so. The Preface records twenty years of process, system and organisational change work "both as an engineer with Bell Laboratories and as a project leader for other companies", a run of failures whose "common theme … was resistance to change", and a colleague's joke that "All of our change initiatives would have gone great if it weren't for all the people involved." The model's origin is a complaint about the change-management literature rather than a research question: "I struggled with the idea that these change management activities were surely not an endpoint by themselves … I was constantly bothered by the absence of an end result that these activities should produce." His method was to interrogate each tactic — "I began to ask the question 'Why?' every time I heard about another change management tactic or approach" — and to name the outcome it was supposed to deliver. Communication was supposed to produce awareness; "This led to the first component of ADKAR: awareness ." The remaining four were derived the same way, "By examining a large number of change management activities and mapping them into their desired results".

Hiatt makes no originality claim, and this is the single most important thing a reader of the second-hand literature does not know: "I did not then, nor do I now, view this model to be some type of breakthrough, but rather a framework for understanding and applying many approaches for managing change. ADKAR is a perspective on change that enables other change management tactics to have focus and direction." He credits "William Bridges, John Kotter, Daryl Conner, David McClelland, Frank Petrock, Peter Block, Jeanenne LaMarsh, Patrick Dolan, Richard Beckhard and Reuben T. Harris" as having shaped his views.

> **The previous note in this file was written without reading the book, and parts of it are wrong.** Six substantive corrections and three material omissions are itemised, with the superseded wording quoted, in `## Verification status`. The most consequential: the old note claimed the model "says which element is blocking but not, in itself, how to move it" — six of the book's fourteen chapters are tactics for moving each element — and it claimed "desire is where most transitions actually fail", which contradicts the only survey finding Hiatt reports on the question.

## The argument in full

**The five elements, in Hiatt's own definitions (Chapter 1).** These are quoted verbatim because every second-hand rendering drifts.

> "A wareness represents a person's understanding of the nature of the change, why the change is being made and the risk of not changing. Awareness also includes information about the internal and external drivers that created the need for change, as well as 'what's in it for me.'"

> "D esire represents the willingness to support and engage in a change. Desire is ultimately about personal choice, influenced by the nature of the change, by an individual's personal situation, as well as intrinsic motivators that are unique to each person."

> "K nowledge represents the information, training and education necessary to know how to change. Knowledge includes information about behaviors, processes, tools, systems, skills, job roles and techniques that are needed to implement a change."

> "A bility represents the realization or execution of the change. Ability is turning knowledge into action. Ability is achieved when a person or group has the demonstrated capability to implement the change at the required performance levels."

> "R einforcement represents those internal and external factors that sustain a change."

Note what the awareness definition contains. It is **not** confined to the reason for the change: "the nature of the change" and "what's in it for me" are both inside it, and the Chapter 8 FAQ says of *what* versus *why* that "These two topics are difficult to separate for most changes." What Hiatt actually argues is a sequencing caution, not a clean separation — project teams "have an almost undeniable urge to share their design work", and "Details about the future state often fall on deaf ears as employees struggle to sort out why a change is even necessary."

**Sequence, and why order matters (Chapter 1).** The argument for the order is *conceptual entailment*, offered without evidence and without hedge:

> "The elements of the ADKAR model fall into the natural order of how one person experiences change. Desire cannot come before awareness because it is the awareness of the need for change that stimulates our desire or triggers our resistance to that change. Knowledge cannot come before desire because we do not seek to know how to do something that we do not want to do. Ability cannot come before knowledge because we cannot implement what we do not know. Reinforcement cannot come before ability because we can only recognize and appreciate what has been achieved."

Chapter 7 makes the requirement explicit and cumulative: "The elements of the ADKAR model must occur in sequence and they are cumulative … All of the building blocks need to remain present in order for the change to be sustained." Chapter 1 states the completeness condition with equal absence of hedge: "It is useful to think of these elements as building blocks. All five elements must be in place for a change to be realized." **No study, no measurement and no counter-case is offered for either the ordering or the completeness claim.** They are argued from the meanings of the words. That is worth stating plainly, because it is the load-bearing structural claim of the model and it rests on nothing external.

One scope limit is stated: "The lifecycle for ADKAR begins after a change has been identified." The model says nothing about whether the change is the right one.

**The barrier point, and how it is actually computed (Chapter 7).** The definition is a *threshold on a rating*, not a binary test of achievement:

> "The ADKAR elements must be established in order. When an element early in the model is weak, then the change begins to break down. Hence the term 'barrier point' is used to refer to the first element of the model that is weak or would be assessed as 'low.' For example, if both awareness and knowledge were considered low for a specific change, then awareness would be the barrier point for this change and must be addressed before knowledge."

In the worked case study he pins the threshold to a number on a self-rated five-point scale: "The barrier point for this change, defined here as the first element that scores a three or lower in the ADKAR assessment, is awareness ." The instrument that produces the scores is described in Chapter 14 — six question areas, the first about the change itself "because many employees have been misinformed about the nature of the change", the remaining five following the elements, "completed with printed ADKAR worksheets or with web-based assessment tools", and "In most cases the assessment is anonymous." **The barrier point is therefore an artefact of an anonymous self-rating on a 1–5 scale with a cut at 3.** No inter-rater agreement, no validation and no calibration study is reported anywhere in the book.

What the diagnosis licenses is weaker than it is usually reported. Hiatt's own formulations are conditional and partial: research funding "will not by itself create a change to address the issue surrounding peak oil production"; on Social Security, "spending more time on how to solve the problem will not be productive until we can increase the overall desire to make a substantial change"; and in Chapter 14, on health-care reform, "In the absence of desire, no amount of knowledge can produce change." That last is absolute and unhedged; the first two are not. The book does not say that effort past the barrier point is wasted.

The consequences of missing elements are stated as *directions*, with one hedged probability and no magnitudes: "In the absence of awareness and desire, you can expect more resistance from employees, slower adoption of the change, higher turnover and delays in implementation. If awareness and desire are extremely low, project failure is likely. In the absence of knowledge and ability, you can expect lower utilization … In the absence of reinforcement, you can expect individuals to lose interest and revert to old behaviors."

**Individual versus organisational change.** Hiatt's position is complementarity, not replacement, and he never claims the model is a competitor to organisational frameworks — see the Preface disclaimer quoted above. Chapter 7 maps standard change-management activities onto the elements rather than replacing them: communications to awareness; sponsorship to awareness, desire and reinforcement; training to knowledge and ability; and the same for the roles, with the executive sponsor owning awareness, desire and reinforcement, HR and the project team owning knowledge and ability, and "Managers and supervisors play a critical role throughout the entire process." Chapter 13 is explicit that this is not a method: "This summary is not intended to prescribe a process or set of steps for managing change. The methods presented in this book … are not meant to be an exhaustive or complete list." Chapter 12's FAQ extends the audience beyond employees: "the building blocks for change as described by the ADKAR model apply equally to customers and suppliers", and to the public and to schools.

**Awareness is not communication (Chapters 2 and 8).** The chapter's strongest and most defensible claim: "A common assumption is that awareness building is equal to communications. Yet, sharing information does not always produce awareness." He demonstrates it with four quoted employee reactions to a single restructuring briefing, ranging from "We have heard all of this before" to a full statement of the business case, and concludes: "Awareness is not achieved based on the messages sent, but rather how the messages are received and internalized by each person. The only way you can measure awareness is through interactions and feedback." Five named factors govern reception — the person's view of the current state, cognitive style (via Kirton's adaptor/innovator spectrum, the book's one substantive external psychological citation), sender credibility, circulating misinformation, and the contestability of the stated reasons. The contestability factor is the sharpest: changes with "external and observable" drivers are easier to justify; internally-motivated changes invite legitimate dispute, and "a reasonable person may debate the grounds for the change."

**Desire cannot be manufactured (Chapters 3 and 9).** "Creating desire poses a challenge, in part because of the limited control we have over another person's choices" — it "remains elusive and, by definition, not under our direct control." The named error is assuming it follows from awareness: "A common mistake made by many business leaders is to assume that by building awareness of the need for change they have also created desire . Resistance to change from employees takes them by surprise". And: "Awareness enables people to begin the process of evaluating a change, but does not necessarily result in a desire to change." Four factors: the nature of the change and "what's in it for me"; organisational and environmental context, including "the success of past changes, how much change is already going on"; personal situation, in which "a person's financial situation or health may cause them to make choices related to a change that on the surface do not appear logical, but when understood make perfect sense"; and intrinsic motivation, which he ties to expectancy — "our internal belief that we could achieve what we want should we choose to move forward", footnoted to Vroom's expectancy theory and adjacent to the self-efficacy literature at [research/04-professional-formation/bandura-self-efficacy.md](/research/04-professional-formation/bandura-self-efficacy.md). His summary line: "Each person has a unique capacity to change."

Chapter 9 then gives five tactics for moving it: sponsor the change effectively, equip managers as change leaders, assess risk and anticipate resistance, engage employees in the change process, and align incentive programmes. The stance is proactive rather than reactive: "you should not introduce a change and then wait to identify those groups or individuals who are resistant … Your goal is not to drag along the unwilling and uncaring, with all your attention focused on this minority." The resistance-management sequence begins with diagnosis — "a manager must first consider which elements or building blocks of the ADKAR model are currently strong or weak for an individual. In other words, what is the barrier point to change for this person?" — and then "stop talking and start listening", because "in many cases employees simply want to be heard", and "Often the resistance is not about the change itself, but rather how the change will impact them personally." This is the same move as principled negotiation's separation of positions from interests; see [research/05-fde-craft/fisher-ury-getting-to-yes.md](/research/05-fde-craft/fisher-ury-getting-to-yes.md).

**The passage the summaries omit.** Chapter 9 also recommends removing persistently resistant managers, and names the practice: "The term sacrificial lamb is often used when referring to the removal of a key manager who is demonstrating persistent and damaging resistance to a change." The intended signal is stated in three lines — "We are serious about this change. / Resistance will not be tolerated. / The consequences for not moving ahead are real and severe." He hedges it — "should be used as a last resort", "does not necessarily imply termination", "Use this method with caution and with involvement of your Human Resource group and legal department" — but the recommendation stands, and it sits uncomfortably beside his own insistence elsewhere that resistance is information. Any teaching use of this model must present the passage rather than quietly drop it.

**Knowledge is not the starting point (Chapters 4 and 10).** The best-constructed argument in the book is the paired call-centre case. Two teams, same programme, same year. The self-service team "began their deployment with a focus on the first two elements of the ADKAR model, awareness and desire . They understood that the customer was ultimately in control", and their five-year adoption projections "were surprisingly accurate". The knowledge-base team "began with knowledge and ability … assumed that 'if you build it, they will use it.' The unspoken assumption this team also made is that employees do not have a choice" — and after deployment "some call center agents were not using the new tool and that other agents used the tool infrequently." The lesson: "knowledge is not the starting point for managing change. Training by itself is not the answer. Attempts to build knowledge are effective only when the 'students of change' want to engage in the change process and are seeking knowledge to help them be successful." The Chapter 9 FAQ states the general form: "Anytime you put training or knowledge ahead of awareness and desire you will be disappointed with the results; conversely, whenever awareness and desire are present, an individual naturally seeks the knowledge of how to succeed."

**Knowledge is not ability (Chapters 5 and 11).** "The presence of knowledge is often insufficient by itself. Someone who recently completed lessons with a golf pro does not walk onto the course and par every hole." And, unusually for a book of this kind: "Some employees, depending on the change, may never develop the required abilities." The Chapter 10 FAQ draws the line cleanly: "Knowledge represents the cognitive understanding of specific information about the change … Ability is the demonstrated capability to implement the change", and the term *knowledge* in the model "refers only to the information and understanding on how to change", whereas "well-designed training includes knowledge transfer and the practice needed to apply this new knowledge to real situations." Five named barriers to ability: psychological blocks, physical abilities, intellectual capability, available time and available support resources — illustrated by "John", a volunteer firefighter who had awareness, desire and an EMT certification, froze at his first road-traffic accident, and left the service within a week.

The Chapter 11 tactics are the most directly transferable material in the book for an engineering programme. Supervisors must make "employees aware that implementing change takes time and practice, and that mistakes or missteps are part of the learning process", must know "How to create a safe environment that allows employees to practice and to make mistakes without retribution", and must open feedback channels — with a warning that is exactly right for software rollouts: "If the feedback channel is not open, then the employee will appear to be failing when the system is actually at fault." That is the same territory as [research/04-professional-formation/edmondson-psychological-safety.md](/research/04-professional-formation/edmondson-psychological-safety.md) and [research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md](/research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md), reached by a different route and with none of their evidence. He also flags the diagnostic ambiguity honestly: "What is difficult to ascertain during this phase … is whether the barrier to change is ability or incomplete knowledge", and, separately, that "Work slowdowns and poor work performance could be disguised forms of resistance."

**Reinforcement (Chapters 6 and 12).** "Reinforcement includes any action or event that strengthens and reinforces the change with an individual or an organization." Four factors govern whether it works: it must be meaningful to the recipient, tied to a real accomplishment ("If no accomplishment has been made, any attempt at rewards or recognition can backfire"), free of countervailing negative consequences such as peer pressure, and backed by accountability systems, which he calls "one of the strongest forms of reinforcement". Three purposes: "reinforcement sustains the change and prevents individuals from slipping back into old behaviors"; "reinforcement builds momentum during the transition"; and "reinforcement creates a history that individuals remember when the next change occurs." The failure case is a bank that deployed a quality programme well, then disbanded the implementation team: "Within one year of disbanding the team, six courses had been cancelled … the program was dead." His diagnosis is the one worth teaching: "The first mistake the implementation team made was to assume that managing change is essentially completing activities in a project plan." **Note what is not in his reinforcement list: removing the old way as a still-available option.** That is not Hiatt's; it was invented by the previous version of this note.

**Two claims inside the book that this repository must not inherit.** First, Chapter 10 asserts a retention hierarchy — "Research indicates that adults retain only a small fraction of what they read, slightly more of what they hear, and about one-half of what they observe in demonstrations. The highest retention modes result from the hands-on application of the learning to an immediate problem." The citation is to a commercial trainer's handbook (Pike, *Creative Training Techniques Handbook*, HRD Press, 2003), not to a primary study. This is a member of the learning-pyramid family of retention-percentage claims, and it has no traceable empirical basis. **Do not cite it, do not repeat the numbers, and do not use this book as a warrant for any claim about retention rates by modality.** The underlying advice — practise on real problems, train close to the point of use — is defensible on other grounds and should be sourced elsewhere. Second, Chapter 8 offers "A rule of thumb is that employees need to hear a message five to seven times before that message is cemented into their thinking", with no source at all. It is a rule of thumb and must be cited as nothing more.

## Structure of the original
The verified table of contents. The book is in **two halves with a hinge**, a structure the previous outline in this file missed entirely: Chapters 2–6 define the elements and what influences each; Chapter 7 assembles them and introduces the barrier point; Chapters 8–12 give tactics for producing each element, one chapter per element and in the same order; Chapters 13–14 summarise and apply.

- **Front matter** — roughly fifteen pages of practitioner endorsements; copyright page; acknowledgments; Preface (origin of the model, the three questions the book sets out to answer, and the disclaimer of originality).
- **Chapter 1 — ADKAR: Overview.** The five definitions, the building-block metaphor, the entailment argument for the sequence.
- **Chapters 2–6 — the elements.** 2 Awareness (five influencing factors); 3 Desire (four factors); 4 Knowledge (four factors); 5 Ability (five factors); 6 Reinforcement (four factors). Each closes with a summary and a question that hands off to the next.
- **Chapter 7 — The ADKAR Model.** Assembly; mapping of change-management activities and of organisational roles onto the elements; the barrier point; three worked ADKAR profiles on a 1–5 scale (peak oil production, US Social Security reform, hotel towel reuse).
- **Chapters 8–12 — the tactics.** 8 Building Awareness; 9 Creating Desire; 10 Developing Knowledge; 11 Fostering Ability; 12 Reinforcing Change. Each ends with a frequently-asked-questions section, which is where several of the book's better hedges are located.
- **Chapter 13 — ADKAR Enabling Elements Summary.** A one-page-per-element consolidation, with an explicit disclaimer that it is not a process and not exhaustive.
- **Chapter 14 — ADKAR Applications.** Five uses: teaching device, plan-completeness checklist, coaching tool, assessment tool for changes under way, and personal planning tool. Contains the health-care-reform teaching case, a sample assessment, and the ADKAR analysis worksheet.
- **References** — grouped by chapter.
- **Back matter** — author biography.

## Key concepts and practices
- Change happens one person at a time; "Successful change, at its core, is rooted in something much simpler: How to facilitate change with one person."
- The five elements are building blocks: "All five elements must be in place for a change to be realized", and they "must occur in sequence and they are cumulative."
- The ordering argument is **entailment, not evidence** — desire cannot precede awareness, knowledge cannot precede desire, and so on down the chain.
- The model "begins after a change has been identified" and says nothing about whether the change is right.
- Awareness covers the nature of the change, the reason for it, the risk of not changing, the internal and external drivers, **and** "what's in it for me" — it is not only the *why*.
- Communicating is not the same as producing awareness: "Awareness is not achieved based on the messages sent, but rather how the messages are received and internalized by each person."
- Reception is governed by five factors, of which contestability of the stated reasons is the one teams most often ignore.
- Desire is "ultimately about personal choice" and "by definition, not under our direct control"; assuming it follows from awareness is the named executive error.
- The correct first move against resistance is to identify the barrier point, then "stop talking and start listening".
- Knowledge is not the starting point; training aimed past an awareness or desire barrier does not work.
- Knowledge is not ability: ability is "turning knowledge into action" at "the required performance levels", and some people never reach it.
- Ability requires practice, time, coaching, obstacle removal and "a safe environment to practice new skills and job roles".
- An unopened feedback channel makes a broken system look like a failing employee.
- Reinforcement must be meaningful, tied to real accomplishment, free of countervailing consequences, and backed by accountability in day-to-day operations.
- The barrier point is "the first element of the model that is weak or would be assessed as 'low'" — operationally, the first element scoring 3 or below on an anonymous 1–5 self-rating.
- Hiatt's own statements about effort past the barrier are conditional ("will not by itself", "will not be productive until"), with one absolute exception: "In the absence of desire, no amount of knowledge can produce change."
- The model is offered as a lens over existing practice, not as a replacement for it, and explicitly not as a prescribed process.
- The elements apply to customers, suppliers, the public and school communities, not only to employees.

## Method and evidence base
This is the section that governs how the repository is allowed to cite this book, and it can now be stated exactly rather than inferred.

**What the book is, structurally.** A practitioner model, published by the consultancy that owns the trademark and sells the training and certification in it. Prosci is both author's employer and publisher; the Change Management Learning Center whose research is cited throughout is, per the Chapter 8 note, "the sponsor" relationship in reverse — "Prosci is the sponsor of the Change Management Learning Center." The Preface reports that ADKAR "has become the most sought-after model from the Change Management Learning Center" and that it was recently "added … to our change management training programs". None of this is disqualifying, and none of it is hidden by the author. It is simply the warrant, and the warrant must travel with the claim.

**Every piece of evidence the book offers, itemised.** There are four kinds and no others.

1. **Prosci's own benchmarking reports.** These carry all of the book's quantitative claims. The 2005 *Best Practices in Change Management* report is cited with its sample — "a 2005 study with 411 companies undergoing major change projects" — and supplies: the number-one reason employees resist change ("lack of awareness of why the change was being made"), the top-five employee resistance reasons, the top-five manager resistance reasons, the three most common executive-sponsor mistakes, and the "what would you do differently next time" communications list. A separate 2005 figure of "190 project managers" underwrites the three primary-sponsor responsibilities. A 2003 report supplies the preferred-sender findings. A 2004 Prosci publication, *Motivating Call Center Agents*, supplies the finding that agents most wanted "a personal thank you and an expression of appreciation by their supervisor". Chapter 8 refers to "four longitudinal studies by Prosci" over eight years for the superiority of face-to-face communication. The Acknowledgments record that "During the eight years of change management research with Prosci that preceded this text" a named team "collected and analyzed data from hundreds upon hundreds of organizations", and the author biography claims "research in change management with more than 900 companies from 59 countries". **These are practitioner self-report surveys of project teams, published by the vendor, with no methodology section reproduced in the book, no sampling frame, no response rate and no independent replication.** They report what practitioners *say* caused outcomes, not measured outcomes.
2. **Case narratives.** Roughly twenty, ranging from a single hotel towel card to US health-care reform, and drawn from published sources, client work and personal observation. They are used illustratively and are generally well chosen — several are failures, and at least one, the paired call-centre initiatives in Chapter 10, comes close to a natural experiment because two teams inside one organisation made opposite assumptions in the same year. Two carry numbers: about one-third/one-third/one-third of salespeople reacted with reluctance, uncertainty and confidence to a training programme, and "Within 90 days, about 20% of the salespeople were able to implement the new program or some part of the process and tools"; and an organisational assessment in which "51% of the employees had moderate-to-low awareness of why the change was even needed." These are single-site descriptive figures, not findings.
3. **The author's own informal polling.** Explicitly labelled as such: "I conducted informal assessments of people's awareness of this issue … Among more than 800 people at seminars and conferences, less than 10 out of 800 (1.25%) indicated any awareness of this concern." A show of hands at conference sessions.
4. **Practitioner testimonial.** The book opens with roughly fifteen pages of endorsements, including "The ADKAR model works! This is fool proof" and "Based on voluminous research, Jeff Hiatt constructed the ADKAR model". This is marketing copy and carries no evidential weight whatever. The second is also a third-party characterisation the author does not make himself; his own claim is markedly more modest.

**What is conspicuously absent.** There is **no independent evaluation of ADKAR anywhere in the book, and none is cited.** Read the References: every external academic citation supports a *component* concept, never the model — Kirton on adaptor/innovator cognitive style, Vroom on expectancy theory, Goleman on emotional intelligence, Knowles and Merriam and Caffarella on adult learning, Pike on training technique, Johnson's *Who Moved My Cheese?*, Starr on health-care reform, and press and conference sources for the case studies. There is no controlled comparison, no pre/post measurement of an intervention selected by barrier point, no test of whether the ordering claim holds, and no attempt to show that teams using ADKAR outperform teams that do not. The strongest empirical statement in the book concerns sponsorship rather than the model — "Project teams have overwhelmingly reported that active and visible executive sponsorship of the change is the number one success factor for a project overall" — and it is a report of practitioner opinion, stated as a rank without a magnitude.

**Therefore, plainly: this is practitioner experience plus self-published, commercially interested benchmarking, not independent study.** That is not a criticism of the book, which never claims otherwise and whose author explicitly declines the status of a breakthrough. It is the warrant, and it fixes what may be cited. This source licenses **direction and mechanism only.** It licenses no ordering strength, no success rate, no effect size, no adoption magnitude and no claim that using the model improves outcomes. Its defensible contributions are conceptual: the awareness-versus-being-told distinction, the awareness-versus-desire distinction, and the knowledge-versus-ability distinction are real, are routinely conflated in practice, and change what a team does when separated. Teach them as well-constructed distinctions with a named origin. Do not teach the model as evidence-based.

One further asymmetry is worth recording because it is easy to get backwards. The **case narratives** repeatedly land on desire as the blocking element — peak oil, Social Security, health-care reform, the cross-selling agent. The **survey evidence** points the other way: the one systematic finding Hiatt reports on why people resist names awareness, not desire, as the leading cause. Any claim about *where changes most often fail* must be sourced to one or the other and labelled accordingly; the book does not reconcile them, and neither should a citation of it.

## Vocabulary the source introduces
Definitions are Hiatt's Chapter 1 wording unless marked otherwise.
- **Awareness** — "a person's understanding of the nature of the change, why the change is being made and the risk of not changing", including internal and external drivers "as well as 'what's in it for me.'"
- **Desire** — "the willingness to support and engage in a change … ultimately about personal choice."
- **Knowledge** — "the information, training and education necessary to know how to change", covering "behaviors, processes, tools, systems, skills, job roles and techniques".
- **Ability** — "the realization or execution of the change … turning knowledge into action", achieved when there is "the demonstrated capability to implement the change at the required performance levels".
- **Reinforcement** — "those internal and external factors that sustain a change"; in Chapter 6, "any action or event that strengthens and reinforces the change with an individual or an organization."
- **Barrier point** (Chapter 7) — "the first element of the model that is weak or would be assessed as 'low'"; operationally, in the worked case, "the first element that scores a three or lower in the ADKAR assessment".
- **ADKAR profile** (Chapter 7) — the five-element bar profile produced by rating each element 1–5 for a given change and population.
- **ADKAR assessment** (Chapter 14) — the six-question, usually anonymous instrument that produces the profile; the first question is about the change itself, deliberately, "because many employees have been misinformed about the nature of the change".
- **WIIFM** (Chapter 2) — "what's in it for me"; Hiatt places it under both awareness and desire.
- **Sponsorship coalition** / **sponsorship cascade** (Chapter 9) — the chain of aligned managers below the primary sponsor; "A weak sponsorship coalition allows resistance to grow in the organization without consequence or recourse."
- **Sponsorship assessment** (Chapter 9) — "an analysis of both the level of support and the sponsorship competency of all key business leaders involved in the change", rendered as a red/amber/green organisation chart.
- **Preferred sender** (Chapters 2 and 8) — the person a given audience expects a given message from; strategic messages from the top, personal-impact messages from the direct supervisor.
- **Sacrificial lamb** (Chapter 9) — the removal of a persistently and damagingly resistant manager, used as a signal to the organisation.
- **Background conversation** (Chapters 2 and 8) — the informal, largely uncontrolled discussion of the change among employees, into which rumour flows if managers do not.
- **Burning platform** (Chapter 8) — "an extremely urgent or compelling business situation" invoked to force awareness, with the caveat that "not every change can have a burning platform."

## What to remember
- Adoption is staged, and the stages are properties of people rather than of the project plan.
- Being told is not awareness; wanting is not knowing; knowing is not being able.
- Awareness includes *what* the change is and what it means for me, not only *why* — but leading with future-state detail still fails.
- Diagnose the barrier point before choosing an intervention, and remember it comes from a subjective 1–5 rating with a cut at 3.
- Training is the default reflex and is frequently aimed at the wrong element.
- Desire is the element a delivery plan cannot manufacture; whether it is where changes *most often* fail is not settled by this book, whose survey evidence names awareness instead.
- Reinforcement matters as much as initial enablement, because regression is the default and "managing change is essentially completing activities in a project plan" is the named fallacy.
- If the feedback channel is closed, a broken system will look like a failing person.
- Plan handover against human readiness, not only against system readiness.
- The model licenses direction and mechanism. It licenses no numbers.

## Why it matters for the course
This is the diagnostic complement to [research/05-fde-craft/kotter-leading-change.md](/research/05-fde-craft/kotter-leading-change.md) and the practical basis for handover planning. Where Kotter tells a team what a change programme should contain, ADKAR tells them where their specific rollout is stuck, which is the question a student team actually faces.

The knowledge-versus-ability distinction is the most valuable import for an AI systems programme, and the book supplies a better instrument for it than the second-hand literature does. Hiatt's Chapter 5 sales case is exactly the shape of an AI-tool rollout: everyone was trained, a third left the room already unwilling, another third were "optimistic, but uncertain if they could really make it happen", and "Within 90 days, about 20% of the salespeople were able to implement the new program". The gap was analytical capability, not instruction. AI tooling has the same profile — users can be shown how to prompt, review or oversee a system and still be unable to do it well under real workload — and mistaking that for a training problem produces more training and no improvement. Chapter 11's warning that a closed feedback channel makes the *system's* faults look like the *person's* is directly reusable in evaluating an assisted-work rollout.

The desire element forces students to confront something they are inclined to skip: a system that automates part of someone's job may be rationally unwelcome, and no amount of communication resolves a genuine conflict of interest. Hiatt is unusually good on this. His Chapter 9 FAQ refuses the lazy generalisation — "you have to be careful not to extend this statement to say, 'People are resistant to change.' As a collective group, we have shown ourselves to be quite adaptable over time" — and grounds resistance in position rather than disposition: "How we react to change is largely rooted in our current circumstances." He also records that "Resistance from mid-level managers is cited as the most common area for resistance to change", because they are the layer whose span of control the change actually moves.

The book has a second, less obvious use for this programme: it is a clean specimen of a **practitioner model with a commercial warrant**, in a body of material otherwise dominated by peer-reviewed work and vendor documentation. Students who will spend careers being handed frameworks by consultancies need one worked example of reading such a framework fairly — taking the distinctions that hold, declining the numbers, and locating the interest. This book is unusually suitable because its author is honest about what it is.

## How to teach it
- Lecture: introduce it immediately after Kotter as the diagnostic layer, using the barrier point to explain why well-run change programmes still stall.
- Lecture: read Hiatt's entailment argument for the sequence aloud and ask what evidence would test it. The answer is that the book offers none, and the students should reach that themselves.
- Lecture: work through the paired call-centre case from Chapter 10 — same organisation, same year, opposite starting assumptions, opposite outcomes — since it is the closest thing to a controlled comparison the source contains.
- Lecture: work through a misdiagnosis case where training is applied to a desire problem, since students will otherwise reach for training reflexively.
- Seminar: put the "sacrificial lamb" passage in front of the room alongside Hiatt's own claim that resistance is information, and require students to argue whether the two are consistent.
- Lab: have teams interview a real user of their system and place that user on the ADKAR sequence with evidence for the placement, then state their confidence in the placement and what would change it.
- Lab: require teams to write the specific intervention implied by the barrier point they identified, and to say what they would expect to observe if it worked.
- Lab: run a reinforcement design exercise asking what would keep the new behaviour in place three months after the team leaves, using Hiatt's five tactics as a checklist rather than a syllabus.
- Assessment: require the handover plan to state, per user group, where that group sits on the sequence and what remains to be done, rather than presenting a generic training schedule.
- Assessment: require an explicit statement of whose interests the change works against, and how the team accounted for that.
- Assessment: require the citation of ADKAR to name its warrant. A submission that cites the model as evidence-based has failed the evidence criterion regardless of the quality of the plan.

## Limits and cautions
- It is a heuristic, not a validated model. Its supporting research is proprietary, practitioner self-reported and commercially interested, and **no independent evaluation of the model is cited anywhere in the book**.
- The sequence and completeness claims — "must occur in sequence", "All five elements must be in place" — are argued from the meanings of the words and are offered **without hedge and without evidence**. They are the model's structural core and its least supported part.
- The barrier point is produced by an anonymous 1–5 self-rating with a cut at 3. No inter-rater agreement, calibration or validation is reported. Two assessors can reach different barrier points on the same change, and the book does not say what to do about that.
- It is individual-level by design and does not address structural, political or resource obstacles that no amount of individual readiness can overcome. Hiatt's own Social Security case shows the failure mode: the diagnosis is correct and the conclusion is that nothing can be done until a price signal arrives.
- It can encourage treating resistance as a deficiency in the individual. Hiatt hedges this well in the FAQs and then undercuts it in the main text with the "sacrificial lamb" recommendation. Teach both.
- **The Chapter 10 retention claim must not be inherited.** "Research indicates that adults retain only a small fraction of what they read, slightly more of what they hear, and about one-half of what they observe in demonstrations" is cited to a commercial trainer's handbook and belongs to the unsupported learning-pyramid family. Neither the numbers nor the ordering may be cited from this source.
- The Chapter 8 "five to seven times" repetition rule of thumb has no source and must be cited as a rule of thumb or not at all.
- Every number in the book is either a vendor survey figure, a single-site case figure, or an informal show of hands. None licenses an effect size, and none should be reported in the wiki as a finding.
- The model is a registered trademark of a firm selling training and certification in it. Cite the model, attribute the trademark, and do not imply endorsement of the ecosystem.
- One boundary condition Hiatt does state, and it matters for defence and emergency-services contexts: where "an organization has a high degree of control over an individual's actions and choices", as with first responders under protocol or soldiers in a crisis, awareness-building may be unnecessary — though "these extraordinary, time-critical situations are more the exception than the rule."

**Corrections to the previous synthesis-only note.** Six substantive errors and three material omissions. The superseded wording is quoted.

1. **"It says which element is blocking but not, in itself, how to move it, particularly for desire."** Wrong, and it was listed as a limitation. Chapters 8 to 12 are tactics for producing each element — six of fourteen chapters, roughly half the book — and Chapter 9, "Creating Desire", is the longest of them, with five named tactics and an eight-technique resistance-management sequence. The book's second half is precisely "how to move it".
2. **"Desire is where most transitions actually fail."** Not supported, and contradicted by the only systematic evidence the book offers on the question: "In a 2005 study with 411 companies undergoing major change projects, the number one reason for resistance to change was lack of awareness of why the change was being made." Hiatt's *case narratives* do repeatedly land on desire; his *survey data* names awareness. The book does not reconcile them and the note should not have picked one silently.
3. **"The barrier point is the first unmet element."** Imprecise in a way that removed the instrument. Hiatt's definition is "the first element of the model that is weak or would be assessed as 'low'", operationalised as "the first element that scores a three or lower" on an anonymous 1–5 self-rating. It is a threshold on a subjective score, not a binary achieved/not-achieved test, and the subjectivity is a limitation the old note therefore could not state.
4. **"Effort spent past the barrier point is wasted."** Stronger than anything in the book. Hiatt's formulations are conditional — research funding "will not by itself create a change"; more work on solutions "will not be productive until we can increase the overall desire". The single absolute is narrower: "In the absence of desire, no amount of knowledge can produce change." "Wasted" is the old note's word.
5. **Reinforcement "including recognition, incentives, measurement, correction of backsliding, and the removal of the old way as a still-available option."** The last item is not in the book. Hiatt's reinforcement tactics are celebrations and recognition, rewards, employee feedback, audits and performance measurement, and accountability systems; his influencing factors add meaningfulness, association with real accomplishment, and absence of negative consequences. Removing the old option is nowhere among them.
6. **"Awareness is about the reason for the change, not the content of it."** Overstated. Hiatt's own definition puts "the nature of the change" and "what's in it for me" inside awareness, and the Chapter 8 FAQ says the two "are difficult to separate for most changes". What he argues is that leading with future-state detail fails, which is a sequencing caution rather than a definitional separation.

Material omissions, now supplied: the **1–5 assessment instrument and ADKAR profile**, which are the model's operational apparatus and the thing that determines the barrier point; the **"sacrificial lamb"** recommendation to remove persistently resistant managers as a signal to the organisation; and the **Chapter 10 retention-by-modality claim**, which a repository teaching learning science must actively decline rather than merely not repeat.
