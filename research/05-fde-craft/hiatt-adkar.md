# Cliff Notes — ADKAR

## Citation and access
- **Author:** Jeffrey M. Hiatt, founder of Prosci
- **Title:** ADKAR: A Model for Change in Business, Government and our Community
- **Publisher and year:** Prosci Learning Center Publications, 2006
- **Access status:** Copyrighted book, not open access. This note is an original synthesis and reproduces no source text.
- **Related public material:** Prosci publishes extensive open descriptions of the ADKAR model, its five elements and the barrier-point concept, along with summaries of its periodic Best Practices in Change Management benchmarking studies.

## What this source is
ADKAR is a model of change at the level of the individual person rather than the organisation. Its claim is that organisational change is nothing more than the aggregate of individual transitions, and that the useful unit of analysis is therefore one person moving through a defined sequence of states. The acronym names those five states.

## The argument in full
The book's founding move is a reframing that sounds trivial and is not. Organisational change frameworks typically operate on the organisation, prescribing what leaders should do at a system level. Hiatt argues that this is one level too abstract, because an organisation does not adopt anything; individual people change how they work, and the organisation's change is simply the sum of those transitions. It follows that the right diagnostic question is not what stage the programme is at but which specific element a specific group of people is missing.

The five elements are presented as a required sequence. Awareness is the understanding of why the change is needed, and Hiatt is careful to distinguish it from having been informed. A person can have received an announcement, attended the briefing and read the memo while still not understanding the business reason, the risk of not changing, or why the current way of working is no longer adequate. Awareness is about the reason for the change, not the content of it.

Desire is the personal decision to participate in and support the change, and it is the element the model handles most honestly. Desire cannot be created by instruction. It depends on the person's own situation, on what the change means for their role, status, competence and workload, on their trust in the people leading it, and on their history with previous changes in the same organisation. Hiatt's practical point is that desire is the element leaders have least direct control over and most often assume, and that the correct response to absent desire is to understand the specific resistance rather than to repeat the awareness message more loudly.

Knowledge is knowing how to change, covering both the skills and behaviours required during the transition and those required in the new state. It is the element that training addresses, and Hiatt's observation is that organisations habitually reach for training first because it is the most tractable intervention, which means training is frequently deployed against problems that are actually awareness or desire problems and therefore has no effect.

Ability is the demonstrated capacity to perform at the required level in practice, and the gap between knowledge and ability is the model's most useful distinction for engineers. Knowing how to do something and being able to do it reliably under real conditions are different states separated by practice, coaching, time and the removal of practical obstacles. A workforce that has been trained and still cannot perform does not need more training; it needs support during application.

Reinforcement is whatever sustains the change and prevents regression, including recognition, incentives, measurement, correction of backsliding, and the removal of the old way as a still-available option. Hiatt's point is that without deliberate reinforcement people revert, not out of resistance but because the previous behaviour is well-practised and the new one is not, and because the surrounding systems frequently continue to reward the old way.

The model's operational value lies in the barrier point. Because the elements are sequential, the first one that has not been achieved is what is actually blocking the transition, and effort spent on later elements is wasted until it is resolved. This converts change management from a set of activities into a diagnosis. If people do not understand why, more training will not help. If people understand why but do not want to, a better vision statement will not help. If people want to and know how but cannot yet perform, more communication will not help, and what is needed is coaching and obstacle removal. If people can perform but are drifting back, the problem is that nothing is sustaining the new behaviour. Each element implies a different intervention, and matching intervention to barrier is the whole discipline.

Hiatt positions ADKAR as complementary to organisational-level models rather than as a competitor to them. Organisational frameworks describe what a change programme should do; ADKAR describes what has to be true for a person, and therefore tells you whether the programme is working and where it is stuck. In the wider Prosci material the model sits alongside a process for preparing, managing and sustaining change, and alongside research on the contribution of active and visible sponsorship to change outcomes.

## Structure of the original
- The premise: organisational change is the aggregate of individual transitions
- Awareness of the need for change
- Desire to participate in and support the change
- Knowledge of how to change
- Ability to implement the required skills and behaviours
- Reinforcement to sustain the change
- Using the model as a diagnostic: identifying the barrier point and matching the intervention to it
- Application at individual, project and organisational levels

## Key concepts and practices
- Change happens one person at a time; the organisation changes only as a consequence.
- The five elements are sequential: awareness, desire, knowledge, ability, reinforcement.
- Awareness is understanding why, not having been told.
- Desire cannot be mandated and is the element leaders control least and assume most.
- Knowledge is what training delivers, and training is habitually misapplied to awareness and desire problems.
- Ability is demonstrated performance under real conditions, and is separated from knowledge by practice, coaching and obstacle removal.
- Reinforcement prevents regression, which is the default outcome without it.
- The barrier point is the first unmet element, and it determines which intervention will work.
- Effort spent past the barrier point is wasted.
- The model complements organisational frameworks rather than replacing them.

## Method and evidence base
ADKAR was developed from practitioner work and from Prosci's benchmarking research with change practitioners, conducted through periodic large-scale surveys. That research is proprietary, commercially tied to the model's training and certification business, and based on practitioner self-report rather than independent outcome measurement. The model's popularity should therefore not be read as validation. Its defensible strengths are conceptual rather than empirical: the awareness-desire distinction and the knowledge-ability distinction are real, they are frequently conflated in practice, and separating them changes what a team does. Teach it as a well-constructed diagnostic heuristic, and say clearly that it is not an evidence-based model in the sense the course otherwise requires.

## Vocabulary the source introduces
- **Awareness** — understanding of the reason for the change and the risk of not changing.
- **Desire** — the personal decision to participate in and support the change.
- **Knowledge** — knowing how to change, covering both transition and end-state skills.
- **Ability** — demonstrated performance of the new behaviour at the required level in practice.
- **Reinforcement** — the mechanisms that sustain the change and prevent regression.
- **Barrier point** — the first element in the sequence that has not been achieved, and therefore the true blocker.

## What to remember
- Adoption is staged, and the stages are properties of people rather than of the project plan.
- Being told is not awareness; wanting is not knowing; knowing is not being able.
- Diagnose the barrier point before choosing an intervention.
- Training is the default reflex and is frequently aimed at the wrong element.
- Desire is where most transitions actually fail, and it is the element a delivery plan cannot manufacture.
- Reinforcement matters as much as initial enablement, because regression is the default.
- Plan handover against human readiness, not only against system readiness.

## Why it matters for the course
This is the diagnostic complement to Kotter and the practical basis for handover planning. Where Kotter tells a team what a change programme should contain, ADKAR tells them where their specific rollout is stuck, which is the question a student team actually faces. The knowledge-versus-ability distinction is particularly valuable in an AI systems course, because AI tools have an unusually wide gap between the two: users can be shown how to prompt, review, or oversee a system and still be unable to do it well under real workload, and mistaking that for a training problem produces more training and no improvement. The desire element also forces students to confront something they are inclined to skip, which is that a system automating part of someone's job may be rationally unwelcome, and that no amount of communication resolves a genuine conflict of interest. Used together with Kotter, ADKAR gives the course a two-level model of adoption that can be assessed: the organisational plan and the individual-readiness diagnosis.

## How to teach it
- Lecture: introduce it immediately after Kotter as the diagnostic layer, using the barrier point to explain why well-run change programmes still stall.
- Lecture: work through a misdiagnosis case where training is applied to a desire problem, since students will otherwise reach for training reflexively.
- Lab: have teams interview a real user of their system and place that user on the ADKAR sequence with evidence for the placement.
- Lab: require teams to write the specific intervention implied by the barrier point they identified, and to say what they would expect to observe if it worked.
- Lab: run a reinforcement design exercise asking what would keep the new behaviour in place three months after the team leaves.
- Assessment: require the handover plan to state, per user group, where that group sits on the sequence and what remains to be done, rather than presenting a generic training schedule.
- Assessment: require an explicit statement of whose interests the change works against, and how the team accounted for that.

## Limits and cautions
- It is a heuristic, not a validated model, and its supporting research is proprietary, self-reported and commercially interested.
- The strict sequence is an idealisation; in practice the elements interact and can be developed partly in parallel.
- It is individual-level by design and does not address structural, political or resource obstacles that no amount of individual readiness can overcome.
- It can encourage treating resistance as a deficiency in the individual when the resistance is a rational response to a genuinely adverse change.
- It says which element is blocking but not, in itself, how to move it, particularly for desire.
- It is embedded in a commercial certification ecosystem, and course materials should cite the model without implying endorsement of that ecosystem.
