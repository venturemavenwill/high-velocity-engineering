## What this source is

It is an **FDE delivery standard being proposed** — a one-page visual handbook asserting how a Microsoft ISE forward-deployed crew should run a customer engagement, from first workshop to handoff, in a fixed number of working days.

It is not research, not a report and not a retrospective. It is a *plan for how work will be done*, assembled by a small group from their own working documents and meeting notes, and rendered as a browsable page so that a crew and a customer can look at the same object. Its genre is closest to a project charter: normative, prospective, and answerable to nothing yet.

Two things make it worth a note despite its provenance problem. First, it is the only artefact this repository has seen that **explicitly sequences the ISE engineering checklist into a dated delivery plan with named owners** — it converts a list of things a team ought to have into a calendar of who produces what and when. Second, it **assigns an AI agent or tool to individual checklist items**, and in doing so exposes a working organisation's actual judgement about which parts of engineering work it currently believes can be automated, which can only be assisted, and which remain irreducibly human. That judgement, expressed as a three-valued status on every item, is the most interesting thing in the document and the part least dependent on any confidential detail.

## The argument in full

The handbook's argument runs in four moves.

**First, that a forward-deployed engagement should be governed by declared principles rather than by improvisation.** It opens with six Team Operating Principles stated in short imperative form. They are not generic engineering virtues; each encodes a specific position about customer-embedded work. *Built for the Front Line* asserts that shipped capability must survive mission-critical operation and generalise across business units, which rules out demonstration-grade work. *Build Capability, Not Dependency* commits every task to pairing a customer engineer with a Microsoft engineer, so that the engagement's success condition includes the customer's independence rather than the vendor's indispensability — an unusual thing for a vendor to write down. *Compound What the Customer Already Owns* requires extending the existing estate before introducing anything new. *Prove It with Working Software* states that demos beat decks and decisions are anchored in running code. *Secure and Responsible by Design* makes security and Responsible AI first-commit requirements rather than pre-launch checkpoints. *AI Accelerates, Humans Decide* requires that every AI output ship only after a human review.

The sixth is the one the rest of the document is built around, and it is stated as an absolute rather than a preference. It reappears in the definition of done, in the pull-request rules, in the backlog-generation rules and in the per-item status taxonomy. Whatever else the handbook is, it is internally consistent on this point.

**Second, that the engagement is a fixed-duration sequence of eight phases with single named owners.** The phases are numbered, each carries a duration in working days, an owning role and a stated purpose, and their durations sum to a fixed post-Discovery window. The claim being made is that a forward-deployed engagement is *plannable* — that the work of discovering a problem, standing up an environment, writing requirements, deciding architecture, and building and launching a system can be laid out in advance, with the largest block by far reserved for execution. Forty of the sixty post-Discovery days go to sprint execution; the seven other phases share twenty. That ratio is itself an argument: the front-loaded governance apparatus is deliberately compressed so that it does not consume the engagement.

**Third, that the phase sequence and an iterative learning model are the same thing viewed at two scales.** The handbook pairs the linear eight-phase standard with an iterative engagement model — Value Mining, Prototype, Environment Readiness and Team Priming, Prototyping and Building, Scale and Launch, Handoff — and maps each iterative stage onto one or more phases, with milestones between them. It then makes a specific claim about the relationship: the Envision, Explore, Design, Develop loop "runs within each phase group, not just between them". This is the handbook's answer to the obvious objection that a numbered eight-phase plan is a waterfall wearing agile clothing. Whether the answer is adequate is a fair question for a seminar; the answer is at least explicitly given rather than dodged.

**Fourth, that the engagement produces a chain of durable artefacts, and that the chain has a direction.** A business requirements document is written first, solution-agnostic, covering stakeholders, service level objectives, release strategy and communications. A backlog is generated from it. Architecture, security and Responsible AI review then produce architecture decision records, a threat model and additional backlog items. Only then is a technical product specification consolidated from the business requirements, the decision records and the security and Responsible AI findings. The rule that makes this a chain rather than a pile is stated explicitly: **changes after the product specification are captured as new architecture decision records, not as edits to the specification.** The specification is therefore a dated consolidation rather than a living document, and the decision record set becomes the authoritative account of drift. This is a genuinely good piece of design and it is fully teachable without touching anything confidential.

Around this spine sit the operational conventions: trunk-based development on a single main branch with no long-lived integration branches; multi-repo by workstream unless the customer already uses a monorepo; a branch-naming convention keyed to the engineer's alias; squash merge as default for clean history and simpler rollback; every pull request requiring at least one non-author reviewer, with continuous integration passing before merge and no hard turnaround service level but a same-day expectation when someone is blocked; pull requests sized as complete shippable units; lightweight daily syncs described as coordination rather than full Scrum ceremony; and a stated definition of done requiring code merged via pull request, decision records captured, security and Responsible AI addressed, and humans having reviewed all AI-generated artefacts.

Two claims about AI usage sit inside this and are the most quotable and least warranted things on the page. The handbook asserts that **AI generates a large majority share of the backlog** — it names a percentage band, which this note does not repeat as a finding — with humans reviewing and correcting before acceptance. It also records an agreed **cap on parallel agents**, stating that the team settled on a small fixed maximum "before it becomes unmanageable". The cap is the more interesting of the two, because it is a concurrency limit derived from human review capacity rather than from any technical constraint, and it is the first instance this repository has recorded of a working team writing down a number for that.

Finally, the handbook is candid that it is unfinished. It carries a list of five capability gaps addressed explicitly to future HVE Core work, a list of ISE checklist items it has merged to reduce redundancy, an open action to formalise a canonical pull-request template, an unresolved question about backlog triage cadence, a demo cadence marked TBD, and an open item to schedule a session to finalise the meeting cadence before kickoff. It is a draft that says it is a draft.

## Key claims and specifics

**The six Team Operating Principles, in the source's own short form**, with its own one-line gloss:

1. **Built for the Front Line** — "Every capability we ship earns its place in mission-critical operations and can scale across every customer business unit."
2. **Build Capability, Not Dependency** — "Every task pairs the customer's engineers with Microsoft engineers so the customer leaves the engagement stronger, not more reliant."
3. **Compound What the Customer Already Owns** — "Extend the customer's existing stack — data historians, ERP, line-of-business systems, work management — before introducing anything new."
4. **Prove It with Working Software** — "Demos beat decks. Decisions are anchored in running code, not slideware."
5. **Secure and Responsible by Design** — "Security and Responsible AI are first-commit requirements, not pre-launch checkpoints."
6. **AI Accelerates, Humans Decide** — "Every AI output ships only after a human reviews it — code, docs, or decision."

**The 8-Phase Delivery Standard**, as displayed:

| # | Phase | Duration | Owner | Stated purpose |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Discovery & Workshop | 2–3 working days (pre-work) | Whole Crew | Customer deep dives, maturity assessments, Design Thinking; AI transcription into structured outputs |
| 2 | Environment & Foundation | 5 working days | SWE | Repo, CI/CD, Copilot instructions, governance structure, observability plumbing, security champion |
| 3 | Strategic Requirements (BRD) | 5 working days | TPM | Solution-agnostic business requirements; stakeholders, SLOs, release strategy, communication plan |
| 4 | Backlog & Sprint Planning | 1 working day | TPM | AI generates the backlog from the BRD; TPM refines stories, adds acceptance criteria, assigns story points; architecture, security and RAI work tracked as Sprint 0 items |
| 5 | Architecture, Security & RAI | 5 working days | SWE + DS | ADRs, STRIDE threat model, Defender triage, a five-phase RAI assessment; findings generate additional work items in the existing backlog |
| 6 | Technical Product Spec (PRD) | 1 working day | Whole Crew | Consolidated from BRD, ADRs, security and RAI; API contracts, data models, ML pipeline, observability |
| 7 | Sprint Execution (RPI) | 40 working days | SWE + DS | "Research → Plan → Implement → Review per work item"; code-with pairing; pull-request reviews with AI audit |
| 8 | Operational Readiness & Launch | 3 working days | Whole Crew | Risk reviews, incident runbooks, documentation, launch planning, handoff, design-win capture |

- **Phases 2 to 8 sum to sixty working days**, which matches the repository README's own statement of "60 working days, post-Discovery" and its framing of the engagement as twelve working weeks. Discovery sits outside that window as pre-work.
- **The owner of Phase 4 is stated inconsistently.** The phase detail names TPM; the iterative-mapping table names SWE + DS for the same phase. Both renderings appear on the same page. This note records the discrepancy rather than choosing.

**Role taxonomy.** Four owner designations are used, and the page expands three of them: **TPM** (Technical Program Manager), **SWE** (Software Engineer), **DS** (Data Scientist), and **Whole Crew**. The allocation is legible as a claim about accountability: the TPM owns the requirements and planning phases, the engineers own environment, architecture and execution, and the whole crew owns the phases that are fundamentally negotiations with the customer — discovery, the consolidated specification, and launch and handoff.

**The iterative engagement model and its mapping.** Value Mining (Envision · Explore) → Phase 1. Prototype (Design · Develop) → Phases 2 and 3, closing on a milestone. Environment Readiness and Team Priming → Phases 4 and 5. Prototyping and Building (Build · Iterate) → Phases 6 and 7, closing on a milestone. Scale and Launch (Validate · Ship) → Phase 8, then Handoff. The source states that the Envision/Explore/Design/Develop loop "runs within each phase group, not just between them".

**The status taxonomy.** Every checklist item carries one of three statuses — **Automated**, **Assisted**, **Manual** — and Assisted and Manual are distinguished by icon in the legend. The page displays a headline total alongside the working-days figure: **21 Automated, 19 Assisted, 16 Manual**. Per-phase counts are also displayed, together with counts of items that are Recommended rather than Must Have, and, at Phase 5, items that are conditional. **The per-phase counts as rendered do not visibly reconcile with the headline total**, most likely because the headline counts Must Have items only while the per-phase strips include Recommended and conditional ones. This note records both figures as displayed and does not assert a reconciliation.

**Tags and the ISE checklist.** Seven tags classify items: Security, HVE, Observability, RAI, AI/ML, Fundamentals, Operations. The page states that these tags map to the ISE checklist and links to an internal short link for it. The item names themselves — "Code Reviews are taking place", "Testing", "Source control (repo, branching, .gitignore)", "CI/CD setup", "Agile process (standups, grooming, retros)", "Governance processes (decision logging, cadence)", "ADRs are available for most important decisions", "Dashboards", "Alerts", "Service level designations" — are recognisably the vocabulary of the ISE Engineering Fundamentals Checklist. **The handbook is best read as an FDE-specific overlay on that checklist**: it does not restate the engineering standard, it sequences it, assigns it owners and dates, and attaches an agent or tool to each item. It also records four consolidations where it merged pairs of checklist items to remove redundancy.

**The artefact chain and its direction.** BRD → backlog → ADRs → PRD, with the PRD explicitly "consolidated from BRD + ADRs + security + RAI", and the rule that **post-PRD changes are captured as ADRs, not PRD edits**.

**The stated definition of done**, verbatim in its short form: "code merged via PR + ADRs captured + security/RAI addressed + humans reviewed all AI-generated artifacts".

**Claims about AI usage.** The handbook asserts that AI agents generate the large majority of the backlog, giving a percentage band which this note does not repeat as a finding, with humans reviewing and correcting before acceptance. **It states this twice with different inputs** — once as generated from the BRD at Phase 4, once as generated from PRDs in the sprint-practice panel — and the two are not reconciled on the page. It also records that the team agreed a **small fixed cap on parallel agents**, on the stated reasoning that beyond it the work becomes unmanageable.

**Engineering conventions**, in brief: multi-repo by workstream as default, monorepo only where the customer already uses one; trunk-based development on a single `main` with no `dev` or `uat` branches; branch naming keyed to engineer alias; per-repo governance comprising a README entry point, contributing guidelines, Copilot instructions and a clear folder structure; requirements documents, decision records, transcripts and templates stored alongside code rather than in a separate document store; repository permissions and client access boundaries resolved during the workshop phase rather than assumed. On review: at least one non-author reviewer per pull request, Microsoft engineers reviewing first with customer engineers optionally involved; squash merge as default; pull requests sized as complete shippable units; continuous integration passing before merge; no hard turnaround service level but a same-day expectation when someone is blocked; inline comments as the primary feedback channel; and **AI reviewers supplementing but humans approving**. A canonical pull-request template is recorded as an open action.

**The cadence template.** A default rhythm-of-business template offering a daily brief on four days of five, a weekly backlog triage, an asynchronous weekly progress newsletter generated from the work-tracking system with no meeting, a cross-functional demo at a cadence still to be agreed with an internal dry run required before each, and a regular retrospective. Three decisions are recorded alongside it: that cadence alignment should include the customer as co-owner rather than being Microsoft-internal; that the team should start from an informed proposal and evolve rather than locking it in; and "fail fast, recover faster", with retrospectives from day one on the reasoning that a prior engagement's single closing retrospective was a mistake. **The daily-sync duration is stated inconsistently** — the operations panel gives a shorter figure than the cadence template.

**Five declared gaps, addressed to future HVE Core work:** CI/CD pipeline scaffolding at Phase 2; a proven-starting-points catalogue at Phase 5; artefact sharing and reuse tracking at Phase 7; incident-response runbook generation at Phase 8; and pre-launch operational drill orchestration at Phase 8.

## Method and evidence base

**This is a standard being proposed, not a standard being reported on.**

Its evidence base is a small number of internal working documents and meeting notes, produced by the same group that authored the handbook, over a period of a few weeks. It is self-attesting: the people describing the method are the people who devised it, writing before the method has been through a full engagement.

It reports **no outcome, no evaluation, no comparison and no evidence whatsoever that following it produces better engagements.** There is no baseline. There is no engagement that ran under this standard and one that did not. There is no defect rate, no delivery-time measurement, no customer-satisfaction result, no adoption figure and no retrospective finding beyond one anecdotal lesson about retrospective frequency. Nothing on the page claims otherwise, and the honest reading is that the authors did not intend it to.

**The durations are planning figures, not measurements.** Five working days for a business requirements document, one for the product specification, forty for execution: these are allocations in a plan, and the page gives no indication that any of them was derived from observed effort on completed work. They should be read as budgets, in exactly the way a seminar minute-budget is a budget.

**The status taxonomy is a judgement, not a measurement.** Marking an item Automated, Assisted or Manual records what the authors believe a tool or agent can currently do for that item. It is not the output of a trial. The counts are counts of those judgements.

**The AI-generation figures are assertions.** No measurement, sampling method, acceptance rate or correction rate is given for them, and the two statements of the claim disagree about what the generation input is. **The parallel-agent cap is explicitly reported as a team agreement**, which is the correct way to report it — it is a decision, and it is presented as one.

The one thing the source does warrant is what a working forward-deployed group **intended** at a moment in time. That is not nothing. It is a primary document about practice-in-formation, and it should be used the way one uses a project charter: as evidence of intent and reasoning, never as evidence of effect.

## Vocabulary the source introduces

- **FDE crew** — the embedded delivery team, referred to collectively as a crew rather than a team, with **Whole Crew** used as an explicit ownership designation for phases nobody role owns alone.
- **8-Phase Delivery Standard** — the numbered, dated, single-owner sequence from discovery to launch.
- **Value Mining** — the discovery stage of the iterative model, framed as extraction of value opportunities from the customer's existing operation rather than as requirements gathering.
- **Environment Readiness and Team Priming** — the stage that treats getting the team ready and getting the environment ready as one activity.
- **RPI** — Research, Plan, Implement, Review, applied **per work item** as the unit of execution during the forty-day sprint phase.
- **Code-with pairing** — the pairing of a customer engineer with a Microsoft engineer as the default working mode, and the mechanism by which the capability-not-dependency principle is operationalised.
- **Automated / Assisted / Manual** — the three-valued status recording the current degree of tool or agent involvement in a checklist item.
- **Must Have / Recommended / conditional** — the obligation level of an item, orthogonal to its status.
- **Sprint 0 items** — architecture, security and Responsible AI work tracked in the backlog before feature execution begins.
- **Design-win capture** — a Phase 8 activity, recording the engagement's outcome as a reusable reference.
- **Engagement ROB** — the rhythm of business, meaning the agreed meeting cadence, treated as an artefact to be negotiated with the customer rather than imposed.
- **Security champion** — a named individual owning security posture, finding triage and remediation tracking for the engagement.
- **Proven starting points** — a catalogue of reusable technical baselines, named as a gap rather than as an existing asset.

## What to remember

- **Provenance first, always.** Confidentiality marking on a personal domain, contradicted by an open-source licence in the same repository. Not citable, not published Microsoft guidance, and this constraint travels with every use of the material.
- The six operating principles are the durable content, and the sixth — that AI accelerates and humans decide — is enforced consistently through the definition of done, the review rules and the per-item status taxonomy.
- **Build Capability, Not Dependency** is the principle most worth teaching, because it defines engagement success partly as the customer's independence from the vendor.
- The phase model's real argument is its ratio: forty of sixty post-Discovery days to execution, twenty to everything else.
- **BRD → backlog → ADRs → PRD, and post-PRD change goes into ADRs rather than into the PRD.** This is the single most reusable mechanism on the page.
- Phase 7 is literally RPI, which ties this document to the [HVE Core corpus](/research/06-microsoft-platform/hve-core-repository.md); the handbook is the engagement-scale plan, the corpus is the work-item-scale implementation.
- The Automated/Assisted/Manual taxonomy is a snapshot of one team's belief about automability, and its value is that the belief was written down and counted.
- The parallel-agent cap is a **human-review-capacity limit**, not a technical one, and is the first written instance of such a number this repository has recorded.
- **Every number on the page is a plan, a judgement or an assertion. None is a measurement.**
- The document says it is unfinished, in five gaps, four merges, several open actions and at least three TBDs. Treat that candour as a feature and the content as provisional.

## Why it matters for the course

Four things, in descending order of durability.

**First, it is a worked example of a delivery standard that names an owner and a duration for every phase.** The [ISE Engineering Fundamentals Playbook](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md) tells a team what it must have; this document tells a crew who produces it and by when. The difference between a checklist and a plan is exactly the difference a student encounters the first time they try to run a project rather than assess one, and having both artefacts lets the course teach the transition rather than assert it.

**Second, it closes a real gap in the repository's account of RPI.** [`microsoft/hve-core`](/research/06-microsoft-platform/hve-core-repository.md) documents RPI as a gated agent workflow applied to a unit of work. This handbook shows the same loop **positioned inside an engagement**: Phase 7 of 8, forty working days, one pass per backlog item, downstream of a consolidated specification and upstream of operational readiness. The link is established three separate ways on the page, and none of them requires anything confidential to state. The phase's own subtitle is "Research → Plan → Implement → Review per work item", which is the corpus's loop minus its Follow-up phase. The handbook's gap list is addressed by name to **future HVE Core work**, identifying five capabilities it wants the corpus to grow — which establishes that these authors regard the corpus as the implementation substrate for the standard, not as an unrelated tool. And HVE appears as one of the seven checklist tags, with an "HVE alignment conversation" as a Must Have item in the very first phase, where the crew and the customer are required to reach shared understanding of HVE practice and its effect on the customer's organisation, roles, workflows and governance. **The course can therefore teach RPI at two scales from two independent sources**, which is considerably stronger than teaching it from the corpus alone.

**Third, it is an unusually clean specimen for the repository's central methodological lesson.** It is sincere, well-organised, internally consistent, written by practitioners, and **evidentially empty**. It asserts a delivery method, a set of durations, an automation share and an agent cap, and warrants none of them. A student who can read it, admire it, extract the mechanisms and still say precisely what it fails to establish has the competence the programme is trying to produce. It pairs naturally with the corpus note, which makes the same point about a much larger artefact.

**Fourth, its provenance problem is itself teachable.** A confidentiality marking and an MIT licence on the same artefact is a live example of the source-classification judgement the [platform namespace](/concepts/namespaces.md) exists to force, and of the discipline recorded on the [Microsoft AI Platform Map](/wiki/program/11-Microsoft-AI-Platform-Map.md) and the [Durable and Perishable Register](/wiki/program/09-Durable-and-Perishable-Register.md). Students will meet this exact situation in industry, usually without anyone flagging it.

## How to teach it

- **Never distribute the source.** Teach the abstracted structure from this note. If the operating principles are shown, show them as an anonymised example of a delivery-principle set, not as Microsoft guidance.
- **Seminar exercise on the artefact chain.** Give students the rule that post-specification changes become decision records rather than specification edits, and ask them to argue both sides: what it buys in auditability, what it costs in the specification's usefulness as a current description of the system. There is no clean answer, which is why it works.
- **Seminar exercise on the principles.** Present the six principles and ask which one a vendor would be most tempted to abandon under commercial pressure. *Build Capability, Not Dependency* is the intended answer and the discussion is the point.
- **Lab: build the plan.** Have each team convert the [ISE Engineering Fundamentals Checklist](/research/05-fde-craft/microsoft-ise-engineering-playbook.md) into their own phased plan with owners and day budgets for their project, then compare against the abstracted eight-phase shape. Assess the reasoning behind their allocation, never its resemblance to the original.
- **Lab: classify automability.** Have students apply Automated / Assisted / Manual to every item in their own plan, then justify each judgement, then revisit the classification at project close and account for every change. The delta is the assessable artefact.
- **Lab: the concurrency cap.** Have teams determine their own maximum number of parallel agents by observation rather than assertion — measuring how many concurrent agent outputs a human reviewer can actually review to standard — and defend the number they land on. This directly instruments the "AI accelerates, humans decide" principle instead of restating it.
- **Assessment: the evidence audit.** Require students to take the abstracted standard and classify every one of its assertions as declared intent, planning figure, expressed judgement, or measured result. The fourth category is empty, and discovering that it is empty is the exercise.
- **Assessment: the provenance decision.** Present the conflict between a confidentiality marking and an open-source licence on one artefact and require a written, reasoned handling decision with a stated default rule. Assess the rule, not the conclusion.
- **Contrast pairing.** Teach alongside the [Palantir practitioner writing](/research/05-fde-craft/palantir-fde-practitioner-writing.md): two forward-deployed traditions, one describing the individual craft from experience, one specifying the organisational process in advance. Ask what each cannot see.

## Limits and cautions

- **Confidentiality is the binding constraint.** The page marks itself confidential. **It must not be cited externally, quoted in published course materials, or presented as Microsoft guidance.** Only the abstracted structure recorded in this note may be carried forward, and the confidentiality status must travel with any use of it. This note has already excluded every individual's name, internal document title and internal meeting date that appears on the source.
- **The provenance is self-contradictory and cannot be resolved from the artefact.** A confidentiality marking and an MIT licence disclaiming official status are both present. This note applies the restrictive reading; a reader who needs a definitive answer must obtain one from Microsoft, and this repository has not.
- **Single-author-site risk.** The material is hosted, maintained, edited and published by one person on a personal domain, with no organisational review gate visible, no versioning of the rendered content, no change history exposed to a reader, and a repository README inviting arbitrary forks. It can change or vanish without notice, and there is no canonical revision to pin. It is not a stable citation target even setting confidentiality aside.
- **Perishability is high and compounding.** The copyright line is dated 2026 and the two internal artefacts behind it are dated within weeks of each other. The page describes a standard still being finalised: five declared gaps, four merged checklist items, an open pull-request template action, an unsettled triage cadence, a TBD demo cadence, and an explicit open item to schedule the session that finalises the meeting rhythm. **This is a draft caught mid-formation.** Its tool and agent assignments are the most perishable part of all, being tied to specific named agents in a corpus that changes weekly.
- **The AI-generation percentages are assertions with no measurement behind them.** No sampling, no acceptance rate, no correction rate, no comparison against a human-authored backlog. The claim is also stated twice with two different inputs, which is itself a reason for caution. **Do not repeat any figure from this source as a finding, and do not repeat the band at all in course materials.**
- **The durations are budgets and the counts are counts of judgements.** Neither is evidence about how long the work takes or about what can actually be automated.
- **Two internal inconsistencies are visible on the page** — the Phase 4 owner and the daily-sync duration — and a third exists between the rendered principle count and the count the repository README advertises. A document that disagrees with itself in three places is a document in motion, and any specific detail taken from it should be re-checked rather than assumed stable.
- **It is one organisation's proposed method for one kind of engagement.** It assumes a Microsoft toolchain throughout, a customer with an existing enterprise estate, a twelve-week window, and a crew with a program manager, engineers and a data scientist. None of that generalises automatically, and the specifics should never be taught as the shape a forward-deployed engagement must take.
