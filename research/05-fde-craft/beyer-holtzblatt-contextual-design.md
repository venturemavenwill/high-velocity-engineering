# Cliff Notes — Contextual Design

## Citation and access
- **Authors:** Hugh Beyer and Karen Holtzblatt
- **Title:** Contextual Design: Defining Customer-Centered Systems (Morgan Kaufmann, 1998). Second edition published as Contextual Design: Design for Life by Karen Holtzblatt and Hugh Beyer (Morgan Kaufmann, 2016); a third edition, Contextual Design: Evolved, exists as a shorter synthesis.
- **Access status:** Copyrighted book, not open access. This note is an original synthesis of the method as published and reproduces no source text.
- **Related public material:** InContext Design, the authors' consultancy, publishes method overviews and articles describing contextual inquiry and the Cool Concepts.

## What this source is
Contextual Design is a complete, named methodology that takes a team from field observation of real work to a validated system design, with a defined artifact produced at each step. It is not an interviewing technique with some analysis attached; it is a pipeline in which each stage consumes the previous stage's output, and that is what makes it teachable and auditable.

## The argument in full
The method's founding premise is a claim about the limits of self-report. People are experts in their own work but are poor at describing it, because expertise is largely tacit. Skilled workers automate the steps they perform constantly, they forget the workarounds they invented, and when asked in a conference room they describe the official process rather than the actual one. Asking what someone does therefore produces a sanitised, idealised account, and a system designed against that account will fail on contact with the real work. The remedy is to go where the work happens and watch it.

The field technique is contextual inquiry, and its governing metaphor is the master-apprentice relationship. The user is the master, working as normal; the interviewer is the apprentice, watching, asking about what is happening as it happens, and learning by observing rather than by being lectured. The authors specify four principles that make the technique reliable. Context means going to the actual workplace and observing ongoing work rather than gathering abstract summaries. Partnership means the interviewer and user jointly explore the work, rather than the interviewer running a script or the user delivering a presentation. Interpretation means the interviewer states aloud what they think an observation means and invites the user to confirm or correct it, so that inferences are validated at the moment they are formed rather than months later. Focus means the interviewer holds a clear point of view about what matters to the project while remaining alert to surprises, which are the signal that an assumption was wrong.

Observation alone produces a large volume of unstructured detail, so the method's second stage is modelling. In the original edition the team builds five work models, each capturing a different dimension. The flow model records who communicates with whom and what passes between them, exposing coordination and handoffs. The sequence model records the detailed step-by-step of a task, including the trigger that starts it and the intent behind each step, which is where automation opportunities become visible. The artifact model records the things people create and use, including annotated printouts and improvised spreadsheets, because artifacts encode the structure of the work. The cultural model records the influences, pressures and expectations that shape behaviour, including policy, management attitude and organisational fear. The physical model records the layout of the environment in which the work happens. Together these give a team five orthogonal views of the same activity, which is far harder to hand-wave than a single narrative.

The third stage is consolidation, and it is the step most often skipped in practice. Individual interviews describe individuals; a design must serve a population. The team builds an affinity diagram by writing each observation on a note and grouping notes bottom-up by natural similarity, then labelling the groups and grouping those in turn, until a hierarchy emerges that reveals the shared structure of the work across users. In parallel, the individual work models are consolidated into population-level models. The output is a picture of how the work is done across the customer base, not a set of anecdotes about specific people.

Only then does the method turn to design. Visioning is a group activity in which the team draws how the work could be done with new technology, deliberately at a level above interface detail, and the strongest vision is developed into storyboards that show the redesigned work step by step. From the storyboards the team derives the User Environment Design, which the authors describe as the floor plan of the system: the set of focus areas the user moves among, the functions available in each, and the links between them. Its value is that it makes system structure explicit and reviewable before any interface is drawn, so structural mistakes are caught while they are still cheap. The final stage is paper prototyping and iteration with users, where rough mockups are tested in the field, changed on the spot, and re-tested, so that design errors surface in hours rather than after implementation.

The second edition, Design for Life, extends the method to consumer and life contexts where work-task framing is insufficient, adding what the authors call the Cool Concepts: a set of dimensions covering what makes a product genuinely valued in a person's life and what makes it pleasant in use, including the friction that erodes adoption and the effort required to learn a system. It also adds newer models addressing daily life, identity, relationships and collaboration, reflecting that modern products are used continuously and socially rather than confined to a desk.

## Key concepts and practices
- Contextual inquiry is structured, one-to-one field observation conducted while the work is happening, not a conference-room interview.
- The master-apprentice relationship is the governing stance: the user demonstrates, the interviewer learns.
- Four principles govern the inquiry: context, partnership, interpretation, focus.
- Interpretation is done aloud with the user present, so inferences are corrected immediately.
- Five work models give orthogonal views of the same activity: flow, sequence, artifact, cultural, physical.
- Affinity diagramming builds structure bottom-up from individual observations to population-level themes.
- Consolidation is what converts a set of individual interviews into a design-relevant picture of the customer base.
- The User Environment Design specifies system structure before interface design begins.
- Paper prototypes are tested in the field and revised during the session.
- The second edition adds the Cool Concepts and models covering daily life, identity, relationships and collaboration.

## Method and evidence base
The method's authority is practitioner-derived rather than experimental. It was developed and refined through consulting engagements across many organisations, and it is validated by adoption and by the durability of its individual techniques, several of which, notably contextual inquiry and affinity diagramming, entered general user-research practice independently of the full method. The book does not offer controlled evidence that contextual design outperforms alternatives, and students should be told this plainly. Its claim is that grounding design in observed rather than reported work reduces a specific and well-documented class of failure.

## Vocabulary the source introduces
- **Contextual inquiry** — field observation of real work conducted as an apprentice to the user.
- **Master-apprentice model** — the interviewer's stance: learn by watching the expert do the work.
- **Interpretation session** — the team debrief immediately after an interview, in which observations are captured and models drawn.
- **Flow, sequence, artifact, cultural and physical models** — the five orthogonal representations of observed work.
- **Affinity diagram** — a bottom-up hierarchical grouping of individual observations that reveals cross-user structure.
- **Consolidation** — combining individual data into a population-level view of the work.
- **Visioning and storyboarding** — group design of the redesigned work, above the level of interface detail.
- **User Environment Design** — the system's floor plan: focus areas, the functions in each, and the links between them.
- **Cool Concepts** — the second edition's dimensions of what makes a product valued in life and pleasant in use.

## What to remember
- People cannot accurately describe work they have automated; observation is not a nicety but a correction for a known bias.
- The four principles are the difference between contextual inquiry and an ordinary interview.
- Interpret aloud and let the user correct you while you are still in the room.
- Artifacts, especially improvised ones, are the fastest route to the real process.
- Consolidation, not observation, is the step that turns research into design input, and it is the step teams skip.
- Structure the system before designing the interface; the User Environment Design exists to make structure arguable.
- Prototype on paper so that being wrong costs an hour.

## Why it matters for the course
This is the methodological backbone of the forward-deployed discovery strand. It supplies what most engineering curricula omit entirely: a defensible, repeatable procedure for finding out what a customer actually needs, with a named artifact at each step that can be reviewed, critiqued and graded. For an AI systems course this matters more than usual, because the dominant failure mode of applied AI projects is building a technically sound system for a workflow that does not exist as described. Contextual design also gives the course its answer to a hard assessment problem: discovery quality is otherwise difficult to grade, but work models, affinity diagrams and a User Environment Design are concrete deliverables that can be assessed against observed evidence.

## How to teach it
- Lecture: open with the self-report problem, ideally by having students describe a routine they perform daily and then watching a recording of themselves performing it.
- Lecture: teach the four principles as a checklist students can be observed against, not as background theory.
- Lab: send pairs to observe a real process on campus, such as a service desk or a lab procedure, using the apprentice stance, then build a sequence model and an artifact model from what they saw.
- Lab: run a full affinity diagramming session with notes pooled across all student pairs, so that consolidation is experienced as a group activity rather than described.
- Lab: have students produce a User Environment Design for a proposed AI assistant before they are permitted to draw any interface.
- Assessment: require the discovery deliverable to include at least one finding that contradicts the team's initial assumption, with the observation that produced it cited; surprise is the evidence that the inquiry was real.

## Limits and cautions
- The method is strongest where students can observe real work; access is often the binding constraint, and remote or regulated environments limit what can be seen.
- The full method is heavy, and teams under time pressure routinely truncate it; the honest teaching position is to name which stages are compressible and which are not.
- Observation is costly in expert time, which is exactly the resource customers guard most closely.
- The original edition is oriented to workplace task work; consumer, continuous-use and social contexts are better served by the second edition's additions.
- Observing changes behaviour, and the method depends on the interviewer's skill at reducing that effect rather than eliminating it.
- Evidence for the method is practitioner experience and adoption, not controlled comparison against alternatives.
