# Cliff Notes — The Mom Test

## Citation and access
- **Author:** Rob Fitzpatrick
- **Title:** The Mom Test: How to talk to customers and learn if your business is a good idea when everyone is lying to you
- **Publisher and year:** Self-published, 2013
- **Access status:** Copyrighted book, not open access. This note is an original synthesis and reproduces no source text.
- **Related public material:** The author maintains a public site and has given widely circulated talks summarising the rules; short summaries are common but frequently reduce the book to its title rule and omit the commitment material, which is the more useful half.

## What this source is
A short, deliberately blunt rulebook for customer conversations, written for founders but directly applicable to any engineer doing discovery. Its distinctive contribution is that it locates the failure in the interviewer rather than the interviewee: people will inevitably tell you what you want to hear, so the discipline required is on your side of the conversation.

## The argument in full
The book's title encodes its premise. A Mom Test question is one you could ask your own mother and still get a truthful answer, because the question is constructed so that her desire to be encouraging cannot corrupt it. Ask her whether your business idea is good and she will say yes, not because she is dishonest but because that is what affection does. The conclusion the author draws is the load-bearing one: it is not the customer's job to tell you the truth, it is your job to ask questions that cannot be answered falsely. Blaming respondents for bad data is an evasion.

Three rules follow. Talk about their life instead of your idea, because the moment your idea is on the table the conversation becomes a social performance about your idea. Ask about specifics in the past instead of generics or opinions about the future, because concrete history is checkable while predictions are not. Talk less and listen more, because interviewer airtime is the clearest measure of a wasted session.

The author then classifies the bad data these rules are designed to exclude, and the taxonomy is what makes the book operationally useful. Compliments are the first category, and the book is severe about them: praise feels like progress and contains no information, so a session that produced enthusiasm and nothing else produced nothing. Fluff is the second: generic statements about what someone always or usually does, hypotheticals about what they would do, and expressions of future intent. The author's position on the last of these is deliberately provocative, that anything involving the future is an optimistic lie, and the practical instruction is to anchor fluff back to a specific past instance whenever it appears. Ideas are the third category. Customers volunteer feature requests constantly, and the request itself is nearly worthless; what matters is the problem underneath it, so the correct response is to dig for the motivating incident rather than to write the feature down.

A memorable diagnostic follows from all this: you should be terrified of at least one of the questions you are asking. If every question in your guide is comfortable, you have designed a session that cannot disconfirm your plan. The questions that threaten your assumptions are the only ones that carry information, and avoiding them is the most common way a discovery process becomes theatre.

The book's second half moves from question quality to a subject most interviewing guides omit, which is how to tell whether a conversation actually meant anything. The author's answer is commitment and advancement. Enthusiasm is free; commitment costs the customer something. The currencies he identifies are time, meaning a scheduled follow-up or a substantial working session rather than a vague promise to reconnect; reputation risk, meaning an introduction to a colleague, a boss or a peer company, which the customer would not make if they thought the work was worthless; and money, whether a deposit, a letter of intent or a budget line. A meeting that ends with none of these ended badly regardless of how warm it was, and the author is explicit that a meeting is not a success just because they said yes.

Surrounding this is a set of process instructions that make the technique repeatable. Know what you are trying to learn before the conversation; if you cannot name the three most important things you need to find out, do not have the meeting. Keep the conversation short and informal, because a casual chat produces more honest material than a formal scheduled meeting, which invites performance on both sides. Segment customers precisely, because a vague segment produces contradictory findings that are actually findings about different populations. Take good notes in the customer's own words and review them with the team promptly, since the value of a conversation decays quickly.

## Key concepts and practices
- The three rules: talk about their life not your idea; ask about specifics in the past not generics or the future; talk less and listen more.
- It is your job to ask good questions, not the customer's job to volunteer the truth.
- Compliments feel like validation and carry no information.
- Fluff comprises generic claims, hypotheticals and statements of future intent; anchor it to a specific past instance.
- Feature requests are symptoms; dig for the underlying problem and the incident that produced it.
- At least one question in your guide should frighten you.
- Commitment currencies are time, reputation risk and money.
- A meeting is not a success because the customer was positive; it is a success if it advanced.
- Know your three most important questions before the conversation, or do not have it.
- Casual beats formal; formality invites performance.
- Segment until the segment is specific enough that its members would answer consistently.

## Method and evidence base
This is practitioner advice distilled from startup customer-development practice, with the author's own failures used as the primary illustrations. There is no experimental evidence and the book does not pretend otherwise; its persuasive force comes from the recognisability of the failure modes it describes. Students should be told this explicitly and encouraged to test the rules rather than accept them, which is straightforward because each rule produces an observable difference within a single conversation.

## Vocabulary the source introduces
- **The Mom Test** — a question construction that yields truthful answers even from someone who wants to encourage you.
- **Compliments** — positive feedback that feels like validation and contains no information.
- **Fluff** — generic, hypothetical or future-tense statements, to be anchored back to concrete past events.
- **Ideas** — volunteered feature requests, to be treated as symptoms of an underlying problem.
- **Deflect, anchor, dig** — the three responses to compliments, fluff and ideas respectively.
- **Commitment and advancement** — the test of whether a conversation had value, measured by what the customer gave up.
- **Currencies of commitment** — time, reputation risk and money.

## What to remember
- Behaviour beats opinion, and past beats future.
- Compliments are not data; a warm meeting can be a total loss.
- Anchor every generality to a specific recent instance before believing it.
- Dig beneath feature requests to the problem and the incident.
- If no question in your guide scares you, redesign the guide.
- Judge the conversation by the commitment it produced, not by the mood in the room.
- Ownership of data quality sits with the interviewer, not the customer.

## Why it matters for the course
This is the source that inoculates students against the most predictable discovery failure, which is collecting flattering responses and reporting them as validation. It is short enough to assign in full and rule-based enough to grade against, which makes it unusually efficient as a teaching text. The commitment and advancement material also gives the course something rare: an objective test for whether a stakeholder conversation succeeded, which converts an otherwise subjective assessment into a checkable one. In an AI systems course this matters acutely, because enthusiasm for AI projects is abundant and cheap, and distinguishing genuine demand from interest is exactly the judgement a forward-deployed engineer must exercise before committing engineering effort.

## How to teach it
- Lecture: run the compliment trap live by having students pitch an idea to each other and count the encouraging responses, then reveal that none of it was data.
- Lab: give students a set of fluff statements and require them to write the anchoring follow-up for each.
- Lab: have students convert a feature request into three candidate underlying problems and design a question that would distinguish them.
- Lab: require each student to identify the question about their own project that frightens them most, then ask it in a real conversation.
- Assessment: require every stakeholder conversation log to record what commitment was obtained, in which currency, or to state explicitly that none was; a log of enthusiasm with no commitment scores as a failed conversation.
- Assessment: pair this with Portigal so that students learn both the rules and the moment-to-moment technique for applying them.

## Limits and cautions
- The book is written for founders validating a business idea, and some framing, particularly around sales commitment, needs translation for internal enterprise contexts where budget authority sits elsewhere.
- It is intentionally simple and memorable, which means it is not a complete interviewing curriculum; pair it with a fuller craft text.
- The commitment test can be misapplied in contexts where a legitimate stakeholder genuinely has nothing to commit, such as an end user in a large organisation.
- The rule against future-tense questions is absolute in the book and needs nuance where planned change, such as an announced regulatory deadline, is a genuine and checkable constraint.
- There is no empirical evidence base; the claims are practitioner heuristics.
