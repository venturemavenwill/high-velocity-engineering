## What this source is
The book is Kapur's own trade-press statement of Productive Failure after roughly two decades of work on it: what the design is, the four mechanisms he says drive it, how to build it for other people and for yourself, and — the part that matters most here — the conditions under which he says it does and does not apply. Dillenbourg's Foreword names the intended audience: "learners, teachers, and policy makers, but also parents, sport coaches, and managers". It is not written for a research audience, and it is structured accordingly: Part I states the problem and the solution, Part II gives the science, Part III gives the design principles.

The construct is proactive, and Kapur insists on that word. "Many people have talked about the value of learning from failure. These accounts, however, are largely reactive; they talk about learning from failure *after* it happens. The idea of Productive Failure is to be proactive; that is, if failure is so powerful for learning, then we should not wait for it to happen. We should intentionally design for it for deep learning." (Introduction, "My Forays into Failure".) The design is two-phase and the order is the whole claim: **problem solving followed by instruction**, which he summarises as "Fail First, Learn Later" (Ch. 2, closing).

The meta-analysis is a separate object. It is quantitative evidence about the *ordering*, aggregated across studies. The book is the author's account of the *design*. They answer different questions, and the boundary conditions each states are different — which is the central finding of this rewrite.

## The argument in full

### The problem the design is answering

Kapur's route into the field was a failure to teach. He taught high-school mathematics for five years on the theory that "if I could engage my students, explain the concepts as clearly as possible, then show them, step-by-step, exactly what to do and how to do it, I'd achieve transformational results" (Introduction, "Into the Classroom"). It did not work, and the reading he did afterwards told him why it does not work for anyone: "The problem was not that we learn poorly from bad lectures, rather that we learn poorly from excellent ones" (Introduction, "Venturing into Academia").

Chapter 1 states three problems of learning — failing to remember, failing to understand, failing to transfer — and grounds each in named literature: Bjork and Bjork's New Theory of Disuse for retention; de Groot, Jongman, and Chase and Simon on expert–novice perception for understanding; Godden and Baddeley, and Brown, Collins and Duguid's situated cognition, for transfer. The understanding problem is the one the design is built to solve, and Kapur states it as a paradox: "A novice, by definition, does not have the knowledge to see what is critical. Yet, the novice needs to somehow be able to see the critical features to be able to develop expertise." His resolution is the single most quotable line in the book, and it is the line this repository's seminar pattern rests on: **"the first job of teaching is actually not to teach. The first job of teaching is to prepare the novice to see with an expert's eyes."** (Introduction, "Finding the Cause and Its Solution".)

### The design, and what it is not

Chapter 2 sets up Direct Instruction against Discovery Learning and concedes the comparison without qualification: "When pitted against each other, science is clear and robust on this question. Direct Instruction beats Discovery Learning hands down." The move he makes is not to rehabilitate discovery but to attack an assumption underneath Direct Instruction — "**making learning easy eases learning**" — which he calls "the foundational block in the Direct Instruction Jenga tower".

Productive Failure turns the two-phase model around: instead of Instruction → Problem Solving, it runs **Problem Solving → Instruction**. He is explicit that this is not discovery learning: "Productive Failure is not the same as Discovery Learning, for it combines the benefits of constrained discovery learning through problem solving, followed by instruction." The instruction phase is not optional and not decorative. Chapter 8 states the dependency in both directions: "Without the initial failure, none of this would be possible. Without the subsequent instruction or scaffold, even the initial failure would be unproductive."

The canonical task is the basketball problem (Figure 2.1): two players' scores across nine games, "who is more consistent?", posed before standard deviation has been taught, with the instruction to "invent as many solutions as possible without worrying about getting them right or wrong". Totals, means, minima, maxima and ranges are all designed to be identical between the two players, so that every solution a novice can reach fails. Students typically produce four to six solutions in 30–45 minutes; none is canonically correct.

### The four mechanisms — the 4As

Part II names four mechanisms and gives each a chapter: **Activation, Awareness, Affect, Assembly**. Kapur's summary: "In the problem-solving phase of Productive Failure, students generate multiple solutions to the problem, but as expected, they fail to solve the problem correctly. This, however, results in a strong activation of the cognitive system because it activates relevant prior knowledge needed for learning. Failure is crucial here, as it also builds awareness of a gap between what we know and what we need to know. This awareness in turn drives affect — needs, interest, curiosity, motivation, and emotions — to learn from subsequent instruction." (Part II opener.)

**Activation (Ch. 3).** Kapur places methods on an "activation spectrum" adapted from Chi's ICAP framework, from listening at the low end to problem solving at the high end, and argues that Productive Failure *extends* the spectrum by moving from "success-based activation" to **failure-based activation**. The mechanism he invokes is the failed generation effect, stated as: "attempting to answer questions about an unfamiliar topic before learning improves learning of that topic even if the initial answers were incorrect, and the effect holds whether you are learning words, reading texts, or learning from videos." He gives three sub-mechanisms — **processing** (Bjork's desirable difficulties), **preparation** (Schwartz and Bransford's preparation for future learning, which he unpacks as activating priors, differentiating priors, encoding variability, and building cognitive flexibility), and **priming** (pretest questions focus attention and reduce mind wandering).

**Awareness (Ch. 4).** The load-bearing study is VanLehn et al. (2003): roughly 125 hours of tutorial dialogue between 42 university students and 2 experienced physics tutors, in which "impasses" were strongly associated with learning gains and — the sharper finding — "when students did not encounter such impasses, they hardly learned anything no matter what the tutor said or explained." Kapur draws the operational instruction directly: "if you are the tutee, remind your tutor to withhold explanations until you are stuck, because explanations are more likely to be useful in those moments than others." **He then concedes the strong form of his own mechanism**: "Impasses, failures, errors, and misconceptions are sufficient, but not necessary. If failure happens or is designed for, it can create an awareness of a gap. It is sufficient. But it is strictly not necessary." He demonstrates the concession by showing that awe also produces knowledge-gap awareness (McPhetres 2019), with no failure involved.

**Affect (Ch. 5).** Failure creates "a cognitive and affective cliffhanger". He builds a four-level ladder of what he calls Affective Boost Levels (ABLes) out of behavioural-economics findings — loss aversion, the endowed progress effect, the goal-gradient effect, and completion-for-its-own-sake — and adds situational interest and curiosity (Loibl and Rummel 2014; Lamnina and Chase 2017, 2019; Kang et al. 2009 and Gruber et al. 2014 on the neuroimaging side), then mastery-goal orientation (Belenky and Nokes-Malach 2012). The emotions section reports his student Tanmay Sinha's 2022 finding, and it is genuinely counterintuitive: **shame was positively correlated with the quality of solutions students produced**, alongside anger, disgust and confusion, while **happiness was positively correlated only with procedural performance and negatively correlated with conceptual understanding and transfer**. His gloss: "Positive emotions such as happiness can be bad for learning, even when experienced in a safe and supportive environment such as Productive Failure."

**Assembly (Ch. 6).** This is the chapter most often lost in second-hand accounts and the one that most constrains the consolidation phase of any teaching design. Kapur's metaphor is Lego blocks, adapted from diSessa's phenomenological primitives: "Good teaching involves identifying parts of a student's thinking or reasoning that can be used for learning." He works the standard-deviation assembly step by step, showing nine student-generated blocks reassembled into the canonical formula, and demonstrates the same move on the force-as-mover misconception in physics: rather than telling students they are wrong, "Your intuition is correct in the sense that there is indeed something the ball has in the direction of its motion, but that thing is not force. It is momentum."

He then states the dependency that makes the whole design fragile: **"the power of Productive Failure cannot be realized without assembly. You can activate all the prior knowledge you want, create heightened awareness, build the right affective state, but without a proper assembly, all of it goes to waste."**

Assembly runs on comparison. Kapur reports Gick and Holyoak's radiation-problem results in full — one analogous story lifts the solution rate from about 10% to about 30%; **making the underlying principle explicit on top of one story moves the needle not at all**; two stories reach about 45%, two stories plus the explicit principle about 60%, and a direct prompt to use the story about 80%. And he reports Loibl and Leuders (2019) with 200 fifth-graders, in which one group studied only correct solutions, one studied correct and incorrect solutions, and one studied both **plus prompts to compare them**: "The mere provision of correct and incorrect solutions was not enough; students had to be explicitly prompted to compare the two, to figure out what worked and what did not, and only then did they learn better."

### Whether the failures stick

Kapur takes the obvious objection seriously — is it not dangerous to activate misconceptions? — and answers it twice, from opposite directions. **Pruning:** the brain removes unused connections given feedback, so generated errors can be suppressed. **Promoting:** errors can serve as mediators, "Sometimes you have to go through the incorrect knowledge to get to the correct knowledge, and there is nothing to worry about that."

Two of his own experiments matter here more than the argument. **Kapur (2011), guided versus unguided:** giving prompts and hints during the problem-solving phase reduced the failure rate and *hurt* learning — "Encouraging or promoting failure facilitates learning. **Reducing failure hurts learning.**" **Kapur (2014), vicarious failure:** a condition in which students studied peers' failed solutions rather than generating their own placed second — Productive Failure > Vicarious Failure > Direct Instruction on deep understanding and transfer, with all three equal on procedural knowledge.

### The design principles

Chapter 7 opens by flagging its own hedges: "You might have noticed that several times in the earlier chapters, I have qualified my claims with caveats like 'when designed well,' 'well curated,' 'based on Productive Failure principles.' I did this to signal that Productive Failure has its own set of design principles, and only when learning is designed based on these principles can we call it Productive Failure, and expect powerful learning outcomes." The principles were worked out with Kate Bielaczyc over about a year, prompted by her question: "Manu, if you were to ask a teacher to design a lesson using your theory of Productive Failure, what exactly would you tell them they should do, and how they should go about doing it?"

The framework is three nested layers — **task**, nested in **participation**, nested in **social surround** — plus iterative calibration.

**Task: seven features**, which he says "often take two to three iterations to develop".
1. **Keep it challenging but accessible.** A task using formal terminology fails because the novice has no "in": "they are likely to simply give up and not produce anything."
2. **Contextualise.** "By contextualizing the challenge, learners explore more aspects of the problem and are given tools to evaluate their progress."
3. **Admit multiple solutions and representations.** With a redesign test attached: "If you design a task that you think invites multiple solutions, yet most students are unable to generate more than one solution, then you need to redesign the task."
4. **Create an affective draw,** via Berlyne's collative properties: novelty, intuitiveness, uncertainty, conflict.
5. **Use contrasting cases** — at least two, "Ideally, good tasks should include contrasts that can be intuitively, and even better if perceptually, seen".
6. **Vary the cases** — hold some features invariant and vary others, so learners are nudged onto the critical feature.
7. **Minimise computational load.** "Students should focus on designing ideas and exploring their properties, rather than on calculating or applying solutions."

He closes the list with the scope claim that matters most for this repository: "**Note also how the features do not contain any information or specificity about the domain itself; that is, the features are not tied to mathematics or any other domain. They remain domain general, meaning that they can be used to design Productive Failure tasks in any domain.**"

**Participation.** Solo or small groups of two to three both work; Mazziotti et al. (2019) found no difference, but "my 2021 meta-analysis led by my then–doctoral student Tanmay Sinha found that working together during the generation phase of Productive Failure had an overall significant effect on learning", and he treats the meta-analysis as the stronger evidence, so the principle is to design for collaboration. He concedes the cost immediately: "not everyone may be skilled or experienced in working as part of a team. Not everyone knows how to collaborate. And not everyone wants to collaborate either... Therefore, when using collaboration as a way of participation, we need to make sure that we help people learn to collaborate so that they can collaborate to learn." His preferred variant is **alone, then together**. Facilitation is reduced to two prompts: **explain your idea** and **hack your idea** — the latter meaning "Deliberately seeking the counterfactual – the situation where the idea does not work anymore".

**Social surround.** Psychological safety, credited to Carl Rogers and to Amy Edmondson's organisational work, operationalised as **re-norming** (state the expectations, prepare students for frustration as "in fact indicators of meaningful learning taking place", have educators share their own failures) and **mind-setting** (growth mindset, via Dweck, Yeager et al. 2019 and Burnette et al. 2023). The norms are not decoration: "**The effectiveness of Productive Failure is greater in contexts that are in alignment with the new norms and expectations.**"

### Where he says it does not work

This is the section second-hand accounts drop, and Kapur states it in three distinct places.

**Learning goals.** "Productive Failure is good for learning things that you need to deeply understand and transfer to novel situations. If, however, the learning goals are more procedural (e.g. going through a protocol, following a procedure, rote memorization), then you do not need to learn using Productive Failure. **Unless of course you need to understand why the procedure is the way it is.**" (Ch. 7, "Iterate and Calibrate".)

**Design fidelity.** Asked to hack his own theory for a 2019 EARLI keynote titled "When Productive Failure Fails", he and Sinha ran the systematic review that became the 2021 meta-analysis. "We found a simple answer. **Productive Failure fails when it is badly designed or implemented.** Essentially suggesting that even if the science underlying it was sound and robust, Productive Failure could still fail if it was not designed well, much like a badly designed Ferrari would." (Ch. 8.)

**Difficulty calibration.** From the self-design chapter: "Of course, the challenge should not be so hard that you completely give up. Nor should it be harmful in any way (the principle of safe space applies). Finding the sweet spot is key." And from the Introduction: "Done wrong, failure can be detrimental... After all, total, inexplicable failure can be intensely frustrating and can push students to drop a subject altogether."

He also draws one boundary inside Chapter 4 that is easy to over-extend: the counter-explanation findings "do not apply to strongly held belief systems, because the latter are not intuitive in nature; instead, they are beliefs we get enculturated into over many years".

### The criticism that it suits only strong or motivated learners

Chapter 7 opens on exactly this objection and rejects it in its first sentence: "**One of the common but unfounded criticisms of Productive Failure is that maybe it is not for everyone.** Perhaps Productive Failure is better suited for those with higher abilities, motivation, and resilience. Because those without these attributes may get frustrated and give up, making failure unproductive."

His argument is a category objection, not an empirical one: "when we attribute a quality like 'motivation' to an individual, such as when we say Julie is motivated but John is not, we are making an error. Motivation is not something an individual possesses in fixed quantities, like having green eyes or black hair. The same is true for ability or resilience. These qualities are not something an individual possesses but instead are best viewed as an interaction between the individual and the learning environment. **In a well-designed learning environment, more people will appear resilient and motivated.** If these concepts are conceived as interactions, the burden is then on the designer."

He has one empirical counter, and it is the strongest thing in the book on this point. Belenky and Nokes-Malach (2012) compared Tell-and-Practice against Invention: "for students who already had a high mastery goal orientation, the type of instruction (Invention or Tell-and-Practice) did not matter much – they excelled either way. But **for those not naturally inclined toward mastering material, the Invention method made a world of difference.** Mastery goal induction helped them the most." He reads the growth-mindset meta-analysis the same way — Burnette et al. (2023) found interventions "especially effective for learners who needed it the most, typically at-risk learners".

**The conditional attached to that counter is the part to keep.** The Yeager et al. (2019) result — roughly 13,000 students across more than 60 US schools — worked only where the school's norms supported it: "Crucially, the effectiveness of the growth mindset intervention was contingent on the existing norms in the school... Therefore, when designing for Productive Failure, it is not sufficient just to emphasize the mindset message, but to actively realign the norms that support the message as well."

### What he concedes

- **Failure is sufficient, not necessary.** Stated verbatim in Chapter 4, and demonstrated with the awe studies. This concedes that a design could reach the same awareness by other means.
- **Positive affect can hurt.** Happiness during the problem-solving phase was negatively correlated with conceptual understanding and transfer.
- **Collaboration has costs** and is not universally wanted or possessed as a skill.
- **The mechanism evidence is student-based.** "Even though evidence for these mechanisms comes mainly from either lab-based or classroom-based experiments with students, the mechanisms themselves apply more universally." The second clause is asserted, not shown.
- **Not everyone arrives equipped.** "Clearly, not everyone can be like them. Not everyone has the innate passion, ability, or preparation to cope with let alone learn from failure." This is his motivation for designing rather than waiting, but it is a concession about the starting distribution.
- **Some concepts cannot be self-generated.** "It is highly improbable that a student in high school would be able to self-generate principles of algebra or calculus on their own without any explicit help or instruction."
- **The design is hard, and iterative.** "Designing for Productive Failure is not easy... Typically, two to three iterations are needed for the design to work well for a particular context."
- **Luck.** The final design principle in Chapter 8 is "Acknowledge luck", and the origin narrative in Chapter 2 turns on it: no school would participate — "Nobody bought it" — until "It was pure luck amidst all the trying to stumble upon a school whose principal happened to be my high school math teacher."

**Magnitudes the book states.** Recorded here for completeness; see `## Limits and cautions` for why none of them may be asserted in the wiki.
- On the 2021 meta-analysis: "more than 50 studies... reporting more than 160 experimental comparisons of students in four different continents", of which "more than 80% of the studies... were independent replications."
- Benchmarks he gives for the same meta-analysis: "the relative effect of learning from Productive Failure was **up to three times (that's 300%) that of learning from a good teacher for one year**" and "Productive Failure students were performing **up to two academic years ahead** of Direct Instruction students."
- On his own experiments: both conditions equal on procedural knowledge; Productive Failure ahead on conceptual understanding and transfer at three-star significance (his convention: *p* < 0.001), with "the effect on transfer... much stronger than on conceptual understanding". He names this the **Productive Failure Effect**.
- Ordering across the three-condition study: Productive Failure > Vicarious Failure > Direct Instruction on deep understanding and transfer; all equal on procedural knowledge (Ch. 6, on Kapur 2014).
- Gick and Holyoak transfer sequence, as reported in Ch. 6: about 10% base rate; about 30% with one analogous story; **about 30% with one story plus the principle stated explicitly — no movement**; about 45% with two stories; about 60% with two stories plus the principle; about 80% with a direct prompt to use the story.
- Failure rates in the failed-generation studies he cites: Kornell et al. (2009), about 1.5 correct out of 60 pairs; Potts and Shanks (2014), about 94% incorrect; Richland et al. (2009), about 95% incorrect on the pretest — and in each the failing group outperformed the studying group afterwards.
- Free play with a novel toy (Sim and Xu 2017, as reported): children not shown how the toy worked were "more than three times as inventive", **and both groups learned equally well how the toy worked**.

**From the meta-analysis abstract (unchanged; still abstract-only).**
- **53 studies, 166 comparisons** of PS-I against I-PS.
- Overall: significant, moderate effect favouring PS-I, **Hedges' *g* = 0.36, 95% CI [0.20, 0.51]**.
- With **high fidelity to Productive Failure principles**, effects stronger: **Hedges' *g* between 0.37 and 0.58**.
- **Productive Failure is characterised as a subset variant of PS-I design** — the specific formulation, not the general ordering. **The book independently confirms this framing.**
- Moderators contributing to PS-I efficacy: **students' grade level, intervention time span, and the (quasi-)experimental nature of the study**.
- **Direction reverses** for **younger learners (second to fifth graders)** and for **learning of domain-general skills**: effect sizes favoured I-PS. **The book does not repeat this finding anywhere** — see `## Verification status`.
- After accounting for publication bias, estimated true effect: **Hedges' *g* = 0.87**, described as a strong effect favouring PS-I. The book states neither 0.36 nor 0.87.

**From the 2008 article's abstract (unchanged; still unread, and not cited by the book).**
- Eleventh-grade science students; Newtonian kinematics; computer-supported collaborative learning; random assignment; ill-structured-first groups produced poor-quality solutions but outperformed the well-structured condition on individual **near- and far-transfer** measures; "a latent productivity in what initially seemed to be failure".

## Method and evidence base

**The book's evidence base is a narrative synthesis of other people's experiments plus the author's own.** It is not itself a study. Its claims rest on three tiers, which it does not always distinguish for the reader but which are traceable through its chapter-by-chapter reference list.

*Tier 1 — Kapur's own experiments.* The origin experiment is described in unusual procedural detail in Chapter 2 and is worth reading for its methodological candour: one school, one teacher, random assignment, controlled variables ("same school, learning the same curriculum, the same concept, with the same teacher, for the same duration, at a similar time of day"), both conditions piloted so that neither is straw-manned ("Both designs need to be well-tuned before comparing"), and three outcome signals — **how** (procedural), **why** (conceptual), **apply** (transfer). The recruitment difficulty is recorded rather than hidden: no school would take part because of the word *failure*. Later experiments manipulate guidance during the problem-solving phase (2011) and add a vicarious-failure condition (2014).

*Tier 2 — the 2021 meta-analysis.* Reported in the book only in benchmark form. The book does not give Hedges' *g*, confidence intervals, heterogeneity statistics, moderator directions or the publication-bias correction. A reader who wants those must read the article, which has not been read for this note.

*Tier 3 — the wider literature.* Roughly 90 references across eight chapters, covering memory (Bjork and Bjork; Slamecka and Graf; Kornell; Potts and Shanks), expertise (de Groot; Chase and Simon), situated cognition (Brown, Collins and Duguid; Godden and Baddeley), tutoring (VanLehn), impasses and misconceptions (Sánchez; Acuña; Loibl and Rummel; Glogger-Frey; Hartmann; Butterfield and Metcalfe; Pacaci, Ustun and Ozdemir), curiosity (Berlyne; Piaget; Bonawitz; Schulz; Gopnik; Kang; Gruber), motivation (Dweck; Elliot; Harackiewicz; Hulleman; Wirthwein), behavioural economics (Kahneman and Tversky; Hull; Kivetz; Nunes and Drèze; Bonezzi; Converse), case comparison (Gick and Holyoak; Alfieri, Nokes-Malach and Schunn) and psychological safety (Edmondson).

**Three cautions attach to using the book as evidence.**

First, **it is a trade book and argues like one**. Chapters open with anecdote — a Fields Medallist at lunch, a knee injury, a six-year-old demolishing a logical argument, a conference attendee correcting "carpenting" to "carpentry". These are illustrative, not evidential, and Kapur does not always mark the transition. The Michael Jordan, Jack Ma and Steven Spielberg examples that open Part II are explicitly hedged — "I do not know the full spectrum of learning experiences they had in their lives" — but they are still doing rhetorical work.

Second, **the reported studies are reported at the author's chosen grain**. Sample sizes, designs and effect directions are given, but confidence intervals, heterogeneity and pre-registration status generally are not. Any figure in this note that traces to the book traces to the book's summary of a study, not to the study.

Third, **the extrapolation from classroom to workplace is asserted, not demonstrated**. Kapur says "I have applied this cycle in multiple contexts, ranging from formal educational settings like schools and universities, to corporate training programs and adult education. It works." No corporate or adult-education study is cited in support of that sentence anywhere in the reference list.

*The meta-analysis's method, unchanged and still abstract-only.* 53 studies, 166 comparisons, Hedges' *g* with confidence intervals, moderator analyses and a publication-bias-corrected estimate. The inclusion of an explicit fidelity dimension is a methodological strength. Two cautions stand: the bias-corrected estimate of 0.87 more than doubles the observed 0.36, which is the opposite of the usual direction and indicates that the correction model is doing substantial work; and the constituent studies are predominantly short school and university interventions in mathematics and science, so extrapolation to multi-week professional project work remains an extrapolation.

## Vocabulary the source introduces

**From the book.**
- **Productive Failure (PF)** — a two-phase design in which learners generate multiple solutions to a task targeting a concept they have not been taught, fail, and are then taught; "at once three things: a design, a science, and a mantra for life."
- **The 4As** — **Activation, Awareness, Affect, Assembly**: the four mechanisms Kapur says drive the effect.
- **Activation spectrum** — a ranking of learning activities by how widely they activate relevant prior knowledge, extended by Kapur from Chi's **ICAP** framework (Interactive, Constructive, Active, Passive) to include **failure-based activation** beyond **success-based activation**.
- **Failed generation effect** (also *unsuccessful retrieval effect*) — attempting an answer before instruction improves later learning even when the attempt is wrong.
- **Assembly** — the instruction-phase work of mining learners' failed solutions for usable components and recombining them with expert knowledge.
- **Lego blocks** — Kapur's term for those components; his rendering of diSessa's **phenomenological primitives (p-prims)**.
- **The three layers** — **task**, **participation**, **social surround**: the nesting of the design principles.
- **Collative properties** — Berlyne's novelty, intuitiveness, uncertainty and conflict, used to build the **affective draw**.
- **Explain your idea / hack your idea** — the two-step facilitation scheme; *hacking* means deliberately seeking the counterfactual in which one's own solution breaks.
- **Re-norming** and **mind-setting** — the two operations that build the social surround.
- **Affective Boost Levels (ABLes 1–4)** — loss avoidance, endowed progress, goal gradient, completion.
- **The first-lap hack / looking-back hack / move-the-goalpost-nearer hack** — self-directed applications of ABLes 2 and 3.
- **Productive discontentment** and **productive discomfort** — the dispositions Kapur says get a learner into the Zone and keep them there.
- **The basic knowledge fallacy** — the belief that foundations can be built efficiently by Direct Instruction and depth added later.
- **The creativity fallacy** — its corollary: correct knowledge given too early constrains inventiveness.
- **Vicarious Failure** — studying peers' failed solutions instead of generating one's own.
- **The learning–performance two-by-two** — **Productive Success**, **Productive Failure**, **Unproductive Failure** (pure discovery: low performance *and* low learning), **Unproductive Success** (rote or Direct Instruction: high performance, shallow learning — "a hollow victory").
- **How / Why / Apply** — his three outcome signals: procedural knowledge, conceptual understanding, transfer.

**From the meta-analysis abstract.**
- **PS-I** (problem solving followed by instruction) and **I-PS** (instruction followed by problem solving).
- **Fidelity to Productive Failure principles** — coded as a moderator.
- **Ill-structured** and **well-structured problem**; **near** and **far transfer**; **latent productivity** (2008 abstract).

## What to remember

- The design is **two-phase and order-dependent**, and both phases are load-bearing. Generation without consolidation is Unproductive Failure; consolidation without generation is Direct Instruction.
- **Assembly is the fragile phase.** Kapur says the whole effect is lost without it, and the experimental evidence says that *showing* correct and incorrect solutions is not enough — learners must be **explicitly prompted to compare them**.
- **Do not scaffold the generation phase.** Kapur's own 2011 experiment shows that prompts and hints that reduce the failure rate reduce learning.
- **The boundary Kapur actually states is about learning goals, not about learners.** Procedural, protocol-following and rote goals do not need Productive Failure — unless the goal is to understand *why* the procedure is as it is.
- **He calls the "only for strong learners" criticism unfounded**, argues that motivation, ability and resilience are person–environment interactions rather than fixed traits, and reports evidence that the design helps *least*-inclined learners most.
- **But he attaches a norms condition to that answer.** Interventions of this kind work better where the surrounding norms already reward taking on challenge; realigning norms is part of the design, not a preliminary.
- **He concedes failure is sufficient but not necessary** for the awareness the design is built to produce.
- **Design principles are domain general** by his explicit claim; the tasks they generate are domain specific.
- **The book never cites his own 2008 founding article**, and never repeats the meta-analysis's young-learner reversal.
- The book states magnitudes in benchmark form (up to three times a year of good teaching; up to two academic years ahead) and **states neither *g* = 0.36 nor *g* = 0.87**.

## Why it matters for the course

This is the source for the ordering of the eight-phase seminar day. The programme's whole teaching pattern puts generation before instruction, and every day in [/wiki/program/04-Seminar-Day-Design-Pattern.md](/wiki/program/04-Seminar-Day-Design-Pattern.md) opens on a task the cohort cannot yet complete. That is Productive Failure, and now that the book has been read the design can be checked against the author's own conditions rather than against a second-hand summary of a meta-analysis abstract.

**The convergence with the training-design literature holds.** [Keith and Frese](/research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md) reach the same conclusion from error-management training: learners should meet the difficulty before they are given the resolution, and the payoff shows on transfer rather than during the session. The two literatures are independent and agree. For a curriculum whose graduates will routinely meet problems nobody has briefed them on, that convergence is the strongest warrant the programme has for its ordering.

**Three of the book's findings tighten the design in ways the previous note did not.**

*Do not scaffold the generation phase.* Kapur's 2011 experiment is the cleanest instruction in the book for a course designer: prompts and hints during problem solving reduced the failure rate and reduced learning. A seminar phase that helps the cohort towards the canonical answer during generation is not a gentler Productive Failure; it is a worse one. The impasse literature says the same from the other side — a tutor's explanation only helps a learner who is already stuck.

*The consolidation phase must prompt comparison explicitly.* Loibl and Leuders (2019) showed that presenting correct and incorrect solutions side by side achieves nothing on its own; only the condition that was *told to compare them* learned more. Gick and Holyoak's radiation results say the same in a different register: stating the principle on top of a single case moved the needle not at all. A consolidation phase that presents the canonical answer, however clearly, and does not force the cohort to compare it against their own attempts, is throwing the mechanism away.

*The norms are part of the design.* The programme already treats psychological safety as substrate — see [Edmondson](/research/04-professional-formation/edmondson-psychological-safety.md) and [Frazier](/research/04-professional-formation/frazier-psychological-safety-meta-analysis.md). Kapur makes the dependency explicit and directional: the effectiveness of Productive Failure is greater where norms align with it, and the growth-mindset evidence he cites worked only in schools whose norms supported challenge. Re-norming is not preamble to the design; it is a layer of it.

**One boundary is a live question for this programme and cannot be settled from the sources held.** Kapur's stated boundary is about *learning goals*: procedural and protocol-following goals do not need Productive Failure unless the point is to understand why the procedure is as it is. Much of what a forward-deployed engineer must learn is exactly of the second kind — the *why* under a procedure — so the boundary as he states it excludes rather little of this curriculum. But the meta-analysis abstract reports a reversal for **domain-general skills** that the book does not repeat, and this programme teaches a great deal that could be described that way: interviewing, scoping, negotiation, written argument. The honest position is that the two sources disagree in emphasis, that only one of them has been read, and that the design should not claim either as a warrant for what it does with domain-general content until the article is read.

**What the book does not license.** It supplies no evidence for adult professional learners. Kapur says the cycle works in corporate training and adult education and cites nothing for it. Every study in the book is a school, university or laboratory study. Applying this design to a multi-week professional engagement is an extrapolation, and should be recorded as one wherever the programme relies on it — including in [/wiki/program/09-Durable-and-Perishable-Register.md](/wiki/program/09-Durable-and-Perishable-Register.md).

## How to teach it

- **Lecture point:** Present the ordering as a design choice with a stated mechanism, not as a philosophy. Kapur's own framing — turning the Direct Instruction model on its head after questioning the assumption underneath it — is a better teaching object than the effect sizes, and it can be taught without asserting any.
- **Lecture point:** Teach the **assembly** step as the hard part. Most people hear "problems first" and stop. Use the physics example: a student who says there is a force in the direction of motion is not wrong about everything, and the teachable move is to name what they were right about before correcting what they were not.
- **Lecture point:** Teach "explain your idea, then hack your idea" as a portable habit. Hacking — deliberately hunting for the case in which your own solution breaks — is the same discipline the programme asks for in falsifiable predictions and in the objection sections of its whitepapers.
- **Lecture point:** State Kapur's concession that failure is sufficient but not necessary. Teaching a mechanism alongside its author's own hedge is the modelling of professional honesty this programme is trying to produce.
- **Lab exercise:** Run a genuine cycle. Give teams an ill-structured problem — "make this retrieval system's answers trustworthy", no method specified — require multiple candidate solutions, allow them to fail, and **do not help them towards the answer**. Then teach the canonical approach with explicit, prompted contrast against what they produced.
- **Lab exercise:** Have students design a Productive Failure task against Kapur's seven features and then run his own redesign test on it: if most learners cannot generate more than one solution, the task is wrong and must be rebuilt.
- **Studio exercise:** Keep the failed candidate solutions and revisit them during consolidation. The comparison artefact is the pedagogical object; discarding it discards the mechanism. Loibl and Leuders makes this a warranted instruction rather than an intuition.
- **Assessment idea:** Assess on **far transfer**. Kapur's own transfer items are structurally unlike the taught problems, and transfer is where he reports the effect is strongest. See [/wiki/program/03-Assessment-Architecture.md](/wiki/program/03-Assessment-Architecture.md).
- **Assessment idea:** An EPA for "attempting an unfamiliar problem before seeking the standard method", evidenced by documented candidate approaches and a written comparison against the canonical solution once learned.
- **Assessment caution:** Do not assess the generation phase on correctness. Kapur's design instruction is to "invent as many solutions as possible without worrying about getting them right or wrong", and the norm that makes that instruction credible has to be visible in how the work is graded.

## Limits and cautions

- **The wiki forbids asserting an effect size for a pedagogy claim, and reading the book does not change that.** This is a namespace rule, not a verification rule: it follows from the warrant standard for the `pedagogy` namespace in [/concepts/namespaces.md](/concepts/namespaces.md), and it applies to a magnitude read verbatim from a full text exactly as it applies to one taken from an abstract. The book's benchmarks — "up to three times that of learning from a good teacher for one year", "up to two academic years ahead" — are recorded above and **may not be asserted in `wiki/`**. Direction and mechanism only. A deeper read buys a better description of the mechanism; it does not buy the right to a number.
- **The two sources disagree about domain generality, and the disagreement is unresolved.** The book says the design principles "remain domain general... they can be used to design Productive Failure tasks in any domain" and lists language, writing, accounting, business and creative fields among successful applications. The meta-analysis abstract reports a reversal favouring instruction-first for the learning of domain-general skills. These are not strictly the same claim — one is about the portability of the design, the other about a class of learning outcome — but the previous note collapsed them, and neither source has been read at a depth that would let the collapse be undone. See `## Verification status`.
- **The book never repeats the young-learner reversal.** It is in the abstract of the meta-analysis Kapur co-authored and it is absent from the trade book he wrote four years later for teachers. Silence is not contradiction, and it is not evidence of retraction. It is a gap, and it should be recorded as one rather than resolved in either direction.
- **The book's evidence is school, university and laboratory evidence.** The claim that the design works in corporate training and adult education is asserted in the author's voice and supported by no citation in the reference list.
- **The book is a trade book.** Anecdote and evidence sit in adjacent paragraphs and the transition is not always marked. Nothing in the anecdotal material should be cited as evidence, including the Michael Jordan, Jack Ma and Steven Spielberg examples, which Kapur himself hedges.
- **Findings reported in the book are reported at the book's grain.** Sample sizes and directions are given; intervals, heterogeneity and pre-registration status generally are not. Any study finding relayed here is relayed at second hand from Kapur's summary.
- **Poorly implemented Productive Failure has a name and a place in his own model.** Unproductive Failure — low performance *and* low learning — is what he says pure discovery learning produces. This is no longer a caution derived from the fidelity moderator; it is the author's stated quadrant.
- **The approach is time-intensive relative to direct instruction.** The book does not address opportunity cost, and its design guidance makes the cost worse rather than better: two to three task iterations, plus norm-setting sustained across the whole implementation.
- **Deliberate difficulty is not automatically desirable.** [Macnamara](/research/04-professional-formation/macnamara-deliberate-practice-limits.md) is the counterweight in this folder to any reading that treats effortful practice as sufficient. Kapur's own difficulty calibration — "the challenge should not be so hard that you completely give up" — is stated without any operational test for where that point is.
- **The 2008 article and the 2021 article remain unread.** Nothing in this note verifies either. Where the two abstracts and the book diverge, no source held here can adjudicate.

### Where the book contradicts or fails to support the previous synthesis-only note

**1. The domain-general prohibition. This is the finding.** The previous note stated, under `## Why it matters for the course`:

> "The boundary conditions are equally important and should be stated to students rather than hidden. Domain-general skills favour instruction first. That means the course should *not* use productive failure for teaching general professional skills such as structured communication or interview technique, while it *should* use it for domain-specific technical content where students have enough prior knowledge to generate differentiated attempts."

**The book contradicts the operational half of this.** Kapur states that the design's features "are not tied to mathematics or any other domain. They remain domain general, meaning that they can be used to design Productive Failure tasks in any domain", and reports applying the cycle in "mathematics, science, engineering, psychology, language, writing, accounting, business, and even in artistic and creative fields. There is nothing in the design that is specific to a particular domain." Writing and business communication are named applications, not exclusions.

The previous note's error was an inference, not a misquotation. The abstract's moderator concerns *domain-general skills as a learning outcome*; the note converted it into a prohibition on *domain-general subject areas*, and then issued teaching guidance on the strength of the conversion. The conversion is not supported by either source and is in tension with the author's own stated scope. **The guidance is withdrawn.** What can honestly be said is that the two sources emphasise different things, that the meta-analysis has not been read past its abstract, and that the question is open.

**2. The young-learner reversal is absent from the book.** The previous note called it "the most important caution and... frequently omitted in secondary citation", and listed it three times. Kapur does not mention it. He cites Mazziotti et al. (2019) — whose title is *Probing boundary conditions of productive failure and analyzing the role of young students' collaboration* — for its solo-versus-group result only, and says nothing about age anywhere in the book. Recorded as a gap, not as a retraction.

**3. The previous note omitted the boundary Kapur actually states.** No version of "procedural, protocol-following and rote learning goals do not need Productive Failure" appears in the previous note, and it is the boundary he gives first, in the design chapter, under "Identify Learning Goals". Nor did the previous note carry his second boundary — "Productive Failure fails when it is badly designed or implemented" — which is his own answer to the question he set himself in a keynote titled *When Productive Failure Fails*.

**4. The previous note's proposed mechanism is partly unsupported.** It said, marked as interpretive: "productive failure appears to require enough prior knowledge to generate meaningful, differentiated attempts at a solution, and enough domain specificity for those attempts to expose structure." The first clause is broadly consistent with the book's task-accessibility feature, though the book locates the requirement in the *task's* design rather than in the *learner's* stock of knowledge. The second clause is contradicted by the domain-generality claim above. The interpretive label was correct and the interpretation was half wrong.

**5. The previous note's framing of who benefits is inverted by the book.** By implying that Productive Failure needs learners with sufficient prior knowledge, the previous note leaned towards the "suits stronger learners" reading. Kapur calls that criticism "unfounded" in the first line of Chapter 7 and reports Belenky and Nokes-Malach (2012) to the opposite effect: the design made the largest difference precisely for students not naturally inclined towards mastery. **The condition attached to that answer — norm alignment — must be carried with it and was also absent from the previous note.**

**6. The previous note treated Kapur (2008) as "the founding study". The book does not cite it at all.** It appears nowhere in the book's chapter-by-chapter reference list. The book's own origin narrative in Chapter 2 is a high-school **mathematics** study of **standard deviation** using the basketball problem, not the eleventh-grade Newtonian-kinematics study the 2008 abstract describes. These may simply be different studies from the same period — Kapur says "several such studies, for a range of topics (e.g. ratios, statistics, and many others), in a number of schools" — but the previous note's designation of the 2008 paper as *the* founding study is not corroborated by the author's own account and is downgraded here.

**7. The previous note's two headline numbers do not appear in the book.** Neither *g* = 0.36 nor *g* = 0.87 is stated anywhere. Kapur reports the same meta-analysis in benchmark terms instead. Not a contradiction; a divergence in what the author chooses to foreground, and worth recording because a reader who knows only the book and a reader who knows only the abstract will quote different magnitudes for the same study.

**8. Two of the previous note's interpretive claims are now warranted.** It said, marked interpretive, that "the course must invest in designing the generation phase properly... and a consolidation phase that explicitly contrasts student attempts against the canonical method. That consolidation step is what converts failure into learning and is the part most often dropped." The book supplies the warrant — Loibl and Leuders (2019) — and strengthens the claim: providing both solution sets is *insufficient* without an explicit prompt to compare them. It also said "Poorly implemented productive failure... may be worse than direct instruction. The fidelity finding implies this but does not test the failure mode directly." The book names the quadrant (Unproductive Failure) and reports a related manipulation (Kapur 2011, guided versus unguided) that does test a form of it.
