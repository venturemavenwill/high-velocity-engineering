# Cliff Notes — Keith & Frese, Effectiveness of Error Management Training

## Citation and access
- **Authors:** Nina Keith, Michael Frese
- **Title:** Effectiveness of error management training: A meta-analysis
- **Venue and year:** *Journal of Applied Psychology*, 93(1), 59–69, January 2008
- **DOI:** 10.1037/0021-9010.93.1.59
- **Source consulted:** https://psycnet.apa.org/doi/10.1037/0021-9010.93.1.59 (official APA abstract record 2008-00266-004)
- **Access status:** Paywalled — synthesis from the official abstract. Full text not read for this note.

## What this source is
A meta-analysis of 24 studies testing whether training that *encourages* learners to make errors outperforms training that tries to prevent them. The answer is yes, with a moderate overall effect, and — critically — the advantage grows as the transfer demand grows. The paper is the strongest single piece of evidence available for the claim that a curriculum should deliberately expose students to failure rather than smooth the path, and it is precise about the conditions under which that holds.

## The argument in full

The training literature had long contained two opposed design philosophies. Proceduralised, error-avoidant training gives learners step-by-step guidance designed to minimise the number of mistakes made during acquisition, on the reasoning that errors waste time, produce frustration, and risk being encoded as incorrect habits. Error management training takes the opposite view: it involves active exploration, and it adds explicit encouragement for learners to make errors during training and to learn from them. Errors, on this account, are informative events. They tell the learner where the boundaries of a procedure lie, they force diagnostic reasoning that correct execution never triggers, and they build a repertoire of recovery behaviour that error-free training cannot produce.

Primary studies comparing these approaches had produced results that varied considerably in magnitude, and some had produced null or negative findings. That variation is the problem the meta-analysis exists to resolve. Keith and Frese pool 24 studies with a combined sample of 2,183 participants and estimate both the overall effect and the moderators that explain the spread.

The overall result is a positive and statistically significant effect favouring error management training, with a Cohen's *d* of 0.44. By the conventions of the training literature this is a moderate effect and a practically meaningful one — comparable to or better than many well-regarded instructional interventions, obtained from a design change rather than from additional instructional time.

The moderator analysis is where the paper becomes genuinely useful, because the moderators are not incidental. The effect was larger for **post-training transfer performance** (*d* = 0.56) than for performance measured *within* training. This is the pattern the theory predicts and the pattern that matters practically. During training itself, error-avoidant instruction can look better, because learners following a procedure make fewer mistakes and appear more competent. The advantage of error management appears only once the scaffold is removed. Any evaluation that measures learning at the end of the training session rather than later will therefore systematically underrate error management training — and most course evaluations do exactly that.

The second moderator is stronger still and is the paper's most quotable finding. The effect was larger for **adaptive transfer** — application to tasks that are structurally *different* from those trained — with *d* = 0.80, than for analogical transfer to tasks similar to those trained. An effect of 0.80 is large. What this says is that error management training's advantage scales with the novelty of the situation the learner eventually faces. If graduates will only ever encounter problems structurally identical to the ones they practised, error-avoidant training is nearly as good. If they will encounter genuinely new problems, error management training is substantially better.

Finally, Keith and Frese decompose the intervention. Error management training as usually implemented bundles two things: active exploration, in which learners work without step-by-step guidance, and explicit error encouragement, in which they are told that errors are expected and useful. A natural sceptical hypothesis is that all the benefit comes from active exploration and the error-encouragement framing is decorative. The meta-analysis rejects this: both active exploration and error encouragement were identified as independently effective elements. The framing does work of its own. Telling learners that errors are part of the process is not merely permission to explore; it changes what they do with the errors they encounter.

The authors' conclusion is stated as a conditional rather than a blanket recommendation: error management training is better suited than error-avoidant training for transfer to *novel* tasks. That conditional is the honest form of the claim and should be preserved when citing it.

## Structure of the original
- Introduction: the error-avoidant versus error-encouraging debate in training design
- Theoretical accounts of why errors might aid learning: exploration, metacognition, emotion control
- Definition of error management training and its comparators
- Hypotheses regarding overall effect and moderators
- Method: literature search, inclusion criteria, coding of moderators, meta-analytic procedure
- Overall effect size
- Moderator analyses: within-training versus post-training transfer; analogical versus adaptive transfer
- Component analysis: active exploration and error encouragement as separable elements
- Discussion: when to use error management training, and boundary conditions

## Key claims, findings, and specifics
- **24 studies, total N = 2,183.**
- Overall mean effect of error management training: **Cohen's d = 0.44**, positive and statistically significant.
- **Post-training transfer: d = 0.56**, larger than the effect on within-training performance.
- **Adaptive transfer to structurally different tasks: d = 0.80**, larger than analogical transfer to similar tasks.
- **Both active exploration and error encouragement were independently identified as effective elements** of error management training.
- Comparators in the primary studies included proceduralised error-avoidant training and exploratory training *without* error encouragement — the latter is what isolates the error-encouragement component.
- Definition used: error management training "involves active exploration as well as explicit encouragement for learners to make errors during training and to learn from them."
- Prior primary studies had shown considerable variation in effect sizes; the moderator analysis accounts for that variation.
- Stated conclusion: error management training is better suited than error-avoidant training for transfer to novel tasks.

## Method and evidence base
A conventional meta-analysis of controlled training studies, with 24 studies and 2,183 participants. The comparison structure is unusually clean for this literature: because some primary studies contrasted error management training against *exploratory training without error encouragement*, rather than only against proceduralised instruction, the design permits separation of the exploration component from the error-encouragement component. That is what licenses the claim that both elements contribute independently, and it is a stronger inference than most training meta-analyses can support. The main limits are sample size — 24 studies is modest for moderator analysis, so the moderator estimates are less precise than the overall effect — and the fact that the underlying studies are predominantly laboratory or short-course training on well-bounded tasks such as software use, which is not the same as multi-year professional formation. Publication bias is a standing concern and the abstract does not state what correction, if any, was applied.

## Vocabulary the source introduces
- **Error management training (EMT)** — training that combines active exploration with explicit encouragement to make and learn from errors.
- **Error-avoidant training** — proceduralised, step-by-step instruction designed to minimise errors during acquisition.
- **Active exploration** — learning by working through a task without step-by-step guidance; one of the two components of EMT.
- **Error encouragement** — explicit framing of errors as expected and informative; the second, independently effective component of EMT.
- **Analogical transfer** — application of training to tasks structurally similar to those trained.
- **Adaptive transfer** — application of training to tasks structurally different from those trained; the condition under which EMT's advantage is largest.
- **Within-training performance** — performance measured during acquisition, where error-avoidant training can appear superior.
- **Post-training transfer** — performance measured after training ends and scaffolding is removed.

## What to remember
- Overall effect is moderate: *d* = 0.44 across 24 studies and 2,183 participants.
- The advantage is larger after training (*d* = 0.56) than during it. Error-avoidant training flatters itself on in-session measures.
- The advantage is largest for structurally novel tasks (*d* = 0.80). The more unfamiliar the future problem, the more error management training pays.
- Both components work independently. Removing the explicit "errors are expected and useful" framing removes real benefit, not just atmosphere.
- The conclusion is conditional, not universal: better *for transfer to novel tasks*. It is not a claim that errors always help.
- This is the evidence-based version of "learn from failure," and it is considerably more specific and better supported than the generic version.

## Why it matters for the course
This is the paper that converts a slogan into a design specification. A forward-deployed engineering curriculum exists to prepare people for problems that will not resemble the ones they practised — new customers, new domains, unfamiliar stacks, models that behave differently from the ones in the lab. Keith and Frese show that the training design which best serves exactly that condition is the one that deliberately induces errors and explicitly frames them as informative, and they attach a large effect size to it. The *d* = 0.80 adaptive-transfer figure is the single most relevant number in this folder for justifying a curriculum built around hard, under-scaffolded problems rather than guided walkthroughs.

The within-training versus post-training moderator carries a warning that any course designer needs to internalise. If the course evaluates itself on how well students perform during the lab session, it will conclude that error-avoidant instruction is working better, and it will drift toward more scaffolding term after term. Evaluating on delayed, structurally novel transfer tasks is not a refinement — it is a precondition for the course being able to see its own effectiveness. This aligns naturally with the Entrustable Professional Activity model, where entrustment is granted on the basis of observed performance in real, varied situations rather than on end-of-module scores.

The component finding also constrains how the course talks about failure. It is not enough to give students hard problems and leave them to struggle; the explicit framing that errors are expected and are the point does independent work. That framing is cheap to deliver and, on this evidence, should not be dropped.

## How to teach it
- **Lecture point:** Present the three effect sizes in order — 0.44 overall, 0.56 post-training, 0.80 adaptive transfer — and let the escalation make the argument.
- **Lecture point:** Teach the within-training illusion explicitly. Show why a well-scaffolded lab produces better-looking sessions and worse-prepared engineers.
- **Lecture point:** Distinguish this from generic "fail fast" or growth-mindset rhetoric. Keith and Frese specify components, conditions and magnitudes; the slogans do not.
- **Lab exercise:** Run paired labs on the same content, one guided and one exploratory with explicit error encouragement, then assess both cohorts weeks later on a structurally different problem. Students experience the moderator directly.
- **Lab exercise:** Build deliberately error-provoking exercises — a retrieval pipeline with a subtly contaminated eval set, an agent harness with a silent timeout — and require students to discover, diagnose and document the failure.
- **Studio exercise:** Institute an error-encouragement norm with a concrete ritual, such as opening each session by naming what broke since last time, so the framing component is actually delivered and not just implied.
- **Assessment idea:** Assess error recovery as a distinct competence: given a broken system, how quickly and accurately does the student localise the fault? This measures what error management training builds and what error-avoidant training does not.
- **Assessment idea:** Make adaptive transfer the assessment default. Final assessments should be structurally unlike anything practised, and this should be stated as policy with this paper as the justification.

## Limits and cautions
- Twenty-four studies is a modest base for moderator analysis. The 0.56 and 0.80 estimates are less precisely estimated than the 0.44 overall effect and should be treated as indicative of direction and rough magnitude.
- Primary studies are largely short training interventions on bounded tasks, frequently software skills, in laboratory or short-course settings. Multi-year professional formation is an extrapolation.
- The abstract does not report publication-bias correction, heterogeneity statistics, or confidence intervals. None are asserted here.
- Errors that are costly, unsafe or irreversible are outside this evidence base. Everything here presumes a training context where mistakes are recoverable.
- Error management training may impose higher cognitive load and may interact with prior knowledge. Learners with very low domain knowledge may lack the base needed to interpret their own errors productively — this is a well-known concern in the adjacent guided-instruction literature and is not resolved by this paper.
- The finding is about *training design*, not about tolerating errors in production. Do not import it as an argument for lax engineering practice.
- Evidence is from 2008 and predates AI-assisted work entirely. Whether error encouragement retains its effect when a model can supply a correct answer on request is an open and important question for this course.
