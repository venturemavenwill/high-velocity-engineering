# Cliff Notes — Keith & Frese, Effectiveness of Error Management Training

## What this source is
A meta-analysis of 24 studies asking whether training that *encourages* learners to make errors outperforms training that tries to prevent them. The headline answer is yes, at a moderate overall magnitude. But the paper is not really about the headline. It states four moderator hypotheses in advance, supports three of them and finds only limited support for the fourth, and the useful content is the resulting map of **when this works and when it does not**.

Two structural facts govern how far anything here can be carried. First, every one of the 24 studies compared error management training against *another training method*; none used a no-training control. All effects are therefore **relative**, not absolute — "better than a tutorial", never "better than nothing". Second, and stated plainly by the authors, **no study in the pool measured on-the-job performance.** Every outcome is a post-training performance task in a laboratory. The transfer this paper establishes is transfer to a novel *task*, not transfer to a job.

## The argument in full

The training literature contained two opposed design philosophies, and the paper opens by naming the opposition rather than assuming it. Skinner (1953) "equated errors with punishment that can inhibit behavior but that does not contribute to learning", and Bandura (1986) "viewed errors as detrimental to learning and promoted a guided and error-free learning environment", writing that "without informative guidance, much of one's efforts would be expended on costly errors and needless toil" (p. 47) and that learners should be "spared the costs and pain of faulty effort" (p. 47) [[page 1]], [[page 2]]. Proceduralised training operationalises that view: detailed step-by-step instructions on correct task solutions, given to prevent participants from making errors.

Error management training takes the opposite view, and the definition is exact: it "involves active exploration as well as explicit encouragement for learners to make errors during training and to learn from them" [[page 1]]. Two elements distinguish it. **Minimal guidance** — participants receive only basic information and then work independently on difficult tasks, "a procedure that almost inevitably leads to many errors". And **error management instructions** — brief instructions that tell participants to expect errors and emphasise their positive informational function, summarised in statements the paper quotes verbatim: *"The more errors you make, the more you learn!"* and *"You have made an error? Great! Because now you can learn something new!"* [[page 2]]. One operational detail matters more than it looks: during the session the trainer repeats these statements and reminds participants to reflect on errors, "but provides no further assistance when an error occurs" [[page 2]]. The framing is delivered; the help is not.

The theoretical basis is action theory, in which action-oriented mental models underlie work performance and errors are feedback that "indicate where one's mental model is not adequately developed and thereby encourage its correction" [[page 2]]. The paper is careful that this goes beyond treating errors as negative feedback: "learners are encouraged to use errors as a basis to think ahead and to try out something new" [[page 2]]. The proposed mechanisms, from the authors' own earlier work, are **emotion control** and **metacognition** — errors "prompt learners to stop and think about the causes of the error" [[page 3]].

Primary studies had not agreed. Early work reported "positive and large effect sizes (Cohen's *d* of about 1)"; later replications were lower; and two studies reversed the sign outright — an electronic-search study in which the proceduralised group won "by three quarters of a standard deviation", and a decision-making study in which a group *instructed to avoid errors* beat the error management group at medium effect [[page 1]]. Resolving that spread is the stated purpose.

**Hypothesis 1** distinguishes performance measured during training from performance on a separate post-training transfer task, and predicts an effect on the second and *not* the first. The reasoning is that manipulations which boost immediate performance can be useless for transfer and vice versa — the paper cites Shea and Morgan's blocked-versus-random practice reversal as the canonical case [[page 3]]. Note the hedge in the authors' own statement of the mechanism: in error management training "immediate performance within the training phase **may not differ or may even be depressed**" [[page 3]]. The hypothesis was supported. Studies using a within-training criterion yielded a **non-significant** mean effect of *d* = −0.15 (k = 4, n = 505, p = .45); studies using a post-training transfer criterion yielded *d* = 0.56 (k = 20, n = 1,678, p < .01); the moderator itself was significant at p < .01 [[page 7]], [[page 8]]. The Discussion states the conclusion at full strength: "EMT appeared to be effective only when posttraining performance and not within-training performance was considered" [[page 8]].

**Hypothesis 2** distinguishes analogical transfer, where transfer-task solutions are familiar or analogous to the trained ones, from adaptive transfer, defined as "using one's existing knowledge base to change a learned procedure, or to generate a solution to a completely new problem" [[page 3]], [[page 4]]. The critical dimension is stated to be *task distinctiveness rather than task difficulty* — the same task under greater time pressure is harder but still analogical [[page 5]]. Supported, and this is the largest contrast in the paper: analogical *d* = 0.20 (k = 13, n = 1,647, p < .05, one-tailed), adaptive *d* = 0.80 (k = 11, n = 536, p < .01) [[page 7]]. Both are positive and both are significant; the analogical effect is small, not absent.

**Hypothesis 3 is the moderator the previous synthesis of this source missed entirely, and it is the most operationally consequential.** Because error management training withholds guidance, "the task itself needs to provide clearly interpretable and informative feedback if EMT is to be effective" [[page 4]]. On tasks whose feedback cannot be readily interpreted — the paper's own examples are a statistics package producing complex output a novice cannot read, and a database search returning records whose relevance the searcher cannot judge — the method may be *worse* than instruction that supplies external guidance. The result: clear-feedback studies *d* = 0.56 (k = 17, n = 1,178, p < .01); unclear-feedback studies *d* = 0.19 (k = 7, n = 1,005, **non-significant**, p = .28). But the moderator test itself reached only p = .08. The authors' verdict is explicitly partial: "This hypothesis received only limited support", and later, "results were mixed, and it cannot be concluded that EMT was effective only when task-generated feedback was clear" [[page 8]]. They attribute the inconclusiveness partly to feedback clarity being useful for the *comparison* methods too, and partly to the low interrater reliability of their own coding of it.

**Hypothesis 4** decomposes the intervention. Comparisons against proceduralised training confound exploration with error encouragement; comparisons against *exploratory training without error instructions* isolate the encouragement component. Both subsamples were significant: against proceduralised training *d* = 0.58 (k = 16, n = 1,257, p < .01), against exploratory training *d* = 0.19 (k = 13, n = 1,158, p < .05, one-tailed, with a 90% confidence interval running from 0.01) [[page 7]]. The authors' own phrasing is markedly weaker than a decomposition: "This finding **can be interpreted to the effect that** both elements of EMT … are effective", followed immediately by "It would be desirable to conduct more studies that included both proceduralized and purely exploratory training in one experimental design to **further examine the feasibility of this interpretation**" [[page 8]]. The inference is across studies, not within a design, and the authors flag it as provisional.

One statistical point governs everything above and is easy to miss. **The homogeneity test was not significant.** Q(23) = 26.46: "from a purely statistical perspective, effect size variability was not any greater than would be expected from sampling error" [[page 8]]. The authors proceed to moderator analysis anyway, on the explicit grounds that the homogeneity test "suffers from low power … and can often lead to the false conclusion that no moderators exist", citing methodological support for testing a priori hypotheses regardless. This is defensible and openly declared, but it means the moderators were not extracted from demonstrated excess heterogeneity. They were theory-driven partitions of a pool that did not statistically require partitioning.

The authors' conclusion is a conditional and is hedged in the abstract itself: "Results suggest that EMT **may be** better suited than error-avoidant training methods for promotion of transfer to novel tasks" [[page 1]]. Two boundary statements accompany it. When the goal is one particular procedure, direct instruction "may also be effective while being **less time consuming and less effortful** than EMT" [[page 8]] — a cost argument, not only an efficacy one. And where making errors oneself is impractical or "too threatening and stressful for participants", the authors point to *vicarious* error management training, in which learners are shown and discuss other people's errors; their example is firefighter training. That variant was **excluded from this meta-analysis** because it does not meet the operational definition, and whether it is as effective as the active form is offered as an open question [[page 9]].

- **The within-training point estimate is negative.** *d* = −0.15 does not establish that error-avoidant training wins during training; the interval spans zero. What it establishes is that the error management advantage **is not present during training at all**.
- **The adaptive-transfer subsample is the smallest in the table.** *d* = 0.80 rests on 11 studies and 536 participants; the analogical estimate of 0.20 rests on three times as many participants. The largest number in the paper is also its least precisely estimated.
- **The two comparison-condition rows do not sum to 24 studies or 2,183 participants.** Five studies used both a proceduralised and an exploratory comparison and are represented twice; the overall test used a dummy over the non-overlapping k = 11 and k = 8 [[page 7]].
- **Three of the 24 study-level effect sizes are negative and one is exactly zero** — Debowski et al. (2001) −0.73, Gully et al. (2002) −0.44, Ivancic (1998) Study 3 −0.24, van Dyck (2007a) 0.00 [[page 6]]. The method is not uniformly beneficial in the primary literature.
- **The largest effects are the oldest.** Table 1's top values — Thiemann (1990) 1.44, Chillarege et al. (2003) 1.35, Greif & Janikowski (1987) 1.16, Dormann & Frese (1994) 1.08, Heinbokel (1990) 1.06, Frese et al. (1991) 0.99 — sit mostly in the earliest and largely unpublished German-language work, while several later studies report 0.09, 0.11, 0.21 and 0.22. The authors acknowledge the pattern in the introduction — early studies at "*d* of about 1", later replications "with slightly lower effect sizes" [[page 1]] — but **publication year is not among the coded moderators and is not tested.**
- **No study used a no-training control**, so nothing here speaks to absolute effectiveness [[page 5]].
- **No study measured on-the-job performance** [[page 9]].
- Definition used for inclusion: active exploration in which participants "were not guided to correct task solutions but worked independently", **and** explicit error encouragement via instructions "that stressed the positive function of errors for learning" [[page 5]].
- Stated conclusion, with its hedge intact: EMT "**may be** better suited than error-avoidant training methods for promotion of transfer to novel tasks" [[page 1]].

## Method and evidence base

**Search.** PsycINFO, Social Sciences Citation Index and the German database Psyndex, supplemented by manual searches of conference programmes and reference lists, plus direct contact with published authors and other researchers in the field. Initial search spring 2004, updated autumn 2006 [[page 5]].

**Inclusion and exclusion.** Two criteria, both required: active exploration without guidance to correct solutions, and explicit error management instructions. The search yielded 24 studies, N = 2,183. **Three further studies that explicitly invoked error management theory were excluded** for failing the operationalisation — participants were shown researcher-selected errors rather than making their own, or received no error management instructions. Those three are Ivancic and Hesketh (2000), Joung et al. (2006) and Lorenzet et al. (2005) [[page 5]]. This is a defensible homogeneity decision and it also means the pool systematically excludes the *vicarious* variant the Discussion later recommends for high-stakes settings.

**Composition of the pool.** Eleven of 24 studies were unpublished — master's theses, dissertations, technical reports or manuscripts in preparation. **All but one were experimental laboratory studies** with random assignment of individuals or small groups. Eighteen taught new software, three taught electronic database search, three used a computerised decision-making task; the Discussion summarises this as "almost all (21 out of 24) studies applied EMT in teaching software skills" [[page 5]], [[page 9]]. Six studies used employees recruited in the community; the remainder used university students. Two studies analysed dyads rather than individuals.

**Aggregation rules.** One effect size per study. Where a study had several comparison conditions or several outcomes, the mean was taken; where outcomes differed in difficulty rather than kind, **only the most difficult task was included**, on theoretical grounds [[page 5]]. That last rule plausibly favours the error management condition and is not sensitivity-tested.

**Coding and reliability.** Four moderators, double-coded on 23 of 24 studies. Cohen's kappa ranged from **.65 for clarity of task feedback** — the weakest, and the one whose moderator test failed — through .83 for type of evaluation outcome and .91 for type of transfer task, to 1.00 for type of comparison training. Disagreements resolved by discussion [[page 5]].

**Analysis.** Hedges and Olkin (1985) procedures; Hedges (1981) small-sample correction; Hedges (1982) ANOVA analogue for dichotomous moderators; random or mixed effects models throughout; Q statistics partitioning explained and residual variance [[page 7]].

**Publication bias.** No funnel plot, trim-and-fill, fail-safe N or any other statistical correction is reported anywhere in the paper. The mitigation is procedural rather than statistical: the search deliberately pursued grey literature and contacted researchers directly, and 11 of the 24 studies are unpublished. That is a real and unusually strong design response, but it is not a bias estimate, and none is offered.

**Causal warrant.** The authors' own strength claim: all but one study "employed a controlled experimental design with randomized assignment to training conditions", which "renders justification for causal inferences" that outcome differences are due to training method rather than pre-training differences [[page 9]]. This is correct and is the strongest thing about the paper.

**Power.** The authors concede the pool is small relative to broader meta-analyses and that small pools carry low power, then add: "It should be noted, however, that we found several significant moderator effects despite this low power — a result that underscores the relevance of the identified moderators" [[page 9]].

## Vocabulary the source introduces
- **Error management training (EMT)** — training combining active exploration with explicit encouragement to make and learn from errors. Both elements are required by the paper's inclusion criteria; a study with only one is not EMT.
- **Error management instructions** — the brief spoken framing that delivers the second element, repeated by the trainer during the session, and accompanied by **no assistance** when an error occurs.
- **Proceduralised training** — step-by-step instructions or close personal guidance to correct solutions during practice.
- **Exploratory training** — practice without guidance but *without* error encouragement. This is the comparator that isolates the framing component.
- **Active exploration** — working independently to find solutions rather than being guided to them.
- **Analogical transfer** — transfer tasks whose solutions are familiar or analogous to the trained ones.
- **Adaptive transfer** — "using one's existing knowledge base to change a learned procedure, or to generate a solution to a completely new problem". Distinguished by **structural distinctiveness, not difficulty**.
- **Within-training performance** — performance scored during a training trial, while errors are still being encouraged.
- **Post-training transfer performance** — performance on a separate later task where participants know they are being evaluated and errors are no longer encouraged.
- **Task-generated feedback, clear versus unclear** — whether the task itself lets a learner detect that an action was wrong, without external guidance or background knowledge. The paper's boundary condition.
- **Relative versus absolute effectiveness** — the paper's own distinction; everything reported is relative to an alternative training method.
- **Vicarious EMT** — a variant in which learners examine and discuss others' errors rather than making their own. Named, recommended for settings where error-making is impractical or threatening, and **excluded from the meta-analysis**.
- **Action theory / action-oriented mental model** — the theoretical frame, in which errors function as feedback marking where a mental model is inadequate.

## What to remember
- The advantage is **absent during training and present afterwards**. The within-training estimate is negative and non-significant; the authors say EMT was effective "only when posttraining performance… was considered".
- The advantage is **largest for structurally novel tasks** and small — though still positive and significant — for tasks like the ones practised.
- **It requires the task to answer back.** If a learner cannot tell from the task itself that they have gone wrong, the effect is not significant. This is the boundary condition, and it is stated as only partially supported.
- **Both components appear to contribute, but the authors call that an interpretation** needing further study, and the incremental effect of the framing over bare exploration is the weakest result in the paper.
- **Everything is relative to another training method.** No no-training control exists in the pool.
- **Nothing here is a job-performance finding.** No included study measured on-the-job outcomes; the authors call it a research gap and label their own reasoning about it speculation.
- The evidence base is **laboratory software training**, almost entirely, mostly with students.

## Why it matters for the course
This is the paper that converts a slogan into a design specification, but the specification has more clauses than the slogan suggests, and three of them constrain this programme directly.

**The transfer argument still holds and is the reason to build the course this way.** A forward-deployed engineering curriculum exists to prepare people for problems that will not resemble the ones they practised — new customers, new domains, unfamiliar stacks, models that behave differently from those in the lab. That is adaptive transfer in the paper's sense, and it is the condition under which error management training is at its strongest. The design commitment to hard, under-scaffolded problems is supported by this source in direction and mechanism.

**The within-training moderator is a warning about self-evaluation, and the full text makes it sharper than the abstract does.** If the course evaluates itself on how students perform inside the lab session, it will see nothing — not a smaller effect, but no effect. It will then conclude that the exploratory design is not working and drift toward scaffolding term after term. Evaluating on delayed, structurally novel transfer tasks is not a refinement but a precondition for the design being able to see itself at all. This aligns with the Entrustable Professional Activity model in [research/08-assessment-epas/ten-cate-epa-framework.md](/research/08-assessment-epas/ten-cate-epa-framework.md), where entrustment rests on observed performance in real, varied situations rather than end-of-module scores.

**The feedback-clarity condition is the finding this programme most needed and previously did not have.** Error management training assumes the task tells you when you are wrong. Much of what this course teaches does the opposite. A retrieval pipeline returns plausible passages whether or not the embedding model matches the index. An agent completes a run and reports success while having silently truncated its context. An evaluator emits a score with no published agreement against human judgement. These are precisely the paper's *unclear feedback* tasks, and on those the measured effect is not significant. The design implication is not to abandon exploration but to recognise that **an exercise on a silent-failure system needs an instrumented feedback path built for it** — a ground-truth set, an assertion, a trace, a diff against a known-good run — or the error management rationale does not apply to that exercise. That is a design obligation this source creates and does not discharge.

**The framing component should still be delivered, and should be described honestly.** Telling learners errors are expected is cheap, and the evidence weakly favours it doing independent work. It should not be presented as an established decomposition, because the authors do not present it that way.

**Two things this source cannot be used for.** It cannot support a claim that error management training improves performance on the job; no study in it measured that, and the authors say so. And it cannot support a claim about a multi-year professional formation programme; the pool is short laboratory software training with student samples. Both are extrapolations of this design's own making and belong in class 3 reasoning under [the whitepaper standard](/archive/bsc-programme/program/05-Whitepaper-Standard.md), not in class 1.

## How to teach it
- **Lecture point:** Teach the *conditions*, not the magnitudes. The three that matter are: the advantage appears only after training ends, it grows with the structural novelty of the eventual task, and it depends on the task giving interpretable feedback. Stating these as directions is what this programme permits; see `## Limits and cautions`.
- **Lecture point:** Teach the within-training result as a **null**, not a reversal. The honest statement is "during training there was no measurable advantage", not "guided training looks better". The point estimate is negative but the interval spans zero, and the authors themselves write only that immediate performance "may not differ or may even be depressed".
- **Lecture point:** Teach the feedback-clarity boundary as an engineering requirement. Ask students to classify systems they work on by whether the system itself reveals an error, and to notice how many do not. This is where the paper meets the rest of the curriculum.
- **Lecture point:** Distinguish this from generic "fail fast" or growth-mindset rhetoric. Keith and Frese specify two required components, name three boundary conditions and report a pool in which three of 24 studies favoured the opposite method. The slogans do none of that.
- **Lecture point:** Use the paper's own excluded-study decision as a lesson in inclusion criteria. Three studies that invoked the theory were dropped for not meeting the operational definition — including the firefighter study the Discussion later leans on.
- **Lab exercise:** Run paired labs on the same content, one guided and one exploratory with explicit error encouragement, then assess both cohorts weeks later on a structurally different problem. Students experience the moderator directly, including the fact that the guided cohort felt better at the time.
- **Lab exercise:** Build deliberately error-provoking exercises — a retrieval pipeline with a subtly contaminated eval set, an agent harness with a silent timeout — and **pair each one with the instrument that makes its failure visible**. Then run one deliberately without the instrument, so students see what an unclear-feedback task does to their own learning.
- **Studio exercise:** Institute an error-encouragement norm with a concrete ritual, such as opening each session by naming what broke since last time, so the framing component is actually delivered and not merely implied. Note that in the source the trainer offers **no help** after an error — which is a design choice this programme may reasonably decline, and should decline explicitly rather than by drift.
- **Assessment idea:** Assess error recovery as a distinct competence: given a broken system, how quickly and accurately does the student localise the fault? This measures what the training is theorised to build.
- **Assessment idea:** Make adaptive transfer the assessment default. Final assessments should be structurally unlike anything practised, and this should be stated as policy with this paper cited for the direction of the argument.

## Limits and cautions

**The magnitudes recorded in this note may not be carried into the wiki, and reading the full text does not change that.** The `pedagogy` namespace licenses **"Direction and mechanism only. No effect size, ever"** — see [concepts/namespaces.md](/concepts/namespaces.md). That is a namespace rule about what a learning-science claim may license in a curriculum, not a verification rule about how well a source is known. The prohibition on this repository's wiki asserting an effect size for a pedagogy claim is **unconditional and is unaffected by the depth of this reading.** A source moving from synthesis-only to fully read gains the right to be described precisely in `research/`; it does not gain the right to have its numbers taught. Anyone tempted to argue that verification now unlocks the figures should note that the design's own standing rule was written to survive exactly this temptation.

Beyond that rule, the source's own limits:

- **The evidence base is laboratory software training.** All but one of 24 studies were laboratory experiments; 21 of 24 taught software or electronic search; most samples were students. The authors themselves call for research on "other technical skills… as well as… completely different types of skills (e.g., social skills, managerial skills)". A multi-year professional formation programme is far outside this envelope.
- **On-the-job performance was never measured by any included study.** The authors state this as a research gap and mark their own reasoning about it as speculation: "it **could be speculated** that EMT benefits on-the-job performance". Any claim in this programme that error management training improves professional practice is this design's extrapolation, not this source's finding.
- **All effects are relative to an alternative training method.** No no-training control exists anywhere in the pool.
- **The homogeneity test was not significant**, so the moderators partition a pool that did not statistically demand partitioning. The authors declare this and justify testing a priori hypotheses regardless. It is honest, and it does weaken the "moderators explain the variation" narrative that the abstract implies.
- **The feedback-clarity moderator failed its own significance test** at p = .08, on the variable with the lowest interrater agreement in the study. Treat it as a serious hypothesis with suggestive subsample evidence, not as an established boundary.
- **The component decomposition is an interpretation the authors ask others to test**, and the incremental effect of error encouragement over bare exploration is the weakest significant result in the paper.
- **Adaptive-transfer studies are the smallest subsample.** The paper's most striking figure has its least data behind it.
- **The pool is not uniformly positive.** Three of 24 effects favour the comparison method and one is zero.
- **Effect sizes decline with study age**, a pattern the authors note in the introduction but do not code or test as a moderator. Eleven of 24 studies are unpublished, which mitigates publication bias by design; no statistical bias correction of any kind is reported.
- **Errors that are costly, unsafe or irreversible are outside the active form of this evidence.** The authors anticipate this and propose *vicarious* error management training for such settings — but that variant was excluded from the meta-analysis by their own inclusion criteria, so **nothing in this paper's results supports it**. Its effectiveness is offered as a question for future research.
- **The method has a cost the authors state plainly.** Where the goal is one specific procedure, direct instruction "may also be effective while being less time consuming and less effortful". Error management training is not free.
- **Prior knowledge and cognitive load are not examined here.** The concern that learners with very low domain knowledge may lack the base to interpret their own errors comes from the adjacent guided-instruction literature and is neither tested nor resolved by this paper.
- **The trainer in these studies gives no help after an error.** If this programme intends to intervene when students are stuck — and it should — it is departing from the operationalisation, and should say so.
- **The finding is about training design, not about tolerating errors in production.** Do not import it as an argument for lax engineering practice.
- **Evidence is from 2008 and predates AI-assisted work entirely.** Whether error encouragement retains its effect when a model can supply a correct answer on request is an open and important question for this course, and nothing here speaks to it.

**Corrections. The previous note contained one claim the full text does not support, one substantial omission, and several overstatements. These are recorded rather than quietly repaired.**

1. **The within-training reversal was asserted and is not supported.** The old note stated: *"During training itself, error-avoidant instruction can look better, because learners following a procedure make fewer mistakes and appear more competent"* and *"Error-avoidant training flatters itself on in-session measures."* The measured within-training effect is *d* = −0.15, **non-significant**, with a 90% interval of −0.47 to 0.17 spanning zero. The authors claim only that immediate performance "**may not differ or may even be depressed**". The correct statement is that the error management advantage is **absent** during training, not that guided training wins. The old note flagged "the within-training illusion" as interpretive in its verification section, which was right, but it then used it as fact in three other sections.
2. **An entire tested moderator was missing.** The old note said *"The moderator analysis is where the paper becomes genuinely useful"* and then described two moderators. There are **three**, plus the comparison-condition analysis. The missing one — **Hypothesis 3, clarity of task-generated feedback** — is the boundary condition most relevant to this curriculum, because it says the method depends on the task revealing its own failures, which the systems this course teaches frequently do not. Its absence also propagated into the old `## Structure of the original` section, which omitted the paper's *Task-Generated Feedback* section entirely.
3. **The component decomposition was overstated.** The old note said *"The meta-analysis rejects this: both active exploration and error encouragement were identified as independently effective elements"* and *"it is a stronger inference than most training meta-analyses can support."* The authors write that the finding "**can be interpreted to the effect that**" both elements are effective and immediately ask for further studies "to further examine the feasibility of this interpretation". The inference is between studies rather than within a design, and the second sentence of the old note's claim — the comparison with other meta-analyses — has no basis in the paper at all.
4. **The heterogeneity narrative was wrong in a way the abstract concealed.** The old note said *"the moderator analysis accounts for that variation."* The homogeneity test was **not significant**; variability was not greater than sampling error would predict, and the authors say so before testing their moderators anyway on a priori grounds.
5. **Two omissions that change what the source can be used for.** The old note did not record that **no study used a no-training control** (so all effects are relative), nor that **no study measured on-the-job performance** (so nothing here reaches professional practice). The second is material: the old `## Why it matters for the course` section leaned on transfer-to-real-work without disclosing that the authors flag it as an unfilled gap and label their own reasoning about it speculation.
6. **The publication-bias statement was imprecise.** The old note said *"Publication bias is a standing concern and the abstract does not state what correction, if any, was applied."* The full text reports **no statistical correction of any kind**, but the search deliberately pursued grey literature and contacted researchers, and **11 of the 24 studies are unpublished**. The concern is addressed procedurally and not statistically — a materially better position than the old wording implied, and still not a bias estimate.
7. **The setting was described too generously.** The old note said the studies were *"predominantly laboratory or short-course training."* All but one were laboratory experiments. There is essentially no field training in the pool.
8. **A teaching instruction conflicted with this repository's own rule.** The old note's first lecture point read: *"Present the three effect sizes in order — 0.44 overall, 0.56 post-training, 0.80 adaptive transfer — and let the escalation make the argument."* Delivering that lecture would put an effect size for a pedagogy claim into wiki-governed teaching content, which the namespace rule forbids unconditionally. It has been replaced with guidance that teaches the conditions instead.

**Verified from the full text and now assertable in this note:** all statistics in the table above; the four hypotheses as stated; the inclusion and exclusion criteria and the three excluded studies; the composition of the pool; the interrater kappas; the aggregation rules including the most-difficult-task rule; the analytic procedures; the verbatim error management instructions; the trainer's no-assistance protocol; the Bandura and Skinner positions with page-cited quotations; the vicarious-EMT proposal and its exclusion; the cost caveat; and the authors' statements about power, causal warrant and generalisability.

**Not established by this source, and not to be asserted from it:** any on-the-job outcome; any absolute effect against no training; any claim about multi-year formation, non-computer skills, social or managerial skills; any effectiveness figure for vicarious error management training; any publication-bias-corrected estimate; any interaction with prior knowledge or cognitive load.
