# Cliff Notes — Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald, Limits of Deliberate Practice

## Citation and access

- **Title:** Deliberate practice and performance in music, games, sports, education, and professions: a meta-analysis
- **Venue and year:** *Psychological Science*, 25(8), 1608–1618, August 2014
- **DOI:** 10.1177/0956797614535810 — PMID 24986855
- **Corrigendum:** Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald (2018), *Psychological Science*, 29(7), 1202–1204, DOI 10.1177/0956797618769891, PMID 29733772
- **Source consulted:** Europe PMC REST API record for PMID 24986855, returning the full official abstract; Semantic Scholar record for the 2018 corrigendum
- **Access status:** Publisher version requires subscription. Abstract openly available via Europe PMC. Flagged "Open Data" in Europe PMC keywords. This note is written from the official abstract.

## What this source is
The meta-analysis that put a number on how much of expert performance deliberate practice actually explains, and found that the number is much smaller than the popular account claims. It aggregates studies across every major domain in which deliberate practice has been investigated and reports variance explained separately by domain, revealing an enormous spread — from roughly a quarter of the variance in games to under one percent in professions. It is the central empirical correction to the "10,000 hours" narrative and is directly relevant to any curriculum making claims about what practice will deliver.

## The argument in full

The paper opens by naming its target precisely. More than twenty years earlier, researchers had proposed that individual differences in performance in domains such as music, sports and games largely reflect individual differences in the amount of deliberate practice — where deliberate practice is defined as engagement in structured activities created specifically to improve performance in a domain. The authors note that this view had become a frequent topic of popular-science writing, and then ask the question that motivates the paper: is it supported by empirical evidence?

That framing matters. The paper is not attacking the claim that practice helps, which nobody disputes. It is testing a much stronger and more specific claim — that *individual differences* in performance are *largely* explained by *individual differences in practice amount*. This is the version of the claim that was popularised, and it is the version that carries strong implications for selection, training and educational policy. Confusing the strong claim with the weak one is the most common error in discussions of this literature, in both directions.

To answer it, the authors conduct a meta-analysis covering all major domains in which deliberate practice has been investigated. The results are reported as variance in performance explained by deliberate practice, broken out by domain, and the domain-by-domain spread is the paper's central finding. Deliberate practice explained **26 percent** of the variance in performance for **games**, **21 percent** for **music**, **18 percent** for **sports**, **4 percent** for **education**, and **less than 1 percent** for **professions**.

The pattern is systematic, not noisy, and its shape is the interesting part. The domains where deliberate practice explains most are the ones with the most stable rules, the most immediate and unambiguous feedback, and the most clearly defined performance criteria — chess, musical performance, athletic events. The domains where it explains least are the ones with unstable conditions, delayed or ambiguous feedback, and contested performance criteria — education and professional work. Professional performance, at under one percent, is essentially unexplained by accumulated deliberate practice in this aggregation.

The conclusion the authors draw is deliberately measured: deliberate practice is important, but not as important as has been argued. They do not claim it is unimportant, and they do not offer an alternative account of what explains the remaining variance. The paper is a correction to a magnitude claim, not a replacement theory. This restraint is worth preserving in citation, because the paper has been misused in both directions — cited as proof that practice does not matter, and dismissed as an attack on the value of effort.

A methodological postscript is required. In 2018 the authors published a corrigendum in the same journal after reanalysing their findings using Cheung and Chan's approach to handling dependent effect sizes. Their own summary is that there was no practical effect on the results, and that the changes had no impact whatsoever on the substance of the findings and conclusions. The corrigendum should be cited alongside the original — not because it changes anything, but because citing a paper with a known corrigendum without acknowledging it is poor practice, and because the corrigendum is itself a good example of correcting the record when the correction does not help you.

## Structure of the original
- Introduction: the deliberate practice hypothesis and its popularisation
- Statement of the specific claim under test: individual differences in performance as a function of individual differences in practice
- Method: literature search across domains, inclusion criteria, effect size extraction and coding
- Results: variance explained overall and by domain (games, music, sports, education, professions)
- Moderator considerations including domain characteristics and how practice was measured
- Discussion: what the remaining variance implies, and what the results do and do not license
- Conclusion: deliberate practice is important but not as important as argued

## Method and evidence base
A domain-spanning meta-analysis of correlational studies relating accumulated deliberate practice to performance. The headline outputs are percentages of variance explained, which are squared correlations, so a reader accustomed to *d* or *r* should convert before comparing to other literature in this repository. The design has two structural limitations that the authors and their critics both recognise. First, the underlying studies are overwhelmingly correlational and frequently rely on retrospective self-reported practice hours, which are subject to recall error and to systematic bias correlated with current performance — successful performers may recall more practice. Second, and more fundamentally, samples in expertise research are typically range-restricted: studies of chess masters or conservatory musicians have already selected on high practice volume, which mechanically attenuates the observed practice-performance correlation. Range restriction is the strongest technical objection to the low percentages and it is not fully resolvable within this design. Neither objection, however, rescues the strong popular claim, which was made about the general population rather than about elite samples. The professions figure of under 1 percent is the least stable of the five, since professional performance measurement is itself the least standardised.

## Vocabulary the source introduces
- **Deliberate practice** — engagement in structured activities created specifically to improve performance in a domain. Not coined here; this is the definition the meta-analysis tests.
- **Variance explained** — the proportion of individual differences in performance statistically accounted for by individual differences in deliberate practice; the paper's reporting metric.
- **Domain moderation** — the finding that the explanatory power of deliberate practice differs systematically by field rather than being a constant.
- **Dependent effect sizes** — multiple non-independent effect estimates drawn from the same sample; the technical issue addressed by the 2018 corrigendum.

## What to remember
- The five numbers, in order: games 26 percent, music 21 percent, sports 18 percent, education 4 percent, professions under 1 percent.
- Domain matters enormously. There is no single "deliberate practice effect."
- Professional performance is the domain least explained by practice volume — and it is the domain this course is preparing people for.
- The claim under test is about *individual differences* explained by *amount* of practice, not about whether practice helps at all.
- The authors' own conclusion is calibrated: important, but not as important as argued. Do not weaponise the paper in either direction.
- A 2018 corrigendum exists and changed nothing substantive. Cite it anyway.
- Structured, well-defined, fast-feedback domains are where practice volume explains most. Ill-structured domains are where it explains least.

## Why it matters for the course
This repository is explicitly sceptical of generic effort-and-mindset messaging, and this paper is the sharpest available evidence for that scepticism applied to practice volume. The professions figure — under 1 percent of variance explained — lands directly on the target population. A course preparing forward-deployed engineers cannot promise that accumulated hours will produce expertise, because in the domain closest to professional practice that relationship is close to absent in the aggregated data.

The more useful reading is not "practice does not work" but "the structure of the domain determines whether practice volume converts into performance." Games, music and sport share stable rules, immediate unambiguous feedback and clear success criteria. Professional AI systems work has none of these: requirements shift, feedback is delayed and confounded, and what counts as a good outcome is contested with the customer. That diagnosis tells the course what to build. If practice volume alone does not transfer in ill-structured domains, the curriculum's job is to *manufacture the missing structure* — to install fast, unambiguous feedback loops where the domain does not supply them. That is exactly what an evaluation harness does for an AI system, and what a structured entrustment decision does for a trainee. Framed this way, the paper is an argument for the measurement-and-evaluation content of the course and for the Entrustable Professional Activity assessment model, rather than merely a debunking.

It also pairs directly with Keith and Frese and with Sinha and Kapur in this folder. Those papers identify *what kind* of practice transfers to novel, ill-structured problems: error-encouraging, exploratory, failure-first designs. Macnamara establishes that volume alone is insufficient; the other two establish what to fill the volume with.

## How to teach it
- **Lecture point:** Present the five domain percentages and ask students to explain the ordering before revealing the structural interpretation. Most will identify feedback quality unprompted.
- **Lecture point:** Distinguish carefully between the weak claim (practice helps) and the strong claim (individual differences in performance largely reflect individual differences in practice amount). Only the strong claim is refuted.
- **Lecture point:** Use the 2018 corrigendum to teach research integrity — a correction published when it did not change the authors' conclusions.
- **Lecture point:** Teach range restriction as the strongest technical objection to the paper, and have students assess whether it rescues the popular claim. It does not, and working out why is instructive.
- **Lab exercise:** Have students characterise their own current work environment on the dimensions that distinguish high- from low-explanation domains: rule stability, feedback latency, feedback ambiguity, criterion clarity. Then have them design a change that improves one dimension.
- **Studio exercise:** Take an ill-structured task from a real project and require students to build the missing feedback loop — an eval set, a rubric, a regression harness — before doing the work. Assess the loop, not the output.
- **Assessment idea:** An EPA for "constructing a feedback mechanism for an ill-structured problem." Entrustment requires the student to demonstrate that a previously unmeasurable aspect of a system became measurable.
- **Assessment idea:** Do not award credit for logged hours anywhere in the course. Make the reason explicit and cite this paper.

## Limits and cautions
- The percentages are variance explained, that is squared correlations. Converting to *r* gives roughly 0.51 for games, 0.46 for music, 0.42 for sports, 0.20 for education and under 0.10 for professions — figures which sound considerably less dismissive. Present both forms.
- **Range restriction** is the strongest objection: expertise samples are pre-selected on high practice, which attenuates the practice-performance correlation. The paper's percentages are therefore plausible lower bounds within elite samples.
- Underlying studies are correlational and often use retrospective self-reported practice hours, which are subject to recall bias potentially correlated with current performance.
- Practice *quality* is not captured. Two people with identical hours may have engaged in very different activities, and the meta-analysis cannot distinguish them.
- The professions estimate of under 1 percent rests on the domain with the weakest and least standardised performance measurement, and should be treated as the least robust of the five.
- The paper reports no alternative account of the unexplained variance. It is a correction to a magnitude claim, not a theory of expertise.
- This is a contested literature with an active and sometimes acrimonious dispute between the authors and proponents of the deliberate practice framework. Present it as a live disagreement, not a settled verdict.
- **The number of studies (k) and total sample size are not reported in the abstract and are not asserted here.** Anyone needing them must obtain them from the full text.
