## What this source is

**The 1977 paper** is a theoretical paper, not an experiment. Bandura proposes that the many apparently different psychological treatments that change behaviour — desensitisation, modelling, persuasion, direct practice — all work through a single common mechanism: they change a person's belief that they can successfully execute the behaviour required to produce a given outcome. The paper introduces *self-efficacy* as that construct, distinguishes it from outcome expectancy, and specifies where efficacy beliefs come from. It is one of the most heavily cited papers in psychology; APA PsycNet reports roughly seventeen thousand citations. **All of this remains a synthesis of the APA abstract and secondary accounts.**

**The 1995 volume is a different kind of object and is the work actually read.** It is a conference collection published eighteen years later, whose organising question is not *what is self-efficacy* but *what happens to efficacy beliefs when the society around them changes fast*. Bandura's own chapter is a mature restatement of the theory — the definition, the four sources, the four processes through which efficacy beliefs act — followed by applications to family systems, intellectual development, career choice and health, and closing with material that has no counterpart in 1977 at all: **collective efficacy**, and a catalogue of the conditions that undermine it. He states the volume's premise in the preface: it is "structured around the central theme that youths' beliefs in their personal efficacy to manage life demands affect their psychological well-being, their accomplishments, and the direction their lives take" (p. ix).

The volume is not a systematic review and does not present itself as one. Bandura's chapter is a densely cited narrative synthesis, a large share of the citations being his own work or his collaborators'; the contributed chapters are each a specialist's review of their own field, several of them reporting the contributor's own unpublished or in-press data. The audience is stated as academic and general — the foreword hopes for "the welcome it deserves, not only from specialists, but also from the general public interested in the welfare of our youth" (p. viii) — and the register is accordingly discursive rather than evidential.

## The argument in full

Quotations below are from the **1995 volume** unless the text says otherwise. Where a section describes the 1977 paper, it says so and marks the claim unverified.

### What the 1977 paper is said to argue — unverified

Bandura begins from a problem in the clinical literature of the 1970s. Behaviour-change techniques derived from very different theoretical traditions were all producing results, and each tradition explained its own results in its own terms. Systematic desensitisation was explained by counter-conditioning, modelling by vicarious learning, performance-based treatments by reinforcement. Bandura argues this proliferation of mechanism-specific explanations is unsatisfying, and that a single cognitive mediator can account for the effectiveness of all of them. The central theoretical move is the separation of an *outcome expectancy* — a belief that a given behaviour will lead to a given outcome — from an *efficacy expectation*, a belief that one can actually perform the behaviour. **This paragraph rests on the APA abstract and on secondary accounts. None of it has been checked against the paper.**

Two contributors to the 1995 volume characterise the 1977 paper directly, and their characterisations are recorded here because they are the closest thing to primary testimony this note has. Zimmerman writes that the historical impasse in measuring academic self-conceptions "was surmounted in 1977 with Bandura's seminal treatise that proposed a theory of the origins, mediating mechanisms, and diverse effects of beliefs of personal efficacy" (p. 202); that "Bandura (1977) hypothesized that efficacy beliefs influence level of effort, persistence, and choice of activities" (p. 204); and that "Bandura (1977, 1986) developed scales to measure perceived academic efficacy as part of a microanalytic procedure to assess its **level, generality, and strength** across activities and contexts" (p. 203). Oettingen writes that "Bandura (1977, in press) specifies four information sources that people use in forming their sense of personal efficacy. The most important source is performance experiences" (p. 149), and attributes to the same paper the claim that "A strong sense of efficacy acquired in one area of functioning may transfer to other areas, thus creating a general sense of personal efficacy" (pp. 149–150). **These are Zimmerman's and Oettingen's readings of the 1977 paper. They are not the paper.**

### The definition, in Bandura's own words

"Perceived self-efficacy refers to beliefs in one's capabilities to organize and execute the courses of action required to manage prospective situations. Efficacy beliefs influence how people think, feel, motivate themselves, and act" (p. 2). The premise licensing the whole construct appears a few lines earlier: "People's level of motivation, affective states, and actions are based more on what they believe than on what is objectively the case" (p. 2). Bandura poses the causal question himself and treats it as settled: "Do efficacy beliefs operate as causal factors in human functioning? The findings of diverse causal tests, in which efficacy beliefs are systematically varied, are consistent in showing that such beliefs contribute significantly to human motivation and attainments" (pp. 2–3).

### The four sources

Bandura introduces them as "four main forms of influence" (p. 3), and **only the first is explicitly ranked**. "The most effective way of creating a strong sense of efficacy is through mastery experiences. They provide the most authentic evidence of whether one can muster whatever it takes to succeed" (p. 3). The others are enumerated as "The second influential way" (vicarious experience through social models, p. 3), "a third way" (social persuasion, p. 4) and "The fourth way" (physiological and emotional states, pp. 4–5). **The chapter does not defend positions two to four as a strict ordering by dependability, and it does not describe verbal persuasion as the weakest source — the fourth position belongs to physiological and affective states.**

Four things Bandura says about the sources are routinely dropped from summaries, and all four bear directly on course design.

**First, a diet of easy successes is counterproductive.** "Successes build a robust belief in one's personal efficacy. Failures undermine it, especially if failures occur before a sense of efficacy is firmly established." Then, immediately: "**If people experience only easy successes they come to expect quick results and are easily discouraged by failure. A resilient sense of efficacy requires experience in overcoming obstacles through perseverant effort. Some difficulties and setbacks in human pursuits serve a useful purpose in teaching that success usually requires sustained effort.** After people become convinced they have what it takes to succeed, they persevere in the face of adversity and quickly rebound from setbacks. By sticking it out through tough times, they emerge stronger from adversity" (p. 3). Mastery is also not habit acquisition: "Developing a sense of efficacy through mastery experiences is not a matter of adopting ready-made habits. Rather, it involves acquiring the cognitive, behavioral, and self-regulatory tools for creating and executing appropriate courses of action to manage ever-changing life circumstances" (p. 3).

**Second, modelling runs on perceived similarity, and it cuts both ways.** "Seeing people similar to themselves succeed by perseverant effort raises observers' beliefs that they, too, possess the capabilities to master comparable activities. By the same token, observing others fail despite high effort lowers observers' judgments of their own efficacy and undermines their level of motivation... The greater the assumed similarity the more persuasive are the models' successes and failures. If people see the models as very different from themselves their beliefs of personal efficacy are not much influenced by the models' behavior" (pp. 3–4). What a model transmits is more than a comparison standard, and Bandura ranks the two: "**Undaunted attitudes exhibited by perseverant models as they cope with obstacles repeatedly thrown in their path can be more enabling to others than the particular skills being modeled**" (p. 4).

**Third, persuasion is asymmetric, and the useful part of it is not the talking.** "It is more difficult to instill high beliefs of personal efficacy by social persuasion alone than to undermine them. Unrealistic boosts in efficacy are quickly disconfirmed by disappointing results of one's efforts. But people who have been persuaded that they lack capabilities tend to avoid challenging activities that can cultivate their potentialities, and they give up quickly in the face of difficulties. By constricting activities and undermining motivation, disbelief in one's capabilities creates its own behavioral validation" (p. 4). He then reframes the source entirely: "**Successful efficacy builders do more than convey positive appraisals. In addition to raising people's beliefs in their capabilities, they structure situations for them in ways that bring success and avoid placing people in situations prematurely where they are likely to fail often. They encourage individuals to measure their success in terms of self-improvement rather than by triumphs over others**" (p. 4). Persuasion in Bandura's account is inseparable from task sequencing and from the choice of comparison standard. He does not dismiss the verbal component either: "To the extent that persuasive boosts in perceived self-efficacy lead people to try hard enough to succeed, self-affirming beliefs promote development of skills and a sense of personal efficacy" (p. 4).

**Fourth, arousal is read, not merely felt.** "It is not the sheer intensity of emotional and physical reactions that is important but rather how they are perceived and interpreted. For example, people who have a high sense of efficacy are likely to view their state of affective arousal as an energizing facilitator of performance, whereas those who are beset by self-doubts regard their arousal as a debilitator" (p. 5). The corresponding intervention is physical and interpretive rather than motivational: "to enhance physical status, reduce stress and negative emotional proclivities, and correct misinterpretations of bodily states" (pp. 4–5). One hedge here bears on the "self-efficacy is domain-specific" claim: "Affective states can have widely generalized effects on beliefs of personal efficacy in diverse spheres of functioning" (p. 5).

Bandura closes the section by insisting that the four sources are inputs and not effects: "Information that is relevant for judging personal efficacy, whether conveyed enactively, vicariously, persuasively, or affectively is not inherently instructive. Rather it gains its significance through cognitive processing" (p. 5). What a person makes of an experience "will depend on people's preconceptions of their capabilities, the perceived difficulty of the tasks, the amount of effort they expended, their physical and emotional state at the time, the amount of external aid they received, and the situational circumstances under which they performed" (p. 5).

### Efficacy beliefs and outcome expectations

The 1995 chapter does not headline this distinction the way the 1977 paper is said to. It appears twice, and in both places the claim is **stronger** than a distinction: efficacy beliefs are prior to, and partly constitutive of, outcome expectancies.

In the treatment of motivation: "In expectancy-value theory, motivation is regulated by the expectation that a given course of behavior will produce certain outcomes and the value placed on those outcomes. But people act on their beliefs about what they can do as well as on their beliefs about the likely outcomes of performance. The motivating influence of outcome expectancies is thus partly governed by efficacy beliefs. **There are countless attractive options people do not pursue because they judge they lack the capabilities for them**" (p. 7).

In the treatment of career choice it is put at its sharpest: "It is now well established that people do not behave like wholly rational utility maximizers. Efficacy beliefs determine the slate of options given any consideration. People do not regard options in domains of low perceived efficacy worth considering, whatever benefits they may hold. Such wholesale exclusions of large classes of options are made rapidly on perceived efficacy grounds with little thought to costs and benefits. **Efficacy beliefs preempt expectancy-valence analyses**" (p. 23).

### Effort, persistence and response to failure

This is the part of the theory the programme uses most, and Bandura states it as a compact list: "Efficacy beliefs contribute to motivation in several ways: **They determine the goals people set for themselves, how much effort they expend, how long they persevere in the face of difficulties, and their resilience to failures.** When faced with obstacles and failures, people who distrust their capabilities slacken their efforts or give up quickly. Those who have a strong belief in their capabilities exert greater effort when they fail to master the challenge" (p. 8). There are **four** terms here, not the three the previous version of this note carried, and goal-setting leads.

The behavioural summary is given twice, once in each direction. On low efficacy: "People who have a low sense of efficacy in given domains shy away from difficult tasks, which they view as personal threats. They have low aspirations and weak commitment to the goals they choose to pursue. When faced with difficult tasks, they dwell on their personal deficiencies, the obstacles they will encounter, and all kinds of adverse outcomes rather than concentrate on how to perform successfully. They slacken their efforts and give up quickly in the face of difficulties. They are slow to recover their sense of efficacy following failure or setbacks. **Because they view insufficient performance as deficient aptitude, it does not require much failure for them to lose faith in their capabilities**" (p. 11). On high efficacy: those with high assurance "approach difficult tasks as challenges to be mastered rather than as threats to be avoided... They quickly recover their sense of efficacy after failures or setbacks. **They attribute failure to insufficient effort or to deficient knowledge and skills that are acquirable**" (p. 11).

The attributional mechanism is stated separately: "People who regard themselves as highly efficacious attribute their failures to insufficient effort or adverse situational conditions, whereas those who regard themselves as inefficacious tend to attribute their failures to low ability" (p. 7). The cognitive cost of low efficacy under load is specific and is the finding most relevant to a live client engagement: "when people are faced with the task of managing difficult environmental demands under taxing circumstances, those who harbor a low sense of efficacy become more and more erratic in their analytic thinking and lower their aspirations, and the quality of their performance deteriorates" (p. 6).

One warning in this section reads as though written against the misuse of the theory: "**The multiple benefits of a resilient sense of personal efficacy do not arise simply from the incantation of capability. Saying something should not be confused with believing it to be so. Simply saying that one is capable is not necessarily self-convincing.** Self-efficacy beliefs are the product of a complex process of self-persuasion that relies on cognitive processing of diverse sources of efficacy information conveyed enactively, vicariously, socially, and physiologically" (p. 11).

### Optimism, and the boundary Bandura actually draws

This is where the volume most sharply contradicts the previous version of this note, so it is set out at length.

Bandura's position is that **mild over-estimation of one's own capabilities is adaptive and should not be corrected.** "Human accomplishments and positive well-being require an optimistic sense of personal efficacy. This is because ordinary social realities are strewn with difficulties... In pursuits strewn with obstacles, realists either forsake the venture, abort their efforts prematurely when difficulties arise, or become cynical about the prospects of effecting significant changes" (pp. 11–12). And directly: "**When people err in their self-appraisal they tend to overestimate their capabilities. This is a benefit rather than a cognitive failing or character flaw to be eradicated.** If efficacy beliefs always reflected only what people could do, routinely they would remain steadfastly wedded to an overly conservative judgment of their capabilities that begets habitual performances. Under cautious self-appraisal, people rarely set aspirations beyond their immediate reach nor mount the extra effort needed to surpass their ordinary performances" (p. 12). He notes from the clinical literature that "it is often the normal people who are distorters of reality" (p. 12), and concludes: "Realists may adapt well to existing realities. But those with a tenacious self-efficacy are likely to change those realities" (p. 13).

**The boundary he draws is on consequence, not on calibration.** "It is widely believed that misjudgment breeds personal problems. Certainly, gross miscalculation can get one into trouble. However, the functional value of accuracy of self-appraisal depends on the nature of the venture. **Activities in which mistakes can produce costly or injurious consequences call for accurate self-appraisal of capabilities.** It is a different matter where difficult accomplishments can produce substantial personal and social benefits and the costs involve one's time, effort, and expendable resources" (p. 12). The second and only other hedge is a single clause: "**If not unrealistically exaggerated**, such personal beliefs foster positive well-being and human accomplishments" (p. 13).

There are therefore exactly two conditions under which Bandura wants accuracy — when errors are costly or injurious, and when the exaggeration is gross. Outside those two, he treats optimistic mis-calibration as a resource to be protected. **That is not the position this note previously asserted.**

### Collective efficacy

The 1977 paper predates this construct and the previous version of this note did not mention it. It is the volume's principal addition.

Bandura first removes a misreading: "Some writers inappropriately equate self-efficacy with individualism and pit it against collectivism. Contrary to this view, a high sense of personal efficacy contributes just as importantly to group directedness as to self-directedness. In collectively oriented systems, people work together to produce the benefits they seek. **Group pursuits are no less demanding of personal efficacy than are individual pursuits**" (p. 34). The universality claim rests on Earley's comparative work and is hedged as such: "Efficacy beliefs function similarly in collectivistic and individualistic societies whether analyzed at the societal level or the individual level" (p. 34); "A low sense of coping efficacy is just as occupationally debilitating and stressful in collectivistic societies as in individualistic ones" (p. 34).

The construct itself: "**The strength of families, communities, social institutions, and even nations lies partly in people's sense of collective efficacy that they can solve the problems they face and improve their lives through unified effort. People's beliefs in their collective efficacy influence the type of social future they seek to achieve, how much effort they put into it, and their endurance when collective efforts fail to produce quick results**" (p. 35). The three consequences are the same three as for personal efficacy — goal, effort, endurance — transposed to a group.

The operational definition, given in the health-policy section, is the most useful one for engineering teams because it is a list of *joint* capabilities rather than an aggregate of individual ones: "perceived efficacy is concerned with people's beliefs in their joint capabilities to make health promotion a national priority, **to forge divergent self-interests into a shared agenda, to enlist supporters and resources for collective action, to devise effective strategies and to execute them successfully, and to withstand forcible opposition and discouraging setbacks**. We do not lack sound policy prescriptions in the field of health. **What is lacking is the collective efficacy to realize them**" (p. 33).

Bandura's catalogue of underminers is unusually concrete: transnational interdependence, where "There are no handy social mechanisms by which people can exercise reciprocal influence on transnational systems that affect their daily lives" (p. 36); dependence on "complex physical technologies that most people neither understand nor believe they can do much to influence" (p. 36); the diffusion of accountability — "**Even the more efficacious individuals, who are not easily deterred, find their efforts blunted by mazy organizational mechanisms that diffuse and obscure responsibility. Pitting oneself repeatedly against bureaucratic gauntlets eventually exacts its toll**" (p. 37), quoting John Gardner's "Getting things done socially is no sport for the short winded"; the delay between action and evidence — "Long delays between action and noticeable results discourage many advocates along the way" (p. 37); and factionalism, "so it is easier to get people to block courses of action than to merge them into a unified force for social change" (p. 37). He records the resulting asymmetry: "Not surprisingly, people have a higher sense of personal efficacy than institutional efficacy" (p. 36), and "As the need for efficacious collective effort grows, so does the sense of collective powerlessness" (p. 36). The closing claim is the one the programme should carry: "**The psychological barriers created by beliefs of collective powerlessness are more demoralizing and debilitating than are external impediments. People who have a sense of collective efficacy will mobilize their efforts and resources to cope with external obstacles to the changes they seek. But those convinced of their collective powerlessness will cease trying even though changes are attainable through perseverant collective effort**" (p. 38).

Collective efficacy also appears in the school setting, where it produces the volume's clearest institutional finding: "The schools' collective sense of efficacy at the beginning of the academic year predicts the schools' level of academic achievement at the end of the year when the effects of the characteristics of the student bodies, their prior level of academic achievement, and the staff's experiential level are factored out" (p. 21). Bandura is careful about the causal direction of the socioeconomic correlate: "student characteristics affect school achievement in large part by altering the staff's beliefs in their collective instructional efficacy" (p. 21). The same claim is made about individual instructors, on Ashton and Webb's authority: "teachers' beliefs concerning their instructional efficacy predicts students' levels of academic achievement over the course of the academic year, regardless of their entering ability" (p. 20).

### The change model Bandura endorses

Set out in the health chapter but stated generally: "Effective models rely on guided mastery experiences as the principal vehicle of personal change" (p. 30), in four components and in order. Information first, and discounted: "factual information alone, much of which is usually redundant with what people already know, usually produces little change" (p. 30). Self-regulatory skills second. Efficacy third, and its mechanism is rehearsal under realistic conditions: "the third component of self-management is aimed at building a robust sense of efficacy by providing the participants with **repeated opportunities for guided practice in applying the skills successfully in situations simulating those they are likely to encounter in their everyday life**" (p. 31). Social support fourth: "Personal change occurs within a network of social influences" (p. 31). The failure mode when the third component is omitted: "If people are not fully convinced of their personal efficacy they undermine their efforts in difficult situations and readily abandon the skills they have been taught when they suffer reverses or fail to get quick results" (p. 31).

The relapse finding transfers directly to how a student handles a bad week: "People who have strong belief in their efficacy tend to regard a slip as a temporary setback and reinstate control. In contrast, those who distrust their self-regulatory capabilities display a marked decrease in perceived self-efficacy after a slip and make little effort to reinstate control" (p. 32).

### What the contributors add

**Zimmerman (Chapter 7, educational development)** is the chapter closest to this programme's concerns. He sets out five properties of the construct as operationalised: judgements of capability to perform activities "rather than personal qualities such as one's physical characteristics or psychological traits"; multidimensionality across domains; context-dependence, so that "students may express a lower sense of efficacy to learn in competitive classroom structures than in cooperative ones"; a mastery rather than a normative criterion; and, decisively for research design, "self-efficacy is measured **before** students perform the relevant activities. This antecedent property provides the temporal ordering for evaluating the role of self-efficacy beliefs in causal structures" (pp. 203–204). He also flags a measurement trap that any naive persistence-based assessment would fall into: "When tasks are solvable in persistence studies, self-efficacious children will solve them quicker (and have no need to persist) than those who doubt their capabilities. Such test conditions can yield the seemingly paradoxical finding that self-efficacious children are less persistent. **The motivation effects of efficacy beliefs are, therefore, best tested on difficult or unsolvable problems**" (pp. 206–207).

**Oettingen (Chapter 5, cross-cultural)** supplies the volume's natural experiment. East Berlin schooling before unification made accurate self-evaluation an explicit educational goal and attached social reward to it, and it worked: "children who evaluated themselves adequately were rewarded with positions of leadership in the class-collective, whereas voicing an optimistic sense of personal efficacy brought social censure" (p. 166). East Berlin children's efficacy beliefs consequently tracked their teachers' grades far more tightly than West Berlin children's did, and most tightly for the least able. Her verdict bears directly on assessment design: "Apparently, the East Berlin school context was successful in forcing less intelligent students to turn their initial performance optimism into a more negative self-view" (p. 165) — and those were the children who "would really need the motivational and affective benefits of a positive sense of efficacy" (p. 165). She refuses to make this a straightforward win for the West, recording that the accurate self-appraisers gained real social standing, so that "The motivational and cognitive benefits of a strong sense of personal efficacy might thus have incurred social costs" (p. 166). Bandura cites her result in his own chapter in support of the optimism argument: "in social systems where children are punished for optimistic beliefs in their capabilities their attainments closely match their conservative view of what they come to expect of themselves" (p. 12).

**Schneewind (Chapter 4, family processes)** supplies the volume's most careful methodological caution and its only clean self-disconfirmation. He reports cross-lagged correlations and immediately bounds their interpretation — the studies "are based on cross-sectional designs and thus make it difficult to disentangle the direction of causation", and he invokes "Kenny's (1979) caveats concerning the causal interpretation of cross-lagged correlations" against his own data (pp. 130–131). His seventeen-year longitudinal data failed to support a hypothesis he had reason to expect: "the intrafamilial correlations do not confirm the modeling hypothesis at either measurement point" (p. 128). **No other contributor reports a disconfirmation of their own prediction**, and that asymmetry should be weighed when reading the rest.

**Jerusalem and Mittag (Chapter 6)** contribute the finding Bandura reuses twice in his own chapter: among East-to-West German migrants, "migrants with a high sense of efficacy treat it as a challenge, whereas those who distrust their coping capabilities view it as a threat" (Bandura, p. 9, citing them). The remaining chapters — Elder on economic hardship and life trajectories, Flammer on the development of control beliefs, Hackett on career choice, Schwarzer and Fuchs on health behaviour, and Marlatt, Baer and Quigley on addiction — were read but are summarised here only where Bandura's own chapter draws on them.

## Key claims, findings, and specifics

### Bandura's own, from the 1995 chapter — verified
- Perceived self-efficacy is "beliefs in one's capabilities to organize and execute the courses of action required to manage prospective situations" (p. 2).
- Efficacy beliefs determine **four** things, not three: the goals people set, how much effort they expend, how long they persevere, and their resilience to failure (p. 8).
- Four sources: mastery experiences, vicarious experience through social models, social persuasion, physiological and emotional states. **Only mastery is explicitly ranked** — "The most effective way" (p. 3).
- **Only easy successes produce a brittle sense of efficacy.** "A resilient sense of efficacy requires experience in overcoming obstacles through perseverant effort" (p. 3).
- **Persuasion is asymmetric**: easier to undermine efficacy than to instil it, and disbelief "creates its own behavioral validation" (p. 4).
- Efficacy builders "do more than convey positive appraisals" — they sequence tasks for reachable success and set self-improvement rather than competition as the comparison standard (p. 4).
- **Efficacy beliefs preempt expectancy-valence analyses**: whole classes of option are excluded on efficacy grounds before costs and benefits are weighed at all (p. 23).
- The efficacious and inefficacious differ in what they attribute failure to — insufficient effort or acquirable skill, versus low ability (pp. 7, 11).
- Low efficacy under taxing demand degrades analytic thinking itself, not just motivation (p. 6).
- **Optimistic mis-calibration is a benefit, not a flaw to be eradicated** (p. 12), bounded only by gross exaggeration and by ventures "in which mistakes can produce costly or injurious consequences" (pp. 12–13).
- Collective efficacy is a belief in *joint* capability — to forge divergent self-interests into a shared agenda, enlist resources, devise and execute strategy, and withstand opposition and setback (p. 33).
- Beliefs of collective powerlessness are "more demoralizing and debilitating than are external impediments" (p. 38).
- People hold "a higher sense of personal efficacy than institutional efficacy" (p. 36).
- Guided mastery is the principal vehicle of change; information alone "usually produces little change" (p. 30).

### From contributed chapters — the contributor's claim, not Bandura's
- **Zimmerman**, p. 204: self-efficacy is measured *before* performance, which is what gives the construct its temporal ordering in causal models.
- **Zimmerman**, pp. 206–207: persistence is a treacherous outcome measure, because efficacious students finish quickly and so persist less; test on difficult or unsolvable problems.
- **Zimmerman**, p. 203: the 1977/1986 scales assess **level, generality and strength** — not "magnitude".
- **Oettingen**, pp. 165–166: an education system that rewards accurate self-appraisal and censures optimism succeeds in suppressing optimism, most strongly in the least able, who need it most — but the accurate self-appraisers gained real social standing, so the trade was not free.
- **Schneewind**, p. 128: seventeen-year longitudinal intrafamilial correlations **did not confirm** the modelling hypothesis for control beliefs.
- **Jerusalem and Mittag**, via Bandura p. 9: high-efficacy migrants construe a wrenching transition as challenge, low-efficacy migrants as threat.

### Magnitudes the volume states
Recorded here for completeness and **not for propagation** — see `## Limits and cautions`.
- **Zimmerman**, p. 207, reporting Multon, Brown and Lent's 1991 meta-analysis of 68 published and unpublished studies from 1977–1989, of which 18 carried motivation measures: "a significant positive effect size of *r* = .34", accounting for "12% of the variance in their task persistence", with sub-measures *r* = .48 (rate), *r* = .34 (semesters completed) and *r* = .17 (persistence time).
- **Zimmerman**, p. 224: efficacy beliefs and strategy use share 16–18% of variance across grades five, eight and eleven; and, citing Britton and Tesser, time-management efficacy predicted achievement four years later and "accounted for three times more variance than did a standardized scholastic aptitude test".
- **Oettingen**, p. 163: for East Berlin children in the lowest third by RAVEN score, "more than 80% of the variance in efficacy beliefs was explained by course grades", 40 percentage points more than the comparable West Berlin group; p. 165, "The unusually high correlation of *r* = .90 between efficacy beliefs and academic performance in the less intelligent East Berlin children".
- **Schneewind**, p. 131: retrospective family-of-origin variables explained 27% of variance in young fathers' and 18% in young mothers' parenting efficacy (*N* = 48).
- **Bandura**, pp. 25–26, citing McGinnis and Foege: "Approximately half the deaths in the United States are caused prematurely by detrimental health habits over which people have some control." This is an epidemiological magnitude, not a pedagogy effect size.
- **Bandura**, preface p. xi, on Oettingen's work, and this is the closest he comes to a magnitude of his own: "Efficacy beliefs correlate with academic achievement, although the size of the relationship varies cross-culturally."

### Still resting only on the unread 1977 paper
- That the efficacy/outcome distinction is the paper's central theoretical move, and the specific claim that a person may believe an action works and still not attempt it.
- The unifying claim that psychological procedures of any form work by altering the level and strength of self-efficacy.
- That the four sources are ranked *against each other* by dependability, rather than mastery simply being first.
- The "subjectively threatening but in fact relatively safe" mechanism and its dual payoff of enhanced efficacy plus reduced defensive behaviour.
- The three dimensions of efficacy expectations, and whether the first is called magnitude or level.
- The existence and design of the microanalyses of enactive, vicarious and emotive treatment modes.
- The characterisation of persuasion-induced beliefs as easily extinguished.
- The approximate citation count.

## Method and evidence base

**The 1977 paper.** Primarily theoretical. Its empirical support is described in the abstract as microanalytic studies in which participants' task-by-task efficacy judgements are correlated with their subsequent task-by-task performance, across enactive, vicarious and emotive treatment conditions. Rather than treating efficacy as a global trait measured once, the design measures it at the granularity of specific tasks at specific difficulty levels, immediately before performance. That granularity is what allows efficacy to be tested as a proximal mediator rather than a vague disposition. The original studies were conducted in a clinical treatment context, which is a real boundary condition on the 1977 evidence base. **All of this remains unverified.**

**The 1995 volume.** A conference proceedings, not a systematic review, and it should be weighed as one. Bandura's chapter is a narrative synthesis: it cites densely, it selects, and it reports no search strategy, no inclusion criteria and no disconfirming study. A large share of the citations are to Bandura himself, to work in press at the time — including the 1997 book *Self-efficacy: The Exercise of Control*, cited throughout as "in press" — or to his students and collaborators. Several of the volume's key results are cited as unpublished manuscripts or conference papers rather than as published work; Ozer's dual-role study and the Caprara, Pastorelli and Bandura study of academic efficacy and interpersonal behaviour are both cited as unpublished, and Elder's inner-city findings as conference papers.

The contributed chapters vary in evidential weight and should not be treated as one body. Oettingen's is the strongest design in the book — a four-society comparison exploiting a natural experiment (East Berlin before unification, West Berlin, Moscow, Los Angeles) with an independent ability covariate. Schneewind's is a seventeen-year longitudinal family study, and is the only chapter that reports its own hypothesis failing. Zimmerman's is a literature review anchored on a published meta-analysis. The remainder are narrative reviews by the field's principal advocates.

**Two structural weaknesses are visible in the volume itself.** The first is directionality. Bandura concedes it in one place — "Mood and perceived efficacy influence each other bidirectionally" (p. 10) — and Schneewind concedes it more fully for his own data, invoking Kenny's caveats against cross-lagged causal inference (pp. 130–131). The second is that the construct is almost everywhere measured by self-report, immediately before a performance that is itself often self-reported or teacher-rated. Zimmerman's antecedent-measurement argument (p. 204) mitigates the ordering problem but not the shared-method problem.

## Vocabulary the source introduces

Terms marked **[1995]** are quoted or defined in the volume read. Terms marked **[1977, unverified]** are conventional in the literature but were not confirmed against the paper.

- **Perceived self-efficacy** — "beliefs in one's capabilities to organize and execute the courses of action required to manage prospective situations" (p. 2). **[1995]**
- **Collective efficacy** — people's beliefs in their *joint* capabilities to solve shared problems through unified effort; specified as a list of joint capacities including forging divergent self-interests into a shared agenda and withstanding opposition and setback (pp. 33, 35). **[1995]**
- **Mastery experience** — direct experience of succeeding at the task; "the most authentic evidence of whether one can muster whatever it takes to succeed" (p. 3). **[1995]**
- **Vicarious experience** — efficacy information obtained by observing similar others perform, weighted by perceived similarity (pp. 3–4). **[1995]**
- **Social persuasion** — the third source; in Bandura's account inseparable from structuring situations for reachable success (p. 4). The 1977 wording is *verbal persuasion*. **[1995]**
- **Physiological and emotional states** — bodily arousal and mood read as evidence about one's own capability, where the interpretation matters more than the intensity (pp. 4–5). The 1977 wording is *physiological states*. **[1995]**
- **Efficacy-activated processes** — the four routes by which efficacy beliefs regulate functioning: cognitive, motivational, affective and selection (pp. 5–10). **[1995]**
- **Selection processes** — the mechanism by which efficacy beliefs shape a life course by determining which environments a person enters, after which "the social influences operating in selected environments continue to promote certain competencies, values, and interests long after the efficacy decisional determinant has rendered its inaugurating effect" (p. 10). **[1995]**
- **Guided mastery** — the four-component change model: information, self-regulatory skills, efficacy-building through repeated guided practice in realistic simulation, and social support (pp. 30–31). **[1995]**
- **Thought control efficacy** — perceived capability to stop ruminative thought; predicts anxiety independently of how often such thoughts occur (pp. 8–9). **[1995]**
- **Triadic reciprocal causation** — the framing in which social-structural and personal determinants are "integrated as cofactors within a unified causal structure" rather than as competing levels of explanation (p. 15). **[1995]**
- **Level, generality, strength** — the three dimensions along which efficacy is scaled; Zimmerman attributes them to Bandura 1977 and 1986 (p. 203). **[1977, unverified]** The term *magnitude*, which this note previously used for the first dimension, appears nowhere in the volume.
- **Efficacy expectation** / **outcome expectancy** — the conviction that one can perform a behaviour, versus the estimate that the behaviour will produce an outcome. The volume treats outcome expectancy inside expectancy-value theory and argues efficacy is prior to it (pp. 7, 23). The paired terminology is **[1977, unverified]**.
- **Microanalysis** — correlating task-specific efficacy judgements with task-specific performance. **[1977, unverified]**; Zimmerman refers to "a microanalytic procedure" at p. 203.

## What to remember

- **Efficacy beliefs are domain-specific but not hermetically so.** Bandura says affective states "can have widely generalized effects on beliefs of personal efficacy in diverse spheres of functioning" (p. 5), and Oettingen attributes cross-domain transfer to the 1977 paper. The blunt formula "self-efficacy is task-specific, never general" overstates it. "Confidence" in the ordinary sense is still not the construct.
- **Mastery is first, and that is the only ordering Bandura defends.** Do not repeat the claim that verbal persuasion is the weakest source; physiological and affective states occupy the fourth position in his enumeration.
- **Only easy successes build a brittle sense of efficacy.** This is the single most important correction the volume makes to the previous version of this note. Real obstacles and real setbacks are part of the mechanism, not a hazard to be engineered away.
- **Persuasion is asymmetric.** Efficacy can be destroyed with words far more easily than it can be built with them, and the damage validates itself by removing the person from the activities that would have disconfirmed it.
- **An efficacy builder structures the situation.** "Successful efficacy builders do more than convey positive appraisals" (p. 4). Sequencing tasks and choosing the comparison standard are the intervention; the encouragement is incidental to it.
- **Efficacy runs ahead of cost–benefit reasoning.** Options in low-efficacy domains are excluded wholesale, "with little thought to costs and benefits" (p. 23). A student who never raises an option has not weighed and rejected it.
- **Bandura wants optimism, not calibration** — except where mistakes are costly or injurious, or where the exaggeration is gross. Assessment schemes that penalise over-confidence are working against him, and Oettingen's East Berlin data are the closest thing the volume has to a demonstration of what that costs.
- **The failure attribution is the diagnostic.** Efficacious people attribute failure to effort or to acquirable skill; inefficacious people attribute it to ability. That is observable in a debrief.
- **Low efficacy degrades thinking, not just persistence.** Under taxing demand it makes analytic reasoning "more and more erratic" (p. 6).
- **Collective efficacy is a separate construct with its own underminers**, and Bandura's list of them — diffuse accountability, long delay between action and visible result, factional interest, dependence on systems nobody understands — reads as a description of a large engineering organisation.
- **Saying it is not believing it.** "The multiple benefits of a resilient sense of personal efficacy do not arise simply from the incantation of capability" (p. 11).
- **The 1977 evidence base is clinical and narrow** relative to the breadth of the theory's later application. The 1995 volume is much broader, but it is a conference collection rather than a systematic review, so breadth here is not the same as weight.

## Why it matters for the course

A university-grade AI systems and forward-deployed-engineer course has to produce graduates who will attempt genuinely hard, ambiguous problems in front of a customer and keep going when the first three approaches fail. That is exactly the goal–effort–persistence–resilience quartet Bandura names at p. 8, and it is why this source sits under the programme's disposition claims rather than its technical ones.

The design constraint the volume actually licenses is narrower and harder than the one this note previously stated. It is **not** "prefer mastery to encouragement", though that remains true. It is that **the efficacy-building work is task design**. "Successful efficacy builders do more than convey positive appraisals... they structure situations for them in ways that bring success and avoid placing people in situations prematurely where they are likely to fail often" (p. 4). The lever is the sequence, the difficulty curve and the timing of exposure — curriculum decisions rather than pastoral ones. Entrustable Professional Activities acquire a psychological rationale on exactly this reading: progressive entrustment is a machine for manufacturing calibrated mastery experiences at increasing levels of consequence. See [research/08-assessment-epas/ten-cate-epa-framework.md](/research/08-assessment-epas/ten-cate-epa-framework.md).

**The correction that matters most is about difficulty.** This note previously licensed a design in which every task is engineered to be survivable and the difficulty is felt rather than real. Bandura forecloses that: "If people experience only easy successes they come to expect quick results and are easily discouraged by failure" (p. 3). The programme's productive-failure and error-management commitments are on the right side of this — see [research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md](/research/04-professional-formation/sinha-kapur-productive-failure.md) and [research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md](/research/04-professional-formation/keith-frese-error-management-training.md) — and this note should no longer be read as pulling against them. Bandura's constraint on genuine difficulty is about *frequency and timing*, not magnitude: avoid placing people where they "are likely to fail often", and avoid failure "before a sense of efficacy is firmly established" (p. 3).

**The correction with the sharpest assessment consequence is about optimism.** Bandura treats mild over-estimation as a resource and reserves accuracy for ventures where errors are costly or injurious (p. 12). A forward-deployed engineer's work spans both regimes inside a single week — speculative architecture exploration, where optimism is the fuel, and production change on a customer system, where it is a hazard. The teachable content is therefore neither "be accurate" nor "be confident" but **recognising which regime you are in**, and Bandura supplies the discriminator himself in the phrase "costly or injurious consequences".

**Collective efficacy is the addition that most changes what this source is for.** The programme's cohort structure, its shared substrate and its team-delivered engagements are all sites where a group can acquire or lose a belief in its joint capability, and Bandura's claim that beliefs of collective powerlessness are "more demoralizing and debilitating than are external impediments" (p. 38) is a direct warning about how such a cohort fails. His underminers list — diffuse accountability, long delays between action and visible result, factional interest, dependence on systems nobody understands — is also a serviceable diagnostic for the customer organisations graduates will be deployed into, and the joint-capability definition at p. 33 reads close to a job description for the role.

Finally, the efficacy-versus-outcome distinction sharpens a diagnosis the course needs constantly, and the 1995 wording sharpens it further. A student who will not attempt an unfamiliar retrieval-evaluation problem may believe the technique works perfectly well and simply not believe they can execute it; the intervention is a scaffolded success, not more evidence that the technique works. Bandura's stronger version is that the student will not have weighed the option at all — "Such wholesale exclusions of large classes of options are made rapidly on perceived efficacy grounds" (p. 23) — so an absence of objection is not evidence of agreement.

## How to teach it

- **Lecture point:** Teach the four sources, then audit the course's own practices against them. Ask students which source each element of their prior education relied on. Most will find it was persuasion. Then give them Bandura's correction — that persuasion's real work is structuring the situation, not delivering the appraisal — and ask what that would have looked like instead.
- **Lecture point:** Teach the efficacy/outcome distinction using a concrete engineering example — a team that agrees an evaluation harness would help and still does not build one. Then escalate to the p. 23 version: the harness was never on the agenda, and nobody noticed its absence.
- **Lecture point (new, and the one the previous version of this note got wrong):** Teach the asymmetry of persuasion, and teach that only-easy-success is itself a failure mode. Bandura's two sentences at p. 3 do the work on their own.
- **Seminar discussion:** Set Bandura's optimism argument (pp. 12–13) against Oettingen's East Berlin data (pp. 163–166) and ask students to identify which parts of their own future work fall inside the "costly or injurious consequences" carve-out. This is the most useful thing in the volume for this programme, and it resolves to a judgement rather than a rule.
- **Lab exercise:** Construct a difficulty-graded ladder of tasks in one technical area — for example building an eval set for a retrieval system. Require students to record a task-specific efficacy rating before each rung and their actual outcome after. Over several weeks this produces individual calibration data. Follow Zimmerman: rate *before* performance (p. 204), and make at least some rungs genuinely hard, because persistence measured on easy tasks inverts (pp. 206–207).
- **Studio exercise:** Design a live client-facing simulation with a real audience and a real time limit. Debrief on the felt experience as well as the technical outcome, and — following Bandura at p. 5 — ask explicitly whether students read their own arousal as energising or debilitating.
- **Assessment idea:** Score the **attribution**, not the confidence. After a failed or partial task, ask the student to account for the failure and record whether they name effort, acquirable knowledge, situational conditions or ability. Bandura treats that split as the efficacy signature (pp. 7, 11), and unlike a confidence rating it is a behaviour a supervisor can observe.
- **Assessment idea:** In EPA entrustment decisions, require the supervisor to note which source of efficacy information the student's readiness rests on. Entrustment justified only by the student's assertion of confidence should not clear the bar — "Saying something should not be confused with believing it to be so" (p. 11).
- **Assessment idea (retained, but now with a warning):** Calibration measures — comparing pre-task efficacy ratings against post-task performance — remain useful *as data*. **Do not grade on them.** The previous version of this note proposed marking down over-confidence and under-confidence alike; Bandura's position is that penalising optimism removes an adaptive resource, and Oettingen's East Berlin cohort is what that intervention looks like at scale. Use calibration data diagnostically, and grade calibration only where the task class carries "costly or injurious consequences".
- **Cohort-level exercise (new):** Take Bandura's underminers of collective efficacy (pp. 36–38) and have teams audit a real customer organisation, or the programme itself, against them. Diffuse accountability and long action-to-evidence delay are both measurable.

## Limits and cautions

**The namespace rule on magnitudes, which is not a verification rule.** This note now records several effect sizes from the 1995 volume, most substantially Multon, Brown and Lent's *r* = .34 via Zimmerman and Oettingen's *r* = .90. **None of them may be asserted in the wiki, and reading the volume in full does not change that.** [concepts/namespaces.md](/concepts/namespaces.md) states the constraint for the `pedagogy` namespace as "Direction and mechanism only. No effect size, ever", and adds that "A `pedagogy` claim may not carry a magnitude into any other namespace" — a rule that is "absolute and applies to vendor material exactly as to research literature". **This is a rule about what a learning-science claim licenses, not about how well the source was read.** A perfectly verified magnitude from a perfectly read source is still prohibited. The magnitudes are recorded above so that a future reader can see what the source says, and so that the prohibition is legible as a choice rather than as an accident of access. See also [wiki/program/09-Durable-and-Perishable-Register.md](/wiki/program/09-Durable-and-Perishable-Register.md).

**The 1977 paper is still unread**, and eight of the items listed under `## Key claims, findings, and specifics` rest on it alone. Any wiki page needing one of those claims specifically — rather than its 1995 restatement — must continue to declare it unverified.

**The 1995 volume is a conference proceedings and its editor is the field's founder.** It reports no search strategy, no inclusion criteria and, across Bandura's forty-five pages, no study whose result went the wrong way. Several load-bearing results are cited as unpublished manuscripts or conference papers. This is the same structural problem recorded for the Edmondson narrative review at [research/04-professional-formation/edmondson-psychological-safety.md](/research/04-professional-formation/edmondson-psychological-safety.md), and it has the same consequence: **the volume is good evidence about what the theory claims and weak evidence about how well the theory holds.**

**Extrapolating to professional engineering formation is an inference, not a finding.** The volume's empirical settings are schools, families, clinics, migration and addiction treatment. It contains no study of adult professionals learning a technical craft, and the closest analogue — Hackett on career choice — concerns which occupations people consider, not how well they perform in them. The 1977 evidence base is narrower still, being clinical throughout.

**Self-efficacy is almost always measured by self-report**, usually immediately before a performance that is itself often self-reported or rated by the same teacher who supplies the comparison grade. Zimmerman's antecedent-measurement argument fixes the temporal ordering but not the shared-method problem. Behavioural indicators are preferable where available, and self-report remains vulnerable to social desirability and to the very miscalibration the course wants to detect.

**Directionality is not settled in field settings**, and the volume concedes it in two places — Bandura on the bidirectionality of mood and efficacy (p. 10), Schneewind on cross-lagged causal inference (pp. 130–131). Efficacy predicts performance, but past performance also produces efficacy, and disentangling the two outside the laboratory is difficult.

**High self-efficacy is not uniformly good — but Bandura's boundary is not the one this note previously drew.** He does not say efficacy should stay grounded in demonstrated capability; for most ventures he says the opposite, and restricts the demand for accuracy to activities "in which mistakes can produce costly or injurious consequences" (p. 12) and to exaggeration that is gross rather than mild. The over-reach hazard in safety-relevant or client-facing work is real, and it is Bandura's own carve-out — but it must be stated as a carve-out and not as the general rule.

**The construct is frequently conflated in practice with growth mindset, grit and generic confidence-building.** Those are separate literatures with separate and generally weaker evidence bases, and this repository keeps them separate. Zimmerman's chapter closes by arguing for the construct's empirical distinctiveness from related constructs, which is the volume's own acknowledgement that the conflation is a live risk.

**Self-efficacy research shares the replication-era problems of mid-century social psychology.** The four-source taxonomy is well established and widely reproduced; effect sizes from individual efficacy-manipulation studies deserve more caution, and Oettingen's *r* = .90 in particular is described by its own author as "unusually high" and rests on a subgroup split three ways by ability.

**The volume is thirty-one years old and its central case study is a country that no longer exists.** Oettingen's East Berlin data were collected on a school system abolished before publication, and Bandura's framing chapter is organised around 1990s anxieties — the information era, mass migration, transnational interdependence. The theory is durable; the illustrations are not, and none should be presented to students as contemporary.

**One claim in this note is unverifiable from either work.** The approximate citation count for the 1977 paper was taken from PsycNet at an unrecorded date and has not been rechecked.

### Where the previous synthesis-only note was contradicted by the volume

The previous version of this note was written from the APA abstract without opening any Bandura text. Its four-source taxonomy and its citation metadata were correct. The following were not, and three of them had propagated into the design's teaching guidance.

**1. The claim that efficacy is built by objectively safe experiences.** The previous note said: *"Bandura is specific that the productive path runs through activities that are subjectively threatening but in fact relatively safe... The threat must be real enough to be felt and the safety must be real enough that the person actually succeeds. Efficacy is built by surviving something that felt risky, not by being reassured that nothing is risky."* And in `## What to remember`: *"The productive zone is subjectively threatening and objectively safe."* **The 1995 volume contradicts the design principle this licensed.** Bandura writes that "**If people experience only easy successes they come to expect quick results and are easily discouraged by failure. A resilient sense of efficacy requires experience in overcoming obstacles through perseverant effort. Some difficulties and setbacks in human pursuits serve a useful purpose in teaching that success usually requires sustained effort**" (p. 3). Oettingen adds that "Successes achieved in the face of adversities are particularly beneficial" (p. 149). The quoted phrase may well be faithful to the 1977 paper, whose setting is phobia treatment, where objective safety is the point. **As a general principle of curriculum design it is wrong, and the previous note stated it as one.** What survives is the timing constraint: Bandura warns against failure "before a sense of efficacy is firmly established" and against situations where people "are likely to fail often" (pp. 3–4).

**2. The claim that Bandura's framing is about mastery-grounded belief rather than maximal belief.** The previous note said: *"High self-efficacy is not uniformly good. Efficacy beliefs that exceed actual capability produce over-reach, and in safety-relevant or client-facing work that is a hazard rather than a virtue. Bandura's framing is about mastery-grounded belief, not maximal belief."* **Bandura's actual position is close to the reverse.** "**When people err in their self-appraisal they tend to overestimate their capabilities. This is a benefit rather than a cognitive failing or character flaw to be eradicated**" (p. 12); "**If not unrealistically exaggerated**, such personal beliefs foster positive well-being and human accomplishments" (p. 13); "Realists may adapt well to existing realities. But those with a tenacious self-efficacy are likely to change those realities" (p. 13). The over-reach hazard is Bandura's own, but he confines it to "Activities in which mistakes can produce costly or injurious consequences" (p. 12). The previous note took a carve-out and stated it as the rule.

**3. The assessment recommendation that followed from (2).** The previous `## How to teach it` said: *"Assess calibration rather than confidence. Compare pre-task efficacy ratings against post-task performance and score the accuracy of the student's self-prediction. Over-confidence and under-confidence are both marked down."* **On Bandura's account this is an intervention that removes an adaptive resource**, and the volume contains something close to a natural experiment on it: Oettingen's East Berlin schools made accurate self-appraisal an explicit goal and attached social reward to it, with the result that "the East Berlin school context was successful in forcing less intelligent students to turn their initial performance optimism into a more negative self-view" (p. 165). The recommendation has been rewritten rather than deleted, because calibration data remain diagnostically useful; what has been removed is grading on them outside the costly-or-injurious carve-out.

**4. "Telling people they can do it is the weakest intervention Bandura describes."** Contradicted by the enumeration itself: social persuasion is the **third** way, physiological and emotional states the **fourth** (pp. 4–5). The previous note also missed the two things Bandura says that make persuasion non-trivial — the asymmetry between instilling and undermining, and that "Successful efficacy builders do more than convey positive appraisals" by structuring situations and setting the comparison standard (p. 4).

**5. "Efficacy expectations determine three things: whether coping behaviour is initiated, how much effort is expended, and how long effort is sustained."** The 1995 formulation has **four** terms and leads with the one the previous note omitted: "**They determine the goals people set for themselves**, how much effort they expend, how long they persevere in the face of difficulties, and their resilience to failures" (p. 8). Zimmerman's summary of the 1977 paper also gives a third term the previous note did not have — "level of effort, persistence, and **choice of activities**" (p. 204).

**6. "Self-efficacy is task-specific and situation-specific, not a general personality trait."** Overstated. Bandura writes that "Affective states can have widely generalized effects on beliefs of personal efficacy in diverse spheres of functioning" (p. 5), and Oettingen attributes to Bandura 1977 and 1986 the claim that "A strong sense of efficacy acquired in one area of functioning may transfer to other areas, **thus creating a general sense of personal efficacy**" (pp. 149–150). Domain-specificity is the default and is what the measures assume; it is not absolute.

**7. "Magnitude" as the name of the first dimension.** The word does not appear in the volume. Zimmerman gives "**level**, generality, and strength" (p. 203). The previous note flagged the three dimensions as provisional, which was correct; the specific term was probably wrong.

**8. Collective efficacy was absent entirely.** This is the volume's principal contribution, occupies roughly the last quarter of Bandura's chapter, and is the part of the theory most directly applicable to the programme's team-delivered work. Its absence was not an error of the previous note — the 1977 paper predates the construct — but it was a substantial gap in what the repository knew Bandura to claim.
