## What this source is
A discussion paper arguing that the standard psychometric conception of validity is the wrong instrument for judging work-based assessment, and proposing a constructivist-interpretivist alternative in its place. It is the theoretical foundation for narrative, judgement-based assessment in professional education — the paper that explains why a supervisor's structured written account of what a trainee did can be more valid, not less, than a numerical rating scale, and what standards such evidence must meet to be trusted.

## The argument in full

The paper begins from an acknowledged tension. Work-based assessment may come closest to assessing *habitual performance* — what a professional actually does in the real conditions of their work, rather than what they can demonstrate in a controlled examination. That is precisely why it is attractive. Yet its use for summative purposes, for decisions that count, remains disputed. The authors locate the source of that dispute precisely: most criticism of work-based assessment stems from approaches to validity consistent with the quantitative psychometric framework. The complaints are the familiar ones — inter-rater reliability is low, ratings are inconsistent across assessors and occasions, and the measurement does not behave like a measurement should.

The authors' response is not to defend work-based assessment against those criticisms on their own terms. It is to challenge the terms. They argue that there is increasing research evidence indicating that the assumptions underlying the predictive, deterministic framework of psychometrics may no longer hold. If the assumptions fail, then the meaningfulness and appropriateness of current validity evidence can itself be called into question. What follows is that alternative strategies are needed — approaches to assessment and to validity inquiry that build on current theories of learning and performance in complex and dynamic workplace settings, rather than on a measurement model imported from psychological testing.

The theoretical work of the paper draws on research across various professional fields to outline key issues in the mechanisms of learning, competence and performance in complex social environments. Reviewing recent socio-cultural learning theory alongside research on performance and performance interpretation in work settings, the authors reach the claim that carries the argument: learning, competence as inferred from performance, and performance interpretations are all inherently **contextualised**, and can only be understood *in situ*. This is a strong statement and its implications are far-reaching. If competence is context-bound, then a rating that abstracts performance away from its context has discarded the information that made it interpretable. Two assessors observing the same trainee in different circumstances are not producing unreliable measurements of one underlying quantity; they are producing valid interpretations of different situated performances.

From this the paper draws its central reframing: **assessment in the context of work settings may be more usefully viewed as a socially situated interpretive act.** The assessor is not a measuring instrument that happens to be noisy. The assessor is an expert participant making an informed interpretation, and the variability between assessors carries information rather than merely error. This inverts the standard diagnosis. Where psychometrics sees a reliability problem to be engineered away through more raters, more occasions and better-anchored scales, the interpretive view sees multiple perspectives that should be preserved and reconciled through judgement.

The paper then proposes **constructivist-interpretivist approaches** to work-based assessment as the means of capturing and understanding contextualised learning and performance in work settings. But the authors do not leave the matter there, and this is what separates the paper from a general argument for subjectivity. Theoretical assumptions underlying interpretivist assessment approaches call for a validity theory that provides the theoretical framework and conceptual tools to guide the validation process in qualitative assessment inquiry. In other words, abandoning psychometric validity does not mean abandoning validity. It means importing the standards appropriate to the method.

Those standards already exist. The authors observe that basic principles of rigour specific to qualitative research have been established, and that they can and should be used to determine validity in interpretivist assessment approaches. Used properly, these strategies generate **trustworthy evidence** — the qualitative-research term of art — which is what is needed to develop the validity argument in work-based assessment. The payoff claimed is that this approach allows for in-depth and meaningful information about professional competence, which numerical ratings, whatever their reliability, do not deliver.

The overall shape of the argument is therefore: work-based assessment is valuable because it captures habitual performance; it is criticised on psychometric grounds; those grounds rest on assumptions that do not hold for situated professional performance; the appropriate response is an interpretivist approach; and interpretivist approaches have their own established, non-optional rigour criteria which must be applied. The final clause is the one that keeps this from being a licence for unaccountable subjectivity, and it is the clause most often dropped when the paper is cited.

## Structure of the original
The abstract is structured in three parts, which correspond to the paper's organisation:
- **Context** — the value of work-based assessment for habitual performance; the dispute over summative use; the psychometric origin of the criticisms; the claim that psychometric assumptions may no longer hold; the call for alternative strategies
- **Methods** — review of research across professional fields on learning, competence and performance in complex social environments; socio-cultural learning theory; research on performance and performance interpretation in work settings; the conclusion that these are inherently contextualised and understandable only in situ; the reframing of assessment as a socially situated interpretive act
- **Discussion** — the proposal of constructivist-interpretivist approaches; the requirement for a validity theory suited to qualitative assessment inquiry; the availability and applicability of established qualitative rigour principles; the generation of trustworthy evidence for the validity argument; the payoff of in-depth, meaningful information about professional competence

## Method and evidence base
This is explicitly a discussion or position paper. Its evidence base is a review of theoretical and empirical literature drawn from various professional fields, principally socio-cultural learning theory and research on performance and its interpretation in work settings. It does not report new data and makes no empirical claims of its own about assessment outcomes. Its force is argumentative: it derives an assessment stance from a theory of situated competence and then imposes an accountability requirement on that stance by importing qualitative rigour criteria. The main limitation is the one common to position papers — the argument that psychometric assumptions "may no longer hold" is asserted with reference to a body of evidence rather than demonstrated within the paper, and the reader cannot evaluate the strength of that evidence from the abstract. The claim is also contested; a commentary published in the same issue indicates active disagreement in the field at the time. As a foundational statement of a position that has since shaped programmatic assessment practice widely, its influence is substantial — over 160 citations recorded — but influence is not the same as empirical validation of the position.

## Vocabulary the source introduces
- **Work-based assessment (WBA)** — assessment conducted in the actual work setting, on real tasks, under real conditions.
- **Habitual performance** — what a professional routinely does in practice, as distinct from what they can demonstrate under examination conditions.
- **Predictive, deterministic framework of psychometrics** — the measurement paradigm assuming a stable underlying trait that assessments estimate with error; the framework whose assumptions the paper challenges.
- **Socially situated interpretive act** — the paper's reframing of assessment: an informed interpretation made by a participant in a social context, not a measurement taken by an instrument.
- **Contextualised competence** — the claim that competence, and interpretations of performance, exist only in relation to their context and can only be understood *in situ*.
- **Constructivist-interpretivist assessment** — the proposed family of approaches suited to capturing contextualised learning and performance.
- **Trustworthiness** — the qualitative-research counterpart to reliability and validity; the standard that interpretivist assessment evidence must meet.
- **Principles of rigour specific to qualitative research** — established criteria that the authors argue can and should determine validity in interpretivist assessment.
- **Validity argument** — the accumulated, reasoned case that an assessment supports its intended interpretation and use.

## What to remember
- Work-based assessment's claim to superiority is that it assesses **habitual performance** — real work, real conditions.
- The standard criticisms of it are psychometric in origin, and the paper's move is to challenge whether the psychometric framework's assumptions apply.
- **Competence and its interpretation are contextualised and can only be understood in situ.** This is the load-bearing theoretical claim.
- **Assessment in work settings is best viewed as a socially situated interpretive act**, not as measurement.
- Rater variability is reconceived as legitimate multiple perspectives rather than as pure error.
- Abandoning psychometric validity does **not** mean abandoning rigour. Qualitative rigour criteria exist, are established, and **should** be applied.
- The output standard is **trustworthy evidence** supporting a validity argument, yielding in-depth, meaningful information about competence.
- This is a discussion paper. It argues a position; it does not test one.

## Why it matters for the course
This paper supplies the theoretical licence for the assessment model this curriculum is built on. A forward-deployed engineering course cannot assess what matters through examinations. Judgement about when a system is ready to ship, calibration about what a model can and cannot be trusted to do, restraint in the face of a customer request that should be refused, the decision to escalate rather than to press on — none of these are examinable in the conventional sense, and all of them are exactly the situated, context-dependent competences Govaerts and van der Vleuten describe. If assessment of such competences is going to happen at all, it will happen in the work setting, through informed interpretation by an observer.

The paper is also the answer to the objection that Entrustable Professional Activities are "just subjective." Under the psychometric framework, a supervisor's narrative judgement scores badly: low inter-rater agreement, no scale, no error term. Under the interpretivist framework, that narrative is the evidence, and the disagreement between two supervisors is data about two different situated performances rather than noise. The EPA model with its narrative entrustment rationale is a direct instantiation of this stance.

But the discipline the paper imposes is what the course must actually implement, and it is the harder part. Interpretivist assessment is not permission to grade on impression. Trustworthiness has established criteria, and a course adopting narrative assessment owes an account of how it meets them — how observations are triangulated across assessors and occasions, how interpretations are checked, how the audit trail from observation to entrustment decision is maintained, and how the reasoning is made inspectable. A programme that adopts narrative assessment without those mechanisms has taken the licence and left the obligation, and it will produce exactly the unaccountable judgement that critics predict. The instruction this paper gives the course is: use narrative judgement, and build the rigour apparatus that makes it defensible.

## How to teach it
- **Lecture point:** Set out the psychometric critique of work-based assessment fairly and in full before presenting the counter-argument. The counter-argument is not persuasive unless the critique is understood.
- **Lecture point:** Teach the contextualisation claim directly, and draw out its consequence — that abstracting performance from context destroys the information needed to interpret it.
- **Lecture point:** Teach the reframe of rater disagreement from error to perspective, and be explicit that this is a theoretical stance with live opposition, not a settled result.
- **Lecture point:** Emphasise the rigour requirement as strongly as the interpretivist proposal. Interpretivism without trustworthiness criteria is not what this paper argues for.
- **Lab exercise:** Have students assess the same recorded engineering interaction twice — once with a numerical rubric, once with structured narrative — then compare what each captured and what each lost.
- **Studio exercise:** Have students write entrustment narratives about each other's work and then subject those narratives to qualitative rigour criteria: is the observation described, is the interpretation distinguishable from the observation, is the reasoning traceable?
- **Studio exercise:** Deliberately surface a disagreement between two assessors about the same student performance and require the pair to reconcile it in writing without averaging. Averaging is the psychometric reflex; reconciliation is the interpretive skill.
- **Assessment idea:** Require every entrustment decision in the course to carry a written narrative rationale that separates what was observed from what was inferred.
- **Assessment idea:** Assess students on their own capacity to assess — an EPA for giving a defensible, evidence-linked judgement of a peer's work. This is a genuine professional competence for anyone who will eventually lead engagements.

## Limits and cautions
- This is a **discussion paper**, not an empirical study. It argues for a position and does not demonstrate that interpretivist assessment produces better decisions.
- The central premise — that psychometric assumptions "may no longer hold" — is asserted with reference to a literature rather than demonstrated in the paper. The abstract does not permit evaluation of that evidence.
- The position is contested. A commentary published in the same issue of *Medical Education* indicates active disagreement at the time of publication.
- Interpretivist assessment can degrade into unaccountable subjectivity if the qualitative rigour criteria are not actually implemented. The paper requires them; adopters frequently omit them.
- The evidence base and framing are from medical education. Transfer to engineering education is plausible given the shared characteristics of workplace-based professional formation, but it is a transfer and should be stated as such.
- Narrative assessment is considerably more labour-intensive than rating scales. The paper does not address cost, scalability or assessor training requirements.
- Bias in narrative judgement — including demographic bias — is a serious known risk in workplace-based assessment and is not addressed in the abstract. A course adopting this model needs a bias-mitigation strategy that this paper does not supply.
- The paper is from 2013. Subsequent programmatic-assessment literature has developed these ideas substantially and should be consulted for implementation guidance.
