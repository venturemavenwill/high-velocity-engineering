## What this source is
"Nuts and bolts of entrustable professional activities" is a short, widely cited practical explainer of the EPA concept aimed at programme directors implementing competency-based assessment. Because no abstract exists and the full text could not be retrieved, the substantive content of this note is grounded on the verified abstract of AMEE Guide No. 99, ten Cate's much fuller treatment of the same framework two years later. EPAs are units of professional practice that can be entrusted to a trainee once sufficient competence has been demonstrated, and the framework's central move is to convert assessment from rating abstract competencies into making explicit decisions about supervised autonomy.

## The argument in full

The problem EPAs address is a failure mode of competency-based education. Competency frameworks decompose professional practice into abstract qualities — communication, professionalism, systems-based practice, medical knowledge — and then ask supervisors to rate trainees on them. Supervisors find this difficult and often meaningless, because they do not observe communication in the abstract; they observe a trainee conducting a handover, or breaking bad news, or arguing for a treatment plan. The competencies are attributes of the person; what is actually observed and what actually matters organisationally are units of work.

The EPA framework inverts the object of assessment. Instead of rating attributes, it identifies discrete **units of professional practice** — the actual tasks a professional performs — and asks a different question about each: can this trainee be entrusted to perform this task, and with how much supervision? This reframing has three consequences that AMEE Guide No. 99 develops in detail. Assessment becomes about tasks rather than traits. The judgement becomes a decision about permitted autonomy rather than a score. And the decision has a consequence in the real world, since granting entrustment changes what the trainee is allowed to do unsupervised.

AMEE Guide No. 99 was written to support educators building a competency-based workplace curriculum, and it lays out the operational sequence. It introduces the concepts and definitions related to EPAs, then gives guidance on their **identification, elaboration and validation** — the work of deciding which units of practice count as EPAs for a given programme, specifying each one in enough detail to be assessable, and validating the resulting set. The Guide explicitly devotes attention to **clarifying common misunderstandings about EPAs**, which is a signal that by 2015 the concept was being widely adopted and widely misapplied. The most common misapplication is treating EPAs as a renamed list of competencies rather than as units of work.

The relationship between EPAs and competencies is handled through a **matrix-mapping approach**, in which EPAs are mapped against competencies rather than replacing them. This is an important structural point. EPAs and competencies are not competing frameworks; each EPA draws on multiple competencies, and each competency contributes to multiple EPAs. The matrix makes that many-to-many relationship explicit, allowing a programme to check that its EPA set covers the competency framework it is accountable to. The Guide relates this to existing concepts including **competency milestones**.

A specific section of the Guide is devoted to **entrustment decision-making as an inextricable part of working with EPAs**. This is the framework's core and the part most often diluted. In using EPAs, assessment in the workplace is translated into entrustment decision-making for **designated levels of permitted autonomy, ranging from acting under full supervision to providing supervision to a junior learner**. That range is the verified statement, and its two poles matter: the bottom of the scale is not "cannot do it" but "acts under full supervision," and the top is not "acts alone" but "supervises others." Supervising a junior is treated as a distinct and higher form of entrustment than performing the task independently, which reflects how professional responsibility actually escalates.

The Guide's final section addresses the use of technology, including **mobile devices and electronic portfolios**, to support feedback to trainees about their progress and to support entrustment decision-making by programme directors or clinical teams. This is not incidental. Entrustment decisions rest on accumulated observations gathered across many encounters and many assessors, and without an infrastructure to collect and surface that record, entrustment reverts to the impression of whoever happens to be in the room. The Guide's funding under the EU WATCHME project, which concerned workplace learning technology, reflects this emphasis.

Taken together, the framework's appeal for professional formation is that it makes a judgement that was always being made — how much do we let this person do alone — into an explicit, evidenced, documented decision, rather than leaving it implicit and unaccountable.

## Method and evidence base
Neither source is an empirical study. "Nuts and bolts" is a two-page editorial or practical commentary. AMEE Guide No. 99 is a guidance document: an overview of the existing literature combined with practical recommendations for implementation, produced under a European research project on workplace learning technology. The evidence base for the EPA framework as a whole is therefore primarily conceptual and experiential rather than experimental. There are no randomised comparisons of EPA-based against conventional assessment, and the framework's widespread adoption across medical education reflects perceived practical fit rather than demonstrated superiority in outcome trials. That is a genuine limitation for a curriculum that intends to build its assessment model on this foundation, and it should be stated plainly rather than obscured by the framework's popularity. The framework's strongest theoretical support comes from the adjacent literature on work-based assessment validity — see the Govaerts and van der Vleuten note in this folder — which supplies the argument for why judgement-based, situated assessment can be valid on its own terms.

## Vocabulary the source introduces
- **Entrustable Professional Activity (EPA)** — a unit of professional practice that can be entrusted to a trainee once sufficient competence has been demonstrated. The precise definitional wording used by ten Cate is **not verified** here; this gloss is the standard summary.
- **Entrustment decision** — the explicit judgement to permit a trainee to perform an EPA at a designated level of supervision; described in AMEE Guide No. 99 as inextricable from working with EPAs.
- **Levels of permitted autonomy** — the designated supervision levels at which an EPA may be entrusted, **verified as ranging from acting under full supervision to providing supervision to a junior learner**.
- **Identification, elaboration and validation** — the three-stage process of developing an EPA set for a programme.
- **Matrix mapping** — the technique of relating EPAs to competencies in a many-to-many grid, ensuring coverage without conflating the two.
- **Competency milestones** — existing developmental markers within competency frameworks, related to but distinct from EPAs.
- **Electronic portfolio** — the accumulated documented record of observations supporting entrustment decision-making.

## What to remember
- EPAs are **units of work**, not attributes of people. This distinction is the whole framework.
- The assessment question changes from "how competent is this person" to **"what may this person be entrusted to do, and with how much supervision."**
- The supervision range is verified as running **from acting under full supervision to providing supervision to a junior learner.** Supervising others is the top of the scale, not independent practice.
- EPAs do **not** replace competencies; they are mapped against them in a matrix, many-to-many.
- Entrustment decision-making is inextricable from EPAs, not an optional add-on.
- Documentation infrastructure — portfolios, mobile capture — is treated as part of the framework, because entrustment rests on accumulated observation across assessors.
- AMEE Guide No. 99 explicitly exists partly to correct **common misunderstandings**, the chief one being treating EPAs as relabelled competencies.
- The evidence base is conceptual and practical, not experimental. Adoption is widespread; controlled outcome evidence is not.

## Why it matters for the course
The EPA framework is the assessment backbone this curriculum has adopted, and the reason is that it solves a problem no examination can. A forward-deployed engineer's readiness is not a score; it is a set of specific things an organisation is willing to let them do without supervision — run a customer discovery session alone, sign off that an evaluation is adequate, decide that a system is not ready to ship, take a production incident. Those are units of work, and phrasing them as EPAs makes the readiness decision explicit and evidenced rather than implicit and political.

The supervision range is directly usable. A course can specify, for each EPA, what observed evidence is required to move a student from acting under full supervision to acting with supervision available, and then to acting independently, and finally to supervising a junior. The top level is particularly valuable for a professional-formation curriculum, because it captures something a technical assessment misses entirely: the transition from competent practitioner to someone who can vouch for another's work. Building that level into the course design gives senior students a defined and assessable role.

The matrix-mapping point protects the course from a specific failure. It would be easy to write a list of EPAs that is really a list of competencies with different labels — "demonstrates good judgement," "communicates effectively." AMEE Guide No. 99 warns against precisely this, and the discipline is to check that every named EPA is something a person could actually be sent to do on a Tuesday. Judgement, calibration, resilience and collaboration — the dispositions this course cares about — should appear in the competency dimension of the matrix, evidenced *through* EPAs, not as EPAs themselves.

The documentation requirement should also be taken seriously rather than treated as administrative overhead. Entrustment rests on accumulated observation across multiple assessors and occasions, which is exactly the triangulation that the Govaerts and van der Vleuten argument requires for trustworthy interpretive assessment. The portfolio is the rigour mechanism, not the paperwork.

## How to teach it
- **Lecture point:** Teach the units-of-work versus attributes distinction first and test it immediately, by having students classify a mixed list into EPAs and competencies.
- **Lecture point:** Present the supervision range with its two verified poles and be explicit that the top level is supervising others, not working alone.
- **Lecture point:** Teach matrix mapping as the mechanism that keeps EPAs and competencies distinct while ensuring coverage.
- **Lecture point:** Be honest that the framework's evidence base is conceptual and practical rather than experimental, and connect it to the Govaerts and van der Vleuten argument for why that can still be defensible.
- **Lab exercise:** Have students draft EPAs for their own intended role, then apply a hard test to each: could you write this on a work ticket and hand it to someone? Anything that fails the test is a competency.
- **Studio exercise:** Run entrustment panels. A small group of staff reviews a student's accumulated evidence for one EPA and reaches a documented supervision-level decision with written rationale.
- **Studio exercise:** Have advanced students supervise juniors on an EPA they have been entrusted at the top level, and assess the supervision itself.
- **Assessment idea:** Make the portfolio the assessment substrate. Every entrustment decision cites specific observed instances, names the assessors, and records the reasoning.
- **Assessment idea:** Require students to self-assess their supervision level for each EPA before the panel meets, and treat the gap between self-assessment and panel decision as a calibration measure in its own right.

## Limits and cautions
- **The primary source could not be read.** "Nuts and bolts" has no abstract and its full text was not retrievable. The content of this note derives from AMEE Guide No. 99 and should be read accordingly.
- **The five-level supervision scale commonly attributed to ten Cate is not verified here.** The commonly cited levels are: (1) observe only, no permission to act; (2) act under direct, proactive supervision present in the room; (3) act under indirect, reactive supervision readily available; (4) act unsupervised; (5) provide supervision to a junior learner. **The AMEE Guide abstract verifies only the *range* — from acting under full supervision to providing supervision to a junior learner — not the existence or wording of five discrete levels.** Treat the five-level formulation as provisional.
- **The formal definition of an EPA is not verified.** The gloss given above is a standard summary, not ten Cate's exact wording.
- **The EPA-versus-competency distinction as stated in "Nuts and bolts" is not verified**, since that text was unavailable.
- No controlled evidence that EPA-based assessment produces better outcomes than alternatives. The framework's authority rests on adoption and conceptual coherence.
- Both sources are from medical education, where supervision structures, regulatory accountability and patient-safety stakes differ substantially from engineering. Transfer is plausible but is a transfer.
- EPA implementation is administratively heavy. Identification, elaboration, validation, panel processes and portfolio infrastructure all carry real cost that the abstracts do not quantify.
- Entrustment decisions inherit the bias risks of all workplace judgement, and neither source's abstract addresses bias mitigation.
- The concept is frequently misapplied. AMEE Guide No. 99's explicit inclusion of a misunderstanding-clarification section is itself evidence of how commonly this happens.
