# Cliff Notes — Edmondson, Psychological Safety and Learning Behavior in Work Teams

## Citation and access
- **Authors:** Amy C. Edmondson
- **Title:** Psychological Safety and Learning Behavior in Work Teams
- **Venue and year:** *Administrative Science Quarterly*, 44(2), 350–383, June 1999
- **DOI:** 10.2307/2666999
- **Source consulted:** Semantic Scholar Graph API record for the paper (full official abstract); green open-access copy under CC BY at Harvard DASH, http://nrs.harvard.edu/urn-3:HUL.InstRepos:37968728
- **Access status:** The journal version is paywalled; an author copy is openly available through the Harvard institutional repository. This note is written from the official abstract. Semantic Scholar records over 10,000 citations.

## What this source is
The paper that defines team psychological safety as a research construct, distinguishes it from team efficacy, and shows that it predicts learning behaviour in real work teams. It is a multimethod field study of 51 teams in a single manufacturing company, and it establishes both the definition and the causal chain — psychological safety enables learning behaviour, and learning behaviour is what actually improves performance — that most subsequent work in this area builds on.

## The argument in full

Edmondson's starting problem is that teams in organisations are widely assumed to learn, and yet observably many do not. Teams face the same errors repeatedly, fail to raise problems that members privately recognise, and do not seek the feedback or help that would improve their work. The behaviours that constitute team learning — asking questions, seeking feedback, discussing errors, requesting help, experimenting — are all *interpersonally risky*. Each one exposes the person doing it to the possibility of appearing ignorant, incompetent, negative or disruptive. This is the key theoretical insight: learning behaviours are not merely cognitively demanding, they are socially costly, and the cost is borne by the individual while the benefit accrues to the team.

From this Edmondson derives the construct. Team psychological safety is defined as *a shared belief held by members of a team that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking*. Three features of that definition matter and are routinely lost when the term is used casually. It is a **shared** belief, a property of the team rather than of the individual, arising from common experience of the team's norms. It is specifically about **interpersonal** risk, not physical safety, job security or comfort. And it is about **risk taking** — the construct describes a context in which risks can be taken, not one in which risks are absent.

The paper's most important discriminant move is to test psychological safety alongside **team efficacy** — the team's shared belief in its own capability to perform. These two constructs are easy to conflate, both being shared team-level beliefs with positive connotations, and prior work had not cleanly separated them. Edmondson models their effects on learning and performance together. The result is that team psychological safety is associated with learning behaviour, but team efficacy is not, once psychological safety is controlled for. Believing the team is good at its job does not make members willing to admit they made a mistake. Believing the team is safe to speak up in does.

The second structural finding is mediational. Psychological safety does not affect team performance directly. It affects learning behaviour, and learning behaviour affects performance. Edmondson finds that learning behaviour mediates the relationship between team psychological safety and team performance. This matters practically because it identifies where an intervention has to land. Raising psychological safety without the team actually doing anything differently — without more questions, more error discussion, more help seeking — should not be expected to improve results. The observable behaviours are the mechanism, not a by-product.

The third contribution is on antecedents. Psychological safety is not a mood that teams happen to have; it is produced by identifiable conditions. Edmondson identifies **context support** — the resources and information the organisation provides the team — and **team leader coaching** as antecedents. The framing is deliberately integrative: the paper argues that both team structures and shared beliefs shape team outcomes, against accounts that privilege one or the other. This makes psychological safety a design variable rather than a matter of team personality, which is what makes it usable by anyone building a curriculum or an organisation.

## Key claims, findings, and specifics
- **Definition:** team psychological safety is "a shared belief held by members of a team that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking."
- Field study of **51 work teams in a single manufacturing company**, with antecedent, process and outcome variables measured.
- Multimethod design combining qualitative and quantitative evidence.
- Team psychological safety **is** associated with learning behaviour.
- Team efficacy is **not** associated with learning behaviour when team psychological safety is controlled for. This is the paper's key discriminant finding.
- **Learning behaviour mediates** the relationship between team psychological safety and team performance.
- Antecedents identified include **context support** and **team leader coaching**.
- The paper argues for an integrative view in which both team structures and shared beliefs shape outcomes.

## Method and evidence base
A multimethod field study within one organisation. The qualitative component — observation and interviews across teams — generated and illustrated the construct; the quantitative component measured psychological safety, team efficacy, learning behaviour and performance across 51 teams and tested the mediated model. The within-organisation design is a genuine strength for internal validity, because it holds constant industry, formal structure, reward systems and culture, so the variation in psychological safety across teams is not confounded by those factors. It is correspondingly a limit on external validity: a single manufacturing company in the 1990s. Fifty-one teams is a reasonable but not large sample for team-level analysis, and the mediation evidence is cross-sectional, so the causal ordering rests on theory rather than on temporal separation. Performance measures are organisation-specific. The paper is careful about these limits and the construct's subsequent survival across hundreds of studies in other settings is the real evidence for its generality — see the Frazier meta-analysis in this folder.

## Vocabulary the source introduces
- **Team psychological safety** — a shared belief held by members of a team that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking.
- **Interpersonal risk** — the exposure to appearing ignorant, incompetent, negative or disruptive that accompanies questions, error admission, help seeking and challenge.
- **Learning behaviour** — the observable team activities of seeking feedback, sharing information, asking for help, talking about errors and experimenting.
- **Team efficacy** — a team's shared belief in its own capability to perform its task; distinct from psychological safety and, in this study, not a predictor of learning behaviour once safety is controlled.
- **Context support** — organisational provision of resources and information to the team; an antecedent of psychological safety.
- **Team leader coaching** — leader behaviour that supports the team's work and norms; an antecedent of psychological safety.

## What to remember
- The definition is precise: a *shared* belief that the team is safe for *interpersonal risk taking*. It is not comfort, not niceness, and not the absence of challenge.
- Psychological safety and team efficacy are different things, and only the former predicted learning behaviour here. Confidence in the team's ability does not make people willing to admit error.
- The causal chain runs safety → learning behaviour → performance. Learning behaviour is the mediator and therefore the thing to observe and assess.
- Psychological safety has identifiable antecedents — context support and leader coaching — so it is a design variable, not a temperament.
- The evidence base is 51 teams in one manufacturing firm. Generality comes from later replication, not from this study alone.
- Because learning behaviours are observable, they are assessable in a way that beliefs are not.

## Why it matters for the course
This folder is deliberately sceptical of generic disposition training, and Edmondson survives that scepticism better than most of the constructs in its neighbourhood, for a specific reason: her theory terminates in *observable behaviour* rather than in self-reported attitude. The mediation finding tells the course exactly what to look at — are students asking questions, disclosing errors, requesting help, and challenging the plan — and those are things a supervisor can see and record. That maps directly onto the Entrustable Professional Activity model, where entrustment decisions must rest on observed performance rather than inferred disposition.

For forward-deployed engineering specifically, the interpersonal-risk framing explains a failure mode that shows up constantly in the field. An engineer who privately doubts a customer's stated requirement, or who has noticed that the evaluation set is contaminated, or who does not understand what a colleague just committed, faces exactly the risk Edmondson describes: raising it means looking ignorant or negative in front of a client or a senior. The competence the course needs to build is not merely the technical ability to notice the problem but the practised willingness to voice it, and the leadership behaviour to make voicing it safe for others. The efficacy discriminant is also directly relevant: a highly capable team that is confident in its own ability is not thereby a team that surfaces problems. Those are separate levers and the course should not treat competence-building as if it delivers candour for free.

## How to teach it
- **Lecture point:** Teach the definition word by word, especially "shared," "interpersonal" and "risk taking," and explicitly contrast it with comfort, agreeableness and low standards.
- **Lecture point:** Present the efficacy null result. It is the most surprising and most useful finding for a technical audience that tends to assume competence solves everything.
- **Lecture point:** Teach the mediation chain and draw the practical conclusion: if the behaviours do not change, the belief change is inert.
- **Lab exercise:** Instrument the observable learning behaviours in student teams. Count and timestamp help requests, error disclosures, and challenges to the plan. Report the data back to teams without grading it initially.
- **Studio exercise:** Structured error-disclosure practice. Each team session opens with every member reporting one thing they got wrong or do not understand since the last session, starting with the most senior person present.
- **Studio exercise:** Have students take turns as team lead and be assessed specifically on the antecedent behaviours — coaching and context support — rather than on their own technical output.
- **Assessment idea:** An EPA for "raising a concern that carries interpersonal cost." Requires observed instances of the student surfacing a problem to a more senior person or a client, with supervisor narrative on how it was handled.
- **Assessment idea:** Assess help-seeking latency: how long a student spends stuck before asking. Both extremes are miscalibrated.

## Limits and cautions
- Single organisation, single industry, 51 teams, 1990s. Internal validity is strong; external validity comes from later work.
- Cross-sectional measurement. The mediation model is theoretically ordered but not temporally identified; reverse and reciprocal causation are not excluded.
- Psychological safety is measured by survey self-report, which shares the weaknesses of all shared-perception measures including consensus bias.
- The construct has been heavily diluted in practice. Popular usage frequently equates it with comfort or conflict avoidance, which inverts the original meaning — Edmondson's teams take risks, they do not avoid them.
- The paper does not establish that interventions to raise psychological safety succeed, only that the construct predicts learning behaviour where it varies naturally.
- Psychological safety is not sufficient for performance. The mediated model requires learning behaviour to actually occur; safety without behaviour change predicts nothing.
- Effect sizes across the wider literature are moderate, and the Frazier meta-analysis in this folder should be consulted before asserting magnitude.
