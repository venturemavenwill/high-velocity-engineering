## What this source is

The 2019 book is Edmondson's own trade-press statement of a construct she introduced in academic work two decades earlier. It is three things at once, and they carry different weight. Part I is a definitional and historical account, including a chapter-length narrative review of roughly a hundred studies. Part II is a set of illustrative organisational case studies drawn almost entirely from secondary sources — published teaching cases, journalism, NTSB reports, a PBS documentary — covering Volkswagen, Wells Fargo, Nokia, the *Columbia* shuttle, Uber, Pixar, Barry-Wehmiller, DaVita, Anglo American and Fukushima Daini. Part III is a prescriptive leadership framework.

The book's most useful contribution for this repository is **Part I's negative definition**: a sustained, explicit account of what psychological safety is *not*. That material has no equivalent in the 1999 abstract, it is the part of the construct most often mangled second-hand, and it is now verified.

The 1999 paper is the empirical article that defined team psychological safety as a research construct, distinguished it from team efficacy, and tested a mediated model in 51 teams. **[1999, unread]**

## The argument in full

Edmondson's starting problem is that people at work routinely withhold what they know. The opening story is a neonatal nurse who notices that a prophylactic surfactant has not been ordered for premature twins, steps forward to say so, and stops — because she had recently overheard the neonatologist "publicly berate another nurse for questioning one of his orders" (p. 4). Edmondson's reading is that the nurse made "a quick, not entirely conscious, risk calculation" and, in doing so, engaged in "discounting the future — underweighting the more important issue of the patients' health, which would take some time to play out, and overweighting the importance of the doctor's possible response, which would happen immediately" (p. 4).

From this she generalises. "No one wakes up in the morning excited to go to work and look ignorant, incompetent, or disruptive. These are called interpersonal risks, and they are what nearly everyone seeks to avoid, not always consciously" (p. 5). The avoidance is learned early and becomes automatic: "Don't want to look ignorant? Don't ask questions. Don't want to look incompetent? Don't admit to mistakes or weaknesses. Don't want to be called disruptive? Don't make suggestions" (p. 5).

The definition follows: "I have defined psychological safety as **the belief that the work environment is safe for interpersonal risk taking**. The concept refers to the experience of feeling able to speak up with relevant ideas, questions, or concerns. Psychological safety is present when colleagues trust and respect each other and feel able — even obligated — to be candid" (p. 8). Note "obligated". Note also that this formulation says *work environment*, not *team*, and does not use the word *shared*.

She offers a looser gloss in the introduction: "Psychological safety is broadly defined as a climate in which people are comfortable expressing and being themselves. More specifically, when people have psychological safety at work, they feel comfortable sharing concerns and mistakes without fear of embarrassment or retribution" (p. xvi). This is the book's own internal tension, discussed under Limits below.

The unit of analysis is the group, not the individual and not the firm: "psychological safety seems to 'live' at the level of the group" (p. 11), and "even in a company with a strong corporate culture, you will find pockets of both high and low psychological safety" (p. 8).

The book's account of **silence** is its second substantial contribution. Silence is invisible: "Silence — when voice was possible — rarely announces itself! The moment passes, and no one is the wiser except the person who held back" (pp. 7–8). Its causes are not personality but a calculus. With Jim Detert she interviewed more than 230 employees in a multinational technology firm and derived what they call **implicit theories of voice** — "taken-for-granted rules" about when it is appropriate to speak to those higher up, tabulated at p. 33: do not criticise something the boss may have helped create; do not speak unless you have solid data; do not speak up if the boss's boss is present; do not say anything negative about the work in a group; speaking up brings career consequences. Underneath them sits the **asymmetry of voice and silence** (p. 34): voice benefits the organisation, after a delay, with low certainty; silence benefits oneself, immediately, with high certainty. Hence "no one was ever fired for silence" (p. 34). One technician explained his silence with "I have kids in college", then conceded on probing, "Oh, everyone knows we never fire anybody" — Edmondson's comment is that "he was admitting that his reticence to rock the boat with what he believed was a good idea was irrational, and deep down he understood that" (p. 32).

The **leadership framework** in Chapter 7 has three categories and seven tasks, tabulated at p. 159. *Setting the stage*: frame the work — "set expectations about failure, uncertainty, and interdependence to clarify the need for voice" — and emphasise purpose. *Inviting participation*: demonstrate situational humility, practise inquiry, set up structures and processes. *Responding productively*: express appreciation, destigmatise failure, and sanction clear violations. The three are illustrated through Julie Morath at Children's Hospital in Minneapolis, who reframed medical error as a systems property, introduced "blameless reporting", ran as many as eighteen focus groups, and opened with the question "Was everything as safe as you would like it to have been this week with your patients?" (p. 156). Edmondson's emphasis is on the question's construction — aspirational, concrete, and pointedly not "did you see lots of mistakes or harm?"

The framework's relationship to **accountability** is stated as a two-by-two, not as a trade-off. Psychological safety and performance standards are "two separate, equally important dimensions" (p. 18), giving four quadrants: apathy (low standards, low safety), comfort (low standards, high safety), anxiety (high standards, low safety) and the **learning and high-performance zone** (high standards, high safety). She is explicit about which one worries her: "What keeps me up at night is the lower right-hand quadrant... Managers in these organizations have unfortunately confused setting high standards with good management" (p. 19).

The **failure taxonomy** — which the book takes from her earlier writing rather than deriving here — has three archetypes (pp. 161–163): **preventable** failures, "deviations from known processes that produce unwanted outcomes", never good news; **complex** failures, "unique and novel combinations of events and actions that give rise to unwanted outcomes", still not good news; and **intelligent** failures, "novel forays into new territory that lead to unwanted outcomes", which "must be celebrated so as to encourage more of them". The prescription is that responses must differ by type (p. 180): training, retraining and system redesign for preventable; failure analysis from diverse perspectives for complex; failure parties, awards and design of the next experiment for intelligent. She reports an informal poll: asked what percentage of failures in their organisations should be considered blameworthy, managers answer "in single digits — perhaps 1% to 4%"; asked what percentage are *treated* as blameworthy, "they say (after a pause or a laugh) 70% to 90%" (p. 176).

Sanction is inside the model, not an exception to it. "Yes, firing can sometimes be an appropriate and productive response — to a blameworthy act. But won't this kill the psychological safety? No... psychological safety is reinforced rather than harmed by fair, thoughtful responses to potentially dangerous, harmful, or sloppy behavior" (p. 178).

## Key claims, findings, and specifics

**From the 2019 book, read in full.**

- **Definition:** "the belief that the work environment is safe for interpersonal risk taking" (p. 8). Broad gloss: "a climate in which people are comfortable expressing and being themselves" (p. xvi).
- **Not immunity:** "Psychological safety is not immunity from consequences, nor is it a state of high self-regard. In psychologically safe workplaces, people know they might fail, they might receive performance feedback that says they're not meeting expectations, and they might lose their jobs" (p. xv).
- **What it substitutes for fear:** in such a workplace people "fear holding back their full participation more than they fear sharing a potentially sensitive, threatening, or wrong idea" (p. xv).
- **Level of analysis:** it "seems to 'live' at the level of the group" (p. 11) and varies within organisations that have strong cultures (p. 8).
- **It is not sufficient:** "psychological safety takes off the brakes that keep people from achieving what's possible. But it's not the fuel that powers the car" (p. 21). "I do not see psychological safety as a panacea. Far from it" (p. 196). It is "better thought of as an enabler" (p. 197).
- **Standards and safety are orthogonal:** the two-by-two at p. 18, with the anxiety zone (high standards, low safety) named as the common and dangerous case (p. 19).
- **Discipline, not safety, limits chatter:** "I don't think you can have too much psychological safety. I do think, however, that you can have not enough discipline" (p. 196).
- **Measurement:** the seven-item survey from the dissertation is reproduced at p. 20 — three items reverse-scored, seven-point Likert. Robustness across item wordings and translations (German, Spanish, Russian, Japanese, Chinese, Korean) is asserted at p. 20 and documented in the appendix (pp. 213–215).
- **The accidental discovery:** in the mid-1990s medication-error study she found a "10-fold difference in the number of human errors per thousand patient days" across units (p. 9) and, to her alarm, a statistically significant correlation in the *wrong* direction — better teams appeared to make more errors. The resolution: "The good teams... don't make more mistakes; they report more" (p. 10). She then hired a research assistant blind to both the hypothesis and the prior data, whose independent observation found the climate differences "almost perfectly correlated with the detected error rates" (p. 11).
- **Silence is prevalent:** citing Milliken, Morrison and Hewlin, "85% of respondents reported at least one occasion when they felt unable to raise a concern with their bosses, even though they believed the issue was important" (p. 5).
- **Implicit theories of voice** (p. 33) and the **voice–silence asymmetry** (p. 34), as above.
- **Leadership inclusiveness** has three measured attributes: leaders were approachable and accessible; they acknowledged their fallibility; and they proactively invited input (p. 169).
- **Situational humility:** "Humility is the simple recognition that you don't have all the answers, and you certainly don't have a crystal ball" (p. 168); Schein's term is "Here-and-Now Humility" (p. 168). "Confidence and humility are not opposites" (p. 168).
- **Rules of thumb for a good question** (p. 170): you do not know the answer; it is not answerable Yes or No; it is phrased so as to help others share their thinking in a focused way.
- **Four phrases available to anyone, boss or not** (p. 200): "I don't know. / I need help. / I made a mistake. / I'm sorry." And: "Sometimes, you have to take an interpersonal risk to lower interpersonal risk" (p. 200).
- **Failure archetypes** and type-differentiated responses (pp. 161–163, 180); the blameworthiness gap of 1–4 per cent against 70–90 per cent (p. 176).
- **Whistle-blowing is a symptom of absence:** "Whistle-blowing... is not a reflection of psychological safety but rather an indication of its absence" (p. 202).
- **Cross-cultural:** "research shows that workplace psychological safety is lower in countries with greater 'power distance'"; she concedes the job "is harder in some countries than others" but argues from the Toyota Production System that "it can be done" (p. 207).
- **It is fragile:** "psychological safety is fragile and needs continuous renewal" (p. 188); "Psychological safety is dynamic. A workplace with unusual candor may shift in the face of new leaders or new circumstances" (p. 189).

**From the 1999 abstract only — [1999, unread] throughout.**

- Team psychological safety defined as "a shared belief held by members of a team that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking."
- A multimethod field study of **51 work teams in a single manufacturing company**, with antecedent, process and outcome variables measured.
- Team psychological safety **is** associated with learning behaviour.
- **Team efficacy is not** associated with learning behaviour once team psychological safety is controlled for. This is the paper's discriminant finding and **it does not appear in the 2019 book at all**.
- **Learning behaviour mediates** the relationship between team psychological safety and team performance. The mediation claim likewise does not appear in the book.
- Antecedents identified include **context support** and **team leader coaching**.
- An integrative argument that both team structures and shared beliefs shape outcomes.

**Where the book and the 1999 abstract describe the same study, the book is not consistent with itself.** At p. 12 she writes that she "studied 51 teams in a manufacturing company in the Midwest". At p. 39 she writes, "When I set out to study 50 teams — including sales, production, new product development, and management teams — in a manufacturing company in the mid 1990s". Fifty and fifty-one. The abstract says 51. The book adds detail the abstract does not carry — Midwest, mid-1990s, the four team types, and two performance measures (confidential self-rating on a one-to-seven scale, and ratings by managers and internal customers) — and reports only that "teams with psychological safety also had higher performance", **without restating the mediation** (pp. 39–40).

## Method and evidence base

**The 2019 book** is not an empirical study and should not be cited as one. Its evidence is of three kinds, of sharply different strength.

*The narrative review (Ch. 2).* "My colleagues and I reviewed the academic literature on psychological safety... Reading more than 100 articles, we found plenty of evidence that psychological safety matters" (p. 29). This is a narrative review, not a systematic one: no search protocol, no inclusion criteria, no quality appraisal, no assessment of publication bias, and — decisively — **no negative or null finding is reported except one she frames as confirmatory** (learn-what activities showed "no statistical relationship whatsoever" with psychological safety, p. 37, which she reads as a discriminant success rather than as a null). A review of a literature in which the reviewer is the most-cited author, reporting no disconfirming study across a hundred-plus articles, cannot support a claim about the state of the evidence. For that, use the Frazier meta-analysis in [this folder](/research/04-professional-formation/frazier-psychological-safety-meta-analysis.md).

*The case studies (Part II).* Secondary throughout. She is explicit about the sourcing in her endnotes — Stanford GSB and HBS teaching cases for DaVita and Anglo American, an NTSB accident report and Sullenberger's memoir for Flight 1549, a PBS *NOVA* episode and a *Harvard Business Review* article for Fukushima Daini, Catmull's *Creativity, Inc.* for Pixar, journalism for Uber and Google. These are illustrations selected to fit the thesis, chosen after the outcomes were known. She concedes the sampling problem herself when a reader raises the mirror-image case (see below), but she does not apply the concession to her own case selection.

*The framework (Part III).* Explicitly part experience: "To develop these behavioral tools, I drew from both research and my years of experience studying and consulting with organizations around the world" (p. 158). **No trial of the framework is reported.** The book contains no evidence that following the tool kit raises psychological safety, and Edmondson does not claim otherwise; she offers the Morath case as illustration and the NASA follow-up with the explicit caveat "I tell this story not as proof of culture change but rather as an illustration" (p. 192).

**The 1999 paper's method** — multimethod, within-organisation, 51 teams, cross-sectional — is described in this note only from its abstract. **[1999, unread]** The within-organisation design's strength for internal validity and its cost in external validity, and the observation that a cross-sectional mediation is theoretically rather than temporally ordered, are inferences from the design as the abstract describes it, not statements read in the paper.

## Vocabulary the source introduces

- **Psychological safety** — "the belief that the work environment is safe for interpersonal risk taking" (2019, p. 8). The narrower 1999 form is "a shared belief held by members of a team that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking" **[1999, unread]**.
- **Interpersonal risk** — exposure to appearing "ignorant, incompetent, or disruptive" through asking questions, admitting mistakes, offering ideas or criticising a plan (2019, pp. 5, 13).
- **Teaming** — "the art of communicating and coordinating with people across boundaries of all kinds — expertise, status, and distance", dynamic collaboration in shifting configurations rather than in stable teams (2019, p. xv). From her 2012 book of that name.
- **Implicit theories of voice** — taken-for-granted rules governing when it is appropriate to speak to those higher up (2019, p. 32).
- **Asymmetry of voice and silence** — voice pays the organisation, later, uncertainly; silence pays the self, now, certainly (2019, p. 34).
- **Learning zone / anxiety zone / comfort zone / apathy zone** — the four cells of psychological safety against performance standards (2019, p. 18).
- **Framing the work** — leader behaviour that sets shared expectations about failure, uncertainty and interdependence (2019, pp. 155, 159).
- **Situational humility** — humility warranted by the situation rather than by temperament (2019, p. 168).
- **Leadership inclusiveness** — approachability and accessibility, acknowledged fallibility, proactive invitation of input (2019, p. 169).
- **Preventable / complex / intelligent failure** — the three failure archetypes (2019, pp. 161–163).
- **Blameworthy act** — a violation warranting sanction, which she holds reinforces rather than damages psychological safety when the response is fair and the boundary was clear in advance (2019, p. 178).
- **Learning behaviour** — seeking feedback, sharing information, asking for help, talking about errors, experimenting **[1999, unread]**. The book uses the term, but its operational list traces to the paper.
- **Team efficacy** — a team's shared belief in its own capability; distinct from psychological safety and, in the 1999 study, not a predictor of learning behaviour once safety is controlled **[1999, unread]**.
- **Context support** and **team leader coaching** — the antecedents named in the paper **[1999, unread]**.

## What to remember

- The verified definition is now the **broader** one: the belief that the **work environment** is safe for **interpersonal risk taking** (p. 8). The narrower team-level, "shared belief" wording that this repository has been quoting is the 1999 paper's, and remains unread.
- **She is emphatic about what it is not**, and this is the most reliably verified material in the book. Not niceness, not comfort, not a personality trait, not trust, not lowered standards, not immunity from consequences, not sufficient for performance. Her exact wording is set out under Limits.
- Safety and standards are **orthogonal dimensions**, not a trade-off. The dangerous common case is high standards with low safety.
- The mechanism is a **calculation**, not a disposition. People discount the future and overweight immediate social cost. That is why framing, invitation and response — which change the perceived cost — are the levers, and why exhortation is not.
- **Sanction is inside the model.** Firing for a blameworthy act reinforces psychological safety when the response is fair and the boundary was clear in advance (p. 178).
- **The tool kit is untested.** It is drawn partly from consulting experience (p. 158) and no evaluation of it appears in the book.
- **The efficacy discriminant and the mediation chain — the two findings that make the 1999 paper theoretically load-bearing — are not in the book.** They still rest on an unread abstract.

## Why it matters for the course

This folder is deliberately sceptical of generic disposition training, and Edmondson survives that scepticism better than most constructs in its neighbourhood for a specific reason: her theory terminates in **observable behaviour** rather than in self-reported attitude. Asking a question, admitting an error, requesting help, challenging a plan — a supervisor can see and record each of these. That maps directly onto the Entrustable Professional Activity model in [ten Cate](/research/04-professional-formation/ten-cate-epas.md), where entrustment must rest on observed performance rather than on inferred disposition, and onto the narrative-judgement apparatus in [Govaerts](/research/04-professional-formation/govaerts-narrative-assessment.md).

The 2019 book strengthens the case in one respect and weakens it in another, and the course should hold both.

It strengthens it by supplying the **negative definition**. The single most common failure mode when this construct reaches a classroom is that it collapses into "be nice to each other", which inverts it. Edmondson's own words are now available — "you could say it's the opposite", "nice, in short, is not synonymous with psychologically safe" (p. 16) — so a teacher can close that route with a citation rather than an assertion.

It weakens it by making the **evidential thinness of the prescription** visible. Before reading the book it was possible to treat the leader's tool kit as though the 1999 paper's warrant stood behind it. It does not. The tool kit is a framework derived partly from consulting practice and offered without evaluation. A course that teaches it should teach it as a hypothesis with a plausible mechanism, and should say so out loud.

For forward-deployed engineering specifically, the interpersonal-risk framing explains a failure mode that recurs in the field. An engineer who privately doubts a customer's stated requirement, or has noticed that the evaluation set is contaminated, or does not understand what a colleague just committed, faces exactly Edmondson's calculation: raising it means looking ignorant or negative in front of a client or a senior, immediately and certainly, while the benefit is deferred and uncertain. The competence to build is not the technical ability to notice but the practised willingness to voice, plus the leadership behaviour that lowers the cost of voicing for others. Her four phrases (p. 200) are directly teachable and directly assessable.

The book also supplies something the paper does not: an account of **why competence does not deliver candour for free**. Google's Project Aristotle found nothing in team composition — "no mix of personality types or skills or backgrounds emerged that helped explain which teams performed well" (p. 41) — across 180 teams, before turning to norms. For a technical audience that assumes capability solves everything, that is the useful result. It is also the same conclusion the 1999 efficacy discriminant reaches by a different route, which is worth noting precisely because that route remains unread.

## How to teach it

- **Lecture point:** Teach the 2019 definition word by word — *belief*, *work environment*, *interpersonal*, *risk taking* — then set the 1999 wording beside it and ask which differences matter. The shift from "shared belief... that the team is safe" to "the belief that the work environment is safe" is a real change of scope, and noticing it is an exercise in reading sources rather than summaries.
- **Lecture point:** Teach the five negations verbatim. This is the highest-value twenty minutes in the source.
- **Lecture point:** Draw the two-by-two (p. 18) and place the course itself in a quadrant. Ask students where they think it sits and where it should sit.
- **Lecture point:** Present the voice–silence asymmetry (p. 34) as a decision problem and have students compute it for a specific situation in their current project. The point is that silence is *rational* under the payoff structure, which is why moralising about it does not work.
- **Lecture point:** Present the blameworthiness gap — 1–4 per cent should be, 70–90 per cent are treated as such (p. 176) — and be honest that it is a show of hands in a workshop, not a measurement.
- **Lab exercise:** Instrument the observable behaviours in student teams. Count and timestamp help requests, error disclosures and challenges to the plan. Report the data back without grading it initially.
- **Studio exercise:** Structured error disclosure. Each session opens with every member reporting one thing they got wrong or do not understand since the last session, **starting with the most senior person present**. This operationalises "acknowledged their fallibility" (p. 169).
- **Studio exercise:** The Morath question. The student acting as lead opens with an aspirational, concrete, non-yes/no question of their own construction; the question is then assessed against her three rules of thumb (p. 170).
- **Studio exercise:** Classify a real failure from the students' own work as preventable, complex or intelligent (pp. 161–163), then design the type-appropriate response (p. 180). Disagreement about the classification is the exercise.
- **Studio exercise:** Rotate the team-lead role and assess the lead specifically on the three inclusiveness attributes rather than on their own technical output.
- **Assessment idea:** An EPA for "raising a concern that carries interpersonal cost". Requires observed instances of the student surfacing a problem to a more senior person or a client, with supervisor narrative on how it was handled.
- **Assessment idea:** Assess help-seeking latency — how long a student stays stuck before asking. Both extremes are miscalibrated.
- **Assessment idea:** Assess the *response* to voice, not only voice itself. Edmondson's claim is that "the true test is how leaders respond when people actually do speak up" (p. 157); an assessment that measures only speaking up misses half the construct.
- **What not to do:** Do not set an exercise that asks students to raise their team's psychological safety and then measure whether performance improved. The book supplies no warrant for that causal chain in an intervention design, and the seven-item survey administered to a cohort that has just been taught the construct will measure demand characteristics.

## Limits and cautions

**The five negations, in her words.** These are the most important verified content in the source and are quoted rather than paraphrased on purpose (pp. 15–18).

1. **Not about being nice.** "Working in a psychologically safe environment does not mean that people always agree with one another for the sake of being nice. It also does not mean that people offer unequivocal praise or unconditional support for everything you have to say. In fact, you could say it's the opposite. Psychological safety is about candor, about making it possible for productive disagreement and free exchange of ideas." She names the pathology directly: companies tell her "We have a problem with '[Company Name] Nice'", meaning politeness in meetings, disagreement afterwards in the hallway, and non-implementation of what was agreed. "Nice, in short, is not synonymous with psychologically safe."
2. **Not ease or comfort.** "In a related vein, psychological safety does not imply ease or comfort. In contrast, psychological safety is about candor and willingness to engage in productive conflict so as to learn from different points of view."
3. **Not a personality factor.** "Research shows that the experience of psychological safety at work is not correlated with introversion and extroversion... psychological safety refers to the work climate, and climate affects people with different personality traits in roughly similar ways."
4. **Not another word for trust.** "A key difference is that psychological safety is experienced at a group level... Trust, on the other hand, refers to interactions between two individuals or parties." And on tense: "psychological safety describes a temporally immediate experience", whereas trust is an expectation about some future moment. Her formulation is worth memorising: "trust is about giving others the benefit of the doubt, and psychological safety relates to whether others will give you the benefit of the doubt when, for instance, you have asked for help or admitted a mistake."
5. **Not about lowering performance standards.** "Psychological safety is not an 'anything goes' environment where people are not expected to adhere to high standards or meet deadlines. It is not about becoming 'comfortable' at work." Managers who equate it with an inability to "hold people accountable" hold, in her words, "a misunderstanding of the nature of the phenomenon."

To which add, from the introduction: **not immunity from consequences** and **not a state of high self-regard** (p. xv); and from Chapter 1: **not sufficient** — "psychological safety takes off the brakes... But it's not the fuel that powers the car" (p. 21).

**The book contradicts itself on comfort.** Negations 2 and 5 deny comfort explicitly. The introduction's broad definition asserts it twice: "a climate in which people are comfortable expressing and being themselves" and "they feel comfortable sharing concerns and mistakes" (p. xvi). The two-by-two then names the "comfort zone" as a *failure* quadrant (p. 18). This is not a small slip. The introductory gloss is the sentence a second-hand reader is most likely to lift, and it is the one the rest of the book spends a chapter refuting. **When quoting a definition from this book, quote p. 8, not p. xvi.**

**Fifty or fifty-one teams.** The book says both (pp. 12, 39). The abstract says 51. Do not assert either number in the wiki without saying which source it came from.

**The wiki forbids asserting an effect size for a pedagogy claim, regardless of how deeply the source has been verified.** This is a **namespace rule, not a verification rule** — see [concepts/namespaces.md](/concepts/namespaces.md) and [09-Durable-and-Perishable-Register](/wiki/program/09-Durable-and-Perishable-Register.md). Reading the book in full does not license a magnitude anywhere in `wiki/`. The book carries several magnitudes; they are recorded here so that a future reader need not re-read to find them, and **none of them may cross into the wiki**:

- Gallup 2017, quoted at p. xvi: only 3 in 10 employees strongly agree that their opinions count at work, and moving that to 6 in 10 "could realize a 27 percent reduction in turnover, a 40 percent reduction in safety incidents and a 12 percent increase in productivity". This is **Gallup's arithmetic on Gallup's data, quoted by Edmondson**, not a finding of hers, and it is a projection from a cross-sectional association. It is the weakest number in the book and the most quotable, which is a bad combination.
- 85 per cent of interviewees reporting at least one occasion of felt inability to raise a concern (Milliken, Morrison and Hewlin, quoted at p. 5).
- A tenfold spread in reported errors per thousand patient days across units in the mid-1990s study (p. 9).
- 1–4 per cent against 70–90 per cent on blameworthiness (p. 176) — an informal poll of workshop audiences, not a measurement.
- Employees spend 50 per cent more time collaborating than twenty years ago (Cross, Rebele and Grant, quoted at p. xiv).

Sample sizes in the reviewed studies (47 German firms; 245 members of 60 Taiwanese R&D teams; 195 French student teams; 117 student project teams; 180 Google teams; 14 innovation teams across 18 nations; nurses in 54 Belgian departments; 100-plus quality-improvement teams in 23 hospitals) are facts about those studies' scale and carry no magnitude.

**The narrative review is not a systematic review** and must not be cited as evidence of the state of the literature. Its author is that literature's most-cited contributor and it reports no disconfirming study across a hundred-plus articles. For magnitude and for heterogeneity, use [Frazier](/research/04-professional-formation/frazier-psychological-safety-meta-analysis.md).

**The prescription is unevaluated.** No trial of the leader's tool kit appears in the book. Edmondson does not claim one, and the repository must not imply one.

**Her own concessions, which should be preserved rather than smoothed away.** She is markedly more careful in the FAQ than in the body.

- On the counterexample of successful, fear-driven firms: "let's remember the fallacy of sampling on the dependent variable, a classic error in research... the success in question may in fact be explained by the leader's arrogance and top-down approach; conversely, it may be explained by other factors: good timing, a market vacuum, a genius idea, or even just plain luck." And: "there's a lack of ready access to counterfactual data... We simply have a case of low psychological safety and high company performance for a particular period of time. The first variable may, or may not, explain the second" (p. 203). **This concession applies with equal force to her own Part II case selection, and she does not apply it there.**
- On whether there can be too much: "My short answer? No." (p. 196). As stated this is close to unfalsifiable, and she immediately relocates the problem to discipline and to feedback rather than to the construct.
- On what it does not deliver: "Psychological safety doesn't guarantee effectiveness. It just makes it easier to find out what people have to offer" (p. 204).
- On culture change: "I tell this story not as proof of culture change but rather as an illustration" (p. 192).
- On durability: "psychological safety is fragile and needs continuous renewal" (p. 188); "Psychological safety is dynamic" (p. 189).
- On individual limits: "Narcissism, borderline personalities, low emotional intelligence, and other limitations will make it more difficult to behave in ways that build psychological safety and, in some cases, impossible" (p. 207).
- On culture: psychological safety is lower under high power distance, and "cultural differences in power distance does mean that the job of creating psychological safety is harder in some countries than others" (p. 207).

**The construct has been heavily diluted in practice**, and the dilution runs in exactly the direction the book repudiates. Popular usage equates it with comfort and conflict avoidance. Edmondson's people take risks; they do not avoid them.

**The book is a trade publication.** It is not peer reviewed, it is written to persuade, and its register is promotional in places ("psychological safety is not a 'nice-to-have'... I'll argue that psychological safety is essential to unleashing talent and creating value", p. 26). Verification depth is now high — every sentence quoted here was read — but verification depth is not evidential weight, and this note must not be used to upgrade a claim's warrant merely because the book has been read.

**What the previous version of this note got wrong.**

The previous version was written from the 1999 abstract alone and said so, so its errors are errors of over-confidence rather than of fabrication. Three are material.

1. **It asserted as *the* definition a wording the author had already broadened.** The old note read:

   > "Team psychological safety is defined as *a shared belief held by members of a team that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking*. Three features of that definition matter and are routinely lost when the term is used casually. It is a **shared** belief, a property of the team rather than of the individual..."

   That is the 1999 wording, correctly attributed there. But the old note then instructed the reader: "The definition is precise: a *shared* belief that the team is safe for *interpersonal risk taking*." In the 2019 book the author's own stated definition is "the belief that the work environment is safe for interpersonal risk taking" (p. 8) — **no "shared", no "team"**. The scope is the work environment; the group is the level at which it is observed, not the thing the definition is about. The old note's insistence on "shared" as load-bearing is not supported by the author's current formulation, and any wiki page teaching "shared" as definitional is teaching a twenty-year-old wording as though it were settled.

2. **It attributed to the study a mediation claim that the author does not restate, and then drew a strong practical inference from it.** The old note read:

   > "The second structural finding is mediational. Psychological safety does not affect team performance directly. It affects learning behaviour, and learning behaviour affects performance."

   The abstract supports the mediation claim, so this is not fabricated. But the old note went on: "Raising psychological safety without the team actually doing anything differently — without more questions, more error discussion, more help seeking — should not be expected to improve results." When the author describes the same study in 2019 she reports only that "teams with psychological safety also had higher performance" (pp. 39–40) and does not mention mediation anywhere in the book. **The inference may still be right; it is not corroborated by the one source that has now been read**, and the note should not have been written as though the mechanism were settled.

3. **It over-claimed the strength of the wider evidence base on the strength of a citation count.** The old note read:

   > "the construct's subsequent survival across hundreds of studies in other settings is the real evidence for its generality"

   Reading the book makes clear that its own review of that literature is narrative, non-systematic, conducted by the construct's originator, and reports no disconfirming finding across a hundred-plus articles. Survival in a literature is not evidence of generality when the survival has not been assessed for publication bias. The old note pointed the reader at the Frazier meta-analysis, which was the right instinct; it should not simultaneously have asserted the conclusion.

**Two smaller corrections.** The old note gave the study as "51 work teams in a single manufacturing company" without qualification; the book gives 50 in one place and 51 in another, and adds Midwest, mid-1990s and four team types. And the old note stated that "The paper does not establish that interventions to raise psychological safety succeed" — that remains true, and reading the book strengthens rather than weakens it: **the book does not establish it either**, and its leadership framework is explicitly drawn in part from consulting experience (p. 158).

**Register consistency.** [research/99-source-register/source-register.md](/research/99-source-register/source-register.md) carries one Edmondson row, for the 1999 ASQ paper, marked `copyrighted | synthesis only`. **That row remains correct** — the paper it names is still unread.
